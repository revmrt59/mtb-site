TWO - ( shenayim , Strong's H8147 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shenayim is the cardinal number "two," representing a pair, duality, partnership, or division. In Ruth 1:8, it identifies Orpah and Ruth, Naomi's widowed Moabite daughters-in-law. This specific count highlights the double weight of Naomi's grief and responsibility, while framing a pivotal moment of decision where these two women must choose between their national identity and the God of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East and biblical literature, numbers often carry significance beyond basic mathematics. The number two (shenayim) fundamentally speaks of companionship, witness, and contrast. It is the minimum number required to establish a matter legally under biblical law, signifying a valid and sufficient testimony. In narrative structures, a pair often introduces a point of comparison or divergence. For Naomi, the "two" daughters-in-law represent the dual remaining threads of her deceased sons' lives, binding her to the land of Moab even as she seeks to return to Judah.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Shenayim (masculine) and its feminine counterpart sheteyim function as the primary cardinal numeral for "two." Lexically, the word denotes a pair, a duplicate, or a couple. It is used to group items or people together for cooperative action, comparison, or division. The root implies a doubling or repeating, indicating that the second item or person corresponds directly to the first, creating a complete unit or set of witnesses.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, shenayim is used in several distinct ways:

• Companionship and Strength: It denotes mutual support, showing that two working together achieve far more than one standing alone.

• Legal Testimony: It is the standard for legal truth and confirmation, as no charge could be sustained without the testimony of at least two witnesses.

• Divergent Paths: It often sets up a narrative contrast where two individuals start in the same circumstance but end in vastly different spiritual realities (such as Cain and Abel, or Jacob and Esau). In Ruth, the two daughters-in-law begin on the same road but ultimately split, representing two different responses to the covenant God of Israel.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of shenayim in the opening movement of Ruth lies in the theme of dual witness and personal agency. Orpah and Ruth serve as a double witness to Naomi’s life, her character, and the God she serves. Their shared crisis highlights a profound spiritual truth: proximity to the covenant people of God demands a personal decision. Both women start from the same pagan background, both marry into an Israelite family, and both experience the same devastating loss. However, when faced with Naomi's plea to return, the "two" divide. One chooses the security of her old gods, while the other clings to Yahweh. This demonstrates that redemption is an individual heart-response, not a collective family guarantee.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:8, Naomi addresses her "two daughters-in-law." This comes immediately after the devastating loss of her husband and her "two" sons (Ruth 1:5, where shenayim is also used). The repetition of this number is not accidental; it emphasizes the symmetry of Naomi's grief. She lost two sons, and now she stands with two daughters-in-law. Naomi's instruction for the "two" to return to their mothers' houses is an act of selfless despair. She believes the hand of the Lord has gone out against her, and she wishes to spare these two young women from her own bitter providence. The subsequent narrative hinges on how these "two" react to Naomi's self-emptying love and her hard-pressed faith.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:16 - "Then God made two great lights: the greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night."

• Genesis 6:19 - "And of every living thing of all flesh you shall bring two of every sort into the ark, to keep them alive with you; they shall be male and female."

• Ruth 1:5 - "Then both Mahlon and Chilion died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband."

• Ecclesiastes 4:9 - "Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their labor."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, shenayim is in the dual number form, a feature of the Hebrew language used specifically for things that naturally come in pairs (like eyes, hands, or feet). Applying this dual form to Orpah and Ruth suggests they were viewed as a singular unit of comfort and grief alongside Naomi. The breaking of this pair when Orpah turns back and Ruth stays makes Ruth’s individual devotion shine even brighter against the backdrop of what was expected of them both.
