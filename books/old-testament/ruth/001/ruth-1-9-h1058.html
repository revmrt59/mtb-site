WEPT - ( bakah , Strong's H1058 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bakah refers to the physical act of weeping, crying, or lamenting, expressing deep emotional distress, grief, or repentance. In Ruth 1:9, it captures the intense, shared sorrow of Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth as they face a heartbreaking separation and an uncertain future after the devastating losses of their husbands. This audible, communal expression of grief highlights the deep relational bonds forged in the furnace of shared suffering.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, weeping was rarely a silent, internal affair. It was typically an outward, physical, and highly vocal expression of intense emotion. Bakah refers to the physical shedding of tears, often accompanied by lifting up one's voice, tearing garments, or putting on sackcloth. It reflects the raw vulnerability of human suffering, bereavement, and deep relational connection. Tears were not viewed as a sign of weakness, but as a genuine, necessary response to the harsh realities of a fallen world.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Bakah is a primitive root meaning to weep, bewail, cry, or shed tears. The term covers a wide semantic range, including:

• Weeping out of intense physical or emotional pain.

• Mourning and lamenting for the dead.

• Weeping in prayer as an expression of desperate supplication or repentance.

• Collective, audible weeping in times of national crisis or calamity.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb occurs over 100 times in the Old Testament. It frequently paints a picture of profound relational grief:

• It is used to describe Abraham mourning and weeping for Sarah upon her death, demonstrating that even giants of faith experience deep sorrow.

• It depicts Joseph’s emotional reunions with his brothers and his father, where weeping serves as a release of years of hidden pain and a marker of reconciliation.

• The word is often paired with the Hebrew phrase nasa qol ("to lift up the voice"), creating a word picture of weeping that is loud, unreserved, and shared.

### 4. Theological Implications

• Bakah reveals that God validates human sorrow. Weeping is not antithetical to faith; rather, it is a vital component of the human experience in a broken world. The Scriptures do not encourage a stoic denial of grief. Instead, tears are often directed toward God in lament, serving as a bridge of raw honesty between the hurting believer and the Creator. God hears the voice of those who weep, and He records their tears, showing that suffering is never unnoticed by Him.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:9, bakah occurs at a painful crossroads. Naomi, having lost her husband and both sons in Moab, decides to return to Bethlehem. As she kisses her Moabite daughters-in-law goodbye, releasing them to return to their families, the text states that "they lifted up their voices and wept."

• This is not a quiet, polite farewell. It is an explosive release of shared grief.

• These three widows have been bound together by tragedy. The weeping highlights the deep love and mutual respect that had developed between Naomi and her pagan daughters-in-law.

• The act of weeping together underscores the sheer weight of their helplessness in a patriarchal society where widowhood often meant poverty and vulnerability.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 23:2 - "So Sarah died in Kirjath Arba (that is, Hebron) in the land of Canaan; and Abraham came to mourn for Sarah and to weep for her."

• Genesis 45:14 - "Then he fell on his brother Benjamin’s neck and wept, and Benjamin wept on his neck."

• 1 Samuel 1:7 - "So it was, year by year, when she went up to the house of the Lord, that she provoked her; therefore she wept and did not eat."

• Joel 2:17 - "Let the priests, who minister to the Lord, weep between the vestibule and the altar; let them say, 'Spare Your people, O Lord...'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The root bakah is closely related to the noun beki, which means "weeping" or "overflowing." This connection shows that weeping is an overflow of the heart. While the book of Ruth begins in the darkness of famine, death, and weeping (bakah), it moves steadily toward redemption, joy, and fullness. The weeping of Ruth 1:9 sets a stark baseline of sorrow that magnifies the beauty of the covenant faithfulness (hesed) and restoration that God provides by the end of the narrative.
