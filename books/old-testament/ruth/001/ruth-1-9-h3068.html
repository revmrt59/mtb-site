LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh (translated as "LORD" in all capital letters in the NKJV) is the personal, covenant-keeping name of the one true God of Israel. Derived from the Hebrew verb meaning "to be," it highlights God's self-existence, eternal presence, and absolute faithfulness to His promises. In Ruth 1:9, as Naomi bids farewell to her grieving daughters-in-law in foreign Moab, she invokes this sacred covenant name. Even in her deep bitterness and despair, Naomi appeals to Yahweh to extend His protective care, covenant loyalty (chesed), and relational security to these Gentile women, demonstrating that God's grace and sovereignty transcend geographic and national boundaries.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Yahweh is the Tetragrammaton, the sacred four-letter name of God (YHWH) revealed uniquely to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus 3:14. It is built upon the Hebrew verb hayah, which means "to be" or "to become."

• Out of deep reverence and fear of taking this holy name in vain, Jewish readers traditionally substituted the title Adonai (Lord) when reading the text aloud. English translations preserve this distinction by rendering Yahweh as "LORD" in small capital letters, distinguishing it from Adonai, which is rendered as "Lord" in standard lowercase.

• Historically, while Elohim represents God as the transcendent, all-powerful Creator of the universe, Yahweh is the deeply personal name by which He binds Himself to His people in formal covenant. It represents a God who is not distant or detached, but actively involved in human history, hearing the cries of His people and delivering them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The name designates the self-existent, eternal, and unchangeable God. It conveys the meaning "He who is," "He who exists," or "He who causes to be."

• Lexically, the name represents the active, living presence of God. It implies that God is the source of all life and history, completely self-sufficient, requiring nothing from His creation, yet choosing to relate to it with absolute fidelity.

• The name carries the inherent qualities of immutability, holiness, justice, and mercy. Because He is Yahweh, He is the ultimate guarantor of oaths, covenants, and promises.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Yahweh is the most frequently used divine name in the Old Testament, occurring over 6,800 times. It is the defining marker of Israelite monotheism.

• The name is frequently paired with covenant-oriented word pictures:

• The Shepherd: Expressing intimate, daily guidance, provision, and protection (Psalm 23:1).

• The Husband: Highlighting the intense, exclusive, and passionate love God has for His covenant people, as well as His grief when they wander (Isaiah 54:5).

• The Strong Tower: Depicting a place of absolute safety and refuge for those who trust in His character (Proverbs 18:10).

• The Redeemer: Depicting a near kinsman who steps in to pay a debt, secure freedom, and restore inheritance—a major underlying theme in the Book of Ruth.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Loyalty: Yahweh is inherently tied to the concept of chesed—loyal, covenant love. Because He is Yahweh, He cannot abandon His promises even when His people are unfaithful.

• Sovereignty in the Ordinary: The name reminds believers that the God who rules the cosmos is also the God who acts in the quiet, mundane details of life. He is sovereign over national famines, family tragedies, and the quiet steps of a widow gleaning in a field.

• Eternal Self-Sufficiency: He has no beginning and no end. His purposes cannot be thwarted by human failure or geographical displacement.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:9, Naomi prays, "The LORD grant that you may find rest, each in the house of her husband." This occurs in Moab, a pagan land outside the borders of Israel, after a decade of tragedy including the deaths of Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion.

• Naomi is bitter and empty, yet her theology remains firmly anchored in the covenant God of Israel. By invoking Yahweh rather than a generic title for God or the local Moabite deity Chemosh, Naomi recognizes Yahweh as the only true source of real blessing, peace, and security (menuchah), even while standing on foreign soil.

• Furthermore, Naomi asks Yahweh to show mercy to Orpah and Ruth. This highlights a profound theological truth: the covenant God of Israel is not a localized deity. His authority, kindness, and ability to grant "rest" extend to Moab and to Gentiles. Naomi prays that Yahweh will provide these young widows with security and a home, which points forward to how Yahweh will ultimately answer this very prayer through Boaz in Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:4: "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens,"

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

• Proverbs 18:10: "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run into it and are safe."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Book of Ruth begins with a lack of God's physical presence (famine, death, exile), but the dialogue of the characters is saturated with the name Yahweh. It is used in blessings, greetings, and oaths (Ruth 1:8-9, 1:17, 2:4, 2:12, 3:10, 4:11). This literary structure emphasizes that although God does not speak directly or perform overt, miraculous signs in the book, His covenant presence (Yahweh) is actively shaping every conversation, relationship, and seemingly accidental turn of events to bring about redemption.
