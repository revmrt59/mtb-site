HUSBAND - ( ish , Strong's H376 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ish primarily means "man" or "husband," denoting a male individual, often in contrast to a woman ('ishshah) or in a specific covenantal relationship. In the context of Ruth 1:9, Naomi uses the word to express her deep desire for Orpah and Ruth to find security, rest, and marital companionship in the homes of new husbands back in their homeland of Moab. This highlights the cultural and spiritual significance of marriage as a place of safety, provision, and identity in the ancient Near East.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'ish is one of the most fundamental terms in the Old Testament. While the generic word 'adam refers to humanity or mankind as a whole, 'ish denotes an individual male, a person of strength, or a specific husband. The term paints a picture of a responsible individual who stands as a pillar of protection and covenant faithfulness.

The relationship between 'ish (man/husband) and 'ishshah (woman/wife) is established in Genesis 2:23-24, where the woman is taken out of man. This phonetic and relational connection underscores a designed partnership. When used in the context of marriage, 'ish represents a man who has entered into a covenant to care for, protect, and provide for a woman, establishing a household (bayit) where life can flourish.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Individual Male: A physical man, distinguished from a woman or a child.

• Husband: A male companion in a covenantal marriage relationship.

• Person of Standing: A man of valor, strength, or high social status (contrasted with adam as a commoner in some contexts).

• Each / Every One: Used distributively in Hebrew idioms to mean "each person" or "every man."

In the marital context, the word signifies a designated protector and provider. Unlike the word ba'al (which can mean "master" or "owner"), 'ish emphasizes partnership, companionship, and relational intimacy.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses 'ish to depict relational closeness. A beautiful word picture of this is found in the prophets, where Israel's restored relationship with God is described in marital terms.

• Intimate Companion vs. Master: In Hosea 2:16, a powerful distinction is made between calling God Baali ("My Master") and Ishi ("My Husband"). The use of 'ish signifies a shift from a relationship of servile fear to one of covenantal love, intimacy, and mutual belonging.

• The Head of the Household: The "house of the husband" (bet 'ish) represents a sanctuary. In the ancient world, a woman's social standing, economic protection, and physical safety were directly tied to her father's house or her husband's house. To have an 'ish was to have a covering and a place of belonging.

### 4. Theological Implications

The usage of 'ish in the Old Testament reveals several key theological truths about God’s design for marriage and community:

• Marriage as a Sanctuary of Rest: God designed the marital union to be a source of menuhah (rest, peace, and security). The husband is meant to be the earthly agent of God's protective care for the wife.

• Redemption of the Vulnerable: In the patriarchal structure of ancient Israel, the widow, the orphan, and the stranger were the most vulnerable members of society. The loss of an 'ish meant a loss of economic security and social protection. God's law provided mechanisms (such as levirate marriage and kinsman-redeemer provisions) to ensure that widows could find restoration and a new 'ish to redeem them from destitution.

• A Picture of Christ and the Church: The Old Testament covenant between God and Israel, viewed through the lens of 'ish and 'ishshah, lays the theological foundation for the New Testament mystery of Christ and the Church.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:9, Naomi prays, "The LORD grant that you may find rest, each in the house of her husband."

Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth have all experienced devastating loss. Naomi's husband ('ish), Elimelech, has died, and her two sons have also died, leaving all three women as vulnerable widows in Moab. In this patriarchal culture, a widow without sons was completely exposed to poverty and social isolation.

When Naomi urges her daughters-in-law to return to Moab, she is speaking out of realistic pastoral concern. She knows that their best hope for survival and a peaceful life is to find a new 'ish who can provide a "house" (bayit) and "rest" (menuhah). In this verse, 'ish is not just a biological male; it represents a sanctuary of security and restoration. Naomi's prayer is that Yahweh will show covenant kindness (hesed) to these young women by leading them to husbands who will provide the physical and emotional rest they so desperately need.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The entire narrative of the Book of Ruth moves from the loss of husbands to the redemption found in a new husband. In Ruth 1:9, Naomi prays for the women to find "rest" (menuhah) in the house of a husband. In Ruth 3:1, Naomi says, "My daughter, shall I not seek security [rest] for you, that it may be well with you?"

This search for rest and security is finally realized in Ruth 4:13, when Boaz takes Ruth and she becomes his wife. Boaz becomes the 'ish who provides the shelter, protection, and legacy that Naomi prayed for in chapter 1. Ultimately, this earthly provision points to the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ, who provides eternal rest for His bride.
