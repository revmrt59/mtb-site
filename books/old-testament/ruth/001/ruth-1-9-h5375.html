LIFTED UP - ( nasa, Strong's H5375 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nasa is a versatile term meaning to lift, carry, take, or bear. It spans a wide range of physical, emotional, and spiritual expressions, ranging from physically lifting an object or raising one's voice in weeping, to bearing the weight of guilt and sin in forgiveness. In Ruth 1:9, the word depicts Orpah and Ruth lifting up their voices in shared, overwhelming grief as they face a heartbreaking parting from Naomi.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary physical concept of nasa is the action of reaching down, taking hold of an object, and raising it upward. It implies effort, movement, and elevation. In the ancient Near East, this word picture of lifting up was applied to many aspects of daily life. To "lift up the feet" meant to begin a journey; to "lift up the eyes" meant to look up with expectation or discovery; to "lift up the hands" was the classic posture of prayer or taking an oath. The term captures both the physical act of hoisting a load and the metaphorical act of elevating one's internal state, thoughts, or emotions.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, nasa means to lift, raise, bear, carry, or take away. The semantic range includes:

• Physical elevation: Raising something high, lifting a banner, or carrying a physical burden.

• Vocal expression: Raising the voice to speak, shout, sing, or weep.

• Legal and spiritual substitution: Bearing guilt or punishment on behalf of another, or lifting and carrying away sin (the foundational Old Testament concept of forgiveness).

• Attitude and desire: Directing one's heart, soul, or desires upward toward a specific object or toward God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Burden Bearer: Nasa is used for carrying a heavy load. It illustrates the physical reality of transport, but also the emotional and mental weight of carrying sorrow, worry, or guilt.

• The Lifted Countenance: When a face is "lifted up," it indicates joy, confidence, and acceptance, contrasting with a fallen countenance which signifies anger, shame, or depression.

• Vocal Release: When paired with "voice" (qol), nasa indicates a sudden, unhindered release of vocal expression. It is the physical raising of one's vocal volume to match the intensity of the heart's condition, whether in joyous praise or agonizing lament.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nasa is one of the most significant terms for forgiveness in the Hebrew Bible. To forgive is to "lift up" and "carry away" the burden of guilt, removing it from the offender. When God forgives, He lifts the crushing weight of sin off the sinner's shoulders. Conversely, when a person must "bear" (nasa) their own iniquity, it means they must carry the heavy, destructive consequences of their rebellion. Furthermore, the term describes God's fatherly care, depicting Him as carrying His people through the wilderness as a father carries his son.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:9, Naomi attempts to release her daughters-in-law from any obligation to her, praying that the Lord grant them rest in the homes of future husbands. She then kisses them goodbye. The text states that they "lifted up their voice and wept." Here, nasa operates in close connection with their physical and emotional crisis. The sudden realization of parting, combined with the accumulated grief of losing their husbands, erupts. Their grief is not silent or subdued; it is lifted up (nasa) in a loud, communal lament. The word highlights the depth of their genuine affection for Naomi and the sheer weight of the tragedy that has dismantled their family.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:16 - "Then she went and sat down opposite him at a distance of about a bowshot; for she said to herself, 'Let me not see the death of the boy.' So she sat opposite him, and lifted up her voice and wept."

• Numbers 6:26 - "The LORD lift up His countenance upon you, and give you peace."

• Psalm 25:1 - "To You, O LORD, I lift up my soul."

• Psalm 32:1 - "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the narrative of Ruth, which begins in the dark valleys of famine, foreign exile, and death, the physical act of "lifting up" serves as a subtle literary pivot. While the characters have spent the first eight verses sinking into deeper loss and sorrow, the lifting of their voices in verse 9 marks the beginning of vocalizing their pain. This outward expression of grief is the necessary precursor to the turning back and eventual restoration that takes place as they journey toward Bethlehem.
