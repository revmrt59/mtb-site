KISSED - ( nashaq, Strong's H5401 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word nashaq primarily refers to kissing, expressing deep affection, familial love, or covenantal loyalty. In the ancient Near East, a kiss was not primarily romantic; rather, it was a culturally significant gesture of greeting, farewell, reconciliation, or homage. In Ruth 1:9, Naomi's kiss is a poignant gesture of blessing and painful departure, signaling her selfless desire for her daughters-in-law to return to their homeland for their own security, despite her own deep grief and loneliness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of nashaq carries the physical concept of touching, bringing things into close, harmonious contact, or joining together. In the ancient Near East, kissing was a physical manifestation of relational binding, kinship, and covenant. It occurred between family members to seal departures, reunions, and treaties. To kiss someone on the head, cheek, or neck was to recognize them as an intimate part of one's life and household. When used at a moment of departure, the kiss was both a blessing of peace and a formal release from immediate familial presence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Nashaq is a primary verb meaning to kiss, touch, or handle. In its literal sense, it denotes the touching of lips to lips, cheek, or hand as an expression of affection, respect, or relational bonding. It occurs over thirty times in the Old Testament. While a secondary, homonymous root of nashaq means to arm or equip for battle (touching or joining weapons), the primary usage throughout the historical narratives represents familial intimacy, social protocol, and covenantal relationship.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs nashaq in several relational dynamics:

• The Kiss of Farewell: Marking a painful parting or the release of an obligation (Genesis 31:55; Ruth 1:9, 14).

• The Kiss of Reunion: Signifying reconciliation, relief, and restored relationship after long separation or conflict (Genesis 33:4; Genesis 45:15).

• The Kiss of Homage and Submission: A sign of allegiance, loyalty, and worship to a ruler, king, or deity (1 Kings 19:18; Psalm 2:12).

• The Figurative Touch: Used poetically to describe abstract virtues coming into perfect, harmonious alignment, such as righteousness and peace "kissing" each other (Psalm 85:10).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nashaq underscores the deeply relational and tactile nature of covenant love (chesed) in the Old Testament. Relationships in scripture are not merely legal or intellectual; they are deeply emotional and physically expressed. Naomi's kiss is an expression of self-sacrificing love. By kissing Orpah and Ruth, she is not merely saying goodbye; she is actively blessing them and releasing them from any filial obligation to care for her in her widowhood. This action highlights the tragedy of the broken home of Elimelech while setting the stage for the extraordinary, covenantal loyalty that Ruth will display in response.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:9, the kiss serves as the catalyst for a major emotional climax. Naomi has lost her husband and her two sons in Moab. As she prepares to return to Judah, she attempts to send Orpah and Ruth back to their mothers' houses. She prays that the Lord will grant them "rest" in the homes of future husbands, and then she kisses them. The response to this physical gesture of release is immediate and intense: "they lifted up their voices and wept."

The contrast in verse 14 deepens the nuance of this word. While both women weep, Orpah kisses (nashaq) Naomi goodbye, accepting the release and returning to her people. Ruth, however, refuses to let the kiss be a final farewell; instead, she "clings" (dabaq) to Naomi. Orpah's kiss was a beautiful, respectful fulfillment of familial duty, but Ruth's clinging transcended customary cultural expectations to forge a lifetime covenant.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 29:11 - "Then Jacob kissed Rachel, and lifted up his voice and wept."

• Genesis 33:4 - "But Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept."

• Genesis 45:15 - "Moreover he kissed all his brothers and wept over them, and after that his brothers talked with him."

• Ruth 1:14 - "And they lifted up their voices and wept again; then Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her."

• 1 Samuel 20:41 - "...and they kissed each other; and they wept together, but David wept more."

• Psalm 85:10 - "Mercy and truth have met together; Righteousness and peace have kissed."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful literary and emotional progression in Ruth 1:9–14: the kiss (nashaq), the weeping, and the clinging (dabaq). For pastors and teachers, this progression illustrates the levels of relational commitment. Orpah's kiss represents the limit of natural affection and cultural duty—it was genuine, loving, and appropriate. Ruth's refusal to merely kiss goodbye, choosing instead to cling, represents supernatural, covenantal commitment. This word study helps contrast normal human affection with the extraordinary chesed (lovingkindness) that mirrors God's relentless commitment to His people.
