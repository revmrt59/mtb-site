GRANT - ( nathan , Strong's H5414 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nathan is a foundational covenant word meaning to give, bestow, set, or commit. In Ruth 1:9, translated as "grant," Naomi uses this word in a prayer of blessing, asking Yahweh to actively bestow the gift of rest and security upon her grieving, widowed daughters-in-law. It highlights that true rest is not a human achievement but a sovereign, gracious gift from God.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root nathan is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Old Testament, occurring over 2,000 times. Its primary concrete action is the physical hand-to-hand transfer of an object from one person to another. From this basic physical gesture of "giving," the word expands metaphorically to describe the granting of abstract realities such as favor, peace, identity, authority, and covenant blessings. In the ancient Near East, giving was rarely a neutral transaction; it established a relationship or confirmed a covenant. When God is the subject of nathan, the word pictures Him as the generous Benefactor whose open hand provides life, land, and security to His people.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of nathan includes:

• To give, present, or bestow a gift.

• To grant a request, permit, or answer a prayer.

• To set, place, or appoint someone to a position.

• To deliver up or surrender an enemy.

In Ruth 1:9, nathan functions as a prayerful wish (an optative or jussive expression of blessing). Naomi asks that Yahweh "give" or "grant" (yitten—a form of nathan) to Orpah and Ruth a specific, life-altering gift: menuchah, which is a state of rest, security, and wholeness.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, nathan is used in several rich ways:

• The Gift of Covenant Land: God repeatedly promises to "give" the land of Canaan to Abraham’s descendants, showcasing His faithfulness to His word.

• The Gift of Answers to Prayer: When a petitioner cries out to God, and God responds by fulfilling the request, He "grants" their desire, demonstrating His personal, relational responsiveness.

• The Gift of a Spouse and Family: Children and spouses are described as heritage and gifts that God "gives" to establish households.

In the book of Ruth, nathan serves as a thematic bookend. In Ruth 1:9, Naomi prays that God would "grant" them rest. In Ruth 4:13, the narrative concludes with the statement that the Lord "gave" (nathan) Ruth conception, leading to the birth of Obed. The prayer asked in the beginning is physically granted by God at the end.

### 4. Theological Implications

Naomi’s use of nathan reveals deep theological truths about God’s character and human need:

• God is the Ultimate Source of Rest: Naomi recognizes that she is powerless to provide for her daughters-in-law. She cannot give them husbands, security, or a future. She turns to Yahweh because He is the only one who possesses the resources to "grant" true rest.

• Grace Supersedes Bitterness: Although Naomi is experiencing profound grief and feels the hand of God has gone out against her (Ruth 1:13, 20-21), she still speaks of Yahweh as a God of grace who gives good gifts.

• The Connection to Covenant Loyalty (Chesed): In the preceding verse (Ruth 1:8), Naomi prays that the Lord will deal kindly (chesed) with them. The "granting" of rest in verse 9 is the concrete expression of that covenant kindness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:9, the context is one of bitter departure and deep grief. Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth are three childless widows in a society where women without male protectors are extremely vulnerable. Naomi is releasing her daughters-in-law to return to their homeland, Moab.

By praying, "The LORD grant that you may find rest," Naomi is asking God to intervene in their desperate circumstances. The "rest" (menuchah) she desires for them is not merely physical sleep or relaxation, but the security, social protection, provision, and peace that came through a stable home and marriage in ancient Israel. Naomi asks God to act as their ultimate Guardian and Provider by giving them new homes.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 1 Samuel 1:17 – "Then Eli answered and said, 'Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant [nathan] your petition which you have asked of Him.'"

• 1 Kings 3:5 – "At Gibeon the LORD appeared to Solomon in a dream by night; and God said, 'Ask! What shall I give [nathan] you?'"

• Ruth 4:13 – "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave [nathan] her conception, and she bore a son."

• Psalm 21:4 – "He asked life of You, and You gave [nathan] it to him— Length of days forever and ever."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The structure of the Book of Ruth shows how God works through human agency to grant the very prayers His people pray. Naomi prays that God would grant them rest in the house of a husband (Ruth 1:9). Later, Boaz acts as the redeemer to provide this security, and Naomi herself seeks "rest" for Ruth (Ruth 3:1). Ultimately, the Lord grants this rest through the marriage of Boaz and Ruth, showing that God's sovereign "granting" of blessings often manifests through the loving, obedient actions of His people.
