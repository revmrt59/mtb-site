VOICES - ( qol , Strong's H6963 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word qol refers to a sound, voice, noise, or call. In the Old Testament, it represents the primary instrument of communication, emotional expression, and divine revelation. In Ruth 1:9, the lifting up of the "voice" in weeping describes a shared, uninhibited outpour of grief and affection between Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth. This expression goes beyond mere quiet crying; it represents an audible, communal lamentation that lays bare the deep relational bonds and severe pain of their shared tragedy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word qol fundamentally denotes any audible sound, whether it is the voice of a human, the sound of an instrument, the rustling of leaves, or the thunderous roar of God. It is an active, dynamic word. In ancient Semitic culture, the voice was not merely acoustic vibration but the external projection of a person's inner soul, character, and immediate emotional state. To "lift up" the voice (nasa qol) is a common Hebrew idiom denoting a transition from silence or normal speech to an intense, public, and passionate vocalization, most frequently associated with weeping, crying out for help, or shouting for joy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun qol refers to a sound, voice, call, or noise. Its semantic range includes:

• The human voice in speaking, singing, or crying out.

• The sound of weeping, lamentation, and mourning.

• The voice of Yahweh, often associated with thunder, command, and prophetic revelation.

• Animal sounds or noises made by inanimate objects (e.g., chariots, musical instruments, wind).

In the grammatical construction of Ruth 1:9, the verb nasa ("to lift up") combined with qol ("voice") indicates an audible, emotional burst of weeping.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Voice of Human Emotion: When individuals in the ancient Near East experienced profound grief, anxiety, or relief, they did not suppress their emotions. They "lifted up their voices." This physical lifting up of sound portrays the emotion rising from the depths of the soul and filling the air.

• The Voice of Nature and God: The same word qol is used for thunder (the "voice" of the Lord) in Psalm 29. This connects the human voice of weeping with the powerful, dramatic forces of life and the cosmos. A voice is an announcement of presence; when they lifted up their voices, they made their grief present and undeniable.

4. Theological Implications

• Lament is a holy and necessary component of the human relationship with God and one another. In the Bible, God hears the qol of the weeping, the oppressed, and the brokenhearted.

• By recording the audible weeping of these three widows, the text honors their grief. God does not bypass or minimize human suffering; the "voice" of sorrow is recorded as an essential part of the redemptive narrative.

• The communal lifting of the voice demonstrates a shared covenantal weight. Their grief is not isolated; it is a shared reality that binds them together before God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:9, Naomi attempts to dismiss her daughters-in-law, blessing them and kissing them goodbye. The sudden eruption of qol (translated "voices" in the plural to reflect the multiple people crying, though the Hebrew uses the singular collective with a plural suffix) breaks the silence of their impending separation.

• This collective weeping reveals that Orpah and Ruth did not view Naomi merely as a mother-in-law by obligation, but held deep, genuine love for her.

• The qol here is the sound of absolute vulnerability. They are three widows in a patriarchal society, facing economic ruin, starvation, and isolation. Their lifted voices represent a cry of helplessness in the face of death and bereavement.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:16 - "Then she went and sat down opposite him at a distance of about a bowshot; for she said to herself, 'Let me not see the death of the boy.' So she sat opposite him, and lifted up her voice and wept."

• Genesis 29:11 - "Then Jacob kissed Rachel, and lifted up his voice and wept."

• Judges 2:4 - "So it was, when the Angel of the Lord spoke these words to all the children of Israel, that the people lifted up their voice and wept."

• 1 Samuel 30:4 - "Then David and the people who were with him lifted up their voices and wept, until they had no more power to weep."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew narrative, the physical gesture of kissing (nashaq) followed immediately by the lifting of the voice (qol) in weeping forms a powerful literary diptych of covenantal affection. The auditory nature of qol reminds the reader that biblical faith is highly auditory—people hear the word of God, and God hears the voice of His people. The audible sighing and weeping of these women is a silent prayer that the God of Israel ultimately hears and answers through the rest of the Book of Ruth.
