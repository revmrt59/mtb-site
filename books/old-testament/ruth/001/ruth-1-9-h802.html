EACH - ( 'ishshah , Strong's H802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ishshah is the standard Old Testament term for "woman" or "wife." In Ruth 1:9, it is translated as "each," serving a distributive grammatical function. Naomi uses this term to address Orpah and Ruth individually, wishing for each young widow to find security, peace, and a new home in the household of a future husband. This word highlights the personal, individual care Naomi desires for both of her daughters-in-law, framing her blessing not just as a general wish for the group, but as a specific, intimate prayer for each woman's personal restoration and marital protection in their native land of Moab.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'ishshah is ancient and foundational, first appearing in the Creation account. It is the feminine counterpart to 'ish (man or husband). The word picture is built on relational correspondence and companionate fit. In Genesis 2:23, the first man declares that she shall be called "woman" ('ishshah) because she was taken out of "man" ('ish).

While the word is rooted in this creation-era depiction of female identity and wifehood, Hebrew grammar also utilizes 'ishshah distributively. When used to address or describe a group of women, it functions as "each" or "every one." This grammatical feature paints a picture of a collective group being dissolved into distinct individuals, ensuring that every single woman is seen, addressed, and cared for in her own right.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of 'ishshah is a female human being, a woman, or a wife.

• A Female Human Being / Woman: It distinguishes a female from a male ('ish) of any age or status.

• Wife: In the context of marriage, 'ishshah specifically designates a wife in covenant relationship with her husband.

• Each / Every One: When applied distributively to a group of women, it functions as a pronoun meaning "each" or "every one."

• One to Another: In idiomatic, architectural, or figurative contexts, when paired with 'achot ("sister"), it is translated as "one to another" or "each to its neighbor," describing how separate parts (such as the curtains of the Tabernacle) interlock to form a unified whole.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, 'ishshah is used over 700 times, illustrating the vital role of women in the covenant community, the home, and the redemptive story.

• The Covenant Companion: In the domestic sphere, the 'ishshah is depicted as the essential partner to the 'ish. She is the helper, the builder of the home, and the mother of children.

• The Distributive Individual: In civic and legal contexts, when instructions are given to women, 'ishshah is used to individualize the instruction, reminding the readers that God's law and grace apply to each woman individually.

• The Picture of Unified Strength: The idiomatic use of 'ishshah in the construction of the Tabernacle (Exodus 26:3) is a beautiful picture. The curtains are coupled "one ['ishshah] to another." This portrays a relational symmetry where separate, individual elements are woven together to form a single, sacred dwelling place for God's presence.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of 'ishshah shines brightest when contrasted with the surrounding ancient cultures, which often viewed women as mere property or collective units. The Hebrew Scriptures consistently elevate the 'ishshah, demonstrating God's personal covenantal care for women as distinct individuals.

Naomi’s prayer in Ruth 1:9 invokes the covenant name of God (Yahweh) to grant "rest" (menuchah) to each ('ishshah) woman. Rest here is not merely physical inactivity, but the security, identity, and protection found within a godly marriage covenant. The theological implication is that God cares for the specific, individual domestic and spiritual security of these vulnerable, widowed Gentile women. Yahweh is not just the God of nations or patriarchs; He is the God who provides "rest" for each woman in her specific hour of grief and destitution.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:9, Naomi is speaking to her two Moabite daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth. Having lost her husband and both of her sons, Naomi is preparing to return to Bethlehem. She urges these young widows to return to their mothers' houses in Moab.

By employing the word 'ishshah (translated "each"), Naomi individualizes her blessing. She prays that Yahweh will grant "rest, each ['ishshah] in the house of her husband ['ish]."

The literary symmetry here is striking and poignant:

• Naomi pairs 'ishshah (each woman/wife) with 'ish (husband).

• This linguistic pairing deliberately recalls the creation ideal of Genesis 2.

• Naomi recognizes that while these three women have suffered a collective tragedy, their restoration must occur individually.

• Naomi is praying that the tragic disruption of their lives will be redeemed by Yahweh returning each 'ishshah to her proper place of marital security and covenantal rest in the house of a future 'ish. It is a prayer for the restoration of the creation order in their broken lives.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural and linguistic relationship between 'ishshah and 'ish represents one of the most prominent wordplays in the Hebrew Bible. It highlights functional complementarity.

Additionally, the idiomatic use of 'ishshah as "one to another" in the Tabernacle's construction instructions (Exodus 26) suggests that the Hebrew mind saw the feminine word for woman/wife as a natural picture of harmonious connection and mutual support. Just as the separate curtains of the Tabernacle were bound together to form a beautiful sanctuary, so individual women in Israel were meant to be joined in community and covenant, forming a dwelling place for God's glory.
