Ruth Chapter 1

Chapter Explanation – Ruth

1. Famine in the Land (1:1-2)

In the opening verses of the book of Ruth, we are introduced to a family living during a dark period in Israel’s history. A famine strikes the land, leading a man named Elimelech to move his wife Naomi and their two sons from Bethlehem into Moab in search of survival and provision. What begins as a temporary journey soon becomes a prolonged stay in a foreign land. Over time, Elimelech dies, and then both of Naomi’s sons die, leaving her in a condition of deep grief, vulnerability, seeming hopelessness, and bitterness.

Ruth 1:1-2 Now it came to pass, in the days when the judges ruled, that there was a famine in the land. And a certain man of Bethlehem, Judah, went to dwell in the country of Moab, he and his wife and his two sons. 2 The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion— Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there. (NKJV))

UNDERSTAND

The story begins “in the days when the judges ruled.” The period of the judges was the time in Israel’s history between the death of Joshua and the establishment of the Israel’s first king under Saul. It is recorded primarily in the book of Judges and spans roughly 300–350 years, somewhere around 1380–1050 BC.

This was one of the darkest and most spiritually compromised periods in Israel’s history. The nation repeatedly fell into idolatry, compromise, and rebellion against God. The book of Judges repeatedly says, “everyone did what was right in his own eyes.” Israel experienced recurring cycles of sin, oppression, repentance, and deliverance during this period.

Sometime during this period, there was a famine in the land. In the ancient world, famine was not a minor inconvenience or temporary economic downturn. There were no grocery stores or steady food sources beyond what could be grown, stored, traded for, or purchased. A famine threatened survival itself. Crops failed, food became scarce, families suffered, and entire communities could be pushed into desperation, migration, debt, or death.

In the Old Testament, famine often carried spiritual significance. At times, God used famine to test and prepare faith, to get His people’s attention, to chasten a nation, or to move people into His larger purposes. Famine in the Promised Land had special significance as God had warned Israel if they turned away from Him, He would use famine as a way of chastening them (Deuteronomy 11:16-17).

Within this context and the broader setting of Judges, the famine in Ruth likely points to God’s discipline upon Israel during a time of widespread unfaithfulness.

Ironically, Bethlehem, the town from which this family came, means “house of bread.” The “house of bread” now has no bread.

To escape the famine and find food for his family, a man named Elimelech decides to leave Bethlehem and travel to Moab with his wife Naomi and their two sons, Mahlon and Chilion. Elimelech’s name means “My God is King.” Naomi’s name means “pleasant.” The family is described as Ephrathites from Bethlehem in Judah, which likely points to a respected family line connected to the region around Bethlehem.

Moab was located about 50–80 miles east of Bethlehem, beyond the Dead Sea.

The Moabites descended from Lot through an incestuous relationship recorded in Genesis 19 after the family escaped Sodom. The Moabites were the ones who hired Balaam to curse Israel. Following the counsel of Balaam, the Moabites seduced and led Israel into compromise, sexual immorality and idolatry, leading God to pronounce the following judgment on Moab:

Deu 23:3 An Ammonite or Moabite shall not enter the assembly of the LORD; even to the tenth generation none of his descendants shall enter the assembly of the LORD forever, (NKJV)

The Moabites were a pagan nation under divine judgment who became a long-standing enemy of Israel frequently leading Israel idolatry and spiritual compromise.

Elimelech went “to dwell” in Moab. What may have begun as a temporary response to famine slowly became their settled home outside the land God had given His people. Verse 2 says, “they remained there.” A short-term response to crisis gradually became a long-term residence in Moab.

Dwell

1. Difficult Seasons Reveal Who We Trust

Elimelech’s name means “My God is King.” Difficult circumstances can test who really is king of our life - whether we truly live as though God is King, or whether we choose to become our own king.

Selah.

2. Famine Tests the Heart

Famine in Scripture often points to more than physical lack. It can speak of scarcity, barrenness, want, dryness, or need.

God can use seasons of lack to test our trust, expose self-sufficiency, get our attention, correct our direction, or move us into His larger purposes.

If there is lack in your life, do not only look for the nearest exit. Stop and inquire of the Lord, as David did in 2 Samuel 21:1. Ask Him what He is revealing, what He is correcting, what He is inviting you to trust Him for, and how He wants you to respond.

Selah.

3. Our Greatest Danger May Be Our Response to Hardship

Sometimes our greatest danger in hardship is not the hardship itself, but the decisions made in response to it.

Fear and pressure can push us to make choices rooted more in survival than trust. Hardship can tempt people to move outside God’s will in search of comfort, security, control, or relief.

Believers must be careful not to let difficult circumstances drive them away from dependence upon God.

Selah.

4. Beware the Easy Way Out

Elimelech left Bethlehem for Moab in search of survival and provision. Moab may have seemed like the practical answer, but it was also a spiritually dangerous place, associated with idolatry, compromise, and opposition to God’s people.

Moab can become a picture of the world’s offer of relief apart from God. In difficult seasons, there is always a temptation to run somewhere other than the Lord for answers, comfort, security, or escape.

Proverbs 14:12 says, “There is a way that seems right to a man, But its end is the way of death.” (NKJV)

The easy way out is not always the way of obedience. Sometimes famine is an invitation to dig deeper wells with God, not to run toward Moab.

Selah.

5. Temporary Decisions Can Become a Way of Life

Rarely do people decide all at once to walk away from God or abandon their spiritual priorities. More often, small decisions made during difficult seasons gradually lead to deeper compromise and begin to alter the course of life.

Elimelech went to dwell in Moab, and eventually his family remained there. What may have begun as a temporary response to crisis became a way of life outside the land of promise.

Temporary decisions made during difficult seasons can slowly become habits, relationships, attachments, and patterns of living.

Stop and examine whether hardship has slowly pulled your heart into compromise.

Selah.

2. Life in Moab (1:3-5)

Ruth 1:3-5 Then Elimelech, Naomi's husband, died; and she was left, and her two sons. Now they took wives of the women of Moab: the name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other Ruth. And they dwelt there about ten years. Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband. (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

1. The Collapse of Naomi's Support System (Ruth 1:3)

After moving to Moab in search of physical provision and relief from the famine, the story takes a painful turn. Naomi’s husband, Elimelech, dies, leaving her alone with her two young sons.

In the ancient world, the phrase “she was left” carried far more weight than we might immediately recognize. Ancient society offered no social safety nets like we have today. A woman’s safety, financial provision, inheritance security, and social standing were often deeply connected to the men in her family.

With her husband’s death, Naomi’s entire support system begins a catastrophic collapse. She is now a widow and a single mother stranded as an outsider in a foreign country. The author intends for us to feel the weight of her loss.

The family left Bethlehem seeking survival, yet their situation continues worsening. At this point, Naomi still has her sons, which means there remains hope for future safety and provision through them, but a downward spiral is already underway: first famine, then leaving their homeland, and now the death of her husband.

2. Deepening the Connection to Moab (Ruth 1:4)

Instead of returning to Bethlehem after this tragedy, the family settles into Moab for about ten years, and the two sons marry local Moabite women named Orpah and Ruth. This shows how deeply the family had become connected to Moab. Their lives were now fully intertwined with the people, culture, and relationships of a foreign land.

The ancient Jewish Targum reflects how many Jewish interpreters viewed the spiritual seriousness of this step, noting that the sons “transgressed the decree of the word of the Lord, and took to themselves strange women.” God had strictly warned Israel not to form marriage alliances with neighboring nations because those relationships could pull their hearts away from Him and into idolatry. These marriages show that the family was no longer just passing through to survive a famine; they had unpacked their bags and joined their lives, family lines, and habits with a culture that did not worship the true God.

3. The End of Human Hope (Ruth 1:5)

The downward spiral reaches rock bottom when both Mahlon and Chilion die at a young age. In a culture where women depended heavily on husbands and sons for survival, the death of all three men is a devastating blow to the family unit.

Naomi is stripped of everything. The passage leaves us with the bleak historical reality of three grieving, penniless widows stranded in enemy territory, isolated from their faith community, cut off from male protection, and left with no clear legal protection, safety, or income. What started as a journey seeking temporary relief ended in tragic loss and complete hopelessness.

Yet unknown to Naomi, God was already placing the person through whom He would begin her restoration right beside her.

Dwell

1. The Ripple Effect of Our Choices

Stop and ask: Am I currently making a high-pressure decision that feels right for me, but might negatively impact the people who depend on me? How will my decisions impact the people closest to me years from now?

Selah.

2. The Slow Trap of Moab

Life in Moab often begins with a promise of relief. It looks like an easy way out, a safe shortcut, or a quick escape from pressure. Moab is a vivid picture of the world’s false promises—the temptation to look for survival apart from relying on God. What first looks useful quickly becomes comfortable. What becomes comfortable soon becomes the place where we unpack our bags and stay. Over time, settling down in the wrong environment dulls our spiritual focus, weakens our trust in God, and actually increases the exact losses we were running away to avoid.

Stop and ask: Have I become comfortable in a place, a habit, a relationship, or a mindset that is slowly pulling my heart away from God?

Selah.

3. The Fragility of Human Security

The sudden deaths of Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion remind us that human security can disappear in a flash. We work so hard to build our sense of safety on changing circumstances, relationships, finances, health, or personal plans. Yet, as the New Testament reminds us, we do not even know what will happen tomorrow. Sudden loss has a brutal way of showing us just how fragile and temporary earthly stability really is.

Stop and ask: Where have I been looking for ultimate safety? Am I anchoring my security in things that can be taken away in a single day?

Selah.

4. Rock Bottom is God's Starting Line

God’s restoring work often begins at the exact spot when we lose hope and we reach the end of our resources. By verse 5, the family's search for worldly safety has shattered into three funerals and total bankruptcy. The story stops at a place of complete emptiness, barrenness, and grief. This rock-bottom moment is a reminder that what feels finished to us is never finished to God. Even when He seems totally silent and you cannot see or feel Him moving, He has not abandoned the story. He is already preparing the way of restoration.

Stop and ask: Am I in a season that feels completely empty or hopeless? Am I willing to trust that God is still writing my story, even in the dark?

Selah.

E. Losses Often Accumulate Slowly

Many people expect compromise to produce immediate disaster, but often it unfolds gradually. Elimelech's family did not lose everything in a day. First came famine. Then relocation. Then settling. Then marriages. Then ten years passed. Then death after death after death. Sin and compromise often work slowly, quietly, and progressively.

Stop and reflect:

Am I continuing down an unhealthy path because the consequences are not immediate?

Am I ignoring warning signs because life seems to be working out at the moment?

What small compromises am I making today that could become major losses tomorrow?

Selah

A Report of Hope (Ruth 1:6–7)

Ruth 1:6-7 Then she arose with her daughters-in-law that she might return from the country of Moab, for she had heard in the country of Moab that the LORD had visited His people by giving them bread. Therefore she went out from the place where she was, and her two daughters-in-law with her; and they went on the way to return to the land of Judah. (NKJV)

After years of tragedy and loss in Moab, Naomi finally hears a report from home. The Lord had visited His people in Bethlehem by giving them bread again.

This is the first glimpse of hope in the story. The famine is over. The “house of bread” has bread again. The same God Naomi feels has dealt bitterly with her has already begun the process of restoration before she even realizes it.

Moved by this news, Naomi decides to leave Moab and return to Judah. She packs up what remains of her life and begins the journey home with her two daughters-in-law, Ruth and Orpah.

This moment marks a turning point in the story. Naomi turns away from Moab, the place where she had experienced years of loss, and begins walking back toward Bethlehem, the place where the Lord had given bread again. .For a family that originally left Bethlehem trying to survive a famine, this report becomes the first sign of hope after more than a decade of sorrow.

Dwell

1. God Is the Source of Our Provision

Naomi hears that the Lord had visited His people by giving them bread again. This provision did not ultimately come from a shift in the weather, better financial planning, or human effort—it came directly from the hand of the Lord. Every true blessing and source of help in our lives comes from Him. He is not only the God of the big, spiritual breakthroughs, but He is also the exact same God who provides our basic daily needs.

Stop and ask: Am I truly recognizing the Lord as the active source of every single provision in my life, or am I giving the credit to my own planning and hard work?

Selah.

2. Coming Back to the Father's House

Naomi’s sudden return home reminds us of the prodigal son in the New Testament. He finally came to his senses only when he remembered that there was always plenty of bread in his father’s house. Similarly, the moment Naomi hears there is food back in Bethlehem, she immediately begins the long journey home. Sometimes, people begin returning to God simply because they are hungry, desperate, or completely aware of their own emptiness. God is so merciful that He can use even our deepest moments of physical lack and desperation to start bringing us back to Himself.

Stop and ask: Has a recent season of lack, hunger, or deep need become an invitation from God to return to the Father’s house?

Selah.

3. The Unseen Work of God

God was already preparing a harvest in Bethlehem while Naomi was still weeping and grieving over three fresh graves in Moab. Long before Naomi ever packed her bags, made a decision, or started her journey home, the Lord had already visited His people. This reveals that God is often actively working on our restoration long before we can see it, feel it, or recognize it in our circumstances.

Stop and ask: Am I willing to trust that God may already be orchestrating my restoration, even while I am still sitting in a season of grief or confusion?

Selah.

4. The Futility of Shortcuts

Elimelech left Bethlehem to find bread in Moab, yet the true provision only came when the Lord visited His people back in Bethlehem. What the family went looking for in an enemy land, God eventually provided in the very place they had abandoned. Human shortcuts always seem to offer quick relief, but real, lasting provision is found from staying within the boundaries of the Lord.

Stop and ask: Am I currently looking for relief or an easy escape in a place God has not called me to dwell?

Selah.

5. Hope Demands a Response

Naomi heard the good news the Lord visited her people and there was bread again in Bethlehem, but she still had to physically get up, pack her belongings, and begin the difficult journey back. Merely hearing that there was bread again in Bethlehem was not enough to save her. She had to make the hard choice to leave Moab behind and start walking home. When we hear a message of hope, or of clear direction, we still must respond with active obedience.

Stop and ask: What specific step of obedience is God asking me to take right now in response to the hope or clear direction He has shown me?

Selah.

6. When Brokenness Meets Hope

Naomi was broken in Moab, but brokenness by itself did not bring her home. She also needed to hear that there was bread again in Bethlehem. In the same way, the prodigal son came to himself when he remembered there was bread in his father’s house. The turning point in people’s lives comes when our need, pain, and brokenness meet the message of hope and good news of provision in the Father’s house.

God often uses holy hunger to bring people back. He lets us feel the emptiness of the far country so we will remember where true bread is found.

Stop and ask: Is my lack, pain, or brokenness becoming an invitation to return to the Father and receive what only He can provide?

Selah.

7. The First Step Home

It is clear from Ruth 1:20-21 that Naomi is bitter and disappointed with God. She is not fully healed, happy, or whole when she begins this journey back home. She is still deeply grieving, wounded, and likely full of unanswered questions, such as “why did this happen to me” and “where was God in all of this”? Yet, she still starts moving her feet in the right direction. This reminds us that spiritual restoration is a journey that begins in woundedness and that it takes time for our healing and emotions to catch up to our obedience.

Stop and ask: What is the next right step of obedience I can take toward God today, even if my heart still feels wounded and broken?

8. Waiting for the Christian Lottery

Naomi “went out from the place where she was.” That set her apart from many people. “Many hear of the good things God is doing in the lives of others and only wish they could experience the same” (Guzik). But Naomi did not stay in Moab simply wishing life were different. She got up and began moving toward the place where God was giving bread.

Wishing is not the same as action. Desire is not the same as obedience. There are moments when we must leave the place where we are and begin walking toward what God is offering.

Stop and ask: Am I only wishing things were different, or am I willing to take the step God is putting in front of me? Am I simply waiting for my “Christian lottery” to come in, or I am actively moving towards what God has in front of me?

Selah.

The Return part 1: Counting the Cost (Ruth 1:8–14)

Naomi blesses and releases her daughters-in-law (8-9)

Ruth 1:8-9 And Naomi said to her two daughters-in-law, Go, return each to her mother's house. The LORD deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me. 9 The LORD grant that you may find rest, each in the house of her husband. So she kissed them, and they lifted up their voices and wept. (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

On the road back to Bethlehem, Naomi stops and makes a sacrificial decision to release her daughters-in-law from any obligation to her. She urges Orpah and Ruth to turn around and return to their mothers’ homes.

Naomi blesses them as they go. She prays that Yahweh will show them Hesed, loyal, covenant-keeping kindness, just as they had shown kindness to her deceased sons and to her

She also asks the Lord to give them “rest.” In this setting, rest meant the security, provision, and stability that would come through remarriage and a new household.

Naomi’s kiss of farewell is a loving attempt to release them. She is putting their future needs ahead of her own need for companionship and support.

By ordinary human wisdom, this made sense. Orpah and Ruth had stronger family ties in Moab than they did in Israel. It would have seemed far safer for them to remain in their own homeland than to travel to Bethlehem with a grieving widow who had no visible means of support.

A Reality Check (Ruth 1:10–13)

Ruth 1:10-13 And they said to her, Surely we will return with you to your people. 11 But Naomi said, Turn back, my daughters; why will you go with me? Are there still sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands? 12 Turn back, my daughters, go—for I am too old to have a husband. If I should say I have hope, if I should have a husband tonight and should also bear sons, 13 would you wait for them till they were grown? Would you restrain yourselves from having husbands? No, my daughters; for it grieves me very much for your sakes that the hand of the LORD has gone out against me! (NKJV)

Understand

When both daughters-in-law weep and insist on staying with her, Naomi begins pointing out the cold hard reality of what would await them in Israel. She wants them to understand the cost of continuing with her.

Naomi points to the ancient practice of levirate marriage, where a deceased man’s brother could marry the widow to protect her and carry on the family name. Naomi reminds them that she has no husband, no sons, and even if she remarried tonight and had baby boys, it would be impossible and unreasonable for Ruth and Orpah to wait years for those sons to grow up.

Naomi is making clear the severe financial and social cost of following her. If they continue with her to Judah, they are leaving behind their homeland, their families, and the most likely path to remarriage, provision, and stability.

Naomi’s bitterness also begins to surface. She believes the hand of the Lord has gone out against her. In her grief, she sees herself as a woman under God’s heavy hand, and she does not want Ruth and Orpah to share in her sorrow.

When she says, “the hand of the LORD has gone out against me,” she may also be recognizing that leaving the land of promise for Moab had spiritual consequences. She does not accuse God of wrongdoing. She acknowledges that her life is under His sovereign hand.

The Valley of Decision (Ruth 1:14)

Ruth 1:14 Then they lifted up their voices and wept again; and Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her. (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

This verse marks a decisive moment on the road between Moab and Judah. Naomi has laid out the cost clearly. If Orpah and Ruth continue with her, they are leaving behind their homeland, their families, and their most likely path to remarriage, provision, and security.

Both women are deeply moved. They both lift up their voices and weep. Both love Naomi. Both feel the weight of the decision. But love and emotion are not the same as commitment. A choice now has to be made.

Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye. From a practical standpoint, Orpah chooses the easier and more reasonable path. She returns to her mother’s house, her people, and the possibility of a more secure future in Moab.

She chooses what is familiar, safer, and more likely to provide earthly security. Ruth, however, clings to Naomi and continues toward Judah. Though Ruth may not yet understand everything this choice will mean, her decision moves her toward the people of God, the land of promise, and ultimately the God of Israel.

Then the text gives the contrast: “but Ruth clung to her.”

The word “clung” carries the idea of holding fast, joining oneself to another, or refusing to let go. It is the same word used in Genesis 2:24 when a man leaves his father and mother and is joined to his wife. Ruth is not simply feeling sad in the moment. She is making a costly decision of loyal commitment.

Orpah chooses the path of practical survival. Ruth chooses the path of sacrificial devotion and faith. Both women wept, but only Ruth clung.

It is one thing to feel deeply. It is another thing to obey, commit, and hold fast when the cost is clear. Ruth’s choice begins to look like a discipleship choice: leaving what is familiar behind in order to move toward the Lord, His people, and His promises.

Dwell

Theme 1: Sacrificial Love and True Rest

1. Love Seeks the Good of Others

Naomi loved Ruth and Orpah enough to release them from any obligation to her. She did not manipulate them, pressure them, or guilt them into staying with her. Even though she was grieving and vulnerable, she still wanted what she believed was best for them.

True love is not possessive. It is not emotionally or otherwise manipulative. It seeks the good of others, even when it may mean personal loss and walking into a lonely future completely alone.

Stop and ask:

Am I holding on to someone mainly because I need them to comfort me, or am I seeking what is truly best for them?

Am I using guilt, pressure, or emotional manipulation to get people to give me what I want or need?

2. Marriage and Home as a Place of Rest

Naomi prayed that the Lord would give Ruth and Orpah “rest” in the house of a new husband. In this setting, marriage was meant to provide a protective covering, safety, provision, stability, and peace.

God intends marriage and the home to be a place of rest, not striving; peace, not turmoil; and refreshment, not constant exhaustion.

Stop and ask: Am I actively helping make my marriage and home a place of rest, peace, and refreshment?

Theme 2: Counting the Cost of Commitment

3. Count the Cost Before You Commit

Naomi does not hide the difficulties ahead. She lays out the cost plainly and honestly. Ruth and Orpah needed to understand that following Naomi into Judah would mean sacrifice, uncertainty, and loss.

Following God is not built on feelings, emotion, or blind sentimentality. There are times when obedience requires sacrifice, inconvenience, loss, and faith.

Stop and ask: Have I counted the cost of following God, or am I only willing to follow Him when the path looks easy?

Selah.

4. Do Not Follow on Emotion Alone

Both Orpah and Ruth wept. Both loved Naomi. Both felt the weight of the moment. But when the cost became clear, their paths separated.

Feelings are not the same as commitment. It is possible to feel deeply, cry sincerely, and still turn back when obedience becomes costly.

Stop and ask: Am I settling for emotional responses to God, or am I making concrete choices to obey Him?

Selah.

ORPAH- WHAT MIGHT HAVE BEEN

5. The Easy Path Is Not Always the Right Path

From a human standpoint, Orpah’s decision made sense. She chose what was familiar, practical, safer, and more likely to provide earthly security.

But the easier path is not always the path that moves us closer to God. Sometimes the life of faith requires us to leave what is familiar in order to move toward the Lord, His people, and His promises.

Stop and ask: Am I choosing comfort and earthly security over faith and obedience?

Selah.

6. Clinging When the Cost Is Clear

The text says Ruth “clung” to Naomi. She did not merely feel compassion for Naomi. She joined herself to Naomi and refused to let go.

This is a vivid picture of costly devotion. True faith does not merely admire God from a distance. It clings to Him, His people, and His promises when the cost is clear.

Stop and ask: What familiar comfort or worldly safety net is God asking me to let go of so I can cling more fully to Him?

Selah.

Theme 3: The Steps of Faith

8. Moving Toward the People of God

Ruth’s decision was not merely about Naomi. Her choice moved her toward the people of God, the land of promise, and ultimately the God of Israel.

Spiritual growth often requires leaving something behind in order to move toward something better. God may call us to leave old patterns, old identities, old relationships, old securities, or old ways of thinking so we can embrace His purposes.

Stop and ask: What am I being called to leave behind in order to move closer to God?

Selah.

9. Trusting God When You Do Not Understand

Ruth could not see the end of the story. She did not know she would become the wife of Boaz, the great-grandmother of David, or part of the family line of Jesus Christ. She simply took the next step of faith.

Many times God does not reveal the whole destination. He only reveals the next step.

Stop and ask: Am I waiting for God to show me the whole plan before I obey the part He has already made clear?

Selah.

9. God’s People Are Worth Clinging To

Ruth’s future blessing was connected to the very person who looked like a burden. Naomi had no visible future, no money, no sons, and no earthly security to offer. Yet Ruth clung to her.

Sometimes God places people in our lives whom we are called to love, serve, and remain faithful to, even when there is no obvious benefit to ourselves.

Stop and ask: Who has God called me to remain faithful to, even when the relationship requires sacrifice?

Theme 4: Faith in the Midst of Severe Pain

10. Confusing Circumstances with God’s Character

Naomi says, “the hand of the LORD has gone out against me.” She recognizes that God is sovereign over her life, but her grief is beginning to affect how she sees Him.

Pain can distort our view of God. In seasons of loss, unanswered prayer, or disappointment, we can begin to interpret God through our wounds instead of interpreting our wounds through the truth of who God is.

Stop and ask: Am I letting painful circumstances distort my view of God’s heart?

Selah.

11. Return Even When You Feel God’s Hand Against You

Naomi feels that the hand of the Lord has gone out against her, yet she is still returning to the land of Israel and the God of Israel. She does not run farther away. She turns back toward home.

This matters. Even when we feel confused, guilty, wounded, or disappointed, the answer is not to stay in Moab. The answer is to return to the Lord.

Stop and ask: Am I moving closer to God with my pain, or farther from Him?

Selah.

12. God’s Hand Will Not Always Feel Against You

Naomi cannot yet see what God is about to do. She feels that His hand is against her, but soon His hand will begin moving for her restoration.

There are seasons when we cannot see the kindness of God yet. But His story is not finished. What feels like His opposition may become the very place where His mercy begins to appear.

Stop and ask: Am I willing to trust God before I can see how He is working for my restoration?

Selah.

The Return part 2: Total Commitment (Ruth 1:15–18)

The Stark Choice (Ruth 1:15)

Ruth 1:15 And she said, Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods; return after your sister-in-law. (NKJV)

After Orpah leaves, Naomi makes one final attempt to persuade Ruth to return to Moab. She points out that Orpah has already gone back to her people and to her gods.

This statement reveals that the choice before Ruth is much bigger than deciding whether to travel with Naomi. Ruth must now choose between two different directions in life. One path leads back to Moab, her family, her culture, and her gods. The other leads toward Israel, the people of God, and the Lord Himself.

Naomi removes any remaining uncertainty. Ruth now understands exactly what she is choosing and exactly what she is leaving behind. The decision must now be made.

Ruth's Total Commitment (Ruth 1:16–18)

Ruth 1:16-18 But Ruth said: Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you; For wherever you go, I will go; And wherever you lodge, I will lodge; Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God. 17 Where you die, I will die, And there will I be buried. The LORD do so to me, and more also, If anything but death parts you and me. 18 When she saw that she was determined to go with her, she stopped speaking to her. (NKJV)

Ruth responds with one of the most remarkable declarations of commitment found anywhere in Scripture. She asks Naomi to stop urging her to leave because her mind is already made up.

Ruth's vow unfolds in stages.

First, she commits herself to Naomi personally: "Wherever you go, I will go; and wherever you lodge, I will lodge." Ruth is choosing to share Naomi's future, whatever it may hold. Whether that future includes hardship, poverty, rejection, or uncertainty, Ruth is determined to remain with her.

Second, she embraces Naomi's people: "Your people shall be my people." Ruth willingly leaves behind her former national identity as a Moabite and joins herself to Israel. She is choosing a new people and a new community.

Finally, she makes the deepest commitment of all: "And your God, my God." This is the spiritual climax of Ruth's confession. She is not merely changing locations or attaching herself to Naomi's family. She is turning away from the gods of Moab and placing her faith in the God of Israel.

This statement also reveals that Ruth had learned something about the Lord through Naomi and her family. During their years together in Moab, Ruth had evidently heard about the God of Israel, His covenant, His promises, and His people. Naomi's life was far from perfect, and her family had experienced great tragedy, yet Ruth saw enough of the Lord in Naomi to choose Him for herself. Ruth's confession reminds us that people are often watching our lives more closely than we realize and that people are listening to what we are saying, also more than we realize..

What begins as loyalty to Naomi becomes identification with God's people and ultimately faith in the God of Israel.

Ruth takes her commitment even further. She declares, "Where you die, I will die, and there will I be buried."

This shows that Ruth does not view this as a temporary decision. She is permanently tying her future to Naomi, to Israel, and to the God of Israel. There is no thought of returning to Moab. There is no thought of going back.

To confirm the seriousness of her commitment, Ruth invokes the covenant name of the Lord, YHWH, and places herself under a solemn oath. She calls upon God Himself as her witness and judge if she should fail to keep her promise.

When Naomi sees Ruth's determination, she stops trying to persuade her otherwise. The matter is settled.

Ruth has counted the cost. She understands what she may lose. Yet she willingly leaves behind her homeland, her former way of life, her old securities, and her old gods in order to identify herself with Naomi, the people of God, and the Lord.

Her decision becomes one of the clearest pictures of faith and discipleship found in the Old Testament.

Dwell

1. Standing at the Crossroads

Ruth now stands at a crossroads which will define the rest of her life: follow Orpah and return, or choose to remain with Naomi. The choice is much bigger than a friendship or a travel itinerary; Ruth stands at the crossroads of two completely different life directions. One path offers the comfort of her old family, her culture, and her familiar gods, while the other offers nothing but an unknown future with the people of God.

In our walk with God, we will all face seasons where the middle ground disappears, and we are forced to decide whether we will retreat to what is comfortable or step out into the costly unknown of obedience.

I think we often look to hear God thunder His voice during these times, but in reality the choice at the crossroads, while difficult, is quite clear. Life or death, past or future, fear or faith, comfort or the unknown path of obedience.

Stop and ask:

Am I currently standing at a spiritual crossroad where God is asking me to choose the narrow path of faith over the comfortable, familiar path of my old life?

Is there an area where God has already made His will clear, but I am delaying the decision because the cost feels too high?

Selah.

2. Faith Requires More Than Feelings

Both Orpah and Ruth loved Naomi. Both wept. Both felt the pain of the moment. Yet Ruth did not allow her feelings to dictate the choice.

Many people feel affection for God, appreciate His people, or enjoy spiritual things. Yet feelings alone do not produce discipleship. There comes a point where faith must move beyond emotion and become obedience.

Stop and ask: Do I make decisions based on “feelings,” or am I making concrete choices to follow and obey God based on His Word and the voice of the Holy Spirit?

Selah.

3. The Cost of Total Commitment

Ruth begins her vow by promising to go wherever Naomi goes and lodge wherever she lodges. She is not signing up for a comfortable life choice; she is making a total commitment simply because of her relationship with Naomi, and what Naomi has shared about her God. Ruth makes this commitment without any promises, fully aware the cost may include poverty, rejection, and uncertainty.

Biblical faith is not blind to the cost. It sees the cost clearly and chooses obedience anyway because God is worth it.

True biblical commitment does not make conditions or bargains with God. It does not say, “I will follow You as long as life stays comfortable.” It anchors itself to the Lord regardless of what earthly circumstances may come.

Stop and ask:

Is my commitment to follow God conditional on my life going smoothly, or am I determined to stay faithful to Him even if the path brings hardship and loss?

What comfort, security, relationship, or familiar pattern is God asking me to release so I can cling more fully to Him?

What cost of discipleship am I currently resisting because it feels too difficult, too uncertain, or too expensive?

Selah.

4. A New Allegiance and Identity

Ruth’s vow moves from Naomi, to Naomi’s people, and finally to Naomi’s God.

By declaring, “Your people shall be my people,” Ruth willingly walks away from her former identity as a Moabite and joins herself to Israel. She chooses to identify with the people of God even though she knows she will enter Bethlehem as an outsider.

The heart of Ruth’s confession is not, “Where you go, I will go.” The heart of her confession is, “Your God, my God.” Ruth is not merely changing locations. She is changing allegiances. She is turning away from the gods of Moab and placing her trust in the God of Israel.

Every believer eventually faces the same question: Who will be my God? Who will rule my life? Who will I trust when the cost is high and the future is uncertain?

Stop and ask:

Am I fully identifying with and investing in the people of God, or am I still clinging to old identities and worldly attachments for my security?

Have I truly surrendered myself to God, or am I simply attached to Christian people, Christian culture, or Christian activities?

Is there something from my old life that I am still trying to hold on to while following Christ?

Where is my ultimate allegiance found?

Selah.

5. The Power of an Imperfect Witness

Ruth’s vow reveals that despite Elimelech’s compromised move to Moab, Naomi’s deep bitterness, and the family’s immense tragedies, Ruth still saw and heard enough of the reality of Yahweh through them to choose Him for herself.

Interestingly, ten years of compromise in Moab did not lead Ruth to embrace the God of Israel. Yet when Naomi turned her face back toward Bethlehem and back toward the Lord, Ruth followed. People are often influenced more by our repentance and renewed obedience than by our attempts to fit in with the world.

God does not demand that we have a flawless, tragedy-free story before He can use us. He can use even a wounded, imperfect witness to draw hungry hearts to Himself.

Stop and ask:

If someone watched my life closely, would they be drawn closer to God or pushed farther away?

Can people see genuine faith in my life through the way I respond to suffering, disappointment, and loss?

Am I willing to let others see God’s faithfulness in the midst of my struggles and imperfections?

Am I waiting until my life is perfect before sharing my faith?

What example am I setting for those who are watching me?

Selah.

6. No Turning Back

Ruth seals her commitment by choosing her final resting place: “Where you die, I will die, and there will I be buried.”

By making this vow, Ruth is declaring that she is never going back. She is burning the bridges behind her. There is no backup plan, no safety net, and no thought of returning to Moab if life becomes difficult.

Many believers struggle because they are trying to follow God while keeping an escape route back to their old life.

When Naomi sees Ruth’s determination, she stops arguing. The matter is settled. There is tremendous power in a heart that has fully surrendered to God. Many believers struggle because they continually revisit decisions that should have been settled long ago.

A settled heart produces stability, direction, endurance, and peace.

Stop and ask:

Have I fully surrendered my future to the Lord, or am I secretly keeping a back door open to old habits, old securities, or old ways of life?

What area of obedience do I need to stop debating and finally settle before God?

Is there a part of my life where I have not fully burned the bridges behind me?

What would complete surrender look like in my current situation?

Selah.

7. The Heart of True Discipleship: Counting the Cost and Following Anyway

Ruth’s radical decision stands as one of the clearest Old Testament pictures of discipleship. Ruth understood what she might lose. Family. Homeland. Culture. Security. Familiarity. Acceptance. Yet she followed anyway.

She willingly counts the cost, embraces the loss of earthly security, leaves behind her homeland and her old gods, and throws herself entirely upon the promises of the Lord.

Ruth’s decision mirrors the call of Jesus to deny ourselves, leave behind competing allegiances, take up our cross, and follow Him.

Biblical faith is not blind to the cost. It sees the cost clearly and chooses obedience anyway because God is worth it.

God rarely reveals our entire future and entire journey upfront and at once. He asks us to trust Him and go forward in faith, walking in the light we have been given.

Stop and ask:

What cost of discipleship am I currently resisting because it feels too difficult, too uncertain, or too expensive?

Am I waiting for God to explain everything before I obey what He has already made clear?

What specific step of obedience is God asking me to take today where fear of the unknown is holding me back from fully trusting Him?

Have I counted the cost of following Jesus and chosen Him anyway?

What does taking up my cross look like in this season of life?

Selah.

8. Embracing a New Community

By declaring, “Your people shall be my people,” Ruth willingly walks away from her national identity as a Moabite and joins herself to Israel. She knew she would be entering Bethlehem as an outsider, yet she chose to identify with God’s people anyway.

True faith often requires us to leave behind old social circles, worldly mindsets, and former identities in order to fully identify with the family of God.

Stop and ask:

Am I fully identifying with and investing in the people of God, or am I still clinging to old identities, relationships, and worldly attachments for my security?

The Return: Coming Home (Ruth 1:19-22)

This passage marks the raw, emotional conclusion to Chapter 1. After ten years of famine, displacement, and the devastating deaths of her husband and both sons in Moab, Naomi returns home to Bethlehem. She doesn't return alone—her loyal daughter-in-law, Ruth, is by her side—but Naomi is too consumed by grief to see any hope yet.

Ruth 1:19 Now the two of them went until they came to Bethlehem. And it happened, when they had come to Bethlehem, that all the city was excited because of them; and the women said, Is this Naomi? (NKJV)

Naomi and Ruth complete the 7 – 10 day journey from Moab to Bethlehem. Bethlehem was just a small village so everyone in the village would have known everyone else and remembered Naomi from years ago.

Bethlehem is stirred or excited by Naomi's return. The Hebrew word translated “excited” in the NKJV indicates a noisy commotion or uproar. The women of the town can hardly believe what they are seeing.

The last time they saw Naomi, she left with a husband, two sons, and hope for a better future in Moab. Now she returns as a widow accompanied by a Moabite woman.

The years of hardship, grief, and the long, rugged journey on foot seem to have taken a physical toll on Naomi. She is barely recognizable.

Ruth 1:20-21 But she said to them, Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me. 21 I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty. Why do you call me Naomi, since the LORD has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me? (NKJV)

This passage reveals Naomi's deep sorrow and how she feels her life has been marked by loss and hardship. Naomi means "pleasant," but she asks to be called "Mara," which means "bitter," reflecting how deeply her losses have affected her after losing her husband, her sons, and the future security she once expected in Bethlehem.

She recognizes that God, referred to here as "the Almighty" (Shaddai), has allowed these difficulties. When Naomi says, "the LORD has testified against me" and "the Almighty has afflicted me," she is expressing her belief that God's hand has been against her. She appears to view her suffering as more than mere tragedy; she may feel that she is experiencing the consequences of leaving the land of promise and going to Moab.

At this point Naomi sees only what she has lost. What she cannot yet see is that God has already begun restoring what appears to be gone. Ruth stands beside her, the harvest is beginning, and the family property that seems beyond recovery will eventually be redeemed through Boaz.

Ruth 1:22 So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab. Now they came to Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest. (NKJV)

Verse 22 closes the chapter by summarizing the situation. Naomi has returned from Moab to Bethlehem; however, she has returned with “Ruth the Moabite”, a foreign widow from a nation with a troubled history with Israel.

. It also tells us they arrived “at the beginning of barley harvest.” This is the first quiet sign that Naomi’s story is beginning to turn. The famine is over. Bread has returned to Bethlehem. Harvest has begun.

While Naomi is looking backward at her losses, declaring herself empty and broken, God is quietly bringing her into a place of literal and spiritual provision. The mention of the harvest signals to the reader that God is already moving behind the scenes to reverse her emptiness.

Dwell

1. When God Brings You Home

Naomi returns to Bethlehem wounded, grieving, and bitter. She is back in the right place, but her heart is still full of pain. This reminds us that returning to God does not always mean we immediately feel whole, healed, or joyful.

Sometimes the first step is simply coming home.

The word “return” is repeated throughout Ruth 1. Naomi is returning to Bethlehem because it was once her home. Yet verse 22 also says Ruth returned with her from Moab. That is striking because Ruth had never lived in Bethlehem before. Naomi is returning to an old home, but Ruth is coming into the place where she truly belongs, because she is coming to the people of God and the God of Israel

Stop and ask:

Is God calling me to return to Him, even if my heart still feels wounded, confused, or bitter?

Am I returning to the Lord as my true home, even if obedience leads me into unfamiliar territory?

Selah.

2. Interpreting Life Through Pain

Naomi looks at her life and says, “I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty.” She evaluates her story almost entirely by what she has lost. Her grief is real, but her pain is affecting how she sees her life.

Pain can become a lens. When we interpret everything through loss, disappointment, guilt, or sorrow, we may miss what God is still doing.

Stop and ask: Am I interpreting my life through my pain, or through the truth of God’s character and promises?

Selah.

3. Faith in the Dark

Naomi is bitter, but she is not an atheist. She does not deny God’s sovereignty. She repeatedly acknowledges God’s hand in her circumstances. Her struggle is not that she thinks God is absent. Her struggle is that she cannot yet see what God is doing.

There are seasons when faith does not sound polished. Sometimes faith speaks through tears, confusion, and honest grief.

Stop and ask: Am I bringing my grief to God honestly, or am I pretending to be fine while my heart is struggling?

Selah.

4. The Danger of Defining Yourself by Your Wounds

Naomi asks them not to call her Naomi, meaning “pleasant,” but Mara, meaning “bitter.” Her pain has become so deep that she begins naming herself by her wound.

Suffering can tempt us to let loss become our identity. We begin to define ourselves by what happened to us, what was taken from us, or what we fear will never be restored.

Stop and ask: Have I allowed a wound, loss, failure, or disappointment to become the name I live under?

Selah.

5. God Is Doing More Than We Can See

Naomi says she has come back empty, but Ruth is standing right beside her. Naomi sees what she has lost, but she cannot yet see what God has already provided.

This is one of the great tensions of Ruth. Naomi’s words are honest, but they are not the full story. God is doing more than she can see.

Stop and ask: Am I so focused on what I have lost that I am overlooking what God has already placed beside me?

Selah.

6. The Harvest Was Already Waiting

The chapter ends by telling us that Naomi and Ruth arrived in Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest. Naomi thinks her story is ending, but harvest is beginning.

That detail is not accidental. The famine is over. Bread has returned. Provision is already waiting before Naomi knows how God will use it.

Stop and ask: Where might God already be preparing provision before I can recognize it? Can I thank Him for it even though I have not yet seen it?

Selah.

7. When You Feel Empty but God Sees Provision

Naomi feels empty, and from her perspective, that is understandable. She has lost her husband, her sons, her security, and the future she expected.

But God sees more than emptiness. He sees Ruth. He sees Bethlehem. He sees the harvest. He sees Boaz. He sees redemption already beginning to unfold.

Stop and ask: Can I trust that God sees provision and restoration in places where I only see emptiness?

Selah.
