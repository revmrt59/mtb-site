Section 1

Ruth 1:1-2 - The Famine

Overview

≡Section Summary

During the days of the Judges, a famine strikes Bethlehem. Elimelech moves his wife Naomi and their two sons from Bethlehem to Moab in search of food and survival. What appears to be a practical, temporary decision becomes the beginning of a journey that will shape the family's future and ultimately God's redemptive plan.

◎Key Observations

  • The story begins during one of Israel's darkest spiritual periods.• Bethlehem ("House of Bread") ironically has no bread.• The famine becomes the catalyst for the entire book.• Elimelech leaves the Promised Land for Moab seeking relief.• The family intends to "sojourn," but eventually remains there.• God's providence is already guiding events, although He is never mentioned as directing them.

★Main Idea

God often accomplishes His redemptive purposes through ordinary events, but the decisions people make during difficult seasons have lasting consequences.

◈Understanding

  • Difficult seasons reveal where we place our trust.• Fear and pressure can tempt us to seek solutions apart from God.• Temporary compromises can become permanent patterns.• God remains sovereign even when His activity is hidden.• Our decisions affect not only ourselves but those around us.• God can weave even imperfect decisions into His larger redemptive plan.

⌖Historical & Cultural Setting

The events occur during the period of the Judges, when Israel repeatedly experienced cycles of rebellion, oppression, repentance, and deliverance. Famine threatened survival because society depended almost entirely on agriculture. Under the Mosaic Covenant, famine could also function as divine discipline when the nation turned from the Lord (Deuteronomy 11:16–17).

▦Historical & Cultural Notes

  • Bethlehem means "House of Bread," making the famine especially ironic.• Moab lay east of the Dead Sea and descended from Lot (Genesis 19).• The Moabites opposed Israel through Balak and Balaam and later led Israel into idolatry (Numbers 22–25).• Deuteronomy 23:3–6 prohibited Moabites from entering the assembly of the LORD because of their hostility toward Israel.• The journey from Bethlehem to Moab was approximately 50–70 miles across rugged terrain.

✎Remarks

This opening section establishes the major theological tension of the book: human beings make real choices in response to difficult circumstances, yet God quietly works behind the scenes to accomplish His sovereign purposes. The irony of leaving the "House of Bread" because of famine prepares the reader for the restoration that begins when bread returns to Bethlehem.

Ruth 1:1

NLT

Ruth 1:1 In the days when the judges ruled in Israel, a severe famine came upon the land. So a man from Bethlehem in Judah left his home and went to live in the country of Moab, taking his wife and two sons with him. (NLT)

◈Understanding

Ruth begins during the period of the Judges when a famine struck Bethlehem. Elimelech decided to move his family to Moab to escape the famine, setting in motion the events that would eventually lead to God's plan of redemption through Ruth and Boaz.

◎Observation

"Now it came to pass"Introduces the account as a true historical event."In the days when the judges ruled" – Places the story during Israel's spiritually dark period described in the Book of Judges."There was a famine in the land" – A severe shortage of food that threatened survival and often carried covenant significance in Israel."A certain man of Bethlehem, Judah" – Introduces Elimelech, whose hometown means "House of Bread.""Went to dwell" – Literally "to sojourn," indicating a temporary stay was intended."Country of Moab" – A neighboring nation east of the Dead Sea with a history of idolatry and hostility toward Israel.

●Exposition

The opening verse immediately establishes both the historical setting and the central tension of the book. The days of the Judges were characterized by spiritual instability and moral compromise. Whether the famine resulted from natural causes or covenant discipline, it became the circumstance through which God began unfolding His sovereign plan. Elimelech's decision to leave Bethlehem appears understandable from a human perspective, yet it moved his family away from the Promised Land into Moab, a nation often associated with spiritual compromise. The narrator neither condemns nor commends the decision, allowing the story itself to reveal its consequences.

✎Remarks

Bethlehem, meaning "House of Bread," ironically had no bread, while bread was sought in Moab. The greatest danger during hardship is often not the hardship itself but the decisions made in response to it. Even when people make imperfect choices, God quietly works through ordinary circumstances to accomplish His redemptive purposes.

Wrap-Up

●Key Truths

Section 2

Ruth 1:3–5 - Life in Moab

Overview

≡Section Summary

Elimelech dies, leaving Naomi a widow. Her sons, Mahlon and Chilion, marry Moabite women named Orpah and Ruth. After living in Moab for about ten years, both sons also die, leaving Naomi, Ruth, and Orpah widowed without husbands, children, or visible security.

◎Key Observations

  • Elimelech's death leaves Naomi without her husband and provider.• Mahlon and Chilion marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth.• The family remains in Moab for about ten years, showing the temporary stay has become permanent.• Both sons die without leaving children.• The narrative moves from famine to repeated personal tragedy.• God is never mentioned as speaking or intervening, yet He remains present behind the scenes.

★Main Idea

Human plans cannot guarantee security or prevent suffering. Even when life seems to unravel and God appears silent, He is still working toward His purposes.

◈Understanding

  • Earthly security can disappear far more quickly than we expect.• Temporary decisions can gradually become settled patterns of life.• Loss often comes progressively rather than all at once.• God does not abandon His people during seasons of silence and sorrow.• What appears to be the end of the story may actually be the place where God begins His restoring work.

⌖Historical & Cultural Setting

In the ancient Near East, a widow without sons faced great economic and social vulnerability. Without a husband or male heir, a woman's future was uncertain, often depending upon extended family or the kindness of others. Naomi's situation would have appeared hopeless to the original readers.

▦Historical & Cultural Notes

  • Ten years was a significant period of residence, indicating the family had settled in Moab rather than merely waiting for conditions to improve.• Childlessness after ten years intensified the tragedy because there were no heirs to continue the family line.• Widows were among the most vulnerable members of society and frequently depended upon relatives or community support for survival.• Ruth and Orpah now shared Naomi's loss, creating a bond through common suffering.

✎Remarks

The narrator intentionally brings Naomi to complete emptiness before revealing God's provision. By the end of this section, every visible source of security has been removed, preparing the reader to see that true hope will come from the Lord rather than human circumstances.

◈Understanding

Elimelech dies, leaving Naomi a widow in a foreign land. The move intended to preserve the family instead leaves Naomi vulnerable and alone.

◎Observation

"Then Elimelech...died"The family's provider and leader dies unexpectedly."Naomi was left" – Emphasizes Naomi's loss and vulnerable condition."Her two sons" – The only remaining male providers in the family.

●Exposition

The narrative moves quickly from famine to personal tragedy. Scripture offers no explanation for Elimelech's death, nor does it suggest it was a direct judgment from God. Instead, the focus is on Naomi's circumstances. A widow in the ancient world faced significant uncertainty, especially while living outside Israel.

✎Remarks

Life can change suddenly. The very plan intended to secure the family's future could not protect them from loss. God's silence should never be mistaken for His absence, even when circumstances become painful and confusing.

Wrap-Up

▣Foundational Truths

  • Earthly security is temporary; only the Lord remains constant.• God continues working even when He appears silent.• Seasons of loss do not cancel God's purposes.• Human emptiness often becomes the setting for God's provision.• God's redemptive work frequently begins where human hope appears to end.

✦Section Insights

  • Temporary decisions can quietly become permanent lifestyles.• Loss often comes one step at a time rather than all at once.• God's silence should never be confused with His absence.• The darkest chapter of a story is often the preparation for God's greatest work.• When every visible support is removed, we are better positioned to recognize God's provision.

Naomi's complete emptiness prepares the way for the appearance of the kinsman-redeemer. Before Boaz enters the story, God is already arranging every detail that will lead to redemption. Section 3

Ruth 1:6-7 - A Report of Hope

Overview

≡Section Summary

After ten years in Moab and the devastating loss of her husband and both sons, Naomi hears that the LORD has visited His people by giving them bread. Encouraged by this report, she prepares to leave Moab and return to Bethlehem with Ruth and Orpah. This marks the turning point of the entire book—from loss toward restoration, from wandering toward home, and from despair toward hope.

◎Key Observations

  • The turning point begins with hearing, not with seeing.• The text specifically says "the LORD had visited His people," emphasizing God's gracious intervention rather than favorable weather or economic recovery.• Bread, absent at the beginning of the chapter, reappears as a sign of God's provision and faithfulness.• Naomi's circumstances have not yet changed—only the news has changed.• Naomi must choose whether to remain in Moab or respond to what she has heard.• Every act of restoration in the book begins with one step of obedient faith.

★Main Idea

God often begins His restoring work by revealing that He is already at work. Faith responds to God's activity before the full outcome can be seen.

Section 4

Ruth 1:8-14 - Counting the Cost

Overview

≡Section Summary

As Naomi and her daughters-in-law journey toward Bethlehem, she urges Ruth and Orpah to return to their mothers' homes where they may find new husbands and security. Naomi blesses them and acknowledges their faithful love toward both the living and the dead. Although both women initially refuse to leave her, Naomi explains that she has nothing more to offer them. Orpah ultimately returns to Moab, while Ruth's response is reserved for the next section.

◎Key Observations

  • Naomi's concern is for Ruth and Orpah rather than herself.• Naomi recognizes and commends the loyal love they have already shown.• The word "return" is repeated throughout the conversation, emphasizing that every person must choose a direction.• Naomi's reasoning is entirely practical—she believes she has no future to offer them.• Naomi sees only her circumstances, while the reader knows God has already begun working.• Both women initially express the same desire, but genuine commitment is revealed only when obedience becomes costly.• This section prepares the reader for Ruth's extraordinary confession of faith in the following verses.

★Main Idea

The call to follow God cannot be made for someone else. Every person must decide whether to pursue what appears safe and familiar or trust the Lord with an uncertain future. True faith is revealed when obedience requires sacrifice rather than convenience.

Section 5

Ruth 1:15–18 - Total Commitment

Overview

≡Section Summary

After Orpah returns to Moab, Naomi gives Ruth one final opportunity to turn back. Instead, Ruth makes one of the greatest declarations of commitment in Scripture, pledging herself to Naomi, Israel, and the LORD. She willingly leaves behind her homeland, family, religion, and future security. Seeing Ruth's unwavering resolve, Naomi stops urging her to return, and together they continue their journey to Bethlehem.

◎Key Observations

  • Naomi gives Ruth one final opportunity to return, removing every possible pressure to continue.• Ruth's commitment extends far beyond Naomi—it includes Naomi's people and Naomi's God.• Ruth willingly exchanges the security of the familiar for the uncertainty of faith.• Her promises become increasingly personal and permanent, climaxing with a covenant oath before the LORD.• Naomi recognizes Ruth's settled conviction and stops trying to persuade her otherwise.• Ruth's decision becomes the spiritual turning point of the book and the foundation for everything that follows.• This passage demonstrates that true faith is measured not by emotion but by enduring commitment.

★Main Idea

Genuine faith willingly leaves behind the old life to embrace God and His purposes, regardless of the personal cost. True discipleship is demonstrated by steadfast commitment that remains firm even when the future is uncertain.

Section 6

Ruth 1:19-22 - An Empty Homecoming

Overview

≡Section Summary

Naomi and Ruth arrive in Bethlehem, causing a stir throughout the town. The women recognize Naomi, but she insists they no longer call her Naomi ("Pleasant"), asking instead to be called Mara ("Bitter") because she believes the Almighty has dealt harshly with her. She laments that she left full and returned empty. The chapter closes by noting that they arrived at the beginning of the barley harvest, a seemingly ordinary detail that quietly announces the dawn of God's provision and sets the stage for everything that follows.

◎Key Observations

  • Naomi's homecoming is marked by both public recognition and personal sorrow.• Naomi changes her own name, revealing how deeply suffering has shaped her identity.• Naomi repeatedly acknowledges God's sovereignty, yet misunderstands His purpose in her suffering.• The contrast between "full" and "empty" becomes one of the chapter's dominant themes.• Naomi believes her story has ended in loss, while the narrator hints that it is about to begin again.• The final statement about the beginning of barley harvest is far more than a chronological note—it quietly signals hope, provision, and the opening of God's next chapter.• The reader now knows what Naomi does not: the Lord has already been working from the opening verse of the book.

★Main Idea

Human perspective is often shaped by present pain, while God's perspective is shaped by His eternal purposes. Even when believers feel empty and abandoned, God may already be preparing the very provision that will restore what has been lost.
