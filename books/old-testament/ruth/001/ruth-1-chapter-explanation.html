Chapter Study

Ruth 1

Verse-by-Verse
Ruth 1:1
Ruth 1:1
NKJV

Ruth 1:1 Now it came to pass, in the days when the judges ruled, that there was a famine in the land. And a certain man of Bethlehem, Judah, went to dwell in the country of Moab, he and his wife and his two sons.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:1 In the days when the judges ruled in Israel, a severe famine came upon the land. So a man from Bethlehem in Judah left his home and went to live in the country of Moab, taking his wife and two sons with him. (NLT)

Main Idea

The story opens in the days when the judges ruled, a troubled era in Israel, with a famine in the land. A man of Bethlehem, Judah, responds by taking his wife and two sons to live in the country of Moab, outside the land of promise.

Verse Explanation

The story opens in the days when the judges ruled, a troubled era in Israel, with a famine in the land. A man of Bethlehem, Judah, responds by taking his wife and two sons to live in the country of Moab, outside the land of promise.

“Now it came to pass, in the days when the judges ruled,”

The book opens with a time stamp that sets the mood for everything that follows. The days of the judges were marked by repeated cycles of unfaithfulness, oppression, and deliverance, and the book of Judges closes by saying that everyone did what was right in his own eyes (Judges 21:25). A small household story is placed inside that troubled national setting. The phrase does not name a particular judge or date, and it does not need to; it tells the reader what kind of world this family lived in.

“that there was a famine in the land.”

“The land” is the land of promise, the land the LORD had given Israel. A famine there was more than an economic hardship; it pressed hard on the question of where God’s people would look for provision. Scripture does not say why this famine came, and it should not be assigned to a particular sin the text never names.

“And a certain man of Bethlehem, Judah, went to dwell in the country of Moab,”

The irony is sharp. “Bethlehem” means house of bread, yet there is no bread there. A man from that town leaves Judah and crosses to “the country of Moab,” east of the Dead Sea, outside the land of promise. To “dwell” here means to live as a resident foreigner in a land not one’s own. The verb itself can describe a short stay or a long one. What the story shows is that this move, made to escape famine, lengthens into years in Moab as the next verses unfold.

“he and his wife and his two sons.”

The whole household goes. The decision of one man shapes the future of his wife and his sons, and the story that follows traces what happened to all of them.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “went to dwell” (1:1) is gur, the ordinary verb for living somewhere as a sojourner or resident alien rather than a native. It can describe either a temporary stay or continuing residence, as with Abraham in Gerar (Genesis 20:1) and Jacob’s family in Egypt (Genesis 47:4).

Judges 17–19 also features people from Bethlehem in Judah during the period of the judges: the Levite who served Micah came from there, and the concubine whose death led to civil war was from Bethlehem. Ruth tells a very different story from the same town and era.

Moab lay on the high plateau east of the Dead Sea, across the Jordan Valley from Judah.

Ruth 1:2
NKJV

Ruth 1:2 The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion"” Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:2 The man’s name was Elimelech, and his wife was Naomi. Their two sons were Mahlon and Kilion. They were Ephrathites from Bethlehem in the land of Judah. And when they reached Moab, they settled there. (NLT)

Main Idea

The narrator names the family: Elimelech, whose name means My God is King, his wife Naomi, and their sons Mahlon and Chilion, Ephrathites of Bethlehem. Going to Moab now becomes remaining there, which places Elimelech’s profession beside his search for relief from famine.

Verse Explanation

The narrator names the family: Elimelech, whose name means My God is King, his wife Naomi, and their sons Mahlon and Chilion, Ephrathites of Bethlehem. Going to Moab now becomes remaining there, which places Elimelech’s profession beside his search for relief from famine.

“The name of the man was Elimelech,”

The man is named only after the move has been reported. “Elimelech” means My God is King. Set against the days of the judges, when Israel had no king, and against the famine he is fleeing, his name places a confession of faith beside a hard test. The question the name raises is whether that profession governed his choices when trust became costly. The text records what he did, not what he thought, so his inner deliberations remain unreported; the warning lies in seeing how a sensible response to hardship can carry a family away from the land of promise.

“the name of his wife was Naomi,”

“Naomi” means pleasant or lovely. The meaning of her name becomes important when she later asks the women of Bethlehem to call her by a different name (1:20).

“and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—”

The two sons are named here and will die in Moab (1:5). Only at 4:10 does the book reveal that Ruth was Mahlon’s widow.

“Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah.”

“Ephrathites” (1:2) identifies the family with Ephrathah, an older name associated with Bethlehem and its clan (Genesis 35:19; Micah 5:2). The description roots them deeply in this particular town of Judah, the same place the book will return to and the place later linked with David.

“And they went to the country of Moab and remained there.”

The verse repeats that they went to Moab and adds that they “remained there.” The family that went to stay for a time is now settled in Moab. Without passing a verdict on Elimelech, the wording shows a temporary relief becoming an extended residence.

Study Notes

The name Elimelech is formed from the Hebrew elements ’eli, my God, and melek, king. Names built on the same pattern, such as Abimelech, appear elsewhere in the Old Testament.

The meanings of Mahlon and Chilion are uncertain. They have often been connected with Hebrew roots meaning sickness and wasting or failing, but the derivations are debated, and the text does not draw any meaning from them.

The Hebrew of “remained there” is simply they were there. It reports where the family was rather than describing a decision, but placed after “went to dwell” (1:1), it shows the stay continuing.

Ruth 1:3
Ruth 1:3
NKJV

Ruth 1:3 Then Elimelech, Naomi's husband, died; and she was left, and her two sons.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:3 Then Elimelech died, and Naomi was left with her two sons. (NLT)

Main Idea

Elimelech dies in Moab, and Naomi is left a widow with her two sons. The narrator reports the first loss plainly, without assigning it a reason.

Verse Explanation

Elimelech dies in Moab, and Naomi is left a widow with her two sons. The narrator reports the first loss plainly, without assigning it a reason.

“Then Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died;”

The first death is reported with stark brevity. Elimelech is now called “Naomi’s husband,” and from this point the story centers on her. Scripture gives no cause and no reason for his death, and it should not be taught as a divine sentence on the move to Moab. The book leaves the loss as a loss.

“and she was left, and her two sons.”

“She was left” describes Naomi as a survivor, a widow in a foreign land with her sons as her only security. In Israel a widow without a husband depended on her sons or her husband’s family for provision and protection, so her future now rests on Mahlon and Chilion.

Study Notes

The verb translated “was left” appears again in 1:5, where Naomi is left without both her sons and her husband. The repetition marks the deepening of her loss.

Ruth 1:4
Ruth 1:4
NKJV

Ruth 1:4 Now they took wives of the women of Moab: the name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other Ruth. And they dwelt there about ten years.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:4 The two sons married Moabite women. One married a woman named Orpah, and the other a woman named Ruth. But about ten years later, (NLT)

Main Idea

The sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and the family stays about ten years. A stay meant to escape famine has become a settled life in Moab, and Ruth enters the story as a woman of Moab, a people who had opposed Israel and were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6).

Verse Explanation

The sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and the family stays about ten years. A stay meant to escape famine has become a settled life in Moab, and Ruth enters the story as a woman of Moab, a people who had opposed Israel and were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6).

“Now they took wives of the women of Moab:”

With their father gone, the sons marry Moabite women. This is significant against Israel’s history with Moab. The Moabites had opposed Israel on the way to Canaan, hired Balaam to curse them, and led Israel into idolatry at Peor (Numbers 22–25). The Law barred a Moabite from entering the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6), and in the days of the judges Eglon king of Moab had oppressed Israel (Judges 3:12–14). These marriages take the family further into life in Moab.

“the name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other Ruth.”

The two wives are introduced by name. “Ruth” appears for the first time, a woman from the very people Israel’s law kept at a distance. That background is what makes the rest of her story such a striking display of God’s redeeming grace. Her Moabite identity is never her personal guilt, and the book will keep calling her Ruth the Moabitess even after she joins herself to Israel’s God.

“And they dwelt there about ten years.”

The verb now is “dwelt” (1:4), the ordinary word for settling and living somewhere. What began as a move to escape famine (1:1) has become a settled life of about a decade. The phrase describes the family’s time in Moab and shows how a short-term answer to famine became a long-term home.

Study Notes

“Dwelt” translates the Hebrew verb yashav, the common verb for sitting, staying, or living in a place. Together with gur in 1:1 and the simple ‘were there’ in 1:2, it marks the family’s lengthening stay as the narrative unfolds, though the verbs alone do not establish how long the family intended to stay or what anyone’s motives were.

The account records the marriages and a stay of about ten years in Moab. It gives no length for either marriage.

Later Jewish tradition debated how Deuteronomy 23:3 applied to Moabite women; the Mishnah (tractate Yevamot, chapter 8) records a view that the prohibition applied to Moabite men only. The book of Ruth itself does not discuss the question.

Ruth 1:5
NKJV

Ruth 1:5 Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:5 both Mahlon and Kilion died. This left Naomi alone, without her two sons or her husband. (NLT)

Main Idea

Mahlon and Chilion also die, so Naomi is left without her two sons and her husband. The household is emptied of its men and its future, the need that the rest of the book will answer.

Verse Explanation

Mahlon and Chilion also die, so Naomi is left without her two sons and her husband. The household is emptied of its men and its future, the need that the rest of the book will answer.

“Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died;”

The second blow falls on both sons. Again the text gives no cause and no explanation. Their deaths should not be read as punishment for their marriages; the book does not say so, and it treats Ruth with honor throughout.

“so the woman survived her two sons and her husband.”

Naomi is now simply “the woman” (1:5), stripped of the relationships that defined her. She has outlived her husband and both sons, and with them she has lost the male line that would carry Elimelech’s name and inherit his land. This is the need that the rest of the book will answer: a household emptied of its men, its heir, and its future, matters that will finally be settled at the city gate (4:9–10).

Study Notes

The word translated “sons” here is the Hebrew word yeled, which usually means child or boy. Its use for grown, married men is unusual and may emphasize Naomi’s sense of loss as a mother. The same word appears again at 4:16 for the child Naomi takes into her arms.

Ruth 1:6–7 — The LORD Gives Bread, and Naomi Sets Out for Home

Ruth 1:6
Ruth 1:6
NKJV

Ruth 1:6 Then she arose with her daughters-in-law that she might return from the country of Moab, for she had heard in the country of Moab that the LORD had visited His people by giving them bread.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:6 Then Naomi heard in Moab that the LORD had blessed his people in Judah by giving them good crops again. So Naomi and her daughters-in-law got ready to leave Moab to return to her homeland. (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi rises with her daughters-in-law to return from Moab because she has heard that the LORD had visited His people by giving them bread. This is the narrator’s first statement of the LORD’s action, and news of His provision moves a grieving widow to turn toward home.

Verse Explanation

Naomi rises with her daughters-in-law to return from Moab because she has heard that the LORD had visited His people by giving them bread. This is the narrator’s first statement of the LORD’s action, and news of His provision moves a grieving widow to turn toward home.

“Then she arose with her daughters-in-law that she might return from the country of Moab,”

Naomi rises to act, and her daughters-in-law rise with her. “Return” is the key word of this chapter, and here it first describes Naomi’s turn back toward home. She begins the journey while still grieving; her response to hope does not wait for her sorrow to lift.

“for she had heard in the country of Moab that the LORD had visited His people by giving them bread.”

Here is the first direct statement of the LORD’s action in the book. To “visit” in this sense means that God came to His people with gracious attention and care. The famine that drove the family out is over, and the house of bread has bread again. News of the LORD’s provision reaches a widow in a foreign land and moves her toward home. The book describes no confession or repentance on Naomi’s part, so none should be imported; what it describes is a grieving woman responding to good news about God’s care for His people.

Study Notes

“Visited” translates the Hebrew verb paqad, a verb with a wide range that includes attending to, caring for, and also punishing. With the LORD as subject and His people as object, it often describes gracious intervention, as in Genesis 50:24, Exodus 4:31, and 1 Samuel 2:21.

This statement and 4:13, where the LORD gives Ruth conception, are the only two places where the narrator directly attributes an action to the LORD. They stand near the beginning and end of the book.

Main Idea

Naomi leaves the place where she has lived, and both daughters-in-law set out with her on the road back to Judah. The return has begun, though whether the younger women will continue with her is still unsettled.

Verse Explanation

Naomi leaves the place where she has lived, and both daughters-in-law set out with her on the road back to Judah. The return has begun, though whether the younger women will continue with her is still unsettled.

“Therefore she went out from the place where she was,”

Naomi leaves the place that had been home for about ten years. The phrase “the place where she was” quietly marks the end of the family’s life in Moab.

“and her two daughters-in-law with her;”

Orpah and Ruth go with her. At this point both women are moving with Naomi, and the reader does not yet know whether they intend to go all the way to Judah or are seeing her off.

“and they went on the way to return to the land of Judah.”

They take the road back toward Judah. The language of returning fits Naomi, who is going home; for the two Moabite women, the land of Judah would be a new and unknown place. That tension sets up the conversation on the road.

Study Notes

The text gives no route or travel details for the journey from Moab to Judah.

Ruth 1:8–14 — Naomi Releases Her Daughters-in-Law

Ruth 1:8
Ruth 1:8
NKJV

Ruth 1:8 And Naomi said to her two daughters-in-law, "Go, return each to her mother's house. The LORD deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:8 But on the way, Naomi said to her two daughters-in-law, “Go back to your mothers’ homes. And may the LORD reward you for your kindness to your husbands and to me. (NLT)

Main Idea

On the way Naomi urges each woman to go back to her mother’s house and prays that the LORD will deal kindly with them, as they have dealt with the dead and with her. Her blessing honors the loyal kindness they have already shown and puts their good ahead of her own need for them.

Verse Explanation

On the way Naomi urges each woman to go back to her mother’s house and prays that the LORD will deal kindly with them, as they have dealt with the dead and with her. Her blessing honors the loyal kindness they have already shown and puts their good ahead of her own need for them.

“And Naomi said to her two daughters-in-law, “Go, return each to her mother’s house.”

On the road Naomi stops and speaks. She urges both women to go back, “each to her mother’s house.” Her words reveal concern for their good rather than her own need. She has nothing to offer them in Judah, and she releases them rather than holding them to herself.

“The LORD deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me.”

Naomi blesses them in the name of the LORD, the God of Israel. She asks Him to “deal kindly” with them, matching the kindness they have already shown to their dead husbands and to her. Her prayer shows that grief has not silenced her faith; she still turns to the LORD on behalf of others. The kindness she names is loyal, faithful love, the quality that will mark Ruth and Boaz throughout the book.

Study Notes

“Kindly” translates the Hebrew noun hesed, steadfast or loyal kindness. This is its first occurrence in Ruth; it returns at 2:20 and 3:10. It describes kindness that goes beyond obligation and holds to a relationship.

The phrase “mother’s house” (1:8) is unusual, since a widow normally returned to her father’s house (compare Genesis 38:11). It appears in a few other passages connected with marriage arrangements (Genesis 24:28; Song of Solomon 3:4; 8:2). Its precise sense here is uncertain.

Main Idea

Naomi prays that the LORD will grant each of them rest in the house of a husband, the security of a settled home. She kisses them goodbye, and all three weep aloud at the parting.

Verse Explanation

Naomi prays that the LORD will grant each of them rest in the house of a husband, the security of a settled home. She kisses them goodbye, and all three weep aloud at the parting.

“The LORD grant that you may find rest, each in the house of her husband.”

Naomi continues her blessing. “Rest” (1:9) here means a settled, secure place in life: the protection, provision, and belonging of a home with a husband. She cannot give them that herself, so she asks the LORD to grant it. This prayer prepares the way for chapter 3, when Naomi will seek security for Ruth (3:1).

“So she kissed them, and they lifted up their voices and wept.”

Naomi kisses them goodbye, and all three weep aloud. The parting is genuinely painful. The scene shows real affection between a mother-in-law and two daughters-in-law who have shared years of loss.

Study Notes

“Rest” (1:9) translates the Hebrew word menuchah, a place of rest or settled security. Naomi’s question in 3:1, “shall I not seek security for you,” uses the closely related word manoach, which ties the two scenes together.

Ruth 1:11
NKJV

Ruth 1:11 But Naomi said, "Turn back, my daughters; why will you go with me? Are there still sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands?(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:11 But Naomi replied, “Why should you go on with me? Can I still give birth to other sons who could grow up to be your husbands? (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi tells them to turn back, asking why they would go with her when she has no more sons to become their husbands. She reasons from the expectation that a dead man’s family would provide his widow with a husband, and she sees no such future for them with her.

Verse Explanation

Naomi tells them to turn back, asking why they would go with her when she has no more sons to become their husbands. She reasons from the expectation that a dead man’s family would provide his widow with a husband, and she sees no such future for them with her.

“But Naomi said, “Turn back, my daughters; why will you go with me?”

Naomi presses them again, calling them “my daughters.” The affectionate address shows that she loves them, and her question asks them to think realistically about what going with her will mean.

“Are there still sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands?”

Naomi reasons from a known custom. When a man died without a son, his brother could marry the widow so that the dead man’s name would continue (compare Deuteronomy 25:5–6). Naomi has no more sons to offer, so she sees no future for them in her family.

Study Notes

The custom Naomi assumes is reflected in Genesis 38 and regulated in Deuteronomy 25:5–10. Her words show the expectation behind her argument; they are not a legal ruling on Ruth’s later marriage.

Verse Explanation

Naomi repeats her plea, saying she is too old to have a husband, and she even imagines a remote possibility: a husband that very night and more sons. Her argument shows how completely she has lost hope of rebuilding her household by human means.

“Turn back, my daughters, go—for I am too old to have a husband.”

For the third time Naomi tells them to turn back. She explains that, in her view, she is past the age of remarriage, so even the hope of new sons through a new husband is closed to her.

“If I should say I have hope, if I should have a husband tonight and should also bear sons,”

Naomi now imagines a remote possibility for the sake of argument: suppose she still had hope, suppose she married that very night and bore sons. She is describing an imagined situation, and the sentence continues into the next verse. It shows how completely she has given up any hope of rebuilding her household by human means.

Main Idea

Naomi asks whether they would wait for such sons to grow up and hold back from marrying. She grieves for their sakes that the hand of the LORD has gone out against her, a lament that still speaks of her life in relation to the LORD.

Verse Explanation

Naomi asks whether they would wait for such sons to grow up and hold back from marrying. She grieves for their sakes that the hand of the LORD has gone out against her, a lament that still speaks of her life in relation to the LORD.

“would you wait for them till they were grown? Would you restrain yourselves from having husbands?”

Naomi completes her argument. Even in that unlikely case, would they wait years for such sons to grow up and refuse other marriages in the meantime? The answer is plainly no. She wants them to see that their best hope of rest lies in Moab.

“No, my daughters; for it grieves me very much for your sakes that the hand of the LORD has gone out against me!”

Naomi grieves “for your sakes,” sorrowing that her own losses have become theirs as well. She speaks of “the hand of the LORD” as having gone out against her. This is the language of lament. She does not doubt that the LORD rules over her life; she feels His hand as heavy and does not understand it. Her words express honest grief from a woman who still interprets her sorrow in relation to God, not unbelief.

Study Notes

The phrase “it grieves me very much for your sakes” can also be translated ‘it is far more bitter for me than for you.’ The Hebrew comparison can be read either way, and both readings fit Naomi’s grief. The NKJV takes it as sorrow on their behalf.

Main Idea

The women weep again, and the decisive difference appears: Orpah kisses her mother-in-law goodbye, but Ruth clings to her. Orpah does what Naomi has urged, while Ruth holds fast in loyal commitment.

Verse Explanation

The women weep again, and the decisive difference appears: Orpah kisses her mother-in-law goodbye, but Ruth clings to her. Orpah does what Naomi has urged, while Ruth holds fast in loyal commitment.

“Then they lifted up their voices and wept again;”

Once again all three weep aloud. The repeated weeping shows that the decision is costly for everyone.

“and Orpah kissed her mother-in-law,”

Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye. She does what Naomi has urged, and nothing in the text condemns her. She had shown kindness to the dead and to Naomi (1:8), and her return is a reasonable choice in her situation. The book does not tell us her inner motives or what became of her.

“but Ruth clung to her.”

The little word “but” marks the decisive difference. Ruth “clung” (1:14) to Naomi, holding fast to her in loyal commitment. The same verb describes a husband being joined to his wife (Genesis 2:24) and Israel holding fast to the LORD (Deuteronomy 10:20). Ruth’s clinging is more than an embrace; it is a binding of herself to Naomi.

Study Notes

“Clung” translates the Hebrew verb davaq, to cling, cleave, or stick to. The word is used of close loyalty in relationships and of devotion to the LORD.

The Septuagint, an ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, adds that Orpah returned to her people, a phrase not present in the Hebrew at this point. The NKJV follows the Hebrew text.

Ruth 1:15–18 — Ruth’s Vow of Allegiance to Naomi and Her God

Ruth 1:15
Ruth 1:15
NKJV

Ruth 1:15 And she said, "Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods; return after your sister-in-law."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 1:15 “Look,” Naomi said to her, “your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods. You should do the same.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi points out that Orpah has gone back to her people and to her gods and urges Ruth to follow her. Her words make plain that returning to Moab means returning to Moab’s gods, and they set the stakes for Ruth’s reply.

Verse Explanation

Naomi points out that Orpah has gone back to her people and to her gods and urges Ruth to follow her. Her words make plain that returning to Moab means returning to Moab’s gods, and they set the stakes for Ruth’s reply.

“And she said, “Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods;”

Naomi points to Orpah’s departure as an example. She describes it as going back not only “to her people” but also “to her gods” (1:15). For a Moabite, returning home meant returning to Moab’s worship, especially Chemosh, the national god of Moab (Numbers 21:29). Naomi’s words make plain what is at stake. Going back to Moab means going back to Moab’s gods.

“return after your sister-in-law.”

Naomi urges Ruth one last time to follow Orpah. This final appeal is addressed to Ruth alone, and it draws out Ruth’s great reply.

Study Notes

“Sister-in-law” translates the Hebrew word yevemet, a word related to the custom of a brother marrying his dead brother’s widow. It appears only here and in Deuteronomy 25:7–9.

“Gods” (1:15) translates the Hebrew word ’elohim. The NKJV takes it as plural, the gods of Moab. Because the same word can refer to a single deity, some interpreters take the reference to be Chemosh, Moab’s national god (compare Judges 11:24).

Main Idea

Ruth begs Naomi not to press her to leave, promising to go where she goes and lodge where she lodges. She declares, “Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God,” joining herself to Israel and to the LORD in costly allegiance, and her turning to Israel’s God begins the story of His redeeming grace toward a woman from Moab.

Verse Explanation

Ruth begs Naomi not to press her to leave, promising to go where she goes and lodge where she lodges. She declares, “Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God,” joining herself to Israel and to the LORD in costly allegiance, and her turning to Israel’s God begins the story of His redeeming grace toward a woman from Moab.

“But Ruth said: “Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you;”

Ruth answers with words that have become some of the best-known in Scripture. She begs Naomi to stop urging her to leave. To “turn back from following after” Naomi would mean returning to Moab, and Ruth refuses.

“For wherever you go, I will go; And wherever you lodge, I will lodge;”

Ruth commits herself to share Naomi’s road and Naomi’s home. Wherever Naomi travels and wherever she stays the night, Ruth will be with her. She chooses an uncertain future with a widow who has nothing to offer her.

“Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God.”

This is the heart of Ruth’s vow and the spiritual climax of the chapter. She binds herself to Naomi’s “people,” Israel, and to Naomi’s “God,” the LORD. Ruth’s commitment grows out of both loyal love for her grieving mother-in-law and a real recognition of the God she had seen in Naomi’s faith, even through Naomi’s sorrow. The text does not tell us when or how Ruth came to trust Israel’s God, and it records no private conversations; it shows the faith expressed in her vow. A woman from Moab, a people long hostile to Israel and barred from the assembly of the LORD, now turns to Israel’s God. That turning begins the story of God’s redeeming grace toward her, a grace that will reach its fullness in Jesus Christ, who gathers people from every nation to Himself. Her new allegiance does not erase her identity; she remains Ruth the Moabitess (1:22).

Study Notes

The vow is written in poetic, parallel lines, which the NKJV sets with capital letters at the start of each line. The pairing of go and lodge, and people and God, gives the words their memorable rhythm.

Ruth’s words were spoken to her mother-in-law, not to a husband. Their later use at weddings applies their loyalty to a new setting.

Ruth carries her commitment through death and burial in Naomi’s land and seals it with a solemn oath in the LORD’s name. Swearing by the LORD, she binds herself to Him as well as to Naomi, so that only death will part them.

“Where you die, I will die, And there will I be buried.”

Ruth extends her commitment to the end of life and beyond. She will die where Naomi dies and be buried there. In the ancient world burial among one’s own people was deeply important, so this promise means that Ruth will never go back to Moab, even in death. She is joining Naomi’s people for good.

“The LORD do so to me, and more also, If anything but death parts you and me.”

Ruth seals her vow with a solemn oath, calling on “the LORD,” the covenant name of Israel’s God, to deal with her severely if anything but death separates them. This is the first time Ruth speaks the LORD’s name. By swearing by Him, she shows that her allegiance to Israel’s God is real; she is binding herself to Him as well as to Naomi.

Study Notes

“The LORD do so to me, and more also” is a standard Hebrew oath formula, found also in 1 Samuel 3:17, 2 Samuel 3:9, and 1 Kings 2:23. The speaker invokes divine judgment on himself or herself if the promise is broken.

The final line can also be read ‘if even death parts you and me,’ meaning that not even death will separate them. The NKJV’s “if anything but death” is the more common rendering.

The journey itself is summarized in a single sentence. “The two of them” travel together, the Israelite widow and the Moabite widow, until they reach Bethlehem.

Their arrival causes a stir throughout the town. “Excited” here describes commotion and buzzing talk. Naomi had been known in Bethlehem, and her return after so many years, without her husband and sons, draws attention.

“and the women said, “Is this Naomi?”

The women of the town ask in surprise, “Is this Naomi?” Their question suggests how greatly the years of loss have changed her. These same women will speak again at the end of the book, when they bless the LORD for the son born to her family (4:14–17).

Study Notes

The verb translated “was excited” (1:19) is the Hebrew verb hum, used for a stir or uproar, as when the earth resounded at the ark’s arrival in Israel’s camp (1 Samuel 4:5) and when the city rang with noise at Solomon’s anointing (1 Kings 1:45).

In Hebrew the form of “the women said” is feminine plural, which the NKJV makes explicit by supplying “the women.”

Naomi asks not to be called Naomi but Mara, meaning bitter, because the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with her. Her words are honest lament from a woman who still understands her sorrow in relation to God.

“But she said to them, “Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara,”

Naomi asks the women not to call her Naomi, pleasant, but “Mara,” bitter. She wants a name that fits her experience. Her request is an honest expression of how she feels after years of loss, and the reader should hear it with compassion.

“for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.”

Naomi names God as “the Almighty” and says He has dealt very bitterly with her. Her words resemble Job’s lament (Job 27:2). Naomi’s bitterness is lament within continuing faith, not unbelief. She does not deny God or turn away from Him; she speaks to Him and about Him in her pain, as a woman who still understands her life in His hands.

Study Notes

“The Almighty” translates the Hebrew name Shaddai, a divine name used frequently in Genesis with the patriarchs and many times in Job. Its precise derivation is uncertain.

Despite Naomi’s request, the narrator continues to call her Naomi throughout the book, and no one in the story uses the name Mara again.

Verse Explanation

Naomi says she went out full but the LORD has brought her home empty, and she asks why they call her Naomi when the LORD has testified against her and afflicted her. Her lament names the emptiness the book will answer, though she speaks it with Ruth standing beside her.

“I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty.”

Naomi sums up her life in two words: full and empty. She left Bethlehem with a husband and two sons and returns without them. This sentence names the emptiness that the rest of the book will answer. Yet as she speaks it, Ruth stands beside her, and the harvest is beginning. Her perception is honest, but it is not the whole story of what God is doing.

“Why do you call me Naomi, since the LORD has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me?”

Naomi asks why they would call her pleasant when the LORD has “testified against” her and “the Almighty has afflicted” her. She speaks of her suffering as coming from God, using the language of a lament. Her words do not establish that God was punishing her family; they express how she experienced her losses. She keeps speaking of the LORD and the Almighty, which shows that her faith, though wounded, has not been abandoned.

Study Notes

“Testified against me” (1:21) translates the Hebrew verb ‘anah with a preposition meaning ‘against,’ the idiom used for bearing witness against someone (compare Exodus 20:16). The ancient Greek translation reads ‘humbled me’ or ‘afflicted me,’ reflecting a related verb, but that sense does not ordinarily take this preposition, which is one reason the NKJV and many translations keep the legal sense. Either way, Naomi describes God’s action as the cause of her sorrow.

The contrast between full and empty is picked up later in the book, when Boaz sends Ruth home with barley so that she will not go back to Naomi “empty-handed” (3:17).

NKJV

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

The narrator sums up the return of Naomi with Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter-in-law, and notes that they came to Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest. Ruth is still called a Moabite, and the harvest signals provision already present before Naomi can recognize it.

“So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab.”

This verse sums up the chapter. Naomi has “returned,” and with her is “Ruth the Moabitess.” The narrator keeps calling her a Moabite, even after her vow, so the reader does not forget where she comes from. The closing words, “who returned from the country of Moab,” sum up this homecoming. The Hebrew construction is not entirely clear, and the words may describe Ruth or may refer back to Naomi. If they describe Ruth, they do not mean that she had been to Bethlehem before; she comes as Naomi’s companion, having already bound herself to Naomi’s people and God in her vow (1:16).

“Now they came to Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest.”

The chapter ends with a note of quiet hope. The women arrive “at the beginning of barley harvest” (1:22), the first grain harvest of the year. The famine that drove the family out is over, and the house of bread is full of grain again. Naomi cannot yet see it, but the LORD’s provision is already present. In the larger story of Scripture, this town will be the home of David and, generations later, the birthplace of Jesus Christ (Micah 5:2; Matthew 2:1), and the Moabite woman who arrives here will stand in His family line (Matthew 1:5).

Study Notes

Barley ripened earlier than wheat and was harvested in the spring. Ruth 2:23 mentions both the barley harvest and the wheat harvest that followed.

The Hebrew verb translated “return” (shuv) occurs repeatedly across 1:6–22 and gives the chapter its central movement. In 1:22 the form behind “who returned” can be read as referring to Ruth or as summing up Naomi’s return; the servant in 2:6 also describes Ruth as the one who “came back with Naomi.”
