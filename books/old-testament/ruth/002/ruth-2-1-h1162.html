BOAZ - ( bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

Boaz, whose name means "in him is strength," is a prominent and godly landowner in Bethlehem during the chaotic period of the Judges. He serves as the kinsman-redeemer for Ruth the Moabitess and her mother-in-law Naomi. Through his righteousness, generosity, and faithfulness to the covenant, Boaz rescues Elimelech’s lineage from extinction, marries Ruth, and becomes the great-grandfather of King David, positioning him as a prominent ancestor of Jesus Christ and a powerful Old Testament type of Christ the Redeemer.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Boaz appears in the Old Testament during the dark and spiritually bankrupt era of the Judges. This was a time characterized by moral compromise, idolatry, and societal decay, where "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). Against this bleak cultural backdrop, Boaz emerges as a beacon of covenant faithfulness, spiritual integrity, and obedience to the law of Moses.

Historically, Boaz was a wealthy landowner in Bethlehem. His lineage is traceably significant; he was the son of Salmon and Rahab, the Canaanite harlot who hid the spies in Jericho. This unique heritage likely shaped his compassionate outlook toward foreigners, preparing his heart to receive, protect, and ultimately redeem Ruth, a Moabitess. Boaz represents the ideal Israelite citizen—one who honors God's Word in his business practices, treats his workers with dignity, and acts with absolute integrity.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name bo'az translates directly as "in him is strength" or "by strength." It is a compound word formed from the prepositional prefix be (meaning "in" or "by") and the noun oz (meaning "strength," "might," or "power").

A secondary, though less common, etymological option suggests the name may derive from an unused root meaning "fleetness" or "alacrity." However, the primary theological and historical consensus points to the theme of strength. The name is also used for the left-hand bronze pillar erected at the vestibule of King Solomon's temple (1 Kings 7:21), standing as a monument of God's enduring strength and stability established in His house.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the book of Ruth, Boaz is introduced with a specific Hebrew description: gibbor chayil (Ruth 2:1). While the NKJV translates this phrase as "a man of great wealth," it carries a far richer semantic range:

• A Man of Valor: Chayil is frequently used in military contexts to describe a mighty warrior or a man of valor. Applied to Boaz, it portrays him as a spiritual warrior of moral excellence and courage.

• A Man of Substance and Standing: The term indicates a man of high social standing, influence, capability, and material wealth, which he uses responsibly to bless others.

• The Counterpart to the Virtuous Woman: The same root word chayil is used to describe Ruth as a "virtuous woman" (eshet chayil) in Ruth 3:11, and the ideal wife in Proverbs 31:10. This creates a powerful word picture of a matching pair: a man of moral strength meeting a woman of moral strength.

### 4. Theological Implications

The narrative of Boaz provides one of the most complete and beautiful Old Testament typologies of Jesus Christ through the office of the goel (kinsman-redeemer). Under Mosaic law, the kinsman-redeemer had the right and duty to buy back land lost by a relative, redeem family members from slavery, and marry the widow of a deceased relative to carry on the family name (levirate marriage).

To qualify as a kinsman-redeemer, three conditions had to be met, all of which Boaz fulfilled, prefiguring Christ:

• The Right to Redeem (Kinship): The redeemer had to be a near blood relative. Boaz was of the family of Elimelech (Ruth 2:1). Jesus became flesh and blood to become our brother and qualify as our redeemer (Hebrews 2:14-17).

• The Power to Redeem (Wealth): The redeemer had to possess the financial resources to pay the redemption price. Boaz was a man of great wealth. Jesus paid our infinite debt not with silver or gold, but with His precious blood (1 Peter 1:18-19).

• The Will to Redeem (Willingness): The redemption could not be forced; it had to be a voluntary act of love. While another closer relative refused to redeem Ruth out of fear of ruining his own inheritance, Boaz gladly and publicly stepped forward to redeem her (Ruth 4:1-10). Jesus willingly laid down His life for us (John 10:17-18).

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:1, the introduction of Boaz is strategically placed immediately after Naomi's sorrowful declaration in chapter 1: "I went out full, and the Lord has brought me home again empty" (Ruth 1:21).

The author immediately counters Naomi’s despair by introducing Boaz. The text transitions from the emptiness of the two widows to the abundance of a man whose very name means "in him is strength." The literary contrast is stark: Naomi and Ruth are weak, destitute, and childless, but God has already positioned a man of immense strength, wealth, and character in Bethlehem to meet their needs. Before Ruth ever steps into the field to glean, God's sovereign provision is already waiting for her in the person of Boaz.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A striking thematic contrast exists between the two main male figures of the Elimelech household and Boaz. Elimelech’s name means "My God is King," yet he abandoned Bethlehem during a famine to seek security in pagan Moab, resulting in tragedy. Conversely, Boaz, whose name means "in him is strength," remained in Bethlehem, trusted in the Lord's provision, and became the source of strength and life for others.

Additionally, the marriage of Boaz and Ruth is a beautiful picture of the inclusion of the Gentiles into the household of faith. This union bridged the gap between Israel and the nations, directly leading to the birth of King David and ultimately the Messiah, Jesus Christ, who extends redemption to all nations.
