GREAT - ( gibbôr , Strong's H1368 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word gibbôr conveys the idea of power, valiant strength, and influential status, typically describing a mighty warrior or a heroic champion. In the context of Ruth 2:1, it describes Boaz not simply as a man of vast financial resources, but as a person of noble character, social prominence, and moral fortitude. This word introduces Boaz as a capable redeemer who possesses both the earthly resources and the spiritual strength to rescue Naomi and Ruth from their empty, vulnerable state.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word gibbôr originates from the verbal root gābar, which means to be strong, to prevail, to exert power, or to behave valiantly. In the ancient Near Eastern world, a gibbôr was primarily a champion of the battlefield—a warrior of extraordinary physical prowess and courage who stood as a protector of his people. The term evokes the image of a shield, a stronghold, or a towering hero who steps into a desperate conflict to turn the tide of battle. When applied to Boaz in an agricultural and family setting, the word paints a picture of a "moral champion." He is not a soldier fighting physical enemies, but a mighty man of social standing and spiritual integrity who is ready to battle the systemic poverty and hopelessness threatening his deceased relative's family.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of gibbôr is mighty, strong, valiant, or a powerful person. It refers to someone who excels in strength, influence, or authority. In the context of Ruth 2:1, it is paired with chayil (which means strength, virtue, capability, or wealth) to form the phrase gibbôr chayil. While some translations focus on the material aspect—rendering it "a man of great wealth"—the lexical range of gibbôr demands a definition that includes physical capability, social influence, and ethical strength. He is a man of substance, both in his bank account and in his character.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, gibbôr is used to describe various types of power:

• Physical and Martial Might: It describes legendary figures of power, such as Nimrod, the first "mighty one" on the earth (Genesis 10:8), and the elite soldiers of David's army known as his "mighty men" (2 Samuel 23:8).

• Divine Power: The term is used metaphorically to describe God Himself. He is the ultimate gibbôr—the "Mighty God" (El Gibbôr) who fights for His people and delivers them from oppression (Isaiah 9:6; Zephaniah 3:17).

• Moral and Spiritual Might: In the wisdom literature, a man who controls his own spirit is declared to be better than the physically mighty (gibbôr) who takes a city (Proverbs 16:32).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the use of gibbôr for Boaz points directly to the character of God as Redeemer. Boaz acts as a type of Jesus Christ. Just as Boaz is the gibbôr who has the resources and the righteous will to redeem Ruth, Jesus is the ultimate gibbôr—the Mighty God who enters our poverty, pays our debt, and redeems us into His family. This demonstrates that God uses those of high standing and strength not for self-aggrandizement, but to act as agents of His covenant love (chesed) toward the weak, the foreign, and the marginalized.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:1, the narrator introduces Boaz to the reader before Ruth even meets him. By labeling him a gibbôr, the text provides immediate hope. Ruth and Naomi are widows, destitute and highly vulnerable. By defining Boaz as a "man of great [strength/wealth]," the author assures the reader that God has already positioned a solution in the field. Furthermore, there is a beautiful literary parallel later in the book: in Ruth 3:11, Boaz calls Ruth an eshet chayil (a virtuous/worthy woman). The matching vocabulary reveals that Boaz and Ruth are spiritual equals; the "mighty man of valor" recognizes the "woman of valor," demonstrating that true greatness in God's eyes is defined by character, not just social status.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound contrast exists between Boaz and the other men in the book of Ruth. Elimelech (Naomi's late husband) fled Bethlehem during a famine, seeking safety in pagan Moab, yet he died there. Mahlon and Chilion also perished. In contrast, Boaz remained in Bethlehem, endured the famine, maintained his righteousness, and became a gibbôr of security for others. His life proves that remaining steadfast in the place of God's promise produces enduring, life-giving strength that outlasts the temporary compromises of fear.
