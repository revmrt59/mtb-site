FAMILY - ( mishpachah , Strong's H4940 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mishpachah refers to a clan, subdivision of a tribe, or an extended family unit sharing a common lineage and geographical area. In the ancient Israelite social structure, the mishpachah served as the vital protective, economic, and legal safety net between the larger tribe and the immediate patriarchal household. In Ruth 2:1, identifying Boaz as belonging to the "family" of Elimelech is not a mere genealogical detail; it is the theological and narrative hinge of the entire story. It immediately alerts the reader that Boaz is legally qualified to act as a kinsman-redeemer, showcasing how God's quiet providence operates through the established structures of family loyalty and covenant responsibility.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, social identity was structured in concentric circles. At the broadest level was the tribe (shevet or matteh), which provided regional identity and political alignment. At the smallest level was the immediate household (bet ab, meaning "house of the father"), consisting of a man, his wife, children, and immediate dependents.

The mishpachah was the crucial bridge between these two. It was a collection of closely related households living in the same vicinity, forming a clan. The clan was the primary functional unit for military mobilization, land preservation, and mutual welfare. If a family member fell into poverty, sold ancestral land, or was forced into slavery, the members of the mishpachah were responsible for stepping in to redeem them. It represents a concrete, localized network of mutual covenantal obligation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term mishpachah denotes a clan, sub-tribe, family group, class, or kind. It derives from an unused root meaning to associate or join together, emphasizing the bound nature of the relationship.

The word is used to describe:

• A subdivision of an Israelite tribe sharing a common ancestral patriarch (e.g., the clan of the Kohathites).

• An extended family unit larger than a single household but bound by blood and land.

• A species or category of animals (Genesis 8:19).

• The nations of the earth, viewed as the extended families of humanity (Genesis 12:3).

Unlike the modern Western concept of the nuclear family, mishpachah emphasizes shared destiny, shared land inheritance, and legal responsibility for one another's survival.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, mishpachah is frequently pictured as a protective hedge or a safety net.

• The Census and Military Unit: In the book of Numbers, the strength of the nation is measured by its mishpachot (plural of mishpachah). Men did not fight as isolated individuals but alongside their clan members, meaning they were fighting to protect their immediate kinsmen.

• The Land Guardian: In Joshua, the Promised Land is distributed not to individuals, but "according to their families" (mishpachot). The land belonged to the clan in perpetuity. If a portion of land was sold due to poverty, it was the duty of the mishpachah to buy it back so that the ancestral inheritance remained intact.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of the mishpachah is deeply tied to God's covenantal design for community and redemption.

• Universal Blessing: When God covenants with Abraham in Genesis 12:3, He promises that "all the families (mishpachot) of the earth" will be blessed through him. God's redemptive plan targets the foundational units of human relationship.

• The Vehicle of Redemption: The laws of the kinsman-redeemer (goel) laid out in Leviticus 25 require that the redeemer must belong to the impoverished person's mishpachah. Redemption is not a bureaucratic state action; it is a personal, relational duty born of covenant love (chesed) within the family.

• The God of the Families: Yahweh is frequently portrayed as the God of all the mishpachot of Israel (Jeremiah 31:1), showing His desire to be intimately involved in the relational structures of His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:1, the narrator introduces Boaz: "There was a relative of her husband’s, a man of great wealth, of the family (mishpachah) of Elimelech; his name was Boaz."

This detail is structurally and theologically monumental for several reasons:

• The Problem of Empty Hands: Naomi and Ruth have returned from Moab completely empty. They have no husbands, no heirs, and no immediate household (bet ab) left to support them.

• The Hope of Redemption: By identifying Boaz as part of the mishpachah of Elimelech, the text signals to the reader that hope is not lost. The ancestral line of Elimelech and the family land can still be redeemed because there is a living kinsman within the protective circle of the clan.

• Sovereign Provision: While Ruth "happens" to glean in Boaz’s field in Ruth 2:3, the reader is already comforted by the knowledge of Ruth 2:1. God has already positioned a member of the redeeming mishpachah in the path of the destitute widow.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:3 - "I will bless those who bless you, and I will curse him who curses you; and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed."

• Leviticus 25:10 - "And you shall consecrate the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land to all its inhabitants. It shall be a Jubilee for you; and each of you shall return to his possession, and each of you shall return to his family."

• Leviticus 25:49 - "...or his uncle or his uncle’s son may redeem him; or anyone who is near of kin to him from his family may redeem him; or if he is able, he may redeem himself."

• Joshua 7:14 - "In the morning therefore you shall be brought according to your tribes. And it shall be that the tribe which the Lord takes shall come according to its families; and the family which the Lord takes shall come by households; and the household which the Lord takes shall come man by man."

• Jeremiah 31:1 - "'At the same time,' says the Lord, 'I will be the God of all the families of Israel, and they shall be My people.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast with the Era of Judges: The book of Ruth is set during the dark period of the Judges, a time characterized by spiritual anarchy and the breakdown of social structures. The functioning of the mishpachah in Ruth stands as a beautiful oasis of covenant faithfulness. While Israel at large was failing to keep the covenant, Boaz and his clan were quietly demonstrating how God's law protects the vulnerable.

• New Testament Connection: In the New Testament, the concept of the mishpachah is elevated to a spiritual reality. Through adoption in Jesus Christ, believers are brought into the "household of God" (Ephesians 2:19). Christ became our near kinsman by taking on human flesh so that He, as our brother from the human family, could legally pay our debt and redeem us.
