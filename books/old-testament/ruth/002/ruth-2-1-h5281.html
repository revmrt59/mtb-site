NAOMI - ( No'omiy , Strong's H5281 )

Summary:

Naomi is a Hebrew proper name meaning "my pleasantness" or "delight." Derived from a root denoting beauty, sweetness, and agreeableness, her name stands in stark, poignant contrast to her self-proclaimed identity of "Mara" ("bitter") following the tragic loss of her husband and sons. In the narrative of Ruth, Naomi represents the empty, grieving soul whom God restores to fullness, illustrating the movement from bitterness to pleasantness through divine providence and covenant faithfulness (hesed).

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The name Naomi comes from the Hebrew root na'em, meaning to be pleasant, lovely, or delightful. In ancient Near Eastern culture, names were not merely labels but often reflected a person's character, destiny, or the parents' circumstances and prayers. The name Naomi carries connotations of a life that is sweet, agreeable, and highly favored.

• The tragic events of Ruth chapter 1 invert this name. After losing her husband Elimelech and her two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, in Moab, Naomi returns to Bethlehem empty-handed. When the women of Bethlehem greet her, she requests, "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (Ruth 1:20). Her original name serves as a constant literary and theological foil to her emotional and spiritual state throughout the early stages of the book.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The name literally translates to "my pleasantness," "my sweetness," or "delightful."

• It is a feminine proper noun derived from the noun no'am, which signifies beauty, kindness, favor, or pleasantness (as seen in Psalm 90:17, "the beauty of the Lord").

• The lexical core reflects harmony, emotional sweetness, and a state of being pleasing or attractive, both in character and circumstances.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The name Naomi occurs 21 times in the Old Testament, exclusively within the Book of Ruth.

• The word picture associated with her name is one of sweetness and communal joy. When she is called Naomi, she represents the original blessing of the covenant community in Bethlehem ("House of Bread").

• When she rejects her name for "Mara" (bitterness), she paints a word picture of a dried-up, bitter spring. Yet, the narrative continues to call her Naomi, signaling that God has not abandoned her true identity. Her "pleasantness" is not permanently lost; it is merely obscured by temporary suffering.

4. Theological Implications

• Providence and Restoration: Naomi's journey from pleasantness to bitterness and back to a restored state of blessing demonstrates the overriding providence of God. Though she claims "the Almighty has afflicted me" (Ruth 1:21), God uses Ruth and Boaz to restore her "pleasantness."

• The Covenant Character of God: Naomi's life shows that God's covenant loyalty (hesed) is designed to turn our "Mara" back into "Naomi." In the final chapter, the women of Bethlehem praise God because Naomi is no longer empty; she has a redeemer (Obed), and her life is once again filled with pleasantness and purpose.

• The Name of Grace: Even when Naomi views herself as bitter and empty, the narrator persistently refers to her as Naomi. This highlights a profound theological truth: our identity in God's eyes is not defined by our current trials, emotional pain, or temporary crises, but by His ultimate purpose for us.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage (Ruth 2:1)

• In Ruth 2:1, the text reads: "There was a relative of Naomi’s husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech. His name was Boaz."

• Here, Naomi is identified as the link connecting Ruth to the family of Elimelech and ultimately to Boaz, the kinsman-redeemer.

• The reference to "Naomi's husband" (Elimelech) underscores her legal and relational standing in Bethlehem. It anchors her to the land and the inheritance that is at stake. Even though Elimelech is dead, Naomi remains the key legal figure through whom the redemption of the land and the family line must occur. Her name in this verse serves as the bridge between the tragedy of the past (her deceased husband) and the hope of the future (Boaz, the man of great wealth).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:2 – "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah."

• Ruth 1:20 – "But she said to them, 'Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.'"

• Ruth 2:2 – "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

• Ruth 4:14 – "Then the women said to Naomi: 'Blessed be the Lord, who has not left you this day without a close relative; and may his name be famous in Israel!'"

• Ruth 4:17 – "The neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'There is a son born to Naomi.' And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between Naomi ("pleasant") and Mara ("bitter") is a classic literary device of reversal (peripeteia) in Hebrew narrative. Her name is restored to its full meaning when she nurses Obed, the grandfather of King David, transitioning her from a widow without a future to a grandmother of Israel's royal line.

• Her name also serves to contrast her character with Ruth’s. While Ruth represents active covenant faithfulness (hesed), Naomi represents the broken covenant community that receives the blessings of that faithfulness.
