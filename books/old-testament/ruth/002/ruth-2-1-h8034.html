NAME - ( shem , Strong's H8034 )

Summary:

In the Ancient Near East, a name (shem) was far more than a convenient label for identification; it represented a person’s character, reputation, authority, and ultimate destiny. In the book of Ruth, names carry profound theological and narrative weight, signaling the preservation of a family lineage and the character of those involved. In Ruth 2:1, the introduction of Boaz by "name" contrasts sharply with the looming threat of the family name of Elimelech being blotted out, setting the stage for redemption and the preservation of a legacy that would ultimately lead to King David and the Messiah.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, a name was intrinsically tied to the existence, essence, and identity of the person or object bearing it. To have no name was equivalent to non-existence, while to have one's name forgotten or blotted out was the ultimate tragedy—amounting to cosmic erasure. The Hebrew word shem carries the concrete concept of a monument, mark, or memorial. It is the enduring sign of a person’s presence on earth. In genealogical and covenantal contexts, shem represents the continuity of a household (bet) across generations. The preservation of a man's name through land ownership and offspring was a vital component of covenant life in Israel, ensuring that his memory and portion in the Promised Land remained forever.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of shem is "name," but its semantic range extends to "reputation," "fame," "renown," and "memorial."

• Individual Identity: It distinguishes one person, place, or thing from another.

• Reputation and Character: It represents the public standing, honor, or moral character of an individual (as in "having a good name").

• Authority and Ownership: To act in someone's name is to act with their delegated authority, and to call a place by one's name denotes ownership and sovereignty.

• Legacy and Continuity: In legal and inheritance contexts, it refers to the survival of a family lineage and its historical memory within the covenant community.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs shem in several vivid conceptual word pictures:

• The Name of Yahweh: God's name is not merely a title but the active manifestation of His presence, power, and covenant accessibility. To call upon His name is to worship Him in accordance with His revealed character.

• Making a Name: In Genesis 11, humanity attempts to build the Tower of Babel to "make a name" for themselves—an act of proud self-deification. In contrast, in Genesis 12, God promises to make Abraham's "name great," demonstrating that true renown is a gift of divine grace, not human effort.

• Cutting Off the Name: This graphic idiom describes the absolute destruction of a family line, where no descendants remain to carry on the family inheritance. It represents spiritual and historical death.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of shem in the Old Testament, particularly in the historical books, centers on God's faithfulness to preserve His people.

• Covenant Preservation: God binds His own shem (reputation) to the preservation of Israel's tribal names and inheritances. The book of Ruth demonstrates that God cares about individual family names, even those of obscure widows.

• Providential Redemption: The preservation of Elimelech's name through the selflessness of Boaz highlights how human actions of loving-kindness (hesed) align with God's sovereign plan.

• The Messianic Line: The ultimate preservation of the "name" in Ruth does not merely rescue a local family from poverty; it preserves the lineage of King David, pointing forward to the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ, whose name is above every name.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:1, the narrator introduces a pivotal character: "There was a relative of Naomi's husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech; his name was Boaz."

• An Answer to Despair: This verse immediately follows Naomi’s bitter lament in chapter 1, where she claimed she returned empty and that God had afflicted her. The explicit mention of Boaz by "name" serves as a literary beacon of hope, assuring the reader that God has already provided the solution.

• A Worthy Character: The name "Boaz" likely means "in him is strength" or "quickness." The narrator couples his name with the description gibbor chayil ("a man of great wealth" or "mighty man of valor"), signaling that his character matches the strength implied by his name.

• The Named vs. the Unnamed: This introduction sets up a brilliant contrast later in the book. While Boaz's name is honorably recorded, the closer kinsman-redeemer who refuses to redeem Ruth is left anonymous in Ruth 4:1, referred to only as peloni almoni ("such a one" or "so-and-so"). Because the unnamed relative feared jeopardizing his own inheritance (his own name), his actual name is blotted out of the biblical text, whereas Boaz’s name is immortalized.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:2 - "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great; and you shall be a blessing."

• Exodus 3:15 - "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you." This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations.'"

• Ruth 4:10 - "Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from his position at the gate. You are witnesses this day."

• Proverbs 22:1 - "A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches, loving favor rather than silver and gold."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew concept of shem directly prepares the way for the New Testament understanding of the name of Jesus (onoma in Greek). To believe in the name of Jesus (John 1:12), to pray in His name (John 14:13), and to be saved by His name (Acts 4:12) means to rely entirely upon His finished work, His authority, and His revealed character. Just as Boaz stepped in to raise up the name of the dead, Christ steps into our spiritual bankruptcy to give us a new name and an eternal inheritance.
