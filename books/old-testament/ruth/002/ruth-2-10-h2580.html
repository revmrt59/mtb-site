FAVOR - ( chen, Strong's H2580 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chen refers to favor, grace, or acceptance shown by a superior to an inferior who has no inherent claim to it. It denotes a disposition of kindness, charm, or attractiveness that moves another to act with unmerited generosity. In the book of Ruth, it highlights the profound, transforming power of unexpected grace extended to a destitute outsider, mirroring God's redeeming grace toward His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of chen is the verb chanan, which means to show favor, be gracious, or bend down in kindness. The concrete word picture behind this root is that of a superior bending down to show kindness to someone of lower status. It describes an action that is entirely voluntary and motivated by the goodwill of the giver, not the merit of the receiver. In ancient Near Eastern culture, where social hierarchies were rigid, a ruler or landowner bending down to raise up a peasant or foreigner was a striking image of condescending mercy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Favor, grace, acceptance, or goodwill.

• Charm, elegance, or attractiveness that wins the admiration of others.

• The state of being approved or receiving kindness from someone in authority.

• It is most frequently used in the covenantal and relational idiom "to find favor in the eyes of" someone, indicating that the recipient has been viewed with pleasure, compassion, and acceptance.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Chen is used throughout the Old Testament to describe the relational warmth that prompts action. It is not a passive feeling but an active posture of benevolence.

• It is often illustrated as a shield of protection or an open door. When an individual finds favor, barriers are removed, and access is granted.

• The word is also used to describe physical and moral gracefulness. Just as physical charm attracts the eye, moral beauty and gracious character win the hearts of others, prompting them to extend favor.

4. Theological Implications

• Chen serves as the essential Old Testament foundation for the New Testament concept of grace (charis). It reveals that God's covenant relationship with His people does not rest on human performance or heritage, but on His sovereign choice to bend down in mercy.

• It demonstrates that true grace is transforming. Finding favor changes the status of the recipient from an outsider to an insider, from an enemy to a friend, and from destitute to abundant.

• It underscores the relational nature of redemption. Grace is not an abstract substance distributed by God; it is the light of His countenance shining upon the helpless.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:10, Ruth expresses profound astonishment at receiving chen from Boaz. As a Moabitess, she is a member of an excluded nation, a widow, and a destitute foreigner with zero legal claim to Boaz's protection or generosity.

• Her question, "Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?" highlights the absolute unmerited nature of his kindness.

• There is a powerful wordplay in the Hebrew text here. The word for "take notice" (nakar) sounds almost identical to the word for "foreigner" (nokriyah). Ruth is marveling: "Why do you recognize me, when I am unrecognizable (an outcast stranger)?" Boaz's favor bridges the massive cultural, social, and spiritual chasm of her foreignness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:8 - "But Noah found favor in the eyes of the Lord."

• Genesis 39:4 - "So Joseph found favor in his sight, and served him. Then he made him overseer of his house, and all that he had he put under his authority."

• Exodus 33:17 - "So the Lord said to Moses, 'I will also do this thing that you have spoken; for you have found favor in My sight, and I know you by name.'"

• Proverbs 3:4 - "And so find favor and high esteem In the sight of God and man."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The phrase "in your eyes" is critical to understanding chen. In Hebrew thought, the eyes represent the seat of judgment and disposition. To find favor "in the eyes" of someone means that when they look at you, their judgment is bypassed by their compassion, and their heart warms toward you.

• Boaz’s favor to Ruth is not blind; he acknowledges her loyalty and kindness to Naomi (Ruth 2:11-12). Yet, the scale of his generosity far exceeds her deeds. His favor provides her with safety, abundant food, and ultimately, a home and a future, showing that while favor may respond to character, its abundance is always a gift of grace.
