WHY - ( maddua , Strong's H4069 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word maddua is an interrogative adverb meaning "why" or "for what reason." Formed as a contraction of the interrogative "what" (mah) and a form of the verb "to know" (yada), it literally asks, "For what known cause?" or "What is to be known?" Unlike other interrogatives that can carry a tone of emotional complaint or protest, maddua seeks the objective, logical reason or the underlying factual explanation for an event. In Ruth 2:10, Ruth uses this term in astonishment to ask why she, a foreign widow, has received such extraordinary grace and favor from Boaz. It highlights the mystery of unmerited grace, which defies human reason and demands an explanation outside of oneself.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word maddua is a compound word formed from mah (the interrogative "what") and the passive participle of yada (the verb "to know"). Literally, it means "for what known reason?" or "what is known [as the cause]?" This etymological background presents a beautiful word picture of a search for a rational, known basis or cause. When a person uses maddua, they are looking for the intellectual or factual foundation of an action. They are asking for the "why" that can be known and understood by human reason.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Maddua functions as an interrogative adverb meaning "why," "wherefore," "for what cause," or "on what account." Its semantic range is focused on identifying the objective cause, origin, or reason of an action. It is frequently used in direct questions to inquire about an unexpected state of affairs or a violation of expected norms. While another common Hebrew word for "why" (lamah) often expresses emotional protest, grief, or purpose ("to what end?"), maddua is more intellectual and investigative, asking for the specific, historical, or logical reason that prompted a situation.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Historically and biblically, maddua is used to initiate an inquiry when there is a discrepancy between what is expected and what is observed.

• It is the word of a seeker confronted with an anomaly. Moses uses it when he sees the burning bush that is not consumed, asking why the bush does not burn up. He is looking for the physical or supernatural cause of the phenomenon.

• It is the word of an authority demanding an explanation. Pharaoh's taskmasters use it to ask the Israelite officers why they have not completed their daily quota of bricks. They demand a logical explanation for the failure.

• In daily discourse, it is used by someone demanding a rational account of the facts, pointing to a standard or expectation that has been broken or exceeded.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theological inquiry often begins with the word "why," and maddua points directly to the limits of human reason when confronted with divine grace and sovereign providence.

• Grace is, by definition, unmerited. Therefore, when human beings inquire into the "known cause" (maddua) of God’s favor, they find no objective cause within themselves.

• The question "Why have I found favor?" reveals that grace has no human explanation. The "known cause" does not reside in the recipient (Ruth, the sinner, the outsider) but entirely in the character and sovereign choice of the giver (Boaz, and ultimately, God).

• It reminds believers that God's electing love and mercy cannot be rationalized by human merit; the only "why" of grace is God's own good pleasure and covenant faithfulness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:10, Ruth has just been granted extraordinary privileges by Boaz: protection, water from his young men's jars, and an invitation to glean among his sheaves. As a destitute Moabite widow, Ruth expected hostility, suspicion, or at best, cold indifference. Boaz's abundant kindness is a shocking anomaly.

• Ruth falls on her face, bows to the ground, and asks, "Why (maddua) have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?"

• By using maddua, Ruth is asking Boaz to provide the "known cause" or rational explanation for his lavish kindness. She knows there is no cause in her—she is a "foreigner" (nokriyah), an outsider with no legal claim or social standing.

• Her question highlights the absolute gratuitousness of Boaz's grace, paving the way for Boaz to explain his actions not based on her status, but on her character, her loyalty to Naomi, and her trust under the wings of the Lord.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 3:3 - "Then Moses said, 'I will now turn aside and see this great sight, why the bush does not burn.'"

• Exodus 5:14 - "So the officers of the children of Israel, whom Pharaoh’s taskmasters had set over them, were beaten and were asked, 'Why have you not fulfilled your task in making brick both yesterday and today, as heretofore?'"

• 2 Samuel 13:4 - "And he said to him, 'Why are you, the king’s son, becoming thinner day by day? Will you not tell me?'"

• Jeremiah 8:22 - "Is there no balm in Gilead, Is there no physician there? Why then is there no recovery For the health of the daughter of my people?"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast with Lamah: While lamah (literally, "for what?") is often used in laments and prayers of desperation where the speaker feels abandoned (such as Psalm 22:1, "Why have You forsaken me?"), maddua is rarely used in prayers of lament to God. Maddua is highly conversational and observational, emphasizing the human attempt to comprehend anomalous actions in the physical and relational realms.

• A Poignant Wordplay: Ruth's self-identification as a "foreigner" (nokriyah) in the same sentence contrasts sharply with Boaz's act of "taking notice" (nakar, which shares the same root letters in Hebrew n-k-r). She is essentially asking: "Why do you recognize (nakar) me, when I am unrecognizable/a foreigner (nokriyah)?" The use of maddua drives this poignant wordplay to the heart of the narrative.
