FOUND - ( matsa' , Strong's H4672 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb matsa' primarily means to find, attain, secure, or acquire something, often representing the discovery of something of immense value or the experience of a new state of being, such as finding favor or peace. In Ruth 2:10, it depicts Ruth's profound amazement at obtaining Boaz's unmerited favor (grace). It highlights her transition from a vulnerable, empty-handed foreigner to a secured recipient of providential care, illustrating how human encounters can become vehicles of divine grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, historical background of matsa' carries the physical concept of coming upon, reaching, or acquiring something after a diligent search, or meeting someone unexpectedly. It stands as the natural opposite of losing (abad) or searching without success. The imagery evokes a traveler who, exhausted by the desert heat, suddenly comes upon a hidden well of fresh water, or a laborer who unearths an unexpected treasure in a field. In the culture of the Ancient Near East, to "find favor" in the eyes of a superior was not a trivial social pleasantry; it was a life-altering event that established a protective covenantal relationship, granting the vulnerable party legal, physical, and economic security.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb matsa' is a primary root occurring over 450 times in the Old Testament. Its fundamental meaning is to find, detect, or encounter. Depending on the grammatical context and literary setting, its semantic range includes:

• To discover something that was lost, hidden, or sought after.

• To reach, attain, or secure a particular spiritual or emotional state, such as rest or favor.

• To acquire or obtain a spouse, wisdom, or material inheritance.

• To happen upon or be overtaken by circumstances, whether blessing or sudden adversity.

• To be sufficient or enough to meet a physical need.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Discovery of Joy: The verb is frequently paired with the acquisition of life's greatest treasures. Finding wisdom or finding a spouse is described using matsa', painting a word picture of receiving a sovereignly prepared gift that alters the trajectory of one's life.

• The Shield of Favor: The highly frequent idiomatic phrase "to find favor in the eyes of another" (matsa' chen be'eyney) is a picture of a supplicant bowing before a superior. When favor is "found," the superior casts a gaze of acceptance, warmth, and protection over the lowly petitioner.

• God's Tender Search: In prophetic literature, the word is used of God "finding" Israel in the wilderness like wild grapes, depicting a tender, choosing love that initiates covenant rescue for an otherwise lost and wandering people.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereign Grace vs. Human Merit: The act of "finding" favor emphasizes that grace is actively received, not earned. Ruth does not negotiate or purchase Boaz’s favor; she receives it as an unmerited gift.

• Divine Providence: What appears to human eyes as a coincidental meeting (Ruth "happening" to glean in Boaz's field) is revealed through matsa' to be the sovereign arrangement of God. Human "finding" is always the result of God’s prior "seeking" and placing.

• The Gateway to Redemption: Throughout Scripture, finding favor is the critical catalyst for redemption. Just as Noah found favor in the eyes of God and was preserved from judgment, Ruth’s finding favor with Boaz secures her redemption and places her directly into the messianic line of David and Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:10, Ruth falls on her face and asks, "Why have I found favor in your eyes...?" This question reveals her deep self-awareness and humility. As a Moabitess and a destitute widow, she possesses no legal leverage or social status to claim protection. The word matsa' here highlights the sheer passivity of her reception of grace. It marks the precise pivot point of the narrative: she who was empty-handed has now "secured" a source of life-giving provision. Her finding favor is directly paired with her wonder that Boaz would "take notice" (nakar) of her, a "foreigner" (nokriyah). The wordplay underscores the beauty of grace—being deeply known and protected when one has no natural right to belong.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:8: "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD."

• Genesis 18:3: "and said, 'My Lord, if I have now found favor in Your sight, do not pass by Your servant.'"

• Proverbs 18:22: "He who finds a wife finds a good thing, And obtains favor from the LORD."

• Jeremiah 31:2: "Thus says the LORD: 'The people who survived the sword Found grace in the wilderness—Israel, when I went to give him rest.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "found favor" (matsa' chen) serves as a major thematic bridge between the patriarchal narratives and the history of Israel. Moses repeatedly uses this exact formulation in Exodus 33 when pleading for God's presence to remain with Israel. When Ruth uses this covenantal language with Boaz, it elevates their relationship beyond a simple agricultural encounter. It frames Boaz as an instrument of Yahweh's covenant loyalty, demonstrating how human kindness mediates the protective wings of the God of Israel.
