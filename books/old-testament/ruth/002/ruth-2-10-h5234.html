TAKE NOTICE - ( nakar , Strong's H5234 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nakar primarily means to recognize, acknowledge, or pay special attention to someone. In the context of Ruth 2:10, it describes Boaz’s intentional decision to look upon a vulnerable, foreign widow with grace and protective care. Ruth is astonished that Boaz would "take notice" of her—an outsider with no social status—highlighting the contrast between her lowliness and his unexpected, redemptive kindness. This word represents the initiating step of grace, where a person of status chooses to see, validate, and protect someone who is otherwise invisible and marginalized.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verbal root nakar has a unique semantic dualism in ancient Hebrew. In its various forms, it can mean to pay close attention to something in order to recognize it, or conversely, to act like a stranger. This creates an intentional, beautiful wordplay in Ruth 2:10. Ruth asks Boaz why he takes notice (nakar) of her, since she is a foreigner (nokriyah, derived from the same root).

The concrete word picture of nakar is that of scanning a crowd, stopping one's gaze on a specific individual, and choosing to recognize their value and identity rather than passing them by. In the ancient Near East, a foreign gleaner was practically invisible, social background noise to be ignored or exploited. For Boaz to stop, look at Ruth, and acknowledge her existence was a radical departure from cultural norms.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In the Hiphil stem (the form used in Ruth 2:10), the verb means to recognize, acknowledge, or regard with special favor. It moves beyond mere visual identification to an active acknowledgment of relationship, duty, or favor.

• To distinguish or discern: To look closely at someone or something to identify its true identity.

• To acknowledge or recognize legally: To formally declare someone's status, rights, or privileges.

• To care for or pay attention to: To look upon someone with an attitude of concern, benevolence, and protection.

In Ruth 2:10, the definition combines cognitive recognition with active, favorable regard. Boaz does not merely identify Ruth as "the Moabite girl"; he recognizes her as someone worthy of honor, protection, and provision.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, nakar is used to paint vivid pictures of recognition and acknowledgment:

• The Drama of Joseph: In Genesis 42:8, the word is used twice when Joseph "recognized" (nakar) his brothers, but they did not "recognize" (nakar) him. This highlights how a change of status and clothing can obscure identity, requiring deep scrutiny to recognize someone.

• The Legal Right of the Firstborn: In Deuteronomy 21:17, a father is commanded to "acknowledge" (nakar) the son of an unloved wife as his firstborn. Here, the word means to legally and publicly validate someone’s rightful inheritance and status.

• The Cry of the Lonely: In Psalm 142:4, David cries out that there is no one who "acknowledges" (nakar) him. This represents the ultimate state of isolation—being completely unseen and uncared for by those around him.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nakar points to the heart of redemptive grace.

• Grace Initiates Sight: True redemption begins when the Redeemer takes notice of the helpless. Ruth did not earn Boaz’s gaze; he freely chose to look upon her with favor. This mirrors God's grace toward believers. We were spiritual foreigners, yet God chose to "take notice" of us and bring us into His covenant family.

• Elevating the Marginalized: God’s righteousness is demonstrated when His people take notice of the orphan, the widow, and the stranger. Boaz acts as an instrument of divine providence, showing that God's eyes are always on the humble.

• Covenantal Acknowledgment: Just as a father acknowledges his son, God recognizes and claims His people. To be "noticed" by God is to be secure in His covenantal protection.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:10, Ruth has just received extraordinary privileges from Boaz: permission to glean close to his young women, protection from the young men, and free access to the drinking water drawn by his servants. Overwhelmed by this generosity, she falls on her face and bows to the ground.

Her question, "Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?" exposes her deep sense of unworthiness. She expects to be ignored as a nokriyah (stranger), but instead, she is nakar (recognized/noticed). Boaz’s notice is not passive; it is a protective shield that transforms her social standing from an endangered alien to a welcomed guest.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 42:8: "So Joseph recognized his brothers, but they did not recognize him."

• Deuteronomy 21:17: "But he shall acknowledge the son of the unloved wife as the firstborn..."

• Psalm 142:4: "Look on my right hand and see, for there is no one who acknowledges me; refuge has failed me; no one cares for my soul."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew thought, to ignore someone is to deny their personhood and vulnerability. Conversely, to "take notice" of someone is an act of creation and elevation; it restores dignity to the downtrodden. When Boaz noticed Ruth, he did not just see her physical presence; he acknowledged her loyalty to Naomi, her faith in Yahweh, and her inherent worth.

This stands in stark contrast to the Book of Judges, where everyone did what was right in their own eyes, leading to widespread abuse of the vulnerable. Boaz demonstrates the righteous alternative: using his eyes to seek out and protect the needy.
