FOREIGNER - ( nokriy , Strong's H5237 )

Summary:

In Ruth 2:10, the Hebrew word nokriy refers to an outsider, a stranger, or someone from a foreign land who lacks the natural-born rights, social status, and protections of a native-born Israelite. When Ruth refers to herself as a "foreigner," she emphasizes her complete lack of claim on Boaz's kindness, highlighting the extraordinary and undeserved nature of the grace she is receiving. This word underscores the theme of redemption, illustrating how God's covenant mercy extends beyond national boundaries to embrace the humble outsider who seeks refuge under His wings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, society was tribal and kinship-based. Safety, provision, and legal rights were tied directly to one's family and nation. A nokriy was an outsider—someone from another land, culture, and religion who stood outside the protective umbrella of the local community. Unlike a resident alien (ger), who had integrated to some degree into Israelite society and enjoyed certain legal protections under the Mosaic Law, a nokriy had no inherent legal standing, no land-inheritance rights, and was often viewed with suspicion or hostility. To be a foreign woman (nokriyyah) from Moab, a nation historically hostile to Israel, meant carrying a double stigma of vulnerability and national enmity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The adjective nokriy denotes that which belongs to another country, person, or family. It describes someone who is alien, foreign, or strange. The root of the word is closely related to the verb nakar, which can mean either to regard/recognize or to act as a stranger. Lexically, nokriy refers to an individual who is unrecognized by the host community, possessing no citizenship, no family ties, and no covenant rights within Israel.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Outsider vs. The Neighbor: In the Old Testament, nokriy is frequently contrasted with one's brother or neighbor. While Israelites were forbidden from charging interest on loans to their fellow brothers, they were permitted to charge interest to a nokriy (Deuteronomy 15:3). This highlights the separation in legal obligations between covenant family members and foreign outsiders.

• The Strange Woman: In the Wisdom Literature, particularly Proverbs, the feminine form of this word (nokriyyah) is often translated as "seductress" or "strange woman." Here, she represents someone outside the boundaries of the covenant marriage, whose path leads to death and departure from God's moral order.

• The Wordplay of Grace: In Ruth 2:10, there is a profound literary and phonetic wordplay between the verb nakar ("to take notice of" or "recognize") and the noun nokriy ("foreigner"). Ruth asks Boaz, "Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice (nakar) of me, since I am a foreigner (nokriy)?" The beauty of the gospel is prefigured here: the redeemer chooses to actively "recognize" and show favor to someone who is legally and socially "unrecognized."

4. Theological Implications

The presence of the nokriy in the narratives of Israel serves as a vehicle for demonstrating the expansiveness of God's grace. While Israel was chosen as God's covenant people, the inclusion of a Moabite nokriy in the genealogy of King David and ultimately Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5) reveals that God's redemptive plan was never intended to be exclusively nationalistic. Ruth’s transformation from a nokriy (foreigner) to a treasured member of the community illustrates the theological reality of election by grace through faith. It foreshadows the New Testament reality where Gentiles, who were once "aliens from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers from the covenants of promise," are brought near by the blood of Christ (Ephesians 2:12-13).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:10, Ruth has just received unexpected protection, generous provisions, and kind words from Boaz. Her response is one of absolute humility: she falls on her face and bows to the ground. When she calls herself a nokriy, she is acknowledging the sheer disproportion between her status and Boaz's kindness. She is a Moabite widow, a destitute outsider with absolutely no claim on his family or his fields. By using this term, Ruth emphasizes that Boaz’s treatment of her is not a matter of legal obligation or social custom, but of pure, overflowing grace (hesed).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 15:3 - "Of a foreigner you may require it; but you shall give up your claim in what is your brother’s,"

• 1 Kings 11:1 - "But King Solomon loved many foreign women, as well as the daughter of Pharaoh: women of the Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites, Sidonians, and Hittites;"

• Psalm 69:8 - "I have become a stranger to my brothers, And an alien to my mother’s children."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between Ruth and Orpah highlights the depth of meaning behind nokriy. Orpah chose to return to "her people and to her gods" (Ruth 1:15), remaining in her native land. Ruth, however, made a conscious choice to leave her homeland and make Yahweh her God. Even though she was ethnically a nokriy, her heart had sought refuge under the wings of the God of Israel. Boaz recognizes this spiritual reality, which prompts him to treat this "foreigner" not as an suspicious outsider, but as a sister in faith, demonstrating that spiritual alignment to Yahweh transcends ethnic boundaries.
