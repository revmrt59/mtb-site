SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the primary Old Testament term for speech, communication, and internal thought. In Ruth 2:10, it goes beyond mere verbalization; it marks a pivotal moment of humble response where Ruth expresses profound gratitude to Boaz. This simple word of speech captures the emotional and spiritual turning point of a vulnerable outsider receiving unexpected, life-altering grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root 'amar is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Hebrew Bible. While the Hebrew word dabar focuses on the act of speaking or the substance of a message, 'amar emphasizes the expression of thoughts, intent, or the declaration of a specific statement. In ancient Near Eastern culture, speech was not merely the transmission of cognitive data but an extension of the self. To speak was to project one's character, authority, or posture into the world. In the context of a vulnerable foreigner like Ruth speaking to a wealthy landowner like Boaz, her speech is framed by a physical posture of absolute humility—falling on her face and bowing to the ground.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'amar means to say, speak, utter, command, or think. In its basic Qal stem, it denotes the verbal expression of an idea or statement. In specific contexts, it can mean "to think" (literally, "to say in one's heart") or "to promise." Its semantic range spans from simple conversation to royal decrees and divine commands. In Ruth 2:10, the verb functions in the Qal imperfect, expressing the immediate, responsive verbal action of Ruth as she addresses her protector.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb 'amar is used throughout Scripture to depict the power of the spoken word to establish reality. The primary word picture of 'amar is found in the creation narrative, where God speaks light and life into existence. In human relationships, the word represents the exposure of the inner life. When a person speaks, they reveal their heart's condition. Ruth’s speech reveals a heart overwhelmed by unmerited favor. Her "saying" is preceded by bowing, showing that her words are an extension of her physical submission and reverence.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, speech in the Old Testament is a vehicle for covenant, relationship, and revelation. God speaks to reveal His will; humans speak to respond to Him and to one another. Ruth's speech in 2:10 is a paradigm of the human response to divine-like grace. Boaz acts as a human channel of God's covenant loyalty (chesed), and Ruth's verbal response is characterized by self-abasement and wonder. True grace, when rightly perceived, always silences pride and evokes a speech of profound, humble inquiry: "Why have I found favor in your eyes?"

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:10, 'amar occurs in a high-stakes, emotional scene. Ruth is a destitute Moabite widow gleaning in a foreign field to survive. Boaz has just shown her extraordinary kindness—granting her protection, access to water, and continuous gleaning privileges. Her physical reaction is immediate: she falls on her face. Her verbal reaction is equally significant. Rather than accepting the favor as a right or remaining silent in fear, she speaks to name her reality: she is a "foreigner" (an outsider with no legal claims), yet she has found "favor." Here, 'amar is the voice of faith and astonishment.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A fascinating idiom in Hebrew narrative is "to say in one's heart" ('amar b'leb), which translates to "thought" or "said to himself." This highlights that in Hebrew thought, there is no hard division between internal monologue and external speech; both are forms of 'amar. While Ruth speaks aloud to Boaz in Ruth 2:10, her outward words perfectly match her inward heart. There is no duplicity in her speech, only the pure, unfiltered expression of a grateful soul.
