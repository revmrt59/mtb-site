EYES - ( ayin , Strong's H5869 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ayin primarily refers to the physical eye but frequently serves as a powerful metaphor for mental and emotional perception, judgment, and favor. In Ruth 2:10, when Ruth asks why she has found "favor" in Boaz's "eyes," the term goes beyond mere sight to denote kind regard, acceptance, and gracious evaluation. It represents the window through which one's disposition and heart toward another are revealed, transforming a physical organ into a profound expression of relational grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word ayin is a concrete noun representing the eye. In the ancient Hebrew alphabet, the sixteenth letter (Ayin) was originally a pictograph of an eye. In the ancient Near East, the eye was viewed not merely as a passive receiver of light, but as an active projector of a person's inner thoughts, desires, character, and attitudes. What is in a person's "eyes" reflects their inner disposition toward others. Additionally, ayin is homonymous with "spring" or "fountain," suggesting a deep connection between the eye welling up with tears or sparkling with joy, and a life-giving spring flowing from the depths of the earth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Physical Organ: The physical eye of humans, animals, or representative divine beings.

• Mental and Spiritual Perception: Understanding, opinion, and judgment (e.g., being "wise in one's own eyes").

• Relational Attitude: Favor, grace, hostility, or pity. To find "favor in the eyes of" someone indicates being received with pleasure, acceptance, and approval.

• Appearance or Surface: The visible surface or color of something (e.g., the surface of the earth or the sparkle of wine).

• Natural Spring: A fountain of water bubbling up from the ground.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Good Eye vs. The Evil Eye: A "good eye" (ayin tovah) is a biblical idiom for generosity, kindness, and goodwill toward others. An "evil eye" (ayin ra'ah) represents greed, stinginess, jealousy, or malice.

• Before the Eyes: Standing "before the eyes" of someone implies accountability, being fully visible, and subjecting oneself to their evaluation.

• The Eye of the Lord: God's eyes represent His omniscience, active watchfulness, and protective care over those who fear Him.

4. Theological Implications

• Grace is Relational: Grace is not an abstract concept; it is personal and visible. When a redeemer looks upon someone with "favor in his eyes," it depicts an intentional decision to show mercy and lovingkindness rather than indifference or judgment.

• The Eyes Reflect the Heart: Because God looks at the heart rather than outward appearances, finding favor in the eyes of a righteous person like Boaz reflects human compassion aligning with divine covenant love (chesed).

• Redemptive Vision: Redemption involves a transformation of how a person is viewed. Ruth, a destitute Moabite outsider, was socially invisible or despised. Finding favor in Boaz's eyes restores her dignity and places her under the protective care of the God of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:10, Ruth falls on her face and bows to the ground, demonstrating profound humility. Her question, "Why have I found favor in your eyes?" highlights the unexpected nature of Boaz's kindness. As a foreigner (nokriyah), she expected to be ignored, exploited, or treated with suspicion. Instead, Boaz's "eyes" view her with dignity, protection, and provision. The phrase "favor in your eyes" (chen be'eyneka) serves as the turning point of the narrative, marking the transition from isolation to covenant inclusion. Boaz's physical sight of her harvesting in his field leads to a spiritual oversight that shields her from harm and guarantees her redemption.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:8: "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD."

• Genesis 18:3: "and said, 'My Lord, if I have now found favor in Your sight, do not pass by Your servant.'"

• Proverbs 3:7: "Do not be wise in your own eyes; Fear the LORD and depart from evil."

• Psalm 33:18: "Behold, the eye of the LORD is on those who fear Him, On those who hope in His mercy,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "to take notice of me" in Ruth 2:10 shares a deep thematic connection with the word ayin. To take notice is to recognize someone visually and acknowledge their value. Boaz does not merely glance at Ruth; he truly sees her situation and her noble character. This foreshadows the ultimate redemptive gaze of Jesus Christ, who looks upon spiritually bankrupt outsiders with eyes of grace, choosing to notice, value, and redeem them.
