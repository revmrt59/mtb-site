GROUND - ( 'erets , Strong's H0776 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'erets most frequently refers to the earth, land, or ground. In Ruth 2:10, it designates the physical soil or ground upon which Ruth prostrates herself before Boaz. This posture of bowing to the ground is a profound cultural and spiritual expression of humility, reverence, and gratitude. As a destitute Moabite widow seeking safety, Ruth recognizes her status as a vulnerable foreigner and casts herself entirely upon the mercy of the landowner, signaling absolute submission and thanksgiving for his unexpected favor.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'erets is one of the most common nouns in the Old Testament, occurring over 2,500 times. Its imagery spans from the cosmic scale of the entire planet to the specific territory of a nation, down to the very dirt under a person's feet.

The physical ground represents stability, origin, and lowliness. In the ancient Near East, the ground was the place of ultimate humility. Humanity was formed from the dust of the ground, and returning to the ground represents mortality. Therefore, when an individual physically descended to the ground in the presence of another, they were visually demonstrating their insignificance, vulnerability, and dependence on the other person’s mercy.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'erets possesses a wide semantic range that is defined by its immediate context:

• The earth in contrast to the heavens or sky.

• A specific country, territory, or land defined by political or geographical boundaries (such as the land of Canaan or the land of Moab).

• The physical ground, soil, or surface of the earth.

In Ruth 2:10, the word appears with the directional suffix he ('artsah), which functions to mean "toward the earth" or "to the ground." It describes the physical trajectory of Ruth’s posture as she prostrates herself in Boaz’s field.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, bowing to the ground ('erets) is a highly symbolic action used in various relational dynamics:

• Political Submission: Subjects fell to the ground before a king or ruler to acknowledge authority and beg for favor.

• Familial Reconciliation: Estranged family members bowed to the ground to signal peace and the removal of pride, as when Jacob met Esau.

• Spiritual Adoration: Worshipers fell to the ground before manifestations of the divine presence, recognizing the vast gulf between the holy God and mortal dust.

In the narrative of Ruth, this physical gesture transforms the physical ground of Boaz's field into a place of covenant encounter. Ruth does not merely curtsey; she places her face in the dust of a foreign land, symbolizing her complete abandonment of self-reliance.

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of 'erets in Ruth 2:10 carries deep theological weight regarding grace and redemption:

• The Posture of Grace: Ruth’s descent to the ground illustrates that God's grace is received through absolute humility. She has no rights to demand, no status to leverage, and no wealth to offer. She meets Boaz at the lowest possible level—the dirt—which is precisely where redemption begins.

• The Land of Promise: Ruth had left her native 'erets (the land of Moab) to come to a foreign 'erets (the land of Judah). By bowing to the ground of Bethlehem, she physically plants herself in the soil of Yahweh’s covenant promises, choosing the dust of Israel over the security of pagan Moab.

• Foreshadowing the Gospel: This act of prostration models the sinner's approach to Christ. We bring nothing but our need, falling to the ground in repentant faith, only to be lifted up by the grace of our Redeemer.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:10, the phrase "bowed down to the ground" highlights the dramatic social contrast between Ruth and Boaz. Ruth is a destitute foreign widow, while Boaz is a wealthy, influential Israelite landowner.

When Boaz offers her protection, provision, and favor, Ruth’s immediate response is overwhelming gratitude. Her physical posture matches her spoken words: "Why have I found favor in your eyes?" By touching her face to the ground, she acknowledges that she has no claim on his harvest. She acts not with entitlement, but with a profound sense of wonder at being noticed. The ground becomes the altar where her desperation meets his generous grace.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 33:3 - "Then he crossed over before them and bowed himself to the ground seven times, until he came near to his brother."

• Joshua 5:14 - "...And Joshua fell on his face to the earth and worshiped..."

• 1 Samuel 20:41 - "...David arose from a place toward the south, fell on his face to the ground, and bowed down three times."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew expression for falling on one's face and bowing to the ground (naphal 'al-paneyha wattistahu 'artsah) is the same vocabulary used when Joshua fell before the Commander of the Lord's Army. While Ruth’s bow is directed toward Boaz as her earthly benefactor, the narrator intentionally utilizes language that evokes the worship of God. This double-layered meaning suggests to the reader that in the providence of God, Boaz is acting as the hands and feet of Yahweh, providing the "wings" of refuge under which Ruth has come to trust.
