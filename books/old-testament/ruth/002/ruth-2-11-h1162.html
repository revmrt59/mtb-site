BOAZ - ( bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

The name Boaz, meaning "in him is strength" or "fleetness," belongs to a wealthy, righteous Bethlehemite landowner who becomes the kinsman-redeemer of Ruth and Naomi. In the biblical narrative, Boaz is the epitome of covenant faithfulness, integrity, and grace. He acts as a human agent of God’s protective care, redeeming the destitute and preserving the messianic lineage that leads directly to King David and Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

Boaz lived during the chaotic and morally dark era of the Judges, a period marked by spiritual decline, social instability, and widespread disobedience to God's covenant. Against this bleak historical backdrop, Boaz stands out as a beacon of righteousness, obedience to the Torah, and social justice. As a wealthy landowner in Bethlehem, he operates his estate under the principles of the Mosaic Law, ensuring that the poor and marginalized—such as widows and foreigners—are cared for through the practice of gleaning. His character represents the ideal Israelite nobleman: strong, honorable, protective, and deeply committed to his community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name Boaz is derived from a compound of the preposition be ("in" or "with") and the noun oz ("strength"), yielding the direct meaning "in him is strength" or "in strength." An alternative etymological path links the name to an unused Semitic root meaning "alacrity," "quickness," or "fleetness," suggesting a person of decisive, prompt action. Both meanings accurately reflect his character: he is a man of social and financial strength, and he acts swiftly and decisively to redeem Ruth and Naomi once the legal path is clear.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name occurs 24 times in the Old Testament, primarily in the narratives of Ruth 2–4 and the historical genealogies of 1 Chronicles 2. Outside the genealogical records, the name is applied to one of the two massive bronze pillars erected by King Solomon at the vestibule of the Jerusalem Temple. The right pillar was named Jachin ("He will establish") and the left pillar was named Boaz ("in Him is strength"). Together, these pillars stood as a permanent visual sermon to Israel that God is both the Creator who establishes His people and the ultimate source of their enduring strength and security.

4. Theological Implications

• The Kinsman-Redeemer (Goel): Boaz is the premier historical illustration of the goel, the family protector who redeems lost property, buys back relatives sold into slavery, and preserves the family line. In this capacity, he is a vivid type of Jesus Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, who took on human flesh to become our close relative, possessed the infinite spiritual wealth required to pay our debt, and willingly gave Himself to redeem us.

• Covenant Faithfulness (Chesed): Boaz models the attribute of chesed (loyal, covenant love). He exceeds the bare minimums of the law by feeding Ruth, protecting her from harassment, and ultimately marrying her to restore the inheritance of her deceased husband.

• Grace to the Outcast: Boaz’s redemption of Ruth, a Moabite widow, demonstrates that God's redeeming grace extends beyond the national borders of Israel to include faithful hearts from all nations.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:11, Boaz responds to Ruth's surprise at his kindness toward a foreigner. Instead of dismissing her, Boaz acknowledges her self-sacrificing love for Naomi. He highlights her willingness to leave her parents, homeland, and familiar culture to trust in the God of Israel. Here, Boaz's name—"in him is strength"—is lived out. He uses his strength, wealth, and social standing not to exploit a vulnerable young foreigner, but to protect her. By validating her character, Boaz becomes the answer to her prayers, providing a safe refuge under his own protection, which ultimately mirrors the protective wings of Yahweh.

7. Other Relevant Insights

According to Matthew 1:5, Boaz was the son of Salmon and Rahab, the Canaanite woman who helped the Israelite spies in Jericho. This family heritage likely shaped Boaz’s compassionate attitude toward Ruth, as his own mother was once a marginalized Gentile outsider who sought refuge in Israel. Furthermore, the legal contrast between Boaz and the anonymous "closer relative" in Ruth 4 is stark. While the closer relative refuses redemption out of fear of ruining his own inheritance, Boaz acts with selfless courage, prioritizing the preservation of Elimelech's name over his own financial interests.
