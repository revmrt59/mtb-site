KNOW - ( yada , Strong's H3045 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yada represents far more than an intellectual grasp of facts; it denotes experiential knowledge, relational intimacy, and active, personal involvement. To "know" in the Old Testament framework is to experience something directly or to have a deep, committed relationship with someone. In Ruth 2:11, Boaz commends Ruth for leaving her parents and homeland to live among a people she did not "know" previously, emphasizing her radical step of faith into an entirely unfamiliar, un-experienced community where she had no relational safety net.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concept of knowledge in the ancient Hebrew mind is dynamic, active, and sensory. Unlike the classic Greek philosophical tradition, which treats knowledge as abstract contemplation or cognitive assent, Hebrew thought ties knowledge directly to action and relationship. To know is to hear, see, taste, feel, and experience. It is the difference between knowing about a person and knowing that person intimately. This word is rooted in personal encounter. When a person knows something in the Old Testament, they have been personally affected by it, have experienced its reality, and are acting in light of that experience.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of yada is exceptionally broad, spanning several aspects of human and divine relationship:

• Experiential perception: Learning, discovering, or realizing something through personal experience.

• Intimate relationship: Deep personal acquaintance, including the unique physical intimacy between a husband and wife.

• Covenantal recognition: Acknowledging a legal or relational bond, together with the duties and loyalty that accompany it.

• Practical skill: Knowing how to perform a task, craft, or trade.

• Active care and concern: Taking notice of someone, choosing them, or looking after their well-being.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb yada appears nearly 1,000 times in the Hebrew Bible, functioning as a key theological term.

• The Intimacy Picture: The word describes the most intimate human relationship, as seen when Adam "knew" Eve, resulting in the birth of Cain. This idiom presents sexual union not merely as physical action, but as the ultimate expression of relational knowledge and oneness.

• The Care and Election Picture: When God declares that He "knows" someone, it indicates His sovereign choice and protective care. In the wilderness, God "knew" Israel, meaning He watched over them, chose them, and sustained them.

• The Ethical Picture: To know God is synonymous with living righteously. Knowing Yahweh is not a theological theory; it is demonstrated by executing justice, defending the poor, and walking in obedience.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of yada centers on a relational, covenant-making God. Yahweh does not seek distant admirers; He seeks an intimate, covenantal relationship with His creation.

• Divine Omniscience and Care: God’s knowledge of humanity is thorough and active. He searches and knows the human heart, meaning He understands individuals completely and cares for them personally.

• Redemptive Purpose: The ultimate goal of God's miraculous acts in history—from the plagues of Egypt to the return from exile—is that both Israel and the nations "may know that I am the LORD." This knowledge is a mixture of awe, recognition of sovereignty, and saving faith.

• Covenant Loyalty: Knowing God demands a response of exclusive loyalty, love, and obedience. To "forget" God is the opposite of knowing Him, representing a breach of covenant.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage (Ruth 2:11)

In Ruth 2:11, Boaz addresses Ruth's vulnerability and extraordinary loyalty. He notes that she left her father, her mother, and her native land to live among "a people whom you did not know heretofore" (literally, "yesterday and the third day").

• In this specific context, yada emphasizes Ruth’s lack of experiential familiarity and relational security. She had no prior history with the people of Bethlehem, no family connections to rely on, and no understanding of their customs.

• Her move was not an informed, low-risk transition. It was a step into the absolute unknown. By highlighting that she came to a people she did not "know," Boaz underscores the sheer magnitude of Ruth's faith. She abandoned the comfort of the known to find refuge under the wings of the God of Israel, whom she had newly come to know and trust.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:1: "Now Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived and bore Cain, and said, 'I have acquired a man from the LORD.'"

• Psalm 1:6: "For the LORD knows the way of the righteous, But the way of the ungodly shall perish."

• Psalm 139:1: "O LORD, You have searched me and known me."

• Jeremiah 1:5: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you; Before you were born I sanctified you; I ordained you a prophet to the nations."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) typically translates yada with ginosko or eido. This link connects the Old Testament concept of experiential knowledge directly to the New Testament, where eternal life is defined as "knowing" the only true God and Jesus Christ (John 17:3).

• The Hebrew noun moda, translated as "kinsman" or "relative" in Ruth 2:1 and 3:2, is derived from the root yada. Boaz is introduced as a moda—someone who is "known," an intimate family acquaintance who has the legal and covenantal right to act as a redeemer. This linguistic connection highlights that while Ruth came to a people she did not know, God had already prepared a "known one" (Boaz) to redeem her.
