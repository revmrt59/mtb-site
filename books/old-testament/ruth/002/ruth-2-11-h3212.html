COME - ( yalak , Strong's H3212 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalak primarily means to walk, go, or journey, depicting active movement from one place to another. In Ruth 2:11, it describes Ruth's deliberate, life-altering departure from her homeland of Moab to journey to an unfamiliar land and people. This word underscores more than physical travel; it represents a profound step of faith, covenant commitment, and the voluntary relocation of one's entire life and destiny to align with the God of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• At its core, yalak presents the concrete, step-by-step physical action of walking. In the ancient world, walking was the primary mode of travel, requiring physical exertion, endurance, and time.

• To journey to another country was not a casual trip; it meant crossing natural barriers, risking exposure to bandits, and leaving behind the safety net of one's clan.

• For a young, widowed Moabite woman, this "coming" to Judah was an act of extreme vulnerability, trading the physical security of her homeland for the unknown.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yalak is a high-frequency verb expressing motion, synonymous with and dynamically related to the root halak.

• The primary definition is to go, walk, travel, or proceed.

• It carries the nuance of departing from an established point of origin and pursuing a specific trajectory toward a destination.

• Figuratively, it denotes a person's behavior, lifestyle, or moral conduct—the "walk" of life.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The term is frequently used to illustrate spiritual allegiance, such as "walking after" other gods (which leads to destruction) or "walking in" the commandments of the Lord (which leads to life).

• It is used of flowing water (rivers "running" to the sea) and the passing of time, illustrating continuous, unstoppable progression.

• In the context of Ruth, yalak functions as a physical demonstration of an internal transition. Ruth's physical steps toward Bethlehem mirrored her spiritual transition into the covenant family of Yahweh.

4. Theological Implications

• Yalak echoes the foundational call of Abraham in Genesis 12, where God commanded him to "go" from his country and his father’s house. Ruth mimics this classic pattern of saving faith, abandoning her ancestral gods and land to enter the inheritance of Israel.

• True biblical faith is kinetic and active. It is not merely intellectual assent; it involves physical movement, displacement, and the willingness to walk into unfamiliar territory at God's bidding.

• This word illustrates the cost of discipleship. To come to the people of God, Ruth had to leave behind her natural family, heritage, and security.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:11, Boaz commends Ruth, noting that she left her parents and land of birth and "came" to a people she did not previously know.

• The contrast is stark between Ruth's action and her sister-in-law Orpah's decision to "return" (shub) to Moab. While Orpah retreated to the familiar, Ruth stepped forward (yalak) into the sovereign plan of God.

• By using this verb, Boaz highlights Ruth's proactive initiative. She was not a passive victim of circumstance dragged to Judah; she actively chose to make this journey, validating her devotion and remarkable character.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:4: "So Abram departed as the LORD had spoken to him, and Lot went with him."

• Ruth 1:16: "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you; For wherever you go, I will go; And wherever you lodge, I will lodge; Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God.'"

• Psalm 23:4: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; For You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint (LXX) translates yalak in Ruth 2:11 using the Greek verb poreuomai, which means to journey or go. This Greek term is frequently used in the New Testament to describe the missionary journeying of the apostles and the path of Christian discipleship.

• Ruth’s journey proves that God’s redemptive plan is inclusive, embracing the outsider who walks in faith to seek refuge under the wings of the Lord God of Israel.
