ALL - ( kol , Strong's H3605 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun kol (often translated "all," "whole," or "every") functions as a comprehensive term of totality. In Ruth 2:11, it highlights the complete and unreserved nature of Ruth’s sacrificial loyalty (chesed) toward Naomi. Rather than a vague generalization, Boaz uses "all" to underscore that every specific detail of Ruth’s self-abandoning love—from leaving her homeland to caring for her mother-in-law—has been observed, recognized, and valued by the community and by God.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word kol is derived from a root meaning to complete, perfect, or make whole. Structurally, it acts as a noun of totality, meaning "the entirety of" or "the whole." It is not merely a mathematical plural; it signifies an unbroken unit or an exhaustive sum. In ancient Near Eastern culture, where family networks and national identity defined a person's entire existence, to give up "all" of one's past to embrace an uncertain future was a radical act of covenant commitment. The background of this word in Ruth 2:11 highlights a totality of action that left nothing held back.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary lexical meaning of kol is "all," "the whole," "every," or "any." When followed by a relative clause (such as "all that you have done" in Ruth 2:11), it functions as an inclusive determiner, encompassing every individual deed, attitude, and sacrifice within a specific scope. It expresses completeness of measure, leaving no remainder. In legal and covenantal contexts, it denotes an absolute, unconditional compliance or devotion.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In the Old Testament, kol is used to describe the totality of creation (the "all" things God made), the entirety of the heart in worship ("with all your heart"), and the complete scope of historical events.

• It frequently paints a picture of a vessel filled to the brim or a boundary that extends to the furthest limit.

• When paired with actions of faithfulness, kol transforms abstract concepts of loyalty into a visible, concrete list of lived realities. It leaves no room for partial commitment.

### 4. Theological Implications

• The theological weight of kol in the narrative of Ruth rests on its connection to chesed (covenant love/kindness). Ruth’s devotion was not partial or selective; it was total. This mirrors the character of God, whose own chesed is comprehensive and exhaustive.

• By recognizing "all" that Ruth had done, Boaz acts as an agent of divine validation. God notes the entirety of our service and sacrifice, even when it is done in secret or in a foreign land.

• It reminds us that true discipleship and covenant love demand an all-inclusive surrender—a willingness to give up "all" (family, country, security) for the sake of the Lord.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:11, Boaz responds to Ruth's question about why she has found favor in his eyes, being a foreigner. His answer begins by emphasizing that her reputation has preceded her: "It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done..."

• The use of kol here binds together the specific actions that follow: leaving her father, her mother, and the land of her birth, and coming to an unknown people.

• The nuance is intensional: Boaz is not merely aware that Ruth was kind; he is intimately acquainted with the full scope of her selflessness. It legitimizes her place in the community and sets the stage for her redemption. Her "all" of sacrifice is met with God’s "all" of provision.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:31: "Then God saw everything [kol] that He had made, and indeed it was very good."

• Deuteronomy 6:5: "You shall love the LORD your God with all [kol] your heart, with all [kol] your soul, and with all [kol] your strength."

• Psalm 103:2: "Bless the LORD, O my soul, And forget not all [kol] His benefits."

• Proverbs 3:5: "Trust in the LORD with all [kol] your heart, And lean not on your own understanding."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The phrasing in Hebrew is kol asher-asit ("all that you have done"). This matches the covenant language used later in Israel's history when they promised to do "all that the Lord has spoken" (Exodus 19:8). Ruth, a Moabitess, is ironically displaying the perfect covenant faithfulness that Israel often struggled to maintain.

• Boaz's acknowledgment shows that godly character cannot remain hidden. Even a destitute widow working in the corners of a field is fully seen by those who possess spiritual discernment.
