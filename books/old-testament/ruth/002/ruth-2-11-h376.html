HUSBAND - ( 'ish , Strong's H376 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ish primarily means "man" or "husband," denoting an individual male, a spouse, or a person of responsibility and standing. In the context of covenant relationships, it highlights the protective, initiating, and binding role of a husband to his wife. In Ruth 2:11, the term refers to Ruth's deceased husband, Mahlon, emphasizing the profound loss she suffered and the radical nature of her enduring devotion to Naomi and the God of Israel despite the dissolution of this primary earthly security.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word 'ish is one of the most foundational nouns in the Hebrew Scriptures. It stands in contrast to 'adam (generic mankind or "man of the earth") and 'enosh (frail, mortal man). It conveys the idea of a male individual in his strength, presence, and capacity to act. From the creation account in Genesis, 'ish is bound up with identity and relational covenant. When the woman ('ishshah) is brought to the man ('ish), their linguistic and covenantal connection is established. To be an 'ish in the family structure of ancient Israel was to carry the responsibility of leadership, spiritual oversight, legal advocacy, and physical protection for the household.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary definition of 'ish is an individual male or a husband.

• In a marital context, it defines the husband as the covenant partner who provides legal status, physical inheritance, and social security to his wife.

• It can also serve as a distributive pronoun meaning "each one" or "every man," or denote a champion or a person of distinguished character (such as a "man of God").

• Lexical studies trace the term to its roots signifying existence, strength, and personal presence, establishing the husband not merely as a legal head, but as the active, initiating protector of the home.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Old Testament employs 'ish to paint rich pictures of security and intimate covenant. In Hosea 2:16, a dramatic redemptive shift is promised where Israel will no longer call Yahweh "My Master" (Baali) but will instead call Him "My Husband" (Ishi). This illustrates a transition from legalistic ownership to intimate, protective covenant.

• In the ancient Near East, a widow without an 'ish was exceptionally vulnerable. She possessed no legal standing to defend her land, no economic means of self-preservation, and no way to carry on her family name. The absence of an 'ish is a picture of ultimate earthly destitution.

4. Theological Implications

• The concept of 'ish underscores the deeply relational and covenantal structure of biblical marriage. God established the connection between 'ish and 'ishshah to reflect His own binding commitment to His people.

• The death of an 'ish represents not just emotional grief, but the loss of a covenantal shield on earth.

• In the book of Ruth, the absence of an 'ish for both Naomi and Ruth drives the entire narrative's tension. It highlights God's sovereignty as the ultimate Husband to the widow, raising up a kinsman-redeemer to restore the name and inheritance of the deceased.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:11, Boaz acknowledges Ruth's extraordinary faithfulness: "all that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband ('ish)."

• The mention of her deceased 'ish emphasizes the boundary line of Ruth's devotion. Legally and socially, the death of her husband severed Ruth's obligations to Elimelech's family. She was entirely free to return to Moab, find a new 'ish among her own people, and secure her future—just as her sister-in-law Orpah did.

• By emphasizing that Ruth acted "since the death of your husband," Boaz highlights the sheer selflessness of her chesed (loyal love). Ruth abandoned her natural search for safety (seeking a new husband in her homeland) to care for an impoverished widow in a foreign land. She willingly chose the vulnerability of having no 'ish in Israel, trusting instead under the wings of the Lord God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:23: "And Adam said: 'This is now bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.'"

• Genesis 3:6: "So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree desirable to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate. She also gave to her husband with her, and he ate."

• Ruth 1:3: "Then Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died; and she was left, and her two sons."

• Hosea 2:16: "'And it shall be, in that day,' says the LORD, 'That you will call Me "My Husband," and no longer call Me "My Master."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• A powerful contrast exists between 'ish and ba'al in the Hebrew Scriptures. While ba'al emphasizes ownership, mastership, and authority, 'ish emphasizes partnership, companionship, and covenantal union.

• The story of Ruth comes full circle when Boaz takes Ruth to be his wife, effectively becoming her new 'ish (Ruth 4:13). This transition demonstrates how God steps into the tragedy of our empty, unprotected states to provide a Redeemer who covers us, restores our inheritance, and places us in a lineage of ultimate redemption.
