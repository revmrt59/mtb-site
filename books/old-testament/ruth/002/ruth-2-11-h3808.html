NOT - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' (H3808) is the primary adverb of negation in the Old Testament, representing absolute, permanent, or objective negation. In Ruth 2:11, it qualifies Ruth’s past relationship with the Israelites ("a people whom you did not know before"). Boaz uses this simple negation to highlight the extraordinary nature of Ruth’s faith and self-sacrifice. She left everything familiar to commit herself to a people and a God she had no prior, experiential knowledge of, underscoring a transition from complete foreignness to covenant belonging.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• lo' is the standard, most frequent negative particle in Biblical Hebrew, occurring over 5,000 times. Unlike the subjective or temporary negative 'al (which expresses a wish, deprecation, or temporary prohibition, such as "do not"), lo' expresses objective, permanent, and absolute negation.

• In the Ancient Near Eastern context, identity, security, and survival were entirely bound up with one's ancestral land, family gods, and kindred. To step into a territory and among a people whom one "did not know" was not merely a change of address; it was a complete stripping of one's social safety net. By emphasizing what Ruth did not know, the narrative paints a picture of a blind step of faith into the unknown, reliant solely on the God of Israel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• lo' acts as an adverbial particle of negation meaning "no" or "not."

• It negates verbs, nouns, adjectives, or entire clauses, establishing a definitive and objective negative fact.

• When paired with a verb in the perfect or imperfect tense, it denies a fact or expresses an absolute prohibition.

• In Ruth 2:11, it negates the perfect verb yada'at ("you knew"), indicating a complete, historical absence of personal or experiential knowledge of the people of Israel prior to her arrival.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Absolute Negation: lo' establishes a definitive boundary. It does not mean "hardly" or "partially," but "completely not."

• Contrastive Use: In Hebrew narrative, lo' is frequently used to set up stark contrasts between past and present, or between human expectation and divine reality. In Ruth's case, the negation of her past knowledge contrasts beautifully with her present inclusion.

• Covenantal Ignorance to Covenantal Knowing: In the Old Testament, "knowing" (yada) implies intimate, experiential relationship. Negating this verb with lo' emphasizes that Ruth had no prior relational, cultural, or covenantal ties to Israel. She was a true foreigner.

4. Theological Implications

• The Grace of God to the Outsider: The use of lo' underscores the magnitude of Gentile inclusion in God's redemptive plan. Ruth had no prior connection to Yahweh's covenant people. Her faith was not built on lifelong familiarity but on a radical response to the God of Israel.

• The Nature of True Faith: Abrahamic faith is characterized by going out to a place one does not know (Genesis 12:1). Ruth exhibits this same Abrahamic pattern. The absolute negation ("did not know") elevates Ruth's actions from mere filial piety to a monumental act of covenantal faith.

• Sovereign Redemptive History: God uses those who were "not" His people to accomplish His grand purposes. Ruth, the one who did not know Israel, becomes a mother in the line of David and Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Boaz is speaking to Ruth in the harvest field. He has heard of her reputation, and his words are meant to comfort and honor her.

• The phrase lo'-yada'at temol shilshom (literally, "not known yesterday [and] the day before") is an idiomatic Hebrew expression for "previously" or "heretofore."

• By pointing out that she came to a people she did "not" know, Boaz validates her vulnerability. She had no natural allies, no childhood friends, and no social leverage in Bethlehem.

• This highlights Boaz's role as the kinsman-redeemer. Because she has nothing and knows no one, she is entirely dependent on the grace of others and the wings of Yahweh, under which she has come to take refuge.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:17 - "but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die."

• Exodus 20:13 - "You shall not murder."

• Deuteronomy 8:3 - "So He humbled you, allowed you to hunger, and fed you with manna which you did not know and which your fathers did not know, that He might make you know that man shall not live by bread alone..."

• Hosea 1:9 - "Then God said: 'Call his name Lo-Ammi, for you are not My people, and I will not be your God.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The phrase "whom you did not know before" serves as a literary foil to the rest of the book. While she did not know them, Boaz makes it his business to know her (Ruth 2:10, where she asks why he takes notice of/knows her, a foreigner).

• The transition from lo' (not knowing/not belonging) to intimate covenant relationship is a central motif of the Book of Ruth, mirroring the believer's transition from being "strangers from the covenants of promise" (Ephesians 2:12) to being brought near by grace.
