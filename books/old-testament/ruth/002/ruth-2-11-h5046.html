FULLY SHOWN - ( nagad , Strong's H5046 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nagad primarily means to declare, announce, report, or make clearly known. In Ruth 2:11, the word is doubled using a Hebrew grammatical device (the Hophal infinitive absolute coupled with the Hophal perfect) to express absolute certainty and fullness, translated in the NKJV as "fully shown." Rather than a casual rumor or passing comment, this word emphasizes that Ruth's sacrificial love, loyalty, and character had been thoroughly, clearly, and conspicuously reported to Boaz. This highlights how genuine, quiet faithfulness cannot remain hidden; it eventually shines brightly and is recognized by those whom God uses to bring restoration.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of nagad carries the idea of bringing something to the front, placing it in plain sight, or making it conspicuous. It is closely related to the preposition neged, which means "in front of" or "opposite to." Therefore, to nagad is not merely to whisper a secret, but to place information directly in front of someone's face so that it is impossible to ignore. It is a visual way of speaking that brings hidden realities out into the open light. In the ancient Near Eastern context, reports of notable character or major events were delivered orally by witnesses who placed the report "before" the ears of rulers, leaders, or judges.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb nagad occurs over 370 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in the Hiphil (causative) stem meaning "to tell, declare, make known, announce, or report."

• In the passive Hophal stem, it means "to be told, to be declared, or to be made known."

• In Ruth 2:11, the verb appears in a highly emphatic Hophal construction: huggad huggad (the Hophal infinitive absolute coupled with the Hophal perfect verb). This grammatical doubling indicates intensity and complete certainty. It means "it has been told repeatedly," "it has been fully reported," or "it has been made thoroughly clear."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• nagad is often used when a critical message or revelation is being brought from a distance to a person of authority (e.g., Genesis 14:13, where a survivor "told" Abram of Lot's capture).

• It is used of divine revelation, where God "declares" His word, His plans, or His righteousness to humanity, bringing the invisible truths of heaven into the plain view of earth.

• It is used in legal contexts of testifying or making a formal declaration that establishes truth in public.

4. Theological Implications

• Ruth's actions in leaving her homeland to care for Naomi were done in secret, out of pure devotion, without seeking reward or recognition. However, nagad in Ruth 2:11 reminds us of the biblical principle that what is done in secret will be brought to light. God ensures that quiet, sacrificial love is eventually made conspicuous.

• It reflects God’s providential eye. Boaz represents the kinsman-redeemer, a type of Christ. Just as Boaz was "fully shown" all that Ruth had done, our Redeemer is fully aware of every act of faith, service, and sacrifice we make, even those done in the "foreign fields" of trial and grief.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:10, Ruth asks Boaz why she has found favor in his eyes, since she is a foreigner. Boaz replies with huggad huggad—"It has been fully shown to me."

• This reveals that Ruth's reputation preceded her. Her quiet, self-sacrificing devotion to Naomi and her abandonment of her pagan home to trust under the wings of the Lord God of Israel (Ruth 2:12) had become a public testimony.

• The passive voice ("it has been shown") suggests that multiple witnesses had spoken of her virtue. Boaz did not need to investigate; the evidence of her godly character was placed directly before him, moving his heart to act as her protector and ultimately her redeemer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:11: "And He said, 'Who told you that you were naked? Have you eaten from the tree of which I commanded you that you should not eat?'"

• Psalm 19:1: "The heavens declare the glory of God; And the firmament shows His handiwork."

• Isaiah 42:9: "Behold, the former things have come to pass, And new things I declare; Before they spring forth I tell you of them."

• Micah 6:8: "He has shown you, O man, what is good; And what does the Lord require of you But to do justly, To love mercy, And to walk humbly with your God?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) translates this emphatic Hebrew construction in Ruth 2:11 using the Greek verb apangello in the passive voice, which means to report, announce, or declare thoroughly. This reinforces the idea of a comprehensive, detailed report.

• The narrative movement of the Book of Ruth shifts from bitter darkness in Moab to a field of grace in Bethlehem. The "telling" (nagad) of Ruth's loyalty acts as the catalyst that bridges her isolation as a foreigner to her inclusion in the lineage of David and Jesus Christ.
