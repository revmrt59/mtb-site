MOTHER - ( em , Strong's H517 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word em represents a biological mother, but its usage extends far beyond genealogy to denote origin, comfort, protection, and the foundational source of life or community. In the pastoral narrative of Ruth 2:11, it highlights the profound cost of Ruth's covenant loyalty, as she willingly severed her deepest maternal and cultural ties in Moab to embrace the God of Israel and care for her grieving mother-in-law, Naomi.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word em is a primary noun of great antiquity. In the ancient pictographic Semitic script, the word is written with the letters aleph (representing strength, leadership, or first/primary) and mem (representing water, liquid, or chaos). Combined, these pictographs convey the concept of the "strong water" or the binding liquid that holds a family together, as well as the amniotic fluid of the womb from which life emerges.

In ancient Israelite society, a mother was the primary source of early nurture, education, and domestic security. She was viewed as a foundational pillar of the home, representing warmth, protective care, and the preservation of family lineage.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of em is a biological mother.

The word also carries several extended and figurative meanings:

• A grandmother or female ancestor.

• A female guardian, leader, or counselor (metaphorically, as a protective figure over a community).

• An origin, point of departure, or source (such as the "mother of a highway" or crossroads).

• A collective designation for a metropolis or capital city that nurtures its surrounding villages (a "mother city").

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• em is frequently used to paint vivid pictures of comfort and safety in the Old Testament. God Himself uses the image of a mother comforting her child to describe His tender, restoring love for His people.

The word is also regularly paired with "father" (ab) to represent the totality of parental authority, honor, and domestic instruction. Under the Mosaic Law, honoring one's mother was a sacred duty tied to national longevity, and treating a mother with contempt carried the gravest consequences. This highlights the high spiritual esteem in which the maternal role was held in Israel, contrasting sharply with many contemporary ancient Near Eastern cultures.

### 4. Theological Implications

Within the covenant community of Israel, the mother was the instrumental vessel through whom the promises of God were nurtured and passed down to the next generation. The honor demanded for mothers reflects God's own character of nurture, protection, and creative authority.

By leaving her mother, Ruth performed an act of profound self-denial. In the ancient Near East, a daughter's relationship with her mother was her primary source of social security, emotional comfort, and domestic identity. Severing this tie to care for Naomi was an act of sacrificial love (chesed) that mirrored the self-giving nature of Yahweh.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:11, Boaz acknowledges Ruth’s remarkable sacrifice: "It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband, and how you have left your father and your mother and the land of your birth, and have come to a people whom you did not know before."

The contrast is striking. Ruth left her biological em (mother) in Moab to serve and sustain Naomi, her chamot (mother-in-law), who had become childless and bitter. Ruth's willingness to abandon the security and comfort of her maternal home demonstrates that covenant faithfulness transcends natural, biological bloodlines. Her sacrifice echoes the call of Abraham, who was likewise commanded to leave his father's house and native land for an unknown future under Yahweh's guidance.

Furthermore, leaving her mother in Moab meant leaving the household gods of her upbringing. Ruth's departure from her em was ultimately a conversion of her entire allegiance—trading the protective shade of her maternal home in Moab to take refuge under the wings of Yahweh, the God of Israel.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "mother-in-law" in the book of Ruth is represented by a completely different Hebrew root (chamot), which emphasizes the legal and marital connection. By contrasting Ruth's biological em in Ruth 2:11 with her chamot Naomi, the author highlights the transition of Ruth's devotion. She traded the safety of her natural mother's tent for the dust and vulnerability of gleaning in a foreign field to provide for her deceased husband's mother.
