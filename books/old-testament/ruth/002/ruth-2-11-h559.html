SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, representing the active verbalization of thought, intent, and decree. In Ruth 2:11, Boaz’s spoken response to Ruth is not a mere casual reply; it is a formal, protective, and public declaration of her noble character and loyalty. By speaking directly and kindly to this foreign widow, Boaz elevates her status, validates her immense sacrifices, and prepares the way for her ultimate redemption under the wings of Yahweh.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• 'Amar is a foundational Hebrew verb of communication. While it is often translated simply as "to say," its ancient background involves the outward expression of one's inner thoughts, intent, or heart.

• In the ancient Near East, spoken words carried immense weight and legal significance. To "say" something, especially from a position of authority, was to establish a reality, make a covenantal commitment, or render a formal judgment.

• In the social structure of Bethlehem during the period of the Judges, Boaz was a "man of great wealth" and community standing. His verbal response to Ruth in the harvest field serves as a public declaration of her worth, effectively offering her social protection and community validation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb 'amar means to say, speak, utter, command, declare, or think (to say in one's heart).

• Depending on the context, the verb encompasses a wide semantic range:

• To declare or announce a matter of fact.

• To command or instruct (as a superior to a subordinate).

• To intend or resolve within oneself.

• To promise or swear an oath.

• In Ruth 2:11, the grammatical form is the Qal imperfect with waw consecutive (wayyo'mer), signaling a direct, historical narrative response: "And he said." It represents a deliberate, intentional verbal engagement.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Divine Speech: In the creation narrative, 'amar is the vehicle of God's sovereign, creative power. When God "said" ('amar), physical reality came into existence out of nothing (Genesis 1:3).

• Internal Dialogue: When paired with "in his heart," 'amar depicts the thinking process or internal motivations of a character, illustrating that speech is the outward manifestation of the heart.

• Covenantal Binding: The verb is frequently used in covenantal contexts where a promise is spoken into existence, binding the speaker to the hearer.

• The Word Picture in Ruth: In this passage, Boaz's speech functions as a protective shield. His words answer Ruth's vulnerability with honor, effectively reshaping her identity from an outcast Moabite to an accepted member of the community.

4. Theological Implications

• Grace Communicated through Speech: Grace in the Scriptures is not merely felt; it is verbalized and declared. Boaz’s spoken words mirror God’s vocalized covenant love (hesed) toward His people.

• The Power of Affirmation: Boaz uses his speech to honor the vulnerable. By speaking well of Ruth, he models the godly responsibility of the strong to protect, validate, and elevate the weak.

• Redemptive Voice: Boaz’s words foreshadow the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ, who speaks peace, justification, and adoption to spiritual outsiders who seek refuge in Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:10, Ruth falls on her face, bows to the ground, and asks why she has found favor in Boaz's eyes, emphasizing her status as a foreigner. Boaz's response (wayyo'mer) in verse 11 is not a brief greeting but a detailed testimony.

• Boaz begins by acknowledging that her deeds have been "fully reported" to him. His "saying" is a public, authoritative confirmation of her extraordinary loyalty to Naomi and her faith in the God of Israel.

• By speaking these words in the presence of his workers, Boaz establishes Ruth’s reputation as highly honorable, protecting her from harassment and integrating her into the community's moral fabric.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3: "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light." (Demonstrates the creative, authoritative power of 'amar).

• Genesis 22:1: "Now it came to pass after these things that God tested Abraham, and said to him, 'Abraham!' And he said, 'Here I am.'" (Illustrates the call-and-response covenantal dialogue).

• Exodus 3:14: "And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And He said, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, "I AM has sent me to you."'" (The ultimate self-revelation of God's name is delivered through 'amar).

• Ruth 3:5: "And she said to her, 'All that you say to me I will do.'" (Ruth's submission and obedience voiced in response to Naomi).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The repetition of speech verbs in the Book of Ruth highlights the transition from silent suffering to vocal redemption. Naomi begins in silent, bitter grief, but through Boaz's spoken blessings and Ruth's faithful responses, the community is restored to joyful praise.

• In the ancient world, a foreign widow had no voice or legal standing. By addressing Ruth directly and speaking honorably to her, Boaz breaks social barriers and demonstrates the inclusive nature of God's kingdom.
