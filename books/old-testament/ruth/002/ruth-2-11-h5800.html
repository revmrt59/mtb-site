LEFT - ( azab, Strong's H5800 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb azab denotes the act of leaving, departing from, abandoning, or relinquishing someone or something. In the context of Ruth 2:11, it highlights Ruth’s costly sacrifice in voluntarily departing from her parents, her native land, and her secure pagan heritage to accompany Naomi and seek refuge under the God of Israel. Her leaving represents a profound demonstration of covenant-like loyalty (chesed), echoing the Abrahamic call to leave country and kindred for an unknown future.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root azab carries the primary physical concept of letting go, loosening, or relinquishing hold of something. In ancient Near Eastern culture, one's security, identity, and survival were completely bound up in family, clan, and homeland. To loosen one’s grip on these structures was a radical step that stripped away all natural protection. It is a word of decisive separation, used of physically departing from a location or relationally abandoning a covenant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb azab means to leave, depart from, leave behind, abandon, or forsake. It is used in several distinct semantic nuances:

• To physically depart from a person, group, or place.

• To neglect or fail to care for someone, such as leaving the poor without help.

• To abandon or forsake a covenant, commandment, or relationship with God.

• To commit or hand over something into another's care or keeping.

In Ruth 2:11, the word describes a physical and relational departure. Ruth did not merely travel; she severed her ties to her Moabite family and her homeland, relinquishing her natural rights to security.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses azab to paint vivid pictures of relational priority and spiritual fidelity. It is the word used in Genesis 2:24 for a man leaving his father and mother to cleave to his wife, signifying a restructuring of life's primary human allegiance. It is also used of agricultural charity, where landowners must leave the corners of their fields for the poor to glean. In a negative sense, the prophets frequently use azab to describe Israel forsaking Yahweh for idols, portraying a tragic breach of marital-like covenant fidelity.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Ruth’s act of leaving (azab) serves as a powerful illustration of conversion and covenant commitment. Her decision to leave her father, mother, and homeland closely mirrors the call of Abraham in Genesis 12:1. Both left behind the safety of their ancestral homes to walk by faith into an unknown land. In doing so, Ruth demonstrated chesed—loyal, self-sacrificing love—which is the very character of God. By leaving her past, she positioned herself to receive the covenant blessings of Israel and became a direct ancestor of King David and Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:11, Boaz recognizes the heavy price of Ruth's actions. He uses azab to contrast what Ruth left behind with what she has gained. She left her natural father and mother—the sources of parental protection and inheritance—and the land of her birth, which represented her native identity and religious comfort. She exchanged these secure, known entities for a foreign people and an uncertain status as a destitute widow. Boaz recognizes this leaving not as a casual move, but as an act of extraordinary devotion, which he immediately connects to her finding refuge under the wings of Yahweh in the following verse.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The use of azab in Ruth 2:11 also highlights the theme of divine reciprocity. Because Ruth chose to leave (azab) her earthly safety net for the sake of Naomi and Yahweh, God did not leave or forsake (azab) her in her time of poverty. While Ruth left everything, she was ultimately brought into a legacy of redemption. This serves as a pastoral reminder that what is surrendered to God in faith is never truly lost; it is exchanged for the far greater security of His eternal covenant.
