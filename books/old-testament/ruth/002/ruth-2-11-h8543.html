BEFORE - ( temol , Strong's H8543 )

Summary:

The Hebrew adverb temol (frequently joined with shilshom to form the idiom temol shilshom) literally translates as "yesterday" or "recently," but contextually communicates "previously," "heretofore," or "in times past." In Ruth 2:11, this term highlights the profound scope of Ruth's faith and sacrifice. By migrating to a land and living among a people whom she did not know "before," Ruth decisively severed ties with her familiar Moabite past to align herself with the covenant people of Yahweh, exemplifying a complete and trusting conversion.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The adverb temol (also spelled etmol or itmol) has its origins in ancient Semitic languages to designate the day immediately preceding the present—"yesterday." In the Hebrew mind, time was often viewed in relation to space and physical boundaries. To describe a broader, indefinite past, Hebrew writers regularly paired temol with shilshom (literally, "three days ago" or "the third day"). This idiomatic combination, temol shilshom, functions as a temporal word picture denoting an established, ongoing status quo in the past. It creates a linguistic boundary line, separating a long-standing historical condition from a sudden, significant change in the present.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, temol refers to a prior point in time. In its narrowest sense, it means "yesterday." Within its wider semantic range, especially when coupled with shilshom, it means "formerly," "previously," or "in time past." It represents an unbroken pattern of life, a habitual state, or a lack of prior contact. In legal texts, it establishes whether an action was premeditated or if an event was an unprecedented departure from past patterns. It signifies a baseline of prior history against which current changes are measured.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, temol and its idiomatic extensions are used to draw sharp contrasts between past habits and present realities:

• Legal Standing: It serves to establish intent or negligence. For instance, if an ox was not known to gore "in time past," the owner was exempt from certain liabilities because there was no prior pattern of danger.

• Uncharted Paths: It marks transitions into completely new territories. Before crossing the Jordan, the Israelites are reminded that they have not passed this way "before," emphasizing their reliance on divine guidance through unfamiliar territory.

• Relational Transitions: It marks a change in human relationships. When Jacob notices that Laban’s attitude is not toward him "as before," it indicates a permanent rupture in their domestic covenant.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, temol in Ruth 2:11 underscores the radical nature of covenant conversion. Ruth’s life is split into two distinct, irreconcilable eras: the "before" (her life in Moab characterized by paganism and spiritual separation) and her "now" (her integration into the community of faith). This word highlights that true faith requires stepping out of the comfortable, established patterns of one's past. Ruth had no prior history, family leverage, or cultural familiarity with Israel. Her willingness to enter a land and dwell with a people she did not know "before" showcases the supernatural draw of grace, illustrating how those who were once far off are brought into the family of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:11, Boaz acknowledges Ruth’s extraordinary devotion to Naomi. He lists her remarkable actions: she left her father, her mother, her native land, and came to a people whom she did not know temol shilshom ("heretofore" or "before"). The inclusion of this phrase magnifies the vulnerability and depth of Ruth’s commitment. She did not migrate to Israel because of pre-existing social connections, political alliances, or historical familiarity. Every human safety net was absent. By emphasizing that she had no prior knowledge of these people, Boaz frames her actions not as a calculated relocation, but as an act of absolute trust under the wings of the Lord God of Israel.

• 1 Samuel 10:11 - And it happened, when all who knew him formerly saw that he indeed prophesied among the prophets, that the people said to one another, "What is this that has come to the son of Kish? Is Saul also among the prophets?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, the Septuagint (LXX), translates the idiomatic phrase temol shilshom in Ruth 2:11 using the phrase echthes kai triten hemeran, which translates literally as "yesterday and the third day." This literal translation of the Hebrew idiom into Greek maintains the graphic temporal boundary. It reinforces the theological truth that Ruth’s entry into Israel was a total break from her previous life, leaving her old identity behind to receive a new name and heritage in the lineage of the Messiah.
