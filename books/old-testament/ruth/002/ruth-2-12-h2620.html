REFUGE - ( chasah , Strong's H2620 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chasah means to seek shelter, flee for safety, or put one's trust in a protective power. While translated as "trust" in the NKJV text of Ruth 2:12, its primary imagery is deeply spatial and physical, depicting a vulnerable creature fleeing to a secure hiding place—such as a rock, a shield, or the protective wings of a parent bird. In the context of Ruth, it describes a radical, life-altering step of faith. Ruth did not merely change her geographic location; she consciously abandoned her homeland, her family, and her national deities to seek ultimate spiritual and physical security under the protective wings of Yahweh, the God of Israel.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word chasah is rooted in the harsh, unpredictable environment of the ancient Near East. It evokes the picture of a sudden, violent desert storm or the threat of a swooping predator. In such moments of danger, survival depends entirely on finding a place of shelter. The word conjures two primary pictures:

• The Avian Metaphor: A vulnerable chick fleeing to the underside of its mother's wings. Here, the wings represent warmth, absolute security, and personal, sacrificial protection.

• The Rocky Fortress: A traveler fleeing a torrential storm or an invading army by squeezing into the cleft of a massive, immovable rock.

Thus, chasah is never a passive, abstract concept. It requires movement. To take refuge means recognizing one's vulnerability, leaving the exposed open country, and running into a designated place of safety.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The lexical definition of chasah is to flee for protection, to take refuge, to hide oneself, or to trust. The semantic range moves from physical sheltering to spiritual reliance on God:

• To take shelter/refuge: The literal action of hiding behind or under a physical barrier to escape danger.

• To put trust/confide in: The spiritual application of the term, where the believer relies on God’s character, covenant, and power as their ultimate safety net.

Unlike other Hebrew words for trust that emphasize cognitive confidence or felt security, chasah specifically emphasizes the action of seeking a safe haven in a time of distress.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, chasah is used almost exclusively in a figurative sense to describe humanity’s relationship with God. It appears frequently in the Psalms as a cry of the persecuted believer.

• The Shadow of Your Wings: This is the most famous expression of chasah. It presents God not as a cold, distant fortress, but as a tender, protective parent.

• The Shield and Rock: God is described as a shield to those who chasah in Him. The shield absorbs the blows of the enemy, and the rock remains unmoved by the storm.

• False Refuges: Scripture contrasts taking refuge in God with taking refuge in false securities, such as political alliances, wealth, falsehoods, or human leaders. These are described as "refuges of lies" that will ultimately be swept away.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chasah teaches that faith is an active, decisive choice to seek shelter in God.

• Acknowledgment of Helplessness: One does not seek a refuge unless one is in danger or recognizes their own inability to self-protect. Therefore, chasah begins with humility.

• The Character of God: God is revealed as a willing, capable, and accessible refuge. He does not shut the door on those who flee to Him; His "wings" are always open.

• Covenant Security: Taking refuge in Yahweh is not a gamble. It is a secure positioning based on His covenant faithfulness (hesed). Those who take refuge in Him are promised ultimate vindication and safety, regardless of present circumstances.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:12, Boaz pronounces a blessing over Ruth: "The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to trust [take refuge]."

• An Act of Leaving and Cleaving: For Ruth, taking refuge under Yahweh's wings meant leaving Moab. Moab was her home, her comfort zone, and the domain of Chemosh (the god of Moab). To "come to take refuge" under Yahweh's wings required a physical and spiritual migration. It was an act of conversion.

• The Human Agency of God's Wings: A beautiful literary and theological connection occurs later in the narrative. In Ruth 3:9, when Ruth approaches Boaz at the threshing floor, she asks him to "spread your wings [NKJV: cloak/skirt] over your maidservant, for you are a close relative." The Hebrew word for "wings" in Ruth 2:12 (kanaph) is the exact same word translated as "wings/cloak/skirt" in Ruth 3:9.

• The Answer to the Prayer: Boaz prayed that Ruth would find refuge under the kanaph (wings) of Yahweh. In chapter 3, Ruth asks Boaz to spread his kanaph (wing/cloak) over her. This reveals that God often provides His divine shelter through the hands, resources, and obedience of His people. Boaz became the answer to his own prayer.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 36:7 – "How precious is Your lovingkindness, O God! Therefore the children of men put their trust under the shadow of Your wings."

• Psalm 57:1 – "Be merciful to me, O God, be merciful to me! For my soul trusts in You; And in the shadow of Your wings I will make my refuge, Until these calamities have passed by."

• Psalm 91:4 – "He shall cover you with His feathers, And under His wings you shall take refuge; His truth shall be your shield and buckler."

• Proverbs 30:5 – "Every word of God is pure; He is a shield to those who put their trust in Him."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

It is helpful to compare chasah with the Hebrew word batach (Strong's H982), which is the standard Old Testament word for "trust."

• Batach represents a state of mind—feeling secure, careless, or confident because one believes they are safe.

• Chasab represents a crisis action—fleeing to a hiding place because one knows they are not safe on their own.

• In the Christian life, both are vital. We chasah (flee to Christ as our refuge from sin, death, and judgment), and as a result, we batach (live with quiet, secure confidence in His daily care).
