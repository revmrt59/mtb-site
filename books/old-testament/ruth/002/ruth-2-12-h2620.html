REFUGE - ( chasah , Strong’s [2620] )

Summary:

To flee for safety, seek shelter, or trust securely in a protective hiding place. In Ruth 2:12, it describes the deliberate act of seeking spiritual and physical shelter beneath the protective care of the God of Israel, illustrating a radical transfer of allegiance and personal security.

Full Study:

  1. Hebrew Roots / Derivation

    • Derived from the primitive root verb chasah, meaning to flee for protection or to take shelter.

    • The word is fundamentally associated with physical safety from storms, danger, or pursuing enemies.

    • Over time, it developed a rich spiritual usage, shifting from seeking literal shelter under a physical rock or tent to seeking personal, covenantal security in God Himself.

  2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

    • The primary range of meaning involves seeking refuge, fleeing for safety, trusting in, or finding shelter.

    • It implies a conscious choice to leave an exposed, hazardous position to hide within a secure stronghold.

    • In the context of Ruth 2:12, the definitive meaning is the active placement of one's total life, future, and trust under the sovereign protection of Jehovah.

    • The text pairs this action directly with the imagery of wings, modifying the nuance from a general concept of trust to an intimate, personal, and absolute reliance on divine custody.

  3. Usage in Ancient Near Eastern & Jewish Culture & Word Pictures

    • The Maternal Shield: In ancient Near Eastern pastoral and agricultural life, the phrase draws from the imagery of a bird gathering her vulnerable chicks under her outspread wings to shield them from predatory birds, scorching heat, or violent desert storms.

    • The Stronghold of the Rock: In the rugged terrain of the ancient world, taking refuge meant fleeing to a high rock fissure or cave where a pursuing army or a flash flood could not reach the traveler.

    • Mental Image: A defenseless traveler caught in a fierce wilderness storm standing in an open field vs. stepping securely inside a deep, unshakeable stone fortress. Human independence leaves a soul exposed to the elements of grief, judgment, and destitution. Taking refuge in God means stepping out of the storm and placing a permanent, secure barrier between yourself and the dangers of the world.

  4. Theological Implications

    • Taking refuge is the Old Testament functional equivalent of saving faith, requiring an active turning away from false securities to rely completely on God's character.

    • It connects structurally to a major recurring theme across Scripture where leaving pagan origins results in a dynamic re-clothing of identity and status within God's family, echoing how God sheltered Israel in the wilderness.

    • This word reveals that God’s character is not merely that of a distant judge, but a warm, accessible sanctuary for the vulnerable, the foreigner, and the outcast.

  5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

    • In Ruth 2:12, Boaz pronounces a blessing over Ruth, recognizing that she has deliberately left her native land, her family, and her Moabite gods.

    • The exact order of events in the narrative is vital: Ruth chose to follow Naomi and serve the true God while she was still a destitute, empty-handed widow. She sought the refuge of God's wings before any human restoration, marriage proposal, or financial provision was in sight.

    • Boaz notes that she has come to take refuge under the wings of the Lord God of Israel, showing that her physical arrival in the Bethlehem grain fields was the direct practical consequence of her prior inward spiritual commitment.

  6. Pastoral Commentary & Homiletical Insights

    • Evangelical commentators frequently note that true faith is an active step. Ruth did not merely admire the God of Israel from a distance in Moab; she uprooted her life to seek shelter under His wings.

    • The pastoral synthesis of this text highlights that God honors radical, self-denying commitment. When a person abandons earthly comforts to trust God in poverty, God frequently uses ordinary human instruments—like the kindness of Boaz—to become the physical expression of His protective wings.

    • Homiletically, the passage exposes the human tendency to seek refuge in wealth, heritage, or family status. Ruth possessed none of these things, proving that the only prerequisite for receiving divine shelter is a recognized personal need for a Savior.

  8. Other Relevant Insights

    • The word acts as a functional bridge between initial faith and subsequent conduct: seeking refuge under God's wings positionally (doctrine) directly empowers Ruth's submissive, diligent work in the harvest fields (conduct).

    • For homiletical structure, Boaz’s blessing outlines a beautiful pastoral framework addressing three distinct layers of human anxiety:

      • The Work is recognized: "The Lord repay your work," addressing the anxiety of futility.

      • The Reward is promised: "A full reward be given you," addressing the anxiety of lack.

      • The Refuge is secured: "...under whose wings you have come to take refuge," addressing the anxiety of vulnerability and isolation.
