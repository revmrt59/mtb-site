LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh (translated "LORD" in all capital letters in most English Bibles) is the personal, covenantal, and self-revealed name of the God of Israel. Expressing His eternal self-existence, sovereign faithfulness, and relational closeness, it signifies a God who binds Himself to His people by oath and promise. In Ruth 2:12, Boaz invokes this sacred name to bless Ruth, a foreign Moabitess, acknowledging that she has sought shelter under the protective wings of the covenant God who actively rewards and protects those who trust in Him.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yahweh is represented in the Hebrew Bible by the four consonants YHWH, known as the Tetragrammaton. It is derived from the Hebrew verb hayah, which means "to be" or "to become." The word picture of this name points to God as the self-existent One who has no beginning and no end. He is the source of all life and being, completely self-sufficient and independent of His creation.

While generic titles for God, such as Elohim, highlight His role as the powerful Creator and Ruler of the universe, Yahweh is His deeply personal name. It is the name God used when He entered into a formal covenant with Abraham and later revealed His character to Moses at the burning bush as "I AM WHO I AM." Therefore, whenever this name appears, it carries the background of historical redemption, relational intimacy, and unchanging faithfulness.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yahweh refers to the one true, living God of Israel. Lexically, the name designates:

• The self-existent, eternal Creator who possesses life in Himself.

• The covenant-keeping God who actively manifests His presence, power, and mercy in history.

• The sovereign Lord who binds Himself to His people through promises and acts of deliverance.

The semantic core of the name is active, dynamic presence. It does not merely signify that God exists in an abstract sense, but that He is actively present with His people to save, guide, provide for, and redeem them.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Yahweh is the most frequently used name for God in the Old Testament, occurring over 6,800 times. It is used in several distinct ways:

• As a Covenant Marker: It appears in contexts where God establishes, maintains, or restores His relationship with Israel.

• In Compound Titles: It is paired with descriptive nouns to illustrate His care for His people, such as Yahweh-Yireh ("The LORD will provide") or Yahweh-Raah ("The LORD is my shepherd").

• With Anthropomorphic Metaphors: Yahweh is described as having "wings" (kanaph) under which His people find refuge. This evokes the imagery of a mother bird fiercely protecting and comforting her young beneath her wings, symbolizing His warm, accessible, and impenetrable protection.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theology of Yahweh is the foundation of biblical faith.

• Immutability and Faithfulness: Because He is Yahweh, God does not change. His promises remain secure across generations. He cannot lie, and He cannot fail to fulfill His word.

• Relational Immanence: God is not a distant, impersonal force. He has a name, He hears the cries of His people, and He invites them into a personal relationship.

• Redemptive Initiative: Yahweh is the God who descends to deliver. Just as He delivered Israel from Egypt, He actively works to rescue individuals from hopelessness and spiritual exile.

• Universal Sovereignty: Although Yahweh is the God of Israel, His sovereignty extends over all nations. He invites outsiders and foreigners to renounce their false gods and find salvation in Him.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:12, Boaz pronounces a blessing over Ruth: "The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge." The double use of Yahweh in this single verse carries immense contextual weight:

• Welcoming the Foreigner: Ruth was a Moabitess, a nation traditionally hostile to Israel. By blessing her in the name of Yahweh, Boaz officially welcomes her into the spiritual community of Israel. He recognizes that she has forsaken the gods of Moab (such as Chemosh) to place her trust in the true God.

• The Reward of the Covenant: Boaz asks Yahweh to repay Ruth's sacrificial love for Naomi. This highlights the character of Yahweh as a God who notices, values, and rewards faith and kindness (hesed).

• The Divine Wings and Human Hands: Boaz speaks of Ruth finding refuge under Yahweh’s "wings" (kanaph). Structurally, this sets up a beautiful wordplay in the narrative. In Ruth 3:9, Ruth asks Boaz to spread his "wings" (translated "corners of your garment" or "skirt," using the exact same Hebrew word kanaph) over her as her kinsman-redeemer. Thus, Boaz becomes the very instrument Yahweh uses to answer his own prayer and provide the refuge he spoke of.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:4 - "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens."

• Exodus 3:15 - "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Psalm 23:1 - "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

• Proverbs 18:10 - "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; the righteous run to it and are safe."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

Due to the immense sanctity of the name Yahweh, post-exilic Jews feared taking the name of God in vain (violating Exodus 20:7). Consequently, they stopped pronouncing the name aloud, substituting it with the Hebrew word Adonai ("Lord") or HaShem ("The Name") during public reading.

When the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek (the Septuagint), translators used the word Kurios ("Lord") to represent Yahweh. This practice carried over into the New Testament and most English translations, which use "LORD" in small capital letters to indicate the Tetragrammaton.

The New Testament writers apply the title Kurios directly to Jesus Christ (such as in Philippians 2:9-11), making the staggering theological claim that Jesus of Nazareth is the physical incarnation of Yahweh, the covenant God of Israel.
