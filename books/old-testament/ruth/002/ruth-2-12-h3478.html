ISRAEL - ( Yisra'el , Strong's H3478 )

Summary:

The name Yisra'el (Israel) originates from Genesis 32:28, meaning "strives with God" or "God strives/rules." In the Old Testament, it transitions from the personal name of the patriarch Jacob to a corporate designation for his descendants, the covenant people of God. In Ruth 2:12, Boaz invokes the "LORD God of Israel," presenting the nation's God not merely as a localized, ethnic deity, but as the sovereign Protector who covenants with His people and welcomes the foreign outcast under His protective "wings."

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yisra'el is a compound of the verbal root sarah (to struggle, fight, or rule) and the noun El (God). Its origin is deeply experiential, forged in the nocturnal wrestling match between Jacob and the mysterious divine messenger at the Jabbok River (Genesis 32:24-30). In that encounter, Jacob's self-reliance is broken, and he is given a new name to mark his new identity as one who prevails through divine grace. Historically and nationally, this name becomes the identity of the entire nation. It serves as a perpetual reminder that the people of God are defined not by their own strength, but by their covenantal wrestling with, dependence upon, and ultimate preservation by God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yisra'el means "God strives," "He who strives with God," or "God rules." Lexically, the term carries three primary designations:

• The personal name given to the patriarch Jacob, representing his transformed identity.

• The collective name for the twelve tribes descended from Jacob's sons, identifying them as the distinct covenant community of Yahweh.

• The geopolitical designation for the Northern Kingdom during the era of the divided monarchy, distinct from the Southern Kingdom of Judah. In post-exilic literature, such as the book of Ruth, it refers to the entire restoration community seeking to live faithfully under God's law.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, the name Israel is associated with several vivid metaphorical pictures:

• The Vineyard: Israel is depicted as a choice vine brought out of Egypt, planted by God, and cared for tenderly, though it often failed to produce the expected fruit of righteousness (Isaiah 5:1-7; Psalm 80:8-11).

• The Firstborn Son: God adopts the nation of Israel, declaring to Pharaoh, "Israel is My son, My firstborn" (Exodus 4:22), highlighting an intimate, protective, and inheritance-based relationship.

• The Bride: God characterizes His covenant with Israel as a marriage, depicting Himself as a loving husband and Israel as His betrothed, emphasizing the fidelity and devotion expected of the relationship (Hosea 2:19-20).

4. Theological Implications

The name Israel is central to Old Testament theology, representing several key concepts:

• Sovereign Election: Israel was not chosen because of their size, righteousness, or power, but purely due to God’s sovereign love and His faithfulness to the oath He swore to their fathers (Deuteronomy 7:7-8).

• Covenant Responsibility: To be identified with the name of Israel meant living under the terms of the Mosaic Covenant. They were to be a "kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:6), representing God to the surrounding Gentile nations.

• Inclusivity of Grace: While Israel is a distinct ethnic and national entity, the theology of Israel always contained an open door for the foreigner. The ultimate purpose of Israel was to be a vehicle of blessing to all nations (Genesis 12:3), a reality beautifully illustrated in the book of Ruth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:12, Boaz pronounces a blessing over Ruth: "The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge."

The use of "Israel" in this context is highly significant. Ruth is a Moabitess, a member of a nation historically excluded from the assembly of Israel due to their past hostility (Deuteronomy 23:3-4). By calling Yahweh the "God of Israel," Boaz acknowledges the specific covenant relationship God has with His chosen people. Yet, by declaring that Ruth has come to seek refuge under this God's wings, Boaz recognizes that the covenant protection of the God of Israel extends to any foreigner who abandons their false gods to pledge allegiance to Him. Ruth's conversion ("Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God" in Ruth 1:16) has effectively brought her into the protective sphere of Israel, proving that faith, not mere genealogy, determines one's standing before the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Joshua 24:2 - "And Joshua said to all the people, 'Thus says the LORD God of Israel: "Your fathers, including Terah, the father of Abraham and the father of Nahor, dwelt on the other side of the River in old times; and they served other gods."'"

• Psalm 121:4 - "Behold, He who keeps Israel Shall neither slumber nor sleep."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound literary and theological connection between Ruth 2:12 and Ruth 3:9. In Ruth 2:12, Boaz prays that the God of Israel will protect Ruth under His "wings" (kanaph). Later, in Ruth 3:9, when Ruth approaches Boaz on the threshing floor, she asks him, "Spread the corner [literally, 'wing', kanaph] of your garment over your maidservant, for you are a close relative."

Through this wordplay, the narrative reveals that God often answers prayers for protection through human agency. Boaz becomes the very answer to his own prayer. By extending his "wing" over Ruth in marriage, Boaz acts as the physical manifestation of the covenant care of the God of Israel.
