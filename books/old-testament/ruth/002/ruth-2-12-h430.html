GOD - ( elohim , Strong's H430 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word elohim is a plural noun of majesty used to designate the one true God as the supreme, all-powerful Creator and Sovereign Ruler of the universe. In Ruth 2:12, it is coupled with the covenant name of God (Yahweh) to emphasize that the transcendent, almighty Creator of the nations is also the personal, covenant-keeping God of Israel. When Ruth seeks refuge under His wings, she is placing her trust not in a localized, tribal deity, but in the ultimate Sovereign of heaven and earth who actively rewards faith, executes justice, and protects the vulnerable.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word elohim is grammatically plural in form, yet when it refers to the one true God of Israel, it is consistently paired with singular verbs and singular adjectives. This grammatical structure is an intensive plural or a plural of majesty. Rather than implying a plurality of gods, it expresses the infinite fullness, supreme majesty, and limitless power of the Creator. In the ancient Near Eastern world, nations worshiped various localized deities referred to as elim or elohim, but the Hebrew scriptures employ this term uniquely to assert that Yahweh embodies all divine power, authority, and glory in His singular being.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word elohim signifies the supreme deity, the Creator, Ruler, and Judge of all things. It is used more than 2,500 times in the Old Testament. Lexically, it conveys ideas of supreme strength, preeminent authority, and ultimate power. While the word can occasionally refer to pagan deities, heavenly messengers, or human judges depending on the context, its primary theological usage denotes the true God of Israel. When joined with the personal covenant name Yahweh (the LORD), as in the phrase "the LORD God of Israel" (Yahweh Elohim Yisrael), it combines God's infinite, transcendent power as Creator with His intimate, self-revealing love as Redeemer.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Creator of All: The word first appears in the opening verse of Scripture, where elohim speaks the heavens and the earth into existence, establishing Him as the source of all life and order.

• The Supreme Judge: As elohim, He holds universal moral authority, assessing the actions of nations and individuals and executing perfect justice.

• The Shelter of Wings: In Ruth 2:12, the majestic power of elohim is beautifully paired with the tender metaphor of "wings" (kenapayim). This image evokes a mother bird shielding her vulnerable young from storms and predators, or the golden wings of the cherubim overshadowing the mercy seat in the Holy of Holies. The supreme God of cosmic power manifests His majesty through intimate, protective sanctuary for those who trust Him.

4. Theological Implications

• Universal Sovereignty: Elohim is not a localized, national deity confined by geography. He is the ruler of all nations, meaning His authority and care extend to the ends of the earth.

• Radical Grace for Outsiders: Because elohim is the creator of all humanity, His covenant shelter is available to all who seek Him by faith, regardless of ethnic origin. Ruth, a marginalized Moabite widow, finds equal protection under His wings.

• Sovereign Recompense: God is a righteous rewarder of faith. The sovereign Ruler of history is personally involved in the details of human lives, actively observing and rewarding quiet acts of faithfulness and devotion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:12, Boaz pronounces a blessing: "The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge." This statement carries immense spiritual weight. Previously, Naomi had urged Ruth to return to her people and to her Moabite "gods" (elohim), specifically Chemosh. In response, Ruth made her monumental confession of faith, declaring that Naomi's God (elohim) would be her God (elohim). When Boaz invokes "the LORD God of Israel," he recognizes that Ruth has made a definitive spiritual exodus. She has abandoned the powerless elohim of Moab to shelter under the all-powerful Elohim of Israel. The use of this word assures Ruth that the God she has chosen to trust is the absolute Master of providence, fully capable of protecting and sustaining her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:1 - "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth."

• Ruth 1:16 - "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you; For wherever you go, I will go; And wherever you lodge, I will lodge; Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God.'"

• Deuteronomy 10:17 - "For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great God, mighty and awesome, who shows no partiality nor takes a bribe."

• Psalm 91:2 - "I will say of the LORD, 'He is my refuge and my fortress; My God, in Him I will trust.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a beautiful literary and practical progression of God's "wings" (kenapayim) in the book of Ruth. While Boaz prays in Ruth 2:12 that Ruth will find refuge under the wings of the LORD God of Israel, God ultimately answers this prayer through Boaz's own actions. In Ruth 3:9, when Ruth asks Boaz to spread his "wings" (the corner of his garment, which is the exact same Hebrew word kanap) over her, she is calling on him to be the physical agent of God's covenant protection. This demonstrates that the transcendent elohim frequently manifests His sovereign care and answers the prayers of the needy through the hands of obedient, compassionate believers.
