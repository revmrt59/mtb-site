REWARD - ( maskoret , Strong's H4909 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word maskoret primarily refers to "wages," "hire," or "recompense" earned through labor. While it is almost exclusively used in the Old Testament to denote the contractual, physical wages paid to a laborer, its use in Ruth 2:12 elevates the concept to a spiritual level. Boaz pronounces a blessing over Ruth, petitioning Yahweh to grant her a "full reward" or "complete wages" for her sacrificial loyalty to Naomi and her faith in the God of Israel. This word highlights God's character as a faithful and generous provider who compensates those who abandon worldly security to seek refuge under His wings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The term maskoret is derived from the verbal root sakar, which means "to hire" or "to pay wages." In the ancient Near East, the relationship between an employer and a laborer was strictly contractual. Wages were the legal, agreed-upon compensation for hard work. When a laborer completed their service, the employer was bound by covenant and law to pay the wages due. This background of honest labor, sweat, and legal obligation forms the backdrop of the word. Applied to God, it presents a beautiful paradox: though no human can place God in His debt, He graciously binds Himself to reward those who diligently seek Him, acting as a faithful master who compensates His servants with perfect justice and abundant generosity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun maskoret means wages, pay, reward, or recompense. It denotes the compensation given for work performed or services rendered. In classical Hebrew, it carries a concrete, economic connotation. It is distinct from words that mean a free gift; rather, it implies that the recipient has performed a task or demonstrated a loyalty that warrants a corresponding return. In the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint), it is translated using the word misthos, which similarly means wages, hire, or reward.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word occurs only four times in the Hebrew Bible. Outside of its theological usage in Ruth 2:12, it is used exclusively in the narrative of Jacob and Laban in Genesis.

• In the Genesis accounts, maskoret represents the hard-earned physical wages that Laban repeatedly manipulated and changed. It depicts the vulnerability of a laborer at the mercy of an earthly employer.

• In Ruth, the word undergoes a dramatic shift. It is lifted out of the secular, mercantile sphere and placed into the sphere of divine providence. God is pictured as the ultimate Employer. Unlike Laban, who cheated his worker, Yahweh is a faithful master whose "wages" are sure, complete, and overflowing. The word picture transitions from a human contract fraught with greed to a divine covenant marked by grace and abundance.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, maskoret in Ruth 2:12 teaches several profound truths about God's character and His relationship with His people:

• God is the Sovereign Rewarder: God does not overlook the quiet, unnoticed acts of obedience, kindness, and faith. He keeps a perfect record of our service.

• The Paradox of Grace and Reward: Although Ruth’s actions were born of love and faith, and though salvation and grace can never be bought, God chooses to frame His blessings as a "full reward." He treats our faith-filled steps of obedience as service that He delights to compensate.

• The Abundance of Divine Recompense: The modifier "full" (shelemah) indicates complete, unbroken, and perfect compensation. God’s rewards are not stingy or partial; they are satisfying and whole.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:12, Boaz observes Ruth gleaning in his fields. He has heard of her extraordinary kindness (chesed) toward Naomi and her radical decision to leave her homeland, parents, and pagan gods to trust in Yahweh. Boaz recognizes that Ruth has made a massive material and social sacrifice. In response, he prays that Yahweh will pay her a "full reward" (maskoret shelemah). The immediate context links this reward to Ruth coming to "take refuge" under the wings of the Lord God of Israel. The ultimate irony and beauty of the passage is that Boaz himself becomes the human instrument through whom God answers this prayer. By redeeming Ruth, marrying her, and bringing her into the covenant family, Boaz delivers the very "wages" of security, family, and legacy that he prayed God would give her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because maskoret occurs only four times in the Hebrew Scriptures, all of its occurrences are listed below, highlighting the contrast between human wages and divine reward:

• Genesis 29:15 – "Then Laban said to Jacob, 'Because you are my relative, should you therefore serve me for nothing? Tell me, what shall your wages be?'"

• Genesis 31:7 – "yet your father has deceived me and changed my wages ten times, but God did not allow him to hurt me."

• Genesis 31:41 – "Thus I have been in your house twenty years; I served you fourteen years for your two daughters, and six years for your flock, and you have changed my wages ten times."

• Ruth 2:12 – "The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The concept of seeking refuge under God's "wings" (kenapayim) in tandem with receiving a "full reward" provides a rich pastoral image. The "wings" evoke the image of a mother bird protecting her defenseless chicks from danger, as well as the wings of the cherubim over the Mercy Seat on the Ark of the Covenant. Ruth’s "wage" is not merely physical wealth or social status, but the intimate protection, presence, and covenant fellowship of God Himself. Furthermore, the root of the word for "full" (shelemah) is closely related to shalom, implying that the reward God gives brings deep, wholistic peace, restoration, and completeness to a broken life.
