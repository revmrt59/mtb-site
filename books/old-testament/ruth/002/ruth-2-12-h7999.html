REPAY - ( shalam , Strong's H7999 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shalam carries the foundational meaning of making something whole, complete, or sound. While often translated as "repay," "restore," or "recompense," its biblical significance goes far beyond a simple financial transaction. In the Old Testament, it represents the process of restoring balance, fulfilling an obligation, paying a vow, or making full restitution so that peace (shalom) and wholeness are reestablished in a relationship or community. In Ruth 2:12, Boaz invokes this word as a prayerful blessing, asking Yahweh to fully compensate Ruth for her sacrificial loyalty, a prayer that Boaz himself would ultimately help answer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root letters sh-l-m form one of the most significant semantic families in the Hebrew Bible. The concrete word picture behind this root is that of a wall that has had its breaches repaired, or a broken vessel made whole again. It describes bringing an unfinished, damaged, or empty state into a state of completion, safety, and soundness. To shalam is to make good on what is lacking, ensuring that no debt, injury, or unfulfilled promise remains outstanding. It is conceptually and etymologically bound to the noun shalom, which is not merely the absence of conflict but the presence of total well-being, harmony, safety, and completeness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its basic form (the Qal stem), the verb shalam means to be complete, finished, or at peace. In the intensive form (the Piel stem), which is the form used in Ruth 2:12, the verb is active and causative, meaning to make complete, to make restitution, to pay off a debt, to perform a vow, or to recompense/repay. The term denotes an active restoration of equilibrium. When a person repays a debt or makes restitution for a wrong, they are performing shalam—restoring the balance of justice and peace.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Hebrew Scriptures employ shalam in several distinct contexts to illustrate the restoration of wholeness:

• Legal Restitution: In the covenant laws, if someone causes loss to another's property, they must shalam (make full restitution) what was lost to ensure justice is served and community peace is maintained.

• Fulfilling Vows: When a worshiper makes a vow to God, they must shalam (pay or perform) that vow. Fulfilling the promise completes the spiritual transaction, leaving no unfulfilled obligation between the worshiper and God.

• Divine Recompense: God is the one who ultimately shalam (repays) human actions. He repays both the wicked for their iniquity (retributive justice) and the righteous for their faithfulness (remunerative grace).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shalam demonstrates that God is a God of order, justice, and restorative grace. He does not leave accounts unsettled. When God repays, He does not merely match a transaction; He restores wholeness to those who have suffered loss for His sake. The word highlights that obedience and faith are never in vain. God is not a debtor to any human being; He takes notice of faithful service and acts to make the believer's reward full and complete.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:12, Boaz pronounces a blessing over Ruth: "The LORD repay (shalam) your work, and a full (shalem) reward be given you." There is a beautiful wordplay here between the verb shalam (repay) and the adjective shalem (full/complete), both coming from the same root. Ruth had experienced devastating losses—the deaths of her husband, father-in-law, and brother-in-law, and the loss of her homeland. By choosing to stay with Naomi and trust the God of Israel, she emptied herself of security. Boaz recognizes her sacrifice and prays that Yahweh will make her whole again, filling her empty life with His abundance. Providential irony shines through this passage: Boaz, the very man praying this blessing, becomes the physical instrument through whom God repays Ruth, providing her with security, a husband, and a son.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Joel 2:25: "So I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten, The crawling locust, The consuming locust, And the chewing locust, My great army which I sent among you."

• Proverbs 20:22: "Do not say, 'I will recompense evil'; Wait for the LORD, and He will save you."

• Psalm 50:14: "Offer to God thanksgiving, And pay your vows to the Most High."

• 1 Samuel 24:19: "For if a man finds his enemy, will he let him go away safely? Therefore may the LORD reward you with good for what you have done to me this day."

• Exodus 22:1: "If a man steals an ox or a sheep, and slaughters it or sells it, he shall restore five oxen for an ox and four sheep for a sheep."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The relationship between shalam (repay) and the noun shalom (peace) reveals that true peace in biblical thought is never cheap or abstract; it requires restoration and the settling of accounts. For peace to exist, what is broken must be paid for or restored. In the New Testament, this concept is fulfilled in the work of Jesus Christ. Our sin created a debt we could not pay, but Christ made complete restitution on our behalf, restoring our relationship with God and establishing our permanent shalom (peace).
