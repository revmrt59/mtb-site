FULL - ( shalem , Strong's H8003 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shalem captures the profound reality of being complete, safe, whole, and fully finished. In Ruth 2:12, Boaz pronounces a blessing over Ruth, praying that her reward from the Lord God of Israel will be "full." Rather than a partial or half-hearted recompense, this word highlights an intact, overflowing blessing that reflects God's generous covenant faithfulness to those who seek refuge under His wings. It speaks of a restorative grace that leaves absolutely nothing lacking, reversing the bitter emptiness of loss into divine fullness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The adjective shalem is derived from the primitive root verb shalam, which means to make safe, complete, make amends, or make peace. This same root gives rise to the familiar Hebrew noun shalom, representing wholeness, well-being, peace, and security. The ancient conceptual background of shalem is not merely the absence of conflict or deficit, but the presence of total harmony and the restoration of what was previously broken or empty. A physical object that is shalem is uncut, whole, and undamaged. When applied to relationships or transactions, it denotes absolute integrity, soundness, and undivided commitment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word shalem describes something that has reached its full measure, leaving no deficiency, defect, or omission. It functions with several key nuances across the Old Testament scriptures:

• Complete or just weights and measures, indicating honesty and legal righteousness.

• Whole, uncut stones used to build an altar, symbolizing purity and devotion untouched by human pride.

• A heart that is fully devoted, loyal, or undivided toward God.

• A full or complete population in captivity.

• A complete or finished reward or wage, which is its specific function in Ruth 2:12.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs shalem in vivid word pictures that illuminate its depth:

• Uncut Altar Stones: The Torah commanded that altars be constructed of "whole" stones (shalem). These stones were to remain exactly as God created them, untouched by human iron tools. This pictures a worship that is pure, unblemished by human self-effort, and completely dedicated to God.

• The Loyal Heart: Scripture frequently describes a king whose heart is "loyal" (shalem) to God. This paints the picture of an undivided heart. It is not a claim of sinless perfection, but rather of a heart that is fully surrendered, integrated, and oriented toward God, rather than being fragmented and shared with foreign idols.

• The Perfect Weight: Merchants were commanded to use "perfect" (shalem) and just weights. This represents absolute integrity and completeness in daily transactions, where nothing is shaved off or falsified.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shalem reveals the character of God's covenant actions and restorations.

• Complete Recompense: God is not a stingy paymaster. The "full reward" is not a meager compensation or a bare-minimum survival wage. It represents the rich, overflowing bounty of God’s grace. When God rewards faith, He does so in a way that is complete and lacking in nothing.

• Divine Wholeness: The connection to shalom shows that God's rewards always aim at the spiritual and physical restoration of the individual. He does not merely provide food; He restores identity, family, inheritance, and peace.

• Sovereign Completeness: God’s works are always finished and complete. When He begins a work of restoration in the life of an outcast, He carries it through to its ultimate, perfect end.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:12, Boaz acts as a conduit of God’s blessing, though he will ultimately become the human instrument through whom God answers this very prayer. The context is crucial: Ruth had left her homeland, her family, and her security to cling to Naomi and the God of Israel. She had emptied herself of her past.

The phrase "a full reward" (maskurtek shelemah) stands in deliberate, beautiful contrast to Naomi’s lament in Ruth 1:21: "I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty." Through Ruth's faithful devotion, God begins to fill the emptiness of this family.

The reward is "full" because its source is Yahweh, "under whose wings you have come to take refuge." By seeking shelter under the wings of the Almighty, Ruth is promised not a partial or temporary relief, but a complete, secure, and permanent restoration that eventually weaves her into the royal lineage of David and the Messiah.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, shalem in Ruth 2:12 is translated using the Greek word pleres, which means full, filled, or complete. This Greek root is prominent in the New Testament to describe spiritual fullness. For example, Jesus is described as "full (pleres) of grace and truth" (John 1:14). Additionally, the apostle John uses this same concept when he exhorts believers to look to themselves so "that we do not lose those things we worked for, but that we may receive a full reward (misthon plere)" (2 John 1:8). This connects the Old Testament promise of a complete covenantal reward with the New Testament reality of abiding in the fullness of Christ.
