COME - ( bo , Strong's H0935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo is a foundational and highly versatile word meaning "to come," "to enter," or "to go in." In Ruth 2:12, it describes Ruth's decisive action of coming to seek refuge under the wings of the LORD, the God of Israel. Rather than a passive arrival, this word denotes a purposeful entry into a new state of relationship, covenant protection, and faith, marking her transition from a foreign outsider to a cherished member of the covenant community.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• bo is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 2,500 times.

• The basic, concrete picture of the word is physical movement across a boundary—crossing over a threshold to enter a tent, a city, or a house.

• In the ancient Near East, entering someone's tent or domain was an appeal for hospitality, protection, and alliance. To "come under" someone's roof or wings meant placing oneself entirely under their guardianship, surrendering one's independence in exchange for their sovereign protection and provision.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of bo is to come in, enter, or go in.

• In the Qal stem (as used in Ruth 2:12, conjugated as ba't—"you have come"), it signifies personal arrival and the physical or metaphorical entry into a specific location or state of being. It expresses a completed, deliberate action of coming to a destination.

• The lexical range encompasses physical entry into a geographic territory, relational entry into a covenant or household, and the historical arrival of events, prophecies, or seasons.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical entry: It is used for entering a tent, house, or land (such as Israel entering the Promised Land).

• Relational entry: It is used for entering into a covenant, a marriage, or a state of safety.

• The combination of bo ("come") with hasah ("to trust" or "take refuge") creates a powerful spatial metaphor in Ruth 2:12: physically walking into a sanctuary to hide under the protective wings of God, much like a young chick seeking warmth and safety under its mother's wings.

4. Theological Implications

• Faith is not merely intellectual assent; it is a movement of the whole person. Ruth's "coming" represents a spatial relocation that mirrors a deep spiritual conversion. She left Moab (her past, her ancestral lands, and her false gods) to enter Israel (her future and the presence of the true God).

• Theologically, bo underscores human agency responding to divine grace. God's protective wings are open, but Ruth made the active, costly choice to come under them.

• It reflects the biblical doctrine of conversion and covenant-making: entering into a new sphere of lordship, safety, and community.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:12, Boaz acknowledges Ruth's extraordinary faith. He uses bo to highlight the permanence and costliness of her decision. She did not just visit Israel; she came to stay and to place her life under the shadow of YHWH's wings.

• The perfect tense of the verb indicates a settled, completed action with ongoing results. Ruth's arrival in Bethlehem was not a temporary excursion but a permanent displacement of her old life.

• This "coming" qualified her for the very "full reward" Boaz prays she will receive. Boaz himself eventually becomes the human instrument through whom God's wings of protection are extended to Ruth when she asks him to spread the corner of his garment over her in chapter 3.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 7:1 - "Then the LORD said to Noah, 'Come into the ark, you and all your household, because I have seen that you are righteous before Me in this generation.'" (A physical entry that results in salvation from judgment).

• Psalm 24:7 - "Lift up your heads, O you gates! And be lifted up, you everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in." (A triumphal entry of the sovereign King into His temple).

• Psalm 95:6 - "Oh come, let us worship and bow down; Let us kneel before the LORD our Maker." (An invitation to enter into the presence of God for worship).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint (LXX) translates bo in Ruth 2:12 using the Greek verb erchomai (specifically the second aorist form hēlthes), which means to come or go.

• This Greek equivalent is frequently used in the New Testament of those who "come" to Jesus for salvation, rest, and life (e.g., Matthew 11:28, "Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest").

• Just as Ruth came to the God of Israel for refuge, New Testament believers come to Christ to find ultimate shelter and eternal rest.
