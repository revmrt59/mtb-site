LORD - ( 'adon , Strong's H113 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the Hebrew word 'adon refers to a master, lord, ruler, or superior who possesses authority, ownership, or protective custody over others. Unlike the divine title Adonai (which is reserved exclusively for the sovereign God), 'adon is frequently used as a term of respect, honor, and polite address toward human superiors. In Ruth 2:13, Ruth addresses Boaz as "my lord" ('adoni), reflecting her humble posture as a vulnerable foreign widow recognizing his authority, protective capability, and elevated social status as a land-owning redeemer who has shown her unexpected grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word 'adon carries the underlying concept of a ruler, master, or someone who exercises sovereign authority within a specific sphere.

• Linguistically, the root is connected to the idea of strength, ruling power, or acting as a firm pillar. In ancient Near Eastern culture, a master or lord was viewed as the pillar or foundation of the household, community, or nation, sustaining and protecting those dependent upon him.

• In the ancient household, the relationship between a servant and an 'adon was not merely transactional; it carried mutual responsibilities. The servant owed obedience and loyalty, while the master owed protection, provision, and justice.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of 'adon is master, owner, lord, or ruler. It designates someone with authority, control, or custody over others.

• When suffixed as 'adoni ("my lord"), the word functions as a highly respectful title of address used toward kings, husbands, fathers, prophets, and wealthy landowners. It is the Old Testament equivalent of the polite titles "sir" or "my master."

• The word stands in contrast to 'ebed (servant) or shiphhah (maidservant). Addressing someone as 'adon voluntarily places the speaker in a position of humility and submission.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• 'adon is used to describe human relationships of authority: masters of servants (Genesis 24:9), husbands of wives (Genesis 18:12), kings of subjects (1 Samuel 22:12), and leaders of nations.

• It is also used of God Himself as the ultimate Sovereign over creation, often phrased as "the Lord of all the earth" (Joshua 3:11).

• The word picture generated by 'adon is that of a protective canopy. To call someone "my lord" is to seek refuge under their authority and to recognize their capacity to act as a defender and provider.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of 'adon establishes a clear social hierarchy that mirrors the spiritual hierarchy between humanity and God. Human lordship, when exercised righteously, reflects the benevolent lordship of God.

• In the book of Ruth, Boaz operates as a historical type of Jesus Christ, our Kinsman-Redeemer. When Ruth addresses Boaz as "my lord," she demonstrates the exact attitude of faith and submission that a believer holds toward Christ.

• The transition from a destitute stranger to one who finds favor under a benevolent master illustrates the doctrine of grace, where the servant finds safety, sustenance, and redemption under the lordship of a compassionate redeemer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:13, Ruth has just received extraordinary and unexpected kindness from Boaz, who has commanded his young men not to touch her, provided her with water, and invited her to glean safely in his fields.

• Ruth responds by saying, "Let me find favor in your sight, my lord." By using 'adoni, she acknowledges the vast social gap between them—she is a destitute Moabite widow, while he is a wealthy, powerful landowner of Bethlehem.

• Her use of the term is laced with deep gratitude. She notes that Boaz has "comforted" her and spoken "kindly" to her, even though she is "not like one of your maidservants." Her submission to his lordship is a direct response to his voluntary display of covenant faithfulness (chesed).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 18:12 - "Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, 'After I have grown old, shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?'"

• Genesis 42:10 - "And they said to him, 'No, my lord, but your servants have come to buy food.'"

• Joshua 3:11 - "Behold, the ark of the covenant of the Lord of all the earth is crossing over before you into the Jordan."

• 1 Kings 18:7 - "Now as Obadiah was on his way, suddenly Elijah met him; and he recognized him, and fell on his face, and said, 'Is that you, my lord Elijah?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• It is vital to distinguish between 'adon (human master), 'adoni (my human lord), and 'Adonai (the Lord God). Hebrew scribes used the plural of majesty 'Adonai exclusively for God to emphasize His supreme sovereignty, while 'adon and its singular forms remained available for civil, respectful human discourse.

• Ruth's address to Boaz as 'adon highlights her assimilation into Israelite culture and her quick adoption of godly, respectful social customs, leaving behind her pagan background to seek shelter under the wings of the Lord God of Israel.
