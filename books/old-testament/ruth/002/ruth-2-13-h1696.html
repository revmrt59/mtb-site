SPOKEN - ( dabar , Strong's H1696 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb dabar signifies the act of communicating, declaring, or speaking. In Ruth 2:13, this word appears in the intensive Piel stem within the idiom "spoken to the heart" (translated as "spoken kindly"). Rather than a formal or distant address, this manner of speaking denotes a deep, relational, and comforting message that alleviates fear, restores dignity, and establishes peace for a vulnerable person.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb dabar is one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over 1,100 times. Unlike the Hebrew verb amar (which primarily focuses on the conceptual content of what is said), dabar emphasizes the dynamic process, the act of speaking, and the relational space created between the speaker and the hearer.

In ancient Near Eastern culture, spoken words were not viewed as fleeting vibrations in the air, but as concrete realities carrying substance, power, and consequence. When a person spoke, they extended their personal influence and character toward another. In Ruth 2:13, the verb is paired with the preposition 'al (upon/to) and the noun leb (heart)—literally, "spoken upon her heart." This vivid idiom conveys a picture of words acting as a physical balm, gently pressing down upon the seat of Ruth’s grief, fear, and exhaustion to soothe her troubled spirit.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of dabar is to speak, declare, converse, promise, or command.

• In the Piel stem (the intensive active stem used in this passage), the verb denotes active, focused communication, often carrying a declarative or relational weight.

• When joined with "the heart" (leb), the semantic range shifts to comforting, reassuring, wooing, or speaking tenderly. It describes speech designed to win someone over, quiet their anxieties, and restore a sense of emotional security.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Power of Substantive Speech: In Hebrew thought, the noun form of this root (dabar) means both "word" and "thing/matter." Thus, to speak (dabar) is to put a tangible reality into motion. Boaz’s speech is not empty talk; it carries the weight of legal protection and physical provision.

• Relational Bridge-Building: The Piel stem of dabar frequently portrays a superior condescending to speak with an inferior, or a redeemer speaking to the needy in a way that establishes a covenant-like bond.

• The Heart as the Target of Grace: By speaking "to the heart," the speaker bypasses formal, social, or racial barriers to speak directly to the core of the listener’s personhood.

### 4. Theological Implications

• God as the Speaking Redeemer: The ultimate pattern of comforting speech comes from Yahweh, who repeatedly speaks to the heart of His distressed covenant people. In the narrative of Ruth, Boaz operates as the human agent of God’s protective wings. His kind speech is the physical mediation of divine chesed (covenant faithfulness) to a destitute foreigner.

• Redemptive Communication: Scripture shows that speech is a primary instrument of redemption. Redemptive speech does not merely pass on information; it actively lifts up the marginalized, reassures the frightened, and imparts value to the brokenhearted.

• A Shadow of Christ: Boaz’s relational, heart-targeted speech to an undeserving Moabite widow prefigures the Lord Jesus Christ, who speaks peace, comfort, and restorative grace to those who are weary and heavy-laden.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:13, Ruth has just received unexpected and extraordinary grace from Boaz. He has protected her from harassment, provided her with water, and invited her to glean safely in his fields. Ruth responds by saying, "for you have comforted me, and have spoken kindly [literally, spoken to the heart] to your maidservant."

The contextual contrast is vital. Ruth is a Moabitess—an outsider from an enemy nation who has no legal claim to charity or protection. She even highlights this, noting, "though I am not like one of your maidservants." Socially, she is at the absolute bottom of the hierarchy. Boaz’s decision to speak directly and kindly to her heart completely reverses her status. His words do not merely tolerate her presence; they welcome, validate, and protect her, replacing her isolation with covenant belonging.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Isaiah 40:2 - "Speak comfort to Jerusalem, and cry out to her, That her warfare is ended, That her iniquity is pardoned; For she has received from the Lord’s hand Double for all her sins."

• Hosea 2:14 - "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, Will bring her into the wilderness, And speak comfort to her."

• Genesis 34:3 - "His soul was strongly attracted to Dinah the daughter of Jacob, and he loved the young woman and spoke kindly to the young woman."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The distinction between dabar (focusing on the act and relationship of speaking) and amar (focusing on the content of the statement) is highly useful for exposition. Boaz is not merely stating a set of rules; he is actively engaging Ruth in a life-changing dialogue.

• The literal construction "to speak on the heart" is consistently used in scripture during moments of transition from estrangement to reconciliation, or from deep sorrow to profound comfort. It is the language of restoration.
