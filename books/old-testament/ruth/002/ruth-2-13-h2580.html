FAVOR - ( chen , Strong's H2580 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun chen refers to favor, grace, acceptance, or charm. It denotes the unmerited goodwill and kindness shown by a superior to an inferior, or the qualities in a person that make them pleasing to others. In the narrative of Ruth, it highlights the transition from a vulnerable foreigner to a protected, accepted member of the community through the active, benevolent grace of Boaz, reflecting the grander theme of divine grace operating through human instruments.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root verb is chanan, which carries the meaning of showing favor, being gracious, or bending and stooping in kindness to an inferior.

• The concrete imagery associated with this root is that of a superior—such as a king, a master, or a wealthy landowner—bending down in kindness to raise up someone of low status, restoring their dignity and providing for their needs.

• In the ancient Near Eastern context, a destitute foreign widow like Ruth possessed no legal claim to protection, land, or sustenance. To survive, she was entirely dependent on the voluntary goodness of another. Seeking chen was an appeal to the sovereign, uncoerced generosity of a benefactor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Chen signifies favor, grace, elegance, or charm.

• It refers to a favorable disposition of one person toward another, which manifests in kind and generous treatment.

• The term carries the sense of finding acceptance or being well-pleasing in the sight of someone who has the power to bless or to curse.

• Unlike wages or legal obligations, chen is freely bestowed. It cannot be earned, demanded, or bartered for; it is a gift arising from the character of the giver.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Finding favor "in the eyes of" (be'ene) is the standard idiomatic expression in Hebrew for securing another person's goodwill.

• The physical metaphor of "eyes" suggests that the superior looks upon the subject with pleasure, warmth, and acceptance rather than anger, indifference, or judgment.

• The word is used to describe interpersonal relationships where a vulnerable party seeks protection or reconciliation, such as Jacob seeking favor from Esau, or Joseph finding favor in the eyes of Potiphar.

• It also describes humanity's relationship with God, representing the divine mercy that spares and sustains human lives despite their weakness and failure.

4. Theological Implications

• Chen is the primary Old Testament foundation for the New Testament concept of grace (charis).

• It illustrates how God acts toward helpless and undeserving humanity. Divine favor is not based on human merit or heritage, but on God's sovereign choice to bend down and show mercy.

• The interaction between Ruth and Boaz serves as a powerful historical type of the sinner's encounter with Jesus Christ, our Kinsman-Redeemer. Like Ruth, humanity is spiritually destitute, foreign, and without claim to the inheritance. Yet, through Christ, believers find ultimate favor in the eyes of the Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:13, Ruth responds to Boaz's overwhelming generosity. Boaz has not only permitted her to glean in his fields, but has commanded his young men to protect her, invited her to drink from his water jars, and ordered extra grain to be purposely dropped for her.

• Ruth's petition, "Let me find favor in your sight, my lord," is an expression of deep gratitude and a prayer for the continuation of his protective custody.

• A sharp contrast is drawn when Ruth notes, "though I am not like one of your maidservants." She recognizes her low status as a Moabitess—an outsider who does not even belong to the household staff. This contrast heightens the unmerited nature of the favor she receives. Boaz's grace bridges the gap between her foreign poverty and his Israelite abundance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:8 - "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the Lord."

• Genesis 39:4 - "So Joseph found favor in his sight, and served him. Then he made him overseer of his house, and all that he had he put under his authority."

• Exodus 33:17 - "So the Lord said to Moses, 'I will also do this thing that you have spoken; for you have found grace in My sight, and I know you by name.'"

• Ruth 2:10 - "So she fell on her face, bowed down to the ground, and said to him, 'Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?'"

• Proverbs 3:4 - "And so find favor and high esteem In the sight of God and man."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The repetition of chen in Ruth chapter 2 structures the development of the narrative. Ruth begins her day hoping she might find favor with someone (v. 2), marvels when she actually finds it with Boaz (v. 10), and prays for its continuation (v. 13).

• This favor transforms Ruth's social standing. She begins as a "foreigner" (v. 10), is elevated to the level of a "maidservant" (v. 13), and is ultimately brought into the family covenant as Boaz's wife (ch. 4), demonstrating how grace restores the outcast to a place of belonging.
