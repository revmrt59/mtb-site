ONE - ( 'echad , Strong's H0259 )

Summary:

In the narrative of Ruth, the word 'echad (translated as "one") serves as a powerful literary marker of the Moabitess's profound humility and her perception of her low social standing. By stating that she is "not like one" of Boaz's maidservants, Ruth uses this basic numerical term to express her absolute exclusion from the protections, privileges, and belonging enjoyed by even the lowest member of Boaz’s household. The word highlights the vast social and spiritual chasm that Boaz’s extraordinary grace bridges, transforming her from a solitary foreign outsider into one who is ultimately brought into the core covenant lineage of Israel.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'echad is primarily the cardinal number "one." Rooted in the concept of gathering, assembling, or uniting together into a single whole, it conveys more than mere mathematical count. In the ancient Near Eastern mindset, numbers carried qualitative weight. "One" represented unity, uniqueness, singularity, and the baseline of community or identity. To be counted as "one" of a group meant sharing in the corporate identity, protections, and status of that group. To be less than "one" or excluded from this count meant total isolation, vulnerability, and insignificance.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term 'echad functions as an adjective, a noun, or an indefinite pronoun. It has several distinct facets of meaning:

• Singularity: A single, individual person, object, or unit.

• Unity: A collective oneness formed by different parts, such as a husband and wife becoming "one flesh" or a group of people acting as "one man."

• Uniqueness: That which is solitary, incomparable, or unique.

• Indefinite Designation: "A certain" or "any single one" within a larger group.

• Ordinal Value: The "first" in a series (e.g., "day one" or the "first day").

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, 'echad describes various forms of oneness:

• The Marriage Union: In Genesis, the union of man and woman establishes a new, singular relational unit ("one flesh"), illustrating how separate entities combine to form a profound, indivisible whole.

• National and Spiritual Solidarity: When the people of Israel act with absolute consensus, they gather "as one man," portraying perfect corporate harmony and shared purpose.

• Individual Selection: The word frequently designates a single representative chosen out of many, such as "one man from each tribe."

• Total Exclusion (Negative Contexts): When paired with a negative particle, it denotes "not even one," emphasizing complete destruction, absence, or absolute lack (e.g., "not one of them remained").

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of 'echad is anchored in the Shema, where it declares the unique, unified, and incomparable nature of God: "The LORD our God, the LORD is one." This foundational truth establishes that God is a unified whole, consistent in character, and the exclusive object of Israel's worship.

Furthermore, 'echad reflects God’s covenant purpose to gather isolated individuals into a unified family. In the metanarrative of redemption, God takes those who are scattered, alienated, and excluded and binds them together into a singular, holy assembly. Ruth's journey from being "not like one of the maidservants" to becoming a key matriarch in Israel's royal line beautifully illustrates this theology of grace, where the outsider is brought in to become "one" with the covenant people of God.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:13, Ruth responds to Boaz's unexpected and overwhelming kindness with deep gratitude: "though I am not like one of your maidservants." Here, Ruth uses 'echad to construct a sharp contrast between herself and Boaz's domestic workers.

Under the societal structures of ancient Israel, Boaz's maidservants (shiphchot) occupied a recognized, protected position within the household. They belonged to the community, had a right to provision, and enjoyed the safety of Boaz's fields. Ruth, as a Moabitess and a destitute widow, was a complete outsider (nokriyah) with no legal or social status. By stating she is "not like one" ('echad) of his maidservants, she recognizes that she does not even qualify for the lowest tier of household belonging. She is lower than the lowest.

This use of 'echad serves to magnify the grace of Boaz. His kindness does not merely treat her as a temporary laborer; it far exceeds her self-perception of being less than "one" of his servants, ultimately paving the way for her full integration into his family through redemption.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 6:4: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!"

• Joshua 23:10: "One man of you shall chase a thousand, for the LORD your God is He who fights for you, as He promised you."

• Ecclesiastes 4:9: "Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their labor."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful distinction exists between 'echad (compound unity or "one" among others) and yachid (solitary, absolute, or only-begotten uniqueness). While yachid emphasizes absolute isolation or exclusive singularity (such as an only child), 'echad frequently allows for a plurality within the oneness (as in a cluster of grapes, a single bundle of many parts, or the unified Godhead).

In Ruth’s declaration, her use of 'echad underscores her longing for community. She does not merely lament being alone; she laments that she is not even "one" of the community of servants who live under the shadow of Boaz's protection. His subsequent actions answer this longing by bringing her into a covenantal, familial oneness that ultimately blesses the entire world through her descendant, Jesus Christ.
