NOT - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

In Ruth 2:13, the absolute negative particle lo' ("not") underscores Ruth's profound humility and acute self-awareness of her status as an outsider. By declaring that she does not even possess the status of Boaz's lowest female servants, Ruth highlights the extraordinary, unmerited nature of the grace and comfort extended to her. This small but powerful word serves as a literary and theological foil, sharpening the contrast between Ruth's perceived unworthiness and the abundant, redeeming covenant-faithfulness of God mirrored in the kindness of Boaz.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew particle lo' is the primary and most frequent adverb of negation in the Old Testament. Unlike subjective or temporary negatives, lo' represents an objective, unconditional, and permanent negation. In the cultural context of the ancient Near East, social hierarchies were rigid and clearly defined. As a Moabite widow, Ruth occupied the lowest possible social tier. By utilizing this absolute negative, Ruth draws a definitive line between herself and those who have a legitimate, legal, or social claim to Boaz's protection. The word picture is one of an impassable social chasm; she is wholly outside the household of privilege, possessing no inherent right to the kindness she is receiving.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The negative particle lo' expresses absolute negation, meaning "no" or "not."

• It function as an adverb of negation modifying verbs, nouns, adjectives, or entire clauses.

• It denies the reality, presence, or possibility of the statement it modifies.

• In contrast to the subjective negative particle 'al (which expresses a wish, deprecation, or temporary prohibition, such as "let not"), lo' establishes an objective fact or an absolute, permanent command.

• In Ruth 2:13, it modifies the verb 'ehyeh ("I am/shall be"), creating an absolute denial of equality: "I am completely not like one of your maidservants."

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Scriptures, lo' is used to draw sharp, uncompromising boundaries.

• In legal contexts, it is the word used in the Ten Commandments ("You shall not...") to establish absolute moral boundaries.

• In narrative contexts, it is used to contrast human limitations with divine realities, highlighting what man cannot do versus what God can do.

• The word picture associated with lo' is a locked gate, a closed road, or an empty vessel. It denotes a complete absence or a total lack of the quality being discussed. When Ruth uses it, she is describing her social capital in Boaz's household as a total void.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, lo' is indispensable for understanding the doctrine of grace. Grace, by definition, is unmerited favor shown to those who have no claim to it. If Ruth were comparable to Boaz's maidservants, his kindness could be viewed as a fair wage, a social obligation, or a standard duty. Because she is not (lo') like them, his protection and generosity are revealed as pure, unadulterated grace (chesed).

• This mirrors the believer's spiritual position before a holy God. Humanity is not righteous, not clean, and not deserving of covenant mercy.

• The recognition of our spiritual "not" is the prerequisite for receiving the abundance of God's "is."

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:13, Ruth says to Boaz, "for you have comforted me, and have spoken kindly to your maidservant, though I am not like one of your maidservants."

• The Hebrew text places the pronoun 'anoki ("I") next to lo' 'ehyeh ("I shall not be"), emphasizing her personal status.

• The word for maidservant here is shipchah, which refers to the lowest class of female servant or slave.

• The nuance is remarkably intense: Ruth is not merely saying she is not Boaz's equal; she is stating that she does not even measure up to the lowest, most vulnerable servant-girls in his employ. The servant-girls at least belong to the community of Israel and have a recognized place of safety. Ruth, as a Moabitess, has no such standing. Her use of lo' exposes her extreme vulnerability and magnifies Boaz's character as a redeemer who reaches past social barriers to rescue the outsider.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The position of lo' in the syntax of Ruth 2:13 highlights the rhetorical movement of the passage. Ruth transitions from acknowledging Boaz’s comfort to confessing her own lowliness. This confession of "not" being worthy sets up the great redemptive reversal of the book. In the outer courts of the field, she is not even a servant; yet by the end of the narrative, she is elevated to the position of Boaz’s beloved wife. God delights in taking those who are not and making them part of His family.
