KINDLY - ( leb , Strong's H3820 )

Summary:

In Ruth 2:13, the English word "kindly" translates the profound Hebrew idiom al-leb (literally, "upon the heart" or "to the heart"), paired with the verb dabar ("to speak"). In ancient Hebrew thought, the heart (leb) is not merely the seat of emotions, but the central engine of the intellect, will, decisions, and moral character. To "speak to the heart" is to communicate in a way that penetrates an individual's deepest vulnerabilities, bypassing intellectual barriers to bring restorative comfort, dignity, and peace. Through this idiom, the text portrays Boaz's speech to Ruth not as mere polite conversation, but as a deliberate act of emotional and social restoration that reassures a vulnerable, grieving foreigner of her safety and value.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun leb (and its variation lebab) represents the physical and metaphorical core of human existence. Unlike Western thought, which divides the mind (reason) from the heart (emotion), the ancient Hebrew worldview saw leb as the unified center of the inner man. It is where a person thinks, feels, makes decisions, experiences fear or courage, and interacts with God.

The word picture behind "speaking to the heart" (dabar al-leb) is that of pouring soothing oil or speaking words that act as a healing balm directly onto a wounded or anxious soul. It implies a face-to-face, intimate, and intentional form of communication meant to dispel fear, heal a breach, or woo someone into a relationship of trust.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun leb refers to the inner man, mind, will, heart, understanding, and seat of emotions. When paired with the preposition al ("upon/to") and the verb dabar ("to speak"), the expression functions as a specific Hebrew idiom meaning "to speak comfortingly," "to speak tenderly," "to reassure," or "to speak kindly."

This idiomatic construct is used when there is a power or emotional disparity between two parties—such as a ruler and a subject, a master and a servant, or an offended party and an offender. The speaker condescends to speak reassuring words that remove distress, guilt, or anxiety from the hearer's inner being.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The idiom of speaking to the heart occurs in pivotal Old Testament narratives to describe deep reconciliation, comfort, and restoration:

• The Reconciliation of the Offended: In Genesis 34:3, after Dinah is violated, Shechem's soul clings to her, and he "spoke kindly" (to the heart of) the young woman in an attempt to win her trust.

• The Reassurance of the Guilty: In Genesis 50:21, Joseph's brothers fear retribution after Jacob’s death. Joseph removes their terror by promising to provide for them, "comforting them" and "speaking kindly" (to their hearts).

• The Wooing of the Wayward: In Hosea 2:14, God describes His plan to discipline Israel and then restore her, promising to allure her into the wilderness and "speak comfort" (to her heart) to win back her devotion.

• Sovereign Proclamation of Grace: In Isaiah 40:2, Yahweh commands His prophets to "speak comfort" (to the heart of) Jerusalem, declaring that her hard service is completed and her sin is pardoned.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of this idiom lies in its depiction of grace. When a superior speaks "to the heart" of an inferior or an outcast, it demonstrates the relational character of God. God does not merely govern from a distance; He speaks directly to the deep emotional and spiritual needs of human beings.

Boaz, acting as a historical type of the ultimate Redeemer, mirrors God's heart. By speaking kindly to Ruth, Boaz shows that redemption is not merely a legal or financial transaction. It is deeply personal, relational, and emotional. It restores the dignity of the marginalized and welcomes them into covenant fellowship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:13, Ruth has arrived in Bethlehem as an impoverished Moabitess widow. She is highly vulnerable to exploitation, abuse, and starvation. When Boaz speaks to her, he instructs his young men not to touch her, invites her to drink from his water jars, and praises her loyalty to Naomi.

Ruth’s response in verse 13 highlights the profound impact of his words: "for you have comforted me, and have spoken kindly to your maidservant." Ruth recognizes that Boaz has addressed her deepest fears of rejection and danger. Even though she is not even equal to one of his hired servant girls, Boaz's "heart-speech" elevates her status, calms her anxieties, and assures her that she has found favor under the wings of the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 34:3 - "His soul clung to Dinah the daughter of Jacob, and he loved the young woman and spoke kindly to the young woman."

• Genesis 50:21 - "Now therefore, do not be afraid; I will provide for you and your little ones.” And he comforted them and spoke kindly to them."

• Isaiah 40:2 - "Speak comfort to Jerusalem, and cry out to her, that her warfare is ended, that her iniquity is pardoned; for she has received from the LORD’s hand double for all her sins."

• Hosea 2:14 - "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, will bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfort to her."

7. Other Relevant Insights

For teachers and pastors, this word study offers a profound model for ministry and pastoral care. Speaking "kindly" in the biblical sense is not about empty flattery, superficial pleasantries, or politeness. It requires:

• Empathy: Understanding the specific fears, grief, and anxieties of the listener.

• Intentionality: Choosing words that deliberately address and disarm those fears.

• Action-backed Speech: Boaz’s words of comfort were validated by his immediate actions—providing food, safety, and water. True "heart-speech" in pastoral ministry must be accompanied by practical love and protection.
