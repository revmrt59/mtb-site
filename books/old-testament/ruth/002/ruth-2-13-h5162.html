Summary

COMFORTED - ( nacham , Strong’s [5162] )

Summary:

To comfort, console, or cause to breathe easily. In Ruth 2:13, it describes the profound emotional and social relief Ruth experiences through Boaz’s words, which speak directly to her vulnerability and elevate her from the status of an outsider to an accepted recipient of favor.

Full Study:

  1. Hebrew Roots / Derivation

    • Derived from the primitive root verb nacham, which originally carried the physical connotation of breathing deeply or sighing with relief.

    • In its intensive forms, the meaning shifted from a physical sigh to the emotional act of comforting, consoling, or showing deep compassion to someone in distress.

    • The root beautifully illustrates a transformation from an internal state of heavy, constricted grief to a state of open, breathing relief.

  2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

    • The primary range of meaning encompasses comforting, consoling, showing compassion, or easing another person's sorrow.

    • It represents a deliberate action designed to alleviate the emotional weight of grief, bereavement, or social isolation.

    • The definitive meaning in Ruth 2:13 is the bestowal of emotional reassurance and relational peace.

    • By declaring that Boaz has comforted her, Ruth is acknowledging that his kind words have broken the heavy silence of her widowhood and vulnerability, transforming her fear into emotional rest.

  3. Usage in Ancient Near Eastern & Jewish Culture & Word Pictures

    • The Deep Breath of Relief: In ancient pastoral contexts, the word captures the picture of a person carrying a crushing physical load who finally drops the burden and draws a deep, unhindered breath.

    • The Consolation of the Bereaved: In Jewish culture, comforting was a mandatory communal duty performed for widows, orphans, and those who had suffered a catastrophic loss of family protection or economic standing.

    • Mental Image: A grieving mourner sitting in the dust of isolation vs. being gently raised up and spoken to kindly by the lord of the estate. Human destitution isolates a person, leaving them to carry the heavy, shallow breathing of anxiety alone. Divine comfort, flowing through human instruments, steps into that isolation, lifts the head, and allows the soul to breathe freely in an atmosphere of total acceptance and safety.

  4. Theological Implications

    • True comfort is not merely sentimental sympathy; it is a powerful covenantal act where God steps into human tragedy to reverse the consequences of brokenness and loss.

    • It connects structurally to the grand biblical theme of redemption, where God promises to comfort His people by changing their status from exiled outcasts to restored heirs.

    • This word reveals that God’s comfort operates directly through human obedience and generosity, transforming an ordinary field into a sanctuary of divine consolation.

  5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

    • In Ruth 2:13, Ruth responds to Boaz's overwhelming kindness by stating that he has comforted her and spoken kindly to her heart.

    • The exact order of events in the text is vital: Ruth had just received a remarkable blessing of protection and reward, yet she immediately notes that she is not even equal to one of his hired maidservants.

    • This context narrows the meaning: Boaz's comfort does not follow a change in Ruth's performance or economic wealth; it enters while she is still fully aware of her low status as a foreign outsider. His words comfort her precisely because they treat her with the dignity of a family member before she has done anything to earn it.

  6. Pastoral Commentary & Homiletical Insights

    • Evangelical commentators frequently note that true pastoral comfort must speak directly to the "heart"—a Hebrew idiom meaning to speak tenderly, reassuringly, and directly to a person's deepest anxieties.

    • The pastoral synthesis of this narrative highlights that the world's comfort is often superficial, but the comfort of the kingdom reaches down into a person's social and spiritual identity. By speaking to her heart, Boaz removes the sting of her foreign background and her widowhood.

    • Homiletically, the text exposes the reality that a broken soul cannot comfort itself. Comfort must be spoken into us from an outside authority who possesses the power, resources, and willingness to protect us.

  8. Other Relevant Insights

    • The word acts as a functional bridge between positional acceptance and practical emotional healing: being comforted by the words of the redeemer (doctrine) provides Ruth with the emotional stability to continue her daily labors with joy and confidence (conduct).

    • For homiletical structure, Ruth's response to Boaz outlines a beautiful pastoral framework that addresses the restoration of a wounded heart:

      • The Favor is acknowledged: "Let me find favor in your sight," addressing the anxiety of rejection.

      • The Comfort is received: "...because you have comforted me," addressing the anxiety of emotional grief.

      • The Kindness is internalized: "...and because you have spoken kindly to your maidservant," addressing the anxiety of low worth and social isolation.
