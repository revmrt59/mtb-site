COMFORTED - ( nacham , Strong's H5162 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nacham fundamentally means to breathe deeply, reflecting a physical sigh of relief, which translates into comforting, consoling, or showing compassion. In Ruth 2:13, it expresses the profound emotional and social relief Ruth experiences through Boaz's unexpected kindness. As a destitute foreign widow, Ruth finds her deep anxieties eased by Boaz's protective words and actions, demonstrating how God uses human instruments to mediate His divine comfort and covenant loyalty (chesed) to those in distress.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete imagery of the ancient Hebrew language connects nacham to the physical act of breathing deeply or sighing. It represents the physical release of tension—drawing a deep, heavy breath after a period of intense grief, sorrow, panic, or physical exhaustion. The word pictures a person whose chest has been constricted by distress, finally finding the freedom to breathe again. In the ancient world, comfort was not an abstract, sentimental feeling; it was a visible, tangible change in state from misery and confinement to relief, safety, and rest.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its primary verbal forms, nacham carries two semantic developments depending on the conjugation: to comfort/console (Piel) and to regret/repent/relent (Niphal). In the Piel stem, which is used in Ruth 2:13, the verb is highly intensive and active. It means to console, to comfort, or to actively ease someone's sorrow. This involves speaking reassuring words and taking decisive action to alleviate the suffering of another. It represents the intentional lifting of grief or anxiety from a person who has suffered severe loss.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• nacham is widely used throughout the Old Testament to describe both human and divine acts of consolation:

• Shepherd's Care: The term is used in the context of protection and guidance, such as the shepherd's rod and staff providing a sense of security and relief to the sheep in dangerous terrain.

• Maternal Tenderness: The verb describes a mother tenderly holding and soothing a crying child, wrapping them in complete warmth and safety to quiet their fears.

• Covenant Restoration: It frequently appears in prophetic literature to describe God's promised restoration of Israel, where He promises to comfort His people by reversing their exile and rebuilding their ruined cities.

4. Theological Implications

Comfort is a primary attribute of God's character and a central theme of covenant relationship. God is the ultimate source of nacham, and when human beings comfort one another in a godly manner, they function as instruments of His grace. True biblical comfort is never merely verbal; it is historically linked to redemption, deliverance, and the manifestation of covenant love (chesed). To receive nacham is to experience the reversal of tragedy through active, sovereign intervention.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:13, Ruth has arrived in Bethlehem as an impoverished, marginalized Moabite widow. She faces the constant threat of starvation, social exclusion, and physical abuse in the fields. When Boaz speaks protectively to her, ensures her safety, and provides for her physical needs, Ruth responds, "you have comforted me."

The context tightly links "comforted" with the parallel phrase, "spoken kindly to your maidservant" (literally, "spoken to the heart"). Boaz's nacham is not a polite platitude; it is a life-altering intervention. His words of affirmation and acts of protection physically relieve the crushing weight of survival anxiety from Ruth's chest, allowing her to breathe freely in a foreign land.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 37:35 - "And all his sons and all his daughters arose to comfort him; but he refused to be comforted; and he said, 'For I shall go down into the grave to my son in mourning.' Thus his father wept for him."

• Psalm 23:4 - "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; for You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me."

• Isaiah 12:1 - "And in that day you will say: 'O LORD, I will praise You; Though You were angry with me, Your anger is turned away, and You comfort me.'"

• Isaiah 40:1 - "Comfort, yes, comfort My people!' Says your God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a close relationship between the verb nacham and the proper name Noah (Noach). In Genesis 5:29, Lamech names his son Noah, stating, "This one will comfort us (nacham) concerning our work and the toil of our hands."

Furthermore, in the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), nacham in Ruth 2:13 is translated using the Greek verb parakaleo. This is the exact root from which the New Testament derives the word Parakletos—the Helper, Advocate, or Comforter (the Holy Spirit). Boaz's active, protective comfort toward Ruth serves as a beautiful Old Testament foreshadowing of the Holy Spirit's comforting ministry to believers and Christ’s redemptive care for His bride.
