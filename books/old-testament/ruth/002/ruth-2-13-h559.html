SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar translates as "said" in Ruth 2:13, capturing Ruth’s humble, reverent, and deeply grateful response to the unexpected grace shown to her by Boaz. As the primary Old Testament term for verbalizing thought, 'amar is not merely the mechanics of speech; it represents the outward expression of the inner heart. In this passage, it marks a pivotal moment where Ruth consciously and verbally embraces her status as a recipient of life-altering kindness, illustrating how faith and gratitude find outward expression in response to grace.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 5,000 times. It depicts the action of putting thoughts into words, establishing communication, and bringing what is internal into the external realm. In ancient Near Eastern culture, speech was viewed as an extension of a person's being and authority. When a person spoke, they set something in motion. Unlike dabar (which emphasizes the substance or log of the speech), 'amar focuses on the personal act of saying, pointing directly to the relationship between the speaker and the listener. It reveals the speaker's posture, intent, and character.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'amar is to say, speak, utter, declare, or promise. It encompasses several nuances depending on the context:

• To verbalize an internal state: Moving a thought from the mind to the mouth.

• To intend or think: Frequently rendered as "said in his heart," meaning to think or purpose.

• To command or declare: When spoken by an authority figure or king, 'amar carries the weight of a decree or law.

• To name or call: Designating a specific status or identity to someone or something.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Creative Voice: The first use of 'amar in Scripture occurs in Genesis 1:3: "Then God said, 'Let there be light.'" Here, the word picture is one of sovereign, creative power. God's speech is not passive; it creates reality.

• The Expression of the Soul: When used of human beings, 'amar is the bridge between the hidden thoughts of the heart and the visible community. What a person "says" reveals their moral and spiritual alignment.

• The Response of Faith: In covenantal dialogues, 'amar is the word of acceptance. When God offers a covenant, the people of Israel respond by saying they will obey. It is the verbal seal of a relationship.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'amar underscores a God who speaks and humans who are designed to listen and respond. God's 'amar brings life, law, and promise. When human beings speak in response to God's initiative, their words carry moral weight. In the context of redemption, our verbal response to grace is vital. Ruth's speech represents the appropriate response of a destitute sinner to the rich grace of a redeemer. She does not remain silent, nor does she demand rights; she speaks words of gratitude, recognizing her unworthiness while resting in his favor.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:13, Ruth has just received Boaz's extraordinary protection, provision, and blessing. Boaz has spoken words of comfort to her, treating her not as a foreign outcast but as a sister in Israel under the wings of Yahweh. Ruth's response is introduced with "Then she said ('amar)."

• Her speech is an act of worshipful reception. She acknowledges her lowliness ("though I am not like one of your maidservants") yet receives his comfort.

• The nuance of 'amar here is one of self-surrender and gratitude. She does not argue with his generosity, nor does her pride prevent her from receiving it. Her verbal response seals the relationship of grace that Boaz has initiated.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3 - "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Genesis 22:11 - "But the Angel of the Lord called to him from heaven and said, 'Abraham, Abraham!' So he said, 'Here I am.'"

• Ruth 2:2 - "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field...'"

• Psalm 14:1 - "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast with Dabar: While dabar represents the objective word spoken (or the matter itself), 'amar represents the personal act of saying. In Ruth 2:13, Boaz has "spoken (dabar) kindly," and Ruth "said ('amar)" her response. This demonstrates a beautiful conversational alignment of grace given and grace received.

• A Model for Prayer: Ruth's speech to Boaz provides a template for prayer. She acknowledges her need, recognizes the kindness of the benefactor, and asks for continued favor.
