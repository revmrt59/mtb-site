MAIDSERVANTS - ( shiphchah , Strong's H8198 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shiphchah denotes a female servant of the lowest social tier, representing complete dependence, humility, and submission. In Ruth 2:13, Ruth uses this term to express her deep sense of unworthiness, acknowledging that as a Moabite foreigner, she does not even possess the standing of the lowest domestic servants in Boaz’s household. This self-deprecation highlights the magnitude of Boaz’s grace, illustrating how covenant kindness (chesed) reaches down to elevate those who recognize their own spiritual poverty and lack of merit.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near Eastern household, social hierarchies were rigid and clearly defined. The shiphchah sat at the very bottom of this domestic ladder. Unlike hired laborers who worked for wages and retained personal independence, a shiphchah was a female slave or maidservant who belonged to the household. She performed the most menial, grueling daily tasks—such as grinding grain, carrying water, and washing feet.

Despite her low status, she was also under the protection and care of the master of the house. In Israelite society, a shiphchah could sometimes be given as a concubine or a secondary wife to secure heirs (as with Hagar, Bilhah, and Zilpah), but her primary identity was one of absolute subordination. She owned nothing, had no social leverage, and relied entirely on the mercy and provision of her master.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term shiphchah refers directly to a maidservant, female slave, or handmaid. It stands in contrast to the free woman (geberet, mistress) and represents a state of legal and social servitude.

The semantic range of shiphchah encompasses:

• A maidservant belonging to a mistress (acting as personal staff or helper).

• A female slave regarded as household property.

• A term of polite, humble self-address used by a woman speaking to a man of higher social, political, or spiritual standing.

Unlike the Hebrew word amah (another term translated as "maidservant" or "bondwoman"), which often carries legal connotations of potential marriageability or legal rights within the family, shiphchah emphasizes the raw reality of menial labor, low class, and foreign or subordinate status.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, shiphchah is used to paint a vivid picture of humility, ownership, and social distinction:

• Property and Wealth: When the scriptures catalog the wealth of the patriarchs, shiphchot (plural) are listed alongside male servants, oxen, donkeys, and camels (Genesis 12:16, 24:35). They were considered assets of a wealthy estate.

• Domestic Subordination: The word describes Hagar in relation to Sarai (Genesis 16:1). Hagar was the personal property of Sarai, illustrating the complete authority a mistress held over her shiphchah.

• Humility in Address: When Abigail seeks to appease David's anger, she repeatedly refers to herself as his shiphchah, even offering to wash the feet of David’s servants (1 Samuel 25:41). This usage demonstrates how the word was employed in speech to show voluntary submission and deep respect.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shiphchah serves as a powerful metaphor for human helplessness and the beauty of divine grace.

• The Posture of Grace: God consistently aligns Himself with the lowly and the outcast. Ruth’s self-identification as a shiphchah reflects the biblical truth that God resists the proud but gives grace to the humble. To receive the blessings of the covenant, one must first recognize their spiritual bankruptcy.

• The Picture of Redemption: Ruth’s journey from a foreign outsider to a shiphchah (Ruth 2:13), then to an amah (Ruth 3:9), and ultimately to a wife (ishah, Ruth 4:13) mirrors the spiritual journey of the believer. We begin as alienated foreigners, are welcomed into service by grace, and are ultimately brought into a covenant relationship of love and inheritance.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:13, Ruth responds to Boaz's overwhelming kindness: "Then she said, 'Let me find favor in your sight, my lord; for you have comforted me, and have spoken kindly to your maidservant, though I am not like one of your maidservants.'"

The nuance here is critical. Ruth does not merely call herself Boaz's maidservant; she states that she is not even equal to one of his maidservants. The maidservants of Boaz's household were Israelites who belonged to the community of the covenant. They had families, legal rights, and a recognized place in Judah. Ruth, by contrast, was a Moabitess—an outsider from a cursed nation, destitute, and widowed.

By declaring "though I am not like one of your maidservants," Ruth acknowledges her lack of any natural claim on Boaz's protection. She recognizes that she has no right to glean in his fields, let alone receive his comfort and favor. This confession magnifies Boaz’s grace. It transforms his acts of protection and provision from standard societal duties into a stunning display of unmerited favor (chesed).

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 16:1: "Now Sarai, Abram’s wife, had borne him no children. And she had an Egyptian maidservant whose name was Hagar."

• Genesis 29:24: "And Laban gave his maidservant Zilpah to his daughter Leah as a maidservant."

• 1 Samuel 25:41: "Then she arose, bowed her face to the earth, and said, 'Here is your maidservant, a servant to wash the feet of the servants of my lord.'"

• 2 Samuel 14:6: "And your maidservant had two sons, and the two fought with each other in the field, and there was no one to part them, but the one struck the other and killed him."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A subtle linguistic shift occurs between chapter 2 and chapter 3 of Ruth. In Ruth 2:13, Ruth uses shiphchah to emphasize her low, foreign status, unfit even for basic domestic service. However, in Ruth 3:9, when she lies at Boaz's feet on the threshing floor and asks him to spread his wings over her as her kinsman-redeemer, she says, "I am Ruth, your handmaid (amah)."

By switching to amah, Ruth uses the term that carries the potential for marriage and legal protection. This intentional shift in vocabulary marks her transition from a desperate foreigner pleading for crumbs to a bold petitioner seeking redemption, security, and a place in the lineage of Israel.
