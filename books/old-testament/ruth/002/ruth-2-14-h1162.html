BOAZ - ( Bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

Boaz is a proper noun meaning "in him is strength" or "alacrity." In the Old Testament, particularly in the Book of Ruth, Boaz is a wealthy Bethlehemite landowner of the tribe of Judah who acts as the kinsman-redeemer (go'el) for Ruth and Naomi. His character represents the ideal of covenant faithfulness (hesed), righteousness, and strength, serving as a powerful type of Jesus Christ, who redeems believers into His family.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Boaz emerges during the dark and chaotic era of the Judges, a time characterized by spiritual decay, moral compromise, and social instability. Against this bleak backdrop, Boaz stands as a beacon of godliness, order, and covenant integrity.

The name is a compound of the preposition be ("in") and the noun oz ("strength"), yielding the meaning "in him is strength." It denotes a person of substance, moral fortitude, and capability. Historically, this strength is not merely physical or financial, but deeply spiritual. Boaz uses his societal power and wealth not to exploit the vulnerable, but to protect, provide for, and elevate them.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Boaz is a proper noun referring to:

• The wealthy Bethlehemite of the family of Elimelech who marries Ruth and becomes the great-grandfather of King David.

• One of the two massive bronze pillars erected by Solomon in the vestibule of the temple (the other being Jachin), symbolizing that Yahweh is the strength and stabilizer of His sanctuary and covenant people.

The lexical root emphasizes strength, power, and swiftness to act. Rather than passive nobility, the name implies active, capable strength that accomplishes redemptive purposes.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name Boaz appears 24 times in the Old Testament, primarily in the Book of Ruth, but also in the genealogical records of 1 Chronicles and the Gospel genealogies of Matthew and Luke.

The primary word picture associated with Boaz is the kinsman-redeemer (go'el). In ancient Israelite law, a kinsman-redeemer was a close relative who had the right and responsibility to:

• Redeem land that a poor relative had been forced to sell.

• Redeem a relative who had sold himself into slavery.

• Carry on the family line of a deceased relative through levirate marriage.

Boaz perfectly embodies this redemptive figure. He is a "mighty man of valor" (gibbor chayil) who possesses both the financial means and the moral resolve to execute these duties. He acts as a protective shield (a "wing" or kanaph) under which the desperate and marginalized can find refuge.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Boaz serves as one of the clearest Old Testament types of Jesus Christ. The parallels are profound:

• The Near Kinsman: Boaz had to be a relative to redeem Naomi's land and Ruth's lineage. Similarly, Jesus took on human flesh, becoming our "near kinsman" so that He might redeem us.

• The Worthy Redeemer: Boaz was a man of immense resources and moral standing, capable of paying the redemption price. Jesus, being sinless and divine, possesses the infinite worth required to pay our debt.

• The Willing Redeemer: Boaz was under no legal compulsion to marry Ruth; he acted out of free, sacrificial love. Jesus willingly laid down His life to redeem His bride, the Church.

• The Inclusive Grace: Boaz brought a foreign Moabite woman into the covenant community of Israel. Christ's redemption breaks down the dividing wall of hostility, bringing Gentiles into the household of God.

• The Messianic Line: Through his union with Ruth, Boaz fathers Obed, the grandfather of David, preserving the physical lineage through which the Messiah would eventually be born.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, the name Boaz carries the weight of authoritative grace and safety. At mealtime, Boaz invites Ruth—a foreign, destitute widow gleaning in his fields—to sit among his workers, eat his bread, and dip her food in the vinegar.

The presence and speech of Boaz redefine Ruth’s status. In a culture where foreign women were highly vulnerable to exploitation, the strength of Boaz ("in him is strength") becomes her fortress. He personally serves her parched grain, ensuring she is not only fed but "satisfied" with abundance left over. The context reveals that Boaz's strength is directed toward hospitality, dignity, and covenant keeping. He does not use his strength to dominate, but to serve and restore.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 2:1: "There was a relative of Naomi’s husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech. His name was Boaz."

• Ruth 4:13: "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."

• 1 Kings 7:21: "Then he set up the pillars by the vestibule of the temple; he set up the pillar on the right and called its name Jachin, and he set up the pillar on the left and called its name Boaz."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

Genealogical records indicate that Boaz was the son of Salmon and Rahab (Matthew 1:5). This background is pastorally significant: Boaz’s own mother was a Canaanite woman of Jericho who had been redeemed by faith into the covenant community of Israel. This family history likely softened Boaz’s heart toward Ruth, another foreign woman seeking refuge under the wings of the Lord God of Israel. His heritage of received grace empowered his active grace toward others.
