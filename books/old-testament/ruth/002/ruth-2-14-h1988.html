HERE - ( halom , Strong's H1988 )

Summary:

The Hebrew adverb halom indicates direction toward a specific, close-at-hand destination, often translated as "here" or "hither." In the Old Testament, it regularly appears in contexts of unexpected invitations, divine appointments, and transitions from exclusion to inclusion. In Ruth 2:14, Boaz’s invitation to Ruth to "come here" represents far more than a polite spatial instruction; it is a profound gesture of redemption, covenant hospitality, and grace that draws an impoverished foreign outsider into the intimate fellowship of the community.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The adverb halom is built from a demonstrative root indicating immediate proximity. Its primary function is to direct someone's attention or movement toward the speaker's exact location. In ancient Near Eastern culture, sharing a meal was a covenantal act of peace, protection, and fellowship. To invite someone halom ("here") to one's table, especially a marginalized foreigner, was a significant breach of social barriers, signaling safety, honor, and welcome. The term implies active movement from a place of distance to a place of close, relational presence.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• halom expresses direction toward a point where the speaker is located. It is translated as "hither," "here," or "to this place." It functions to establish a new position or status for the one being addressed, shifting them from a distance to close proximity. Unlike general words for "here" (such as poh), halom carries a directional dynamic, implying an active drawing near or pulling into a specific sphere of influence and protection.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Place of God's Presence: halom appears in key historical moments where God or His representative draws someone near for a specific, life-altering purpose. At the burning bush, God commands Moses, "Do not draw near this place (halom)" (Exodus 3:5), establishing holy boundaries while initiating a divine call.

• The Place of Unexpected Honor: The term is used by those astonished at their own elevation, such as David sitting before the Lord and asking, "Who am I... that You have brought me this far (halom)?" (2 Samuel 7:18). Here, halom represents an unexpected station of honor, royalty, and grace.

• The Place of Divine Provision: It occurs in contexts where weary wanderers find a sudden oasis of rest, highlighting that "here" is not just a geographic coordinate but a place of safety provided by God.

### 4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Hospitality and Inclusion: The movement implied by halom mirrors the heart of God in drawing the outcast and the stranger near. Under the Mosaic Law, gleaners were permitted to work the edges of the fields, but they remained socially distanced. By commanding Ruth to "come here," Boaz exemplifies the spirit of the law over its mere letter, acting as a human agent of God’s protective wings.

• Grace to the Outsider: Ruth was a Moabitess, a member of a nation excluded from the assembly of Israel. The invitation to step here (halom) represents the breaking down of ethnic and national barriers through grace, foreshadowing the inclusion of the Gentiles in the family of God.

• A Picture of Redemption: The transition from the hot, dusty perimeter of the field to the shaded, communal table of the master illustrates the believer's transition from alienation to sonship.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, Ruth is sitting at a distance, gathering grain under the heat of the day. Boaz commands her, "Come here (halom), and eat of the bread..." This invitation shifts Ruth from the position of a tolerated foreign gleaner to an honored guest at the master’s table. It marks the transition from self-preservation to providential care. Ruth does not push her way in; she is drawn in by the master's word. The simple command "come here" transforms her labor into fellowship and her isolation into community.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The word halom is intimately tied to unexpected encounters with God's sovereignty. Hagar marvels that she saw God "here" (halom)—in a desolate wilderness where she expected only death. Similarly, Ruth is in a metaphorical wilderness of grief, poverty, and foreignness. When Boaz says "come here," he becomes the instrument through whom God meets her in her desolate place. There is a beautiful parallel between halom in the mouth of Boaz and the invitation of Christ to "come to Me" (Matthew 11:28). Both invitations offer rest, bread, and a transition from distance to intimacy.
