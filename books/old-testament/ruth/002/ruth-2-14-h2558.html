VINEGAR - ( chomets , Strong's H2558 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chomets refers to vinegar or sour wine, a fermented liquid derived from wine or beer. In the ancient Near East, it served as a refreshing, acid-sharp condiment that stimulated salivation and quenched thirst, making it highly valued by laborers working under the intense heat of the harvest sun. In Ruth 2:14, Boaz's invitation to Ruth to dip her bread in this vinegar represents an act of extraordinary grace, hospitality, and inclusion. This gesture elevated her from a marginalized foreign gleaner to a welcomed guest at his table, foreshadowing the redeeming work of Christ who welcomes outsiders into His fellowship.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun chomets comes from the root verb chamats, which means to be sour, leavened, or chemically fermented.

• In the ancient world, vinegar was produced through the natural fermentation of grape juice, wine, or beer. Left open to the air, these liquids oxidized, creating a sharp, acidic substance.

• While undiluted vinegar was too harsh to drink, it was commonly mixed with water to create a cheap, refreshing beverage for Roman soldiers, field hands, and laborers.

• During the harvest season in the dry climate of Judea, workers used chomets as a dipping sauce. The acidity of the liquid helped moisten dry bread, made food easier to digest, stimulated saliva production, and provided immediate cooling relief from the scorching sun.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary definition of chomets is vinegar, sour wine, or fermented acid beverage.

• It signifies a liquid that has undergone acetic fermentation, losing its sweetness and acquiring a sharp, pungent flavor.

• The term represents a common, inexpensive commodity rather than a luxury beverage, associated with daily labor, simplicity, and physical refreshment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Chomets is used literally in the Old Testament as a restricted substance for those under special vows, as a daily provision for workers, and as a cheap beverage.

• It is used metaphorically to describe irritation, sharp pain, or incompatibility.

• In wisdom literature, it represents something that causes immediate discomfort. Just as vinegar sets the teeth on edge, a lazy messenger brings irritation to those who rely on him.

• It also depicts emotional or social friction, illustrating how singing happy songs to a grieving heart is as jarring and destructive as pouring acid on a base substance.

4. Theological Implications

• The grace of inclusion: Ruth was a Moabite widow, legally and socially excluded from the congregation of Israel. When Boaz offered her chomets, he extended chesed (covenant-keeping love and kindness). He treated a foreign scavenger with the dignity of a valued worker.

• The anticipation of redemption: Boaz acted as a kinsman-redeemer (goel). His invitation to the table shows how God provides for the vulnerable. The provision of bread and vinegar demonstrates that redemption satisfies physical hunger while restoring social and spiritual dignity.

• The messianic connection: The provision of refreshing chomets to a weary worker in Ruth stands in stark contrast to the vinegar offered to the suffering Messiah on the cross. While Ruth received vinegar as an invitation to fellowship and life, Christ received vinegar in His thirst as an expression of humanity's rejection, taking our bitterness so we could feast at His table.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:14, the setting is the heat of the noon meal during the barley harvest. Ruth sat "beside the reapers," a position she did not inherit or deserve.

• Boaz’s command to "dip your piece of bread in the vinegar" was a direct counter-cultural act. A wealthy landowner would not normally share his household's private, refreshing condiments with an impoverished foreigner.

• By sharing the chomets, Boaz signaled to his workers that Ruth was under his protection. The acid-wine transformed a dry, dusty crust of bread into a refreshing meal, demonstrating that under the wings of the God of Israel, even the bitterest circumstances of life are made sweet by grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 6:3 - "he shall separate himself from wine and similar drink; he shall drink neither vinegar of wine nor vinegar of similar drink, neither shall he drink any grape juice, nor eat fresh grapes or raisins."

• Psalm 69:21 - "They also gave me gall for my food, / And for my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink."

• Proverbs 10:26 - "As vinegar to the teeth and smoke to the eyes, / So is the lazy man to those who send him."

• Proverbs 25:20 - "Like one who takes away a garment in cold weather, / And like vinegar on soda, / Is one who sings songs to a heavy heart."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Ancient Near Eastern customs dictated that sharing a meal established a covenant of peace. By inviting Ruth to dip her bread in his vinegar, Boaz established an informal covenant of protection and peace with her, which later culminated in their marriage.

• For sermon preparation, the chomets serves as an excellent illustration of how God meets us in our dry, exhausted, and "sour" seasons. He does not wait for our circumstances to become sweet before He invites us to His table; He meets us in the heat of our labor and offers us His refreshing grace.
