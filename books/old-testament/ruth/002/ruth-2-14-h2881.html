DIP - ( tabal , Strong's H2881 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb tabal means to dip, plunge, or wet an object in liquid. In the context of Ruth 2:14, it refers to the physical action of dipping bread into a sour wine or vinegar mixture to soften and flavor it during a meal. This invitation from Boaz to Ruth is a profound gesture of grace, hospitality, and covenant-like protection extended to an impoverished foreign widow who had no legal claim to such kindness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root tabal conveys the action of putting an object into a liquid and immediately withdrawing it, or immersing it partially or fully. In ancient Near Eastern culture, sharing a meal was an act of covenant making, friendship, and mutual protection. Dipping bread into a common vessel of liquid (such as vinegar or oil) was a communal practice that signified intimate fellowship and shared life. In agricultural settings, the midday meal provided essential refreshment during exhausting labor under the hot sun.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb tabal primarily means to dip, dye, plunge, or wet. It is used sixteen times in the Hebrew Bible. The term does not imply permanent submersion but rather a deliberate, purposeful dipping of an object—such as a finger, a branch of hyssop, a piece of meat, or bread—into a liquid (blood, oil, water, honey, or vinegar). The focus of the word is the contact made between the object and the liquid for a specific ceremonial, practical, or dietary purpose.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, tabal serves several distinct purposes:

• Ritual Cleansing and Sanctification: In the Levitical sacrificial system, priests dipped (tabal) their fingers in blood or oil to sprinkle it before the Lord, consecrating the tabernacle and making atonement (Leviticus 4:6; 14:16).

• Purification: A clean person dipped hyssop into water containing the ashes of the red heifer to purify those who were ceremonially unclean (Numbers 19:18). Naaman dipped himself seven times in the Jordan River to be healed of his leprosy (2 Kings 5:14).

• Deception and Tragedy: Joseph's brothers dipped his multi-colored tunic in the blood of a goat to deceive their father into believing Joseph had been killed by a wild beast (Genesis 37:31).

• Sustenance and Refreshment: Jonathan dipped his rod into a honeycomb to receive instant physical renewal during a weary battle (1 Samuel 14:27). Similarly, in Ruth 2:14, dipping bread in vinegar provides immediate physical refreshment to a weary worker.

4. Theological Implications

While tabal in Ruth 2:14 describes a common mealtime action, its theological weight is found in the context of redemption and grace. Boaz, acting as a type of the Kinsman-Redeemer (goel), invites Ruth—a Moabitess and an outsider—to dip her bread in his vinegar. This mirrors the heart of God, who welcomes the outcast to His table. In scripture, sharing food and dipping into the same dish represents the ultimate extension of peace, reconciliation, and covenant fellowship. Boaz's invitation foreshadows the radical grace of God, who invites believers from every nation to dine at His table, transforming strangers into family members.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, Boaz tells Ruth, "Come here, and eat of the bread, and dip your piece of bread in the vinegar." The "vinegar" (chomets) was a cheap, fermented wine diluted with water, commonly used by reapers to quench thirst and cool the body in the heat of harvest. Ruth, as a destitute gleaner, would have expected to eat only the meager scraps she could find, apart from the hired workers. By inviting her to sit with his young women and dip her bread in the shared condiment, Boaz elevates her status from a lowly scavenger to an honored guest. This act of dipping is not a solitary survival measure but a communal celebration of abundance and protection under the wings of the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 37:31: "So they took Joseph's tunic, killed a kid of the goats, and dipped the tunic in the blood."

• Joshua 3:15: "and as those who bore the ark came to the Jordan, and the feet of the priests who bore the ark dipped in the edge of the water (for the Jordan overflows all its banks during the whole time of harvest),"

• 1 Samuel 14:27: "But Jonathan had not heard his father charge the people with the oath; therefore he stretched out the end of the rod that was in his hand and dipped it in a honeycomb, and put his hand to his mouth; and his countenance brightened."

• 2 Kings 5:14: "So he went down and dipped seven times in the Jordan, according to the saying of the man of God; and his flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child, and he was clean."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, the Greek equivalent for dipping at a meal is bapto or embapto. This connection becomes highly significant during the Last Supper. Jesus identifies His betrayer by saying, "He who dipped his hand with Me in the dish will betray Me" (Matthew 26:23). To share a dipped morsel with someone was an honor, making Judas's betrayal after sharing the dipped bread a profound violation of ancient hospitality and friendship. In contrast, Boaz’s invitation to Ruth to dip her bread represents a true and lasting covenant of peace that leads to redemption.
