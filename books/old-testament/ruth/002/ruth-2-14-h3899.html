BREAD - ( lechem , Strong's H3899 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word lechem refers to bread as the primary staple of ancient Near Eastern life, frequently serving as a synecdoche for food or sustenance in general. In the narrative of Ruth, lechem carries deep thematic weight, transitioning from the tragedy of famine (a lack of bread) in Bethlehem ("House of Bread") to the abundant harvest and Boaz's gracious invitation to Ruth to share in his meal. In Ruth 2:14, the word signifies not only physical nourishment but also covenant hospitality, redemption, and the unfolding of God's providential care for a destitute foreign widow.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near Eastern world, bread was not a mere side dish; it was the "staff of life," representing basic human survival. The process of producing lechem was labor-intensive, requiring plowing, sowing, harvesting, threshing, winnowing, grinding grain into flour, kneading dough, and baking it daily. Because life literally depended on this daily cycle, bread was the ultimate symbol of physical life and divine provision. To be without bread was to face death.

This reality establishes the dramatic irony of the book of Ruth. Elimelech and his family fled Bethlehem, which literally translates as "House of Bread" (Bet-lechem), because there was a famine—a lack of lechem in the very place named for it. The narrative arc begins to resolve when Naomi hears that the Lord has visited His people by giving them lechem once again.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Lechem primarily denotes bread made from wheat or barley. Beyond this specific meaning, the word has a broad semantic range that encompasses food of any kind, including meat, grain, and the sacrificial offerings made to God.

• Primary baked staple: The finished loaf of bread prepared for consumption.

• Food in general: The collective term for nourishment, meals, or provisions necessary to sustain life.

• The raw material: Grain or wheat from which bread is produced.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture utilizes lechem in various figurative and literal expressions to illustrate spiritual realities:

• The Bread of Adversity: Representing times of severe trial, grief, and spiritual testing (Isaiah 30:20).

• Bread from Heaven: Referencing the manna in the wilderness, which served as a physical picture of God's supernatural, daily sustaining power (Exodus 16:4).

• The Bread of the Presence (Showbread): Twelve loaves set before the Lord in the Tabernacle and Temple, symbolizing God's desire for ongoing communion and fellowship with the twelve tribes of Israel.

• Eating Bread Together: A powerful cultural idiom representing the establishment of a covenant, peace, and mutual trust between parties.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, lechem underscores the absolute dependency of humanity upon the Creator. Human effort alone cannot produce grain; it requires the rain, sun, and soil provided by God's sovereign hand. Therefore, bread is a physical manifestation of God's grace.

Furthermore, lechem is deeply linked to covenant community. In Israelite society, sharing bread with the poor, the stranger, the widow, and the orphan was a moral imperative reflecting God's own character. The ultimate theological trajectory of lechem points forward to the New Testament, where Jesus Christ—born in Bethlehem, the House of Bread—declares Himself to be the "Bread of Life" who satisfies humanity's ultimate spiritual hunger.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, Boaz says to Ruth at mealtime, "Come here, and eat of the bread, and dip your piece of bread in the vinegar." This invitation carries immense cultural and spiritual significance:

• Overcoming Social Barriers: Ruth was a Moabitess, a destitute foreigner, and a widow. By law and custom, she had no right to sit at the table of an honorable Israelite landowner. By inviting her to eat of his lechem, Boaz transcends these rigid social and ethnic boundaries.

• From Gleaner to Guest: Ruth went to the field hoping to merely glean the dropped ears of grain behind the reapers. Boaz elevates her from a scavenger on the margins of the field to an honored guest sharing in the community meal.

• A Sign of Covenant Protection: To invite someone to eat of your bread was to pledge your peace, friendship, and protection. Boaz’s offer of lechem is a physical expression of the wings of the Lord under which Ruth had come to trust.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:19: "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread Till you return to the ground, For out of it you were taken; For dust you are, And to dust you shall return."

• Genesis 14:18: "Then Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine; he was the priest of God Most High."

• Exodus 16:4: "Then the LORD said to Moses, 'Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you. And the people shall go out and gather a certain quota every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in My law or not.'"

• Ruth 1:6: "Then she arose with her daughters-in-law that she might return from the country of Moab, for she had heard in the country of Moab that the LORD had visited His people in giving them bread."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast in Ruth's life is marked by the presence or absence of lechem. She left a land of temporary physical abundance (Moab) to align herself with the God of Israel. Upon arriving in Bethlehem, her faith is rewarded not just with the permission to glean, but with an abundance of food. Ruth not only eats the lechem until she is satisfied, but she also has leftovers to take back to Naomi (Ruth 2:18). This abundance of bread signals the reversal of the family’s bitter fortunes and prefigures the complete redemption of Elimelech's lineage through Boaz.
