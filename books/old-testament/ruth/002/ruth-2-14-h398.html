EAT - ( akal , Strong's H398 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb akal refers fundamentally to the act of consuming food, eating, or devouring. Beyond physical nourishment, it carries deep covenantal, social, and spiritual significance throughout the Old Testament. In the context of ancient Near Eastern hospitality and covenant relationships, sharing a meal symbolizes peace, acceptance, and communal bonding. In Ruth 2:14, Boaz's invitation to Ruth to "eat" represents far more than mere physical sustenance; it is a profound gesture of redemption, inclusion, and grace extended to a destitute foreign widow, bringing her from the margins of society into the circle of covenant fellowship.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient agrarian world of Israel, food was not merely fuel but a central element of community life, family structure, and divine worship. The root akal occurs over eight hundred times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most frequent verbs. Sharing a meal was an act of covenant-making and covenant-maintenance. Eating together established a bond of peace and mutual obligation between the parties. For a foreigner like Ruth, being invited to eat with the landowner's workers—and specifically at Boaz's own invitation—was an extraordinary elevation of her social status from a desperate gleaner to an accepted guest.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of akal is to eat, consume, or devour. It describes the physical ingestion of food by humans or animals.

• In the Qal stem, the verb denotes the literal eating of food by humans (Genesis 3:6) or the consuming of crops by insects or famine (Joel 1:4).

• In the Niphal stem, it refers to that which is edible, or to being consumed and destroyed.

• In the Pual and Hophal stems, it signifies being devoured or consumed, often by fire or the sword.

• In the Hiphil stem, it means to feed or cause to eat, illustrating God's proactive provision for His people (Psalm 81:16).

The semantic range spans from literal eating to figurative consuming, such as a fire devouring wood or a sword devouring enemies.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Literal Eating: The most common usage, representing daily sustenance and the enjoyment of God's physical provisions (Deuteronomy 8:10).

• Figurative Devouring: Used to describe destruction, judgment, or consumption by non-living agents, such as fire (Joel 1:19) or war (2 Samuel 2:26).

• Covenant and Communion: Eating in the presence of God (Exodus 24:11) represents fellowship, peace, and the celebration of God's faithfulness.

• Internalizing Scripture: Figuratively used for absorbing God's word, where eating God's words means taking them deep into one's soul (Jeremiah 15:16).

### 4. Theological Implications

Eating is a primary indicator of humanity's dependence on God as the Creator and Provider. The first commandment to humanity involved what they could and could not eat (Genesis 2:16-17). Throughout Scripture, God's ultimate restoration is often pictured as a lavish feast (Isaiah 25:6). In Ruth, the physical provision of food through the grain of Bethlehem (literally "House of Bread") mirrors the spiritual provision of the Lord's redeeming grace. Boaz, acting as a type of Christ, provides bread, wine (vinegar), and parched grain in abundance, foreshadowing the Messianic banquet where the marginalized are fully satisfied.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, the verb akal occurs twice ("eat of the bread" and "she ate and was satisfied"). This context is highly significant. Ruth is a Moabitess, an outcast by law and culture. Boaz invites her to "come here, and eat," which breaks down the rigid social barriers between landowner, Israelite harvester, and foreign gleaner. She does not merely eat scraps in isolation; she sits "beside the reapers" and eats "of the bread" dipped in vinegar—a refreshing, sour wine sauce designed to revive exhausted workers in the heat. The passage emphasizes that she ate and was "satisfied" (saba), with leftovers remaining. This highlights the abundance of grace: God's provision through Boaz does not merely keep Ruth alive; it overflows, allowing her to take food back to her mother-in-law, Naomi.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:6: "So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree desirable to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate. She also gave to her husband with her, and he ate."

• Exodus 24:11: "But on the nobles of the children of Israel He did not lay His hand. So they saw God, and they ate and drank."

• Deuteronomy 8:10: "When you have eaten and are full, then you shall bless the LORD your God for the good land which He has given you."

• Psalm 22:26: "The poor shall eat and be satisfied; those who seek Him will praise the LORD. Let your heart live forever!"

• Jeremiah 15:16: "Your words were found, and I ate them, and Your word was to me the joy and rejoicing of my heart; for I am called by Your name, O LORD God of hosts."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between the famine that drove Elimelech's family out of Bethlehem and the abundance now experienced in Bethlehem is completed through the action of eating. Bethlehem (House of Bread) once again lives up to its name.

• Boaz serving Ruth personally ("he passed parched grain to her") mirrors the servant-hearted leadership of Jesus Christ, who feeds His flock directly.

• The remnants of Ruth's meal (what she "kept some back") sustain Naomi, showing how blessing received under the wings of God's covenant extends to others.
