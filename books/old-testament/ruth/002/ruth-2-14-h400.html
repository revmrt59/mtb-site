MEALTIME - ( okel , Strong's H400 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun okel refers fundamentally to food, sustenance, or the act of eating. Derived from the verb akal ("to eat"), it represents the physical nourishment necessary to sustain life. In Ruth 2:14, combined with the preposition and noun for "time," it is translated as "mealtime." Beyond mere physical refueling, a meal in the ancient Near East was a sacred hour of fellowship, boundary-setting, and covenantal relationship. Boaz's invitation to Ruth to join him at the table marks a pivotal transition from her status as an impoverished, foreign gleaner to an accepted, protected guest under his wing, foreshadowing her eventual integration into the covenant community of Israel.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb akal signifies the act of consuming food or devouring. From this root, the noun okel represents the food itself—the vital sustenance that wards off hunger and death. In an agrarian society, food was not a highly processed convenience but the direct product of intensive labor, rain, and divine favor. Thus, okel carries a sense of physical dependency and providential gift.

In ancient Near Eastern culture, sharing a meal was an act of profound social and spiritual consequence. To invite someone to eat of your food was to offer them peace, protection, and an alliance. It transformed a stranger into an ally or a guest. For a wealthy landowner like Boaz to invite Ruth, a Moabitess and a destitute widow, to share in his household's mealtime (okel) was an extraordinary breach of conventional social barriers, signaling a remarkable degree of grace and covenantal favor.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun okel denotes food, provisions, or sustenance. It refers specifically to:

• The substance eaten for nourishment, such as grain, bread, or meat.

• The store of provisions laid up for future use, such as the grain reserves in Egypt during the seven-year famine.

• The act or opportunity of eating, which, when combined with et (time) in Ruth 2:14, points to the designated hour of the day when laborers gathered to rest and eat together.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, okel is used primarily in three ways:

• Literal Sustenance: It describes the physical nourishment provided by the ground, crops, or animals. It represents the basic survival needs of humanity and beast alike.

• National and Communal Provision: In the Joseph narrative, okel refers to the strategic reserves of food gathered during the years of plenty to survive the impending famine. In this sense, it represents salvation from physical death and the preservation of a remnant.

• A Picture of Divine Hospitality: By inviting Ruth to eat of his food and dip her bread in the vinegar, Boaz creates a physical picture of redemption. The food becomes a tangible medium of grace, moving Ruth from the margins of the field to the intimate center of the harvest community.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, okel underscores humanity's role as dependent creatures and God's role as the ultimate Provider. Humanity is fundamentally dependent on what God causes the earth to yield.

Furthermore, the sharing of okel with the marginalized reflects the heart of God's covenant law. The Torah explicitly commanded Israel to leave the corners of their fields for the poor and the stranger. Boaz goes beyond the letter of the law by not only allowing Ruth to glean but also inviting her to share in the prepared food of his household.

This act prefigures the Messianic banquet, where those who were once far off (Gentiles, represented by Ruth the Moabitess) are invited to sit and feast with the King of Kings. It is a beautiful picture of justification and reconciliation through divine hospitality.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, the phrase lêʿēt hāʾōḵel ("at mealtime") occurs at a crucial moment in the narrative. Ruth has spent the morning laboring hard in the heat of the sun. She expects nothing more than to gather the leftover scraps of grain.

Boaz's invitation is highly intentional. He says, "Come here, and eat of the bread, and dip your piece of bread in the vinegar." In this context, okel is not just about relieving physical hunger. It is about status and belonging.

By seating her beside his reapers, Boaz elevates her social standing. He ensures she eats "until she was satisfied, and kept some back." This abundance of food demonstrates that Boaz's redemption is not stingy or minimal; it is overflowing, leaving her with leftovers to take back to Naomi, which in turn sparks Naomi's realization that Boaz is their close relative (Kinsman-Redeemer).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The use of vinegar (chomets) at this mealtime is culturally significant. Vinegar was a cheap, refreshing wine-product used by laborers to quench thirst and cool the body in the midday heat. Dipping the bread (lechem) into the vinegar was a common practice.

• Boaz’s personal involvement in serving Ruth ("he passed parched grain to her") reveals his humility and genuine care. He did not merely command his servants to feed her; he personally handed her the food.

• The remnant of the food that Ruth "kept back" (yatar) serves as a narrative bridge. It shows that the grace received at Boaz's table is not meant for Ruth alone but extends to Naomi, representing the restoration of the entire family of Elimelech.
