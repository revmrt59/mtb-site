COME - ( nagash , Strong's H5066 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nagash means "to draw near," "to approach," or "to come close." In Ruth 2:14, Boaz uses this word to invite Ruth, a destitute foreign widow, to step out of her isolation and join his household at the mealtime table. This invitation transcends cultural, social, and national boundaries, transforming a simple command to eat into a profound act of covenant-like redemption, protection, and grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root nagash conveys the physical action of stepping forward to bridge a gap between two entities. It is used when a person of lower status approaches a superior, such as a subject approaching a king, or a worshiper approaching an altar.

• In the ancient Near East, social hierarchies strictly dictated who could draw near to whom. A foreign gleaner like Ruth, carrying the double stigma of being a Moabitess and a widow, was expected to remain at the extreme periphery of the field. She had no right to approach the owner or his workers.

• By commanding Ruth to nagash ("come near"), Boaz acts as the initiator who dismantles these rigid social and ethnic barriers. The word picture is one of welcome, transforming an outsider into an insider by granting them access to a secure, shared space.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb nagash is a primary Hebrew root meaning to draw near, approach, or come close.

• In the Qal stem, it refers to a physical approach toward a person, place, or object for various purposes, including conversation, intimacy, or alliance.

• In the Hiphil stem, it carries the causative sense of bringing near, presenting an offering, or presenting a legal case.

• In the context of Ruth 2:14, the verb appears in the Qal imperative form (gosi), which functions as an active, warm invitation. It commands a physical transition from a place of distant observation to a place of close fellowship and shared sustenance.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Covenant and Relationship: The term is used when Jacob approaches Isaac for his blessing (Genesis 27:27) and when Judah approaches Joseph to plead for Benjamin's life (Genesis 44:18). In these contexts, drawing near is associated with vulnerability, deep emotion, and familial reconciliation.

• Worship and Sanctuary: It frequently describes priests drawing near to the altar or to the presence of God to minister (Exodus 19:22). This usage highlights that drawing near requires a state of clean standing or a mediator.

• Confrontation and Assembly: It can describe drawing near for legal arbitration, covenant renewal, or military engagement. The action of drawing near demands a response or a resolution.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereign Grace: Ruth did not push her way to the table; she was invited. This reflects the biblical pattern of divine grace, where God initiates the covenant and invites those who are spiritually "far off" to draw near to Him.

• Redemptive Fellowship: Eating together in Israelite culture was a seal of peace and covenant fellowship. Boaz's invitation for Ruth to draw near and dip her bread in the vinegar serves as a historical foreshadowing of Christ inviting the Gentiles into the household of God to partake of the bread of life.

• Overcoming the Curse: As a Moabitess, Ruth was under a ancestral exclusion (Deuteronomy 23:3). The invitation to draw near shows that lovingkindness (chesed) transcends legalistic exclusion, prefiguring the gospel where the middle wall of separation is broken down.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:14, the invitation occurs "at mealtime." This timing is significant because mealtime was a sacred period of rest and community for the harvesters.

• Ruth was likely sitting apart, exhausted and hungry. Boaz's command to "come here" (draw near) is followed by his personal action of passing her the parched grain.

• The proximity allowed by nagash results in her complete satisfaction ("she ate and was satisfied, and kept some back"). The physical act of drawing near to the redeemer's table results in immediate abundance, security, and surplus.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 44:18: "Then Judah came near to him and said: 'O my lord, please let your servant speak a word in my lord's hearing, and do not let your anger burn against your servant; for you are even like Pharaoh.'"

• Genesis 27:27: "And he came near and kissed him; and he smelled the smell of his clothing, and blessed him and said: 'Surely, the smell of my son is like the smell of a field which the LORD has blessed.'"

• Exodus 24:2: "And Moses alone shall come near the LORD, but they shall not come near; nor shall the people go up with him."

• Isaiah 29:13: "Therefore the Lord said: 'Inasmuch as these people draw near with their mouths and honor Me with their lips, but have removed their hearts far from Me, and their fear toward Me is taught by the commandment of men...'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Old Testament), nagash in Ruth 2:14 is translated using the Greek verb proserchomai, which means to come or go to, to approach.

• This same Greek word (proserchomai) is used in the New Testament to describe believers drawing near to the throne of grace (Hebrews 4:16) and drawing near to God through Christ (Hebrews 7:25). Boaz’s physical table is a beautifully clear Old Testament type of the New Testament throne of grace.
