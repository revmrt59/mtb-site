SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the primary Old Testament word for "to say," "to speak," or "to command." In the context of Ruth 2:14, where Boaz speaks to Ruth at mealtime, 'amar goes beyond a mere exchange of information. It represents an initiatory act of covenant kindness (chesed), invitation, and inclusion. By speaking directly to a foreign widow, Boaz breaks social barriers, transitioning Ruth from an outsider gleaning in the margins to an honored guest sharing a meal at the master's table.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb 'amar is used over 5,000 times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most frequent words in the Hebrew Scriptures. In the culture of the ancient Near East, the spoken word was not merely a carrier of abstract thoughts, but a concrete extension of the speaker's personality, authority, and will. To speak to someone was to establish a relational connection. For a powerful landowner like Boaz to speak directly to a destitute Moabite widow was highly unusual. His spoken word serves as a protective canopy, establishing a relational bridge that overrides her status as an impoverished foreigner.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning of 'amar is to say, speak, utter, declare, or command. It is also used to describe internal speech, translated as "to say in one's heart" or "to think."

• Active Speaking: Expressing thoughts, intentions, or commands vocally to an audience.

• Relational Address: Unlike dabar (which emphasizes the substance or logistics of the word spoken), 'amar focuses primarily on the act of personal address and direct communication between parties.

• Injunction/Command: In contexts of authority, the word carries the weight of a royal decree or master's instruction.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word 'amar operates across several key theological domains:

• Creative Power: It is the vehicle of divine creation. In Genesis 1, God speaks ('amar) light and life into existence, demonstrating that the spoken word carries inherent power to alter reality.

• Covenant Initiation: God uses this word to initiate relationships with the patriarchs, delivering promises that bind Him to His people.

• The Power of Invitation: When used by a superior to an inferior, it signals favor. In a cultural landscape where marginalized people were often ignored or spoken about rather than spoken to, the act of direct address ('amar) was a powerful vehicle of validation.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Boaz’s speech in Ruth 2:14 mirrors the initiating grace of God.

• Grace Initiated: God does not wait for humanity to speak first; He speaks to us. Boaz initiates dialogue with Ruth when she has no legal claim to his attention.

• Redemption by the Word: The spoken word of the redeemer brings the outsider into the household. Boaz's spoken invitation transforms Ruth's identity from a scavenger to an invited guest.

• A Picture of Christ: Boaz’s words point forward to the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ, whose spoken invitations ("Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden") offer rest, sustenance, and inclusion to spiritual outsiders.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, the text states, "Now Boaz said ('amar) to her at mealtime..." The timing and setting of this speech are highly significant. Mealtime was a private, communal boundary marker. By speaking to Ruth and saying, "Come here, and eat of the bread," Boaz uses his voice to break down the ethnic and socioeconomic walls separating them. His speech is not a passive greeting; it is an authoritative command of hospitality that overrides the potential objections of his workers. His words physically position her "beside the reapers" and provide her with food until she is fully satisfied.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The narrative of Ruth relies heavily on dialogue to advance the plot. Over half of the verses in the Book of Ruth contain direct speech. This emphasizes that redemption is not a series of silent, mechanical events, but a personal drama driven by verbal commitments, promises, and direct relational engagements. When Boaz speaks, the course of Ruth's life changes.
