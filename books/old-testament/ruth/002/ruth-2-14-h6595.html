PIECE - ( pat , Strong's H6595 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun pat refers to a fragment, morsel, or broken piece of bread, representing the basic, daily sustenance required for survival. In the agricultural and social setting of the ancient Near East, breaking and sharing a piece of bread was a fundamental expression of fellowship, hospitality, and covenant relationship. In Ruth 2:14, Boaz’s invitation to Ruth—a destitute Moabitess—to dip her piece of bread into the communal vinegar goes far beyond mere tolerance. It is an act of extraordinary favor and pastoral care that elevates her from a marginalized outsider to an honored guest at the redeemer's table, beautifully prefiguring the abundant grace of God extended to all who seek refuge under His wings.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, bread was the primary staple of daily life, so much so that the Hebrew word for bread (lechem) is often used as a general term for food. Loaves of bread were not sliced with knives as they are today; instead, they were baked as flat, pliable cakes. To eat this bread, one would break off a small fragment, piece, or morsel (pat) with the hands.

This broken piece served multiple functions at the table. It was used as an utensil to scoop up stews, gravies, or thick broths, and it was dipped into liquids like wine-vinegar to soften the dry texture and cool the body during hot harvest days. Because bread-making was a laborious daily task, sharing a single piece of bread with someone was an intimate gesture of peace, acceptance, and mutual obligation. To invite someone to share one's bread was to offer them protection and bring them into one's social sphere.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun pat denotes a fragment, piece, or morsel, and it is almost exclusively associated with bread. The word is derived from the root verb patat, which means to crumble, break up, or divide into small pieces.

Lexically, pat does not represent a whole loaf or an abundant feast, but rather the smallest unit of food necessary to sustain life. It is the basic portion given to an individual at a meal. The term captures the physical reality of bread that has been broken apart to be shared or consumed.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Scriptures, pat is used to paint vivid pictures of human relationships, spiritual reality, and economic status:

• The Vehicle of Hospitality: When Abraham welcomes the three heavenly messengers in Genesis 18:5, he humbly offers to fetch "a morsel of bread" (pat-lechem) to refresh their hearts. This demonstrates that even the smallest piece of bread, when offered with a servant's heart, becomes an instrument of deep honor and hospitality.

• Humiliation and Destitution: In 1 Samuel 2:36, the judgment on the house of Eli is illustrated by his descendants coming to beg for a "piece of silver and a morsel of bread," showing that being reduced to begging for a mere pat is the ultimate sign of poverty and loss of divine blessing.

• Intimacy and Family Belonging: In Nathan’s parable to David in 2 Samuel 12:3, the poor man's cherished ewe lamb is described as eating of his own "morsel" (pat). This word choice emphasizes that the lamb was treated not as livestock, but as a beloved family member sharing the owner's personal, life-sustaining food.

• Wisdom and Peace: Proverbs contrasts physical abundance with relational peace, stating that a dry "morsel" (pat) with quietness is far better than a house full of feasting with strife (Proverbs 17:1).

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of pat lies in its testimony to God's providential care and the moral responsibility of His people. Because human life is fragile, we are dependent on God for even the smallest piece of bread to survive.

Furthermore, how a person treats the "morsel" reveals the condition of their heart. Job defends his righteousness by declaring that he has not eaten his "morsel" alone while the fatherless starved (Job 31:17). In God’s economy, the righteous are called to share their pat with the hungry.

Ultimately, the transition from a dry, lonely morsel to a shared, satisfying meal illustrates the heart of redemption. God does not merely throw scraps to the needy; He invites them into fellowship, transforming a desperate piece of bread into a feast of covenant relationship.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage (Ruth 2:14)

In Ruth 2:14, the harvest setting is critical. Ruth is a foreign widow, gleaning in the hot sun behind the harvesters—a position of extreme vulnerability, poverty, and social isolation. At mealtime, Boaz addresses her directly: "Come here, and eat of the bread, and dip your piece of bread in the vinegar."

The word "piece" (pat) in this verse refers to the portion of bread allocated for the meal. By telling Ruth to "dip your piece in the vinegar," Boaz is performing several radical actions:

• Inclusion: He invites an foreign outsider, a Moabitess, to sit directly beside his workers, breaking down rigid social, national, and economic barriers.

• Provision: He validates her ownership of the food. It is "your piece" of bread. He does not treat her as a beggar receiving crumbs from the floor, but as a guest with a designated portion.

• Refreshment: The "vinegar" (chomets) was a sour wine mixture that served as a refreshing, thirst-quenching dip for laborers working in the intense heat of the Judean sun. Dipping the dry pat into this liquid made it palatable and nourishing.

Through this simple invitation to dip her pat, Boaz acts as a type of Christ, our Kinsman-Redeemer. He provides security, dignity, and fellowship to the weary seeker, ensuring she is fully satisfied and has abundance left over to share with Naomi.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Grace of the Lord's Table: For pastors and teachers, Boaz's invitation to Ruth is a beautiful Old Testament shadow of the Lord's Supper and the Gospel call. Just as Ruth brought nothing to the table but her need, believers come to Christ empty-handed, only to be offered the Bread of Life.

• The Power of Small Gestures: A pat is small, but in the hands of a generous redeemer, it is more than enough. Ruth ate her piece, was satisfied, and kept some back to give to Naomi (Ruth 2:14). God's grace always exceeds our immediate needs, leaving an abundance to be shared with others.

• Covenant Protection: In the ancient world, once a host shared bread with a guest, they were bound by honor to protect that guest. By giving Ruth her pat and inviting her to dip it, Boaz was publicly declaring that Ruth was now under his protection and care.
