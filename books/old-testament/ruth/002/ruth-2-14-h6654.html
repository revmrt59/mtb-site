REACHED - ( tsabat , Strong's H6654 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb tsabat occurs only once in the Old Testament, representing a rare and highly specific gesture of hospitality. Meaning "to reach out," "to hand over," or "to heap up," it depicts Boaz's active, personal care as he extends roasted grain to Ruth. Rather than merely allowing her to gather leftovers, Boaz serves her directly and abundantly, illustrating the transition from a marginalized stranger to an honored guest under his protective care.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The setting of this word is an agricultural mealtime during the barley harvest in Bethlehem. Harvest workers typically gathered in the shade to rest and eat a simple midday meal. Ruth, as a poor foreigner and a gleaner, occupied the absolute lowest rung of the social ladder. By custom, she would have stood far off, hoping at best for permission to drink from the common water jars. Instead, Boaz invites her to join the circle. The word tsabat captures the physical movement of the master of the field bending or reaching across the social and cultural divide to personally hand food to this destitute widow. It paints a picture of deliberate, hands-on kindness that restores dignity to the recipient.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb tsabat means to reach out, hold out, hand over, or offer a portion of food. Cognate Semitic languages, including Arabic and Ethiopic, indicate a physical gesture of pinching, grasping with the fingers, or gathering a small bundle to present to someone. Rather than a formal, detached serving of food, it denotes a direct, hand-to-hand extension of sustenance.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Because tsabat is used only once in the Hebrew Bible, its word picture is entirely bound up with the actions of Boaz. The word picture is one of personal touch and active service. In ancient Near Eastern culture, the host or master of the estate would rarely serve a common laborer, let alone a foreign gleaner. Typically, a servant would be commanded to distribute food. By performing this action himself, Boaz elevates Ruth. The physical extension of his hand with the parched grain acts as a visual bridge of reconciliation and acceptance, signaling to everyone in the field that Ruth is under his personal favor and protection.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, tsabat serves as a beautiful illustration of the mechanics of divine grace (chesed).

• Personal Intervention: Grace is never administered at a cold, sterile distance. Just as Boaz personally reached out the food to Ruth, God reaches down into human brokenness to deliver sustenance and salvation directly.

• Superabundant Sufficiency: The hand of the redeemer does not offer a stingy portion. What is reached out is so abundant that the recipient is fully satisfied and has a surplus to share with others.

• Elevation of the Outcast: The act of handing food directly to Ruth transforms her status from an isolated observer to an invited participant at the table, foreshadowing the inclusion of the Gentiles into the covenant blessings of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, the use of tsabat occurs at a crucial turning point. Ruth has spent the morning laboring in the heat. When mealtime arrives, Boaz invites her to dip her bread in the sour wine (vinegar), which was used to quench thirst and cool the body. While she sits beside the reapers, Boaz reaches (tsabat) her the parched grain. Parched grain (kali) was fresh barley roasted on an iron plate, a delicious and valued harvest treat. The text emphasizes that she ate, was satisfied, and kept some back. The nuance of tsabat here is not just a polite pass of a plate, but a lavish, piling gesture of generosity that left her with an abundance to take home to her mother-in-law, Naomi.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This Hebrew verb is a hapax legomenon (a word that occurs only once in the entire Hebrew Bible). Therefore, there are no other verses containing this exact Hebrew word. Its sole occurrence is:

• Ruth 2:14 - "Now Boaz said to her at mealtime, 'Come here, and eat of the bread, and dip your piece of bread in the vinegar.' So she sat beside the reapers, and he reached her parched grain, and she ate and was satisfied, and kept some back."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Ancient translations offer additional flavor to the meaning of tsabat. The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) translates this word using the Greek verb bounizo, which means "to heap up" or "to pile up." This suggests that when Boaz reached out the parched grain, he did not merely hand her a few kernels, but heaped up a massive, generous portion before her. This translation choices reinforces the theological truth that the grace of the redeemer always exceeds the bare minimum required for survival.
