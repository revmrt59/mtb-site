PARCHED GRAIN - ( qali , Strong's H7039 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word qali refers to freshly harvested grain that has been roasted over an open fire. Serving as a nutritious, flavorful, and easily preserved staple in the ancient Near East, it represents immediate, satisfying sustenance. In Ruth 2:14, Boaz’s act of personally passing this roasted grain to Ruth—resulting in her eating until she was fully satisfied with leftovers remaining—serves as a powerful picture of unexpected, abundant grace, covenant hospitality, and the transition from severe famine to divine overflow.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient agrarian culture of Israel, qali was a common and beloved food, particularly during the barley and wheat harvest seasons. To prepare it, harvesters gathered green, freshly cut heads of grain, placed them on a flat iron pan or a metal sheet, and roasted them directly over an open fire. This roasting process caramelized the natural sugars within the moist kernels, giving the grain a pleasant, sweet flavor and a crunchy texture. It also made the grain easily digestible without the time-consuming labor of milling it into flour, kneading dough, and baking bread. Because it was highly nutritious, lightweight, and resistant to spoilage, parched grain was the ideal field ration for agricultural workers, travelers, and soldiers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The masculine noun qali means "parched grain," "roasted grain," or "roasted ears of corn." It is derived from the root verb qalah (Strong's H7096), which means "to roast," "to parch," or "to burn with fire." The noun refers specifically to the finished product of this roasting process. The semantic range of qali is tightly focused, consistently referring to processed agricultural yield ready for immediate consumption. It represents the immediate bounty of the harvest, requiring no further preparation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, qali is utilized in contexts of provision, hospitality, and transition:

• The Provision of the Land: In the wilderness, Israel ate manna. However, upon entering the Promised Land, the manna ceased, and they immediately ate of the yield of Canaan, specifically bread and parched grain. This marked the official end of their wandering and the beginning of covenant inheritance.

• Emergency and Military Rations: When Jesse sent young David to visit his brothers on the battlefront against the Philistines, he sent an ephah of qali as a vital, ready-to-eat food source.

• Covenant Hospitality: When Abigail sought to appease David’s anger and protect her household, she brought a massive peace offering that included five seahs of qali. Similarly, when David fled from Absalom, loyal subjects brought him beds, basins, and heavy provisions, explicitly listing qali to sustain his weary men in the wilderness.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of qali in the Old Testament, particularly in Ruth, rests on the themes of redemption, grace, and divine sufficiency:

• Sovereign Provision: Ruth and Naomi returned to Bethlehem completely destitute, arriving at the very beginning of the barley harvest. The presence of parched grain indicates that God had visited His people by giving them bread, ending the famine that drove Elimelech's family away.

• Superabundant Grace: In the ancient Near East, a gleaner was entitled only to the scraps left behind by the harvesters. By inviting Ruth to sit at the table and offering her qali, Boaz shattered social barriers. He treated a foreign widow not as a beggar, but as an honored guest, illustrating the expansive, unconditional grace of God toward outsiders.

• Messianic Foreshadowing: Boaz’s provision to Ruth prefigures the ministry of Jesus Christ. Just as Boaz personally served Ruth until she was fully satisfied and had leftovers to share, Christ feeds those who come to Him, satisfying their spiritual hunger completely and leaving an abundance of grace to be shared with others.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, the contextual placement of qali is highly deliberate. Ruth had been working hard in the heat of the day. At mealtime, Boaz invited her to dip her bread in the vinegar (a refreshing, hydrating sour wine mixture). Then, Boaz himself "passed her parched grain."

The detail that Boaz personally served her demonstrates his deep respect and protective care. The text notes that Ruth "ate and was satisfied, and kept some back." The Hebrew verb for "was satisfied" (sava) indicates complete repletion; she was fully filled. The leftover parched grain was not wasted; Ruth kept it and later gave it to Naomi (Ruth 2:18). This demonstrates that God’s blessing through the redeemer-figure Boaz was so rich that it immediately overflowed to rescue the older widow waiting at home, showcasing the communal reach of covenant kindness (chesed).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 23:14: "You shall eat neither bread nor parched grain nor fresh heads of grain until the same day that you have brought an offering to your God; it shall be a statute forever throughout your generations in all your dwellings."

• 1 Samuel 17:17: "Then Jesse said to David his son, 'Take now for your brothers an ephah of this parched grain and these ten loaves, and run to the camp to your brothers.'"

• 1 Samuel 25:18: "Then Abigail made haste and took two hundred loaves of bread, two skins of wine, five dressed sheep, five seahs of parched grain, one hundred clusters of raisins, and two hundred cakes of figs, and loaded them on donkeys."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Levical law, qali held an important place in the worship of Israel. Leviticus 2:14 dictates that if a grain offering of firstfruits is brought to the Lord, it must consist of "green heads of grain parched on the fire." This demonstrates that the very food Boaz served to Ruth at his table was identical in preparation to the holy firstfruit offerings presented to Yahweh at the tabernacle. What was fit for the altar of God was generously shared with the foreign wanderer.
