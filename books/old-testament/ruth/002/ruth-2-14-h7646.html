SATISFIED - ( saba, Strong's H7646 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb saba refers to being filled to satisfaction, sated, or fully content. It goes beyond merely taking in food to denote the state of complete physical repletion and emotional contentment, where no further lack is felt. In the narrative of Ruth, her satisfaction at Boaz's table represents not only the physical relief of a destitute gleaner but also a profound spiritual picture of Yahweh's abundant provision and grace overflowing to the marginalized.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word saba is deeply rooted in the agricultural life of ancient Israel. It depicts the transition from lack and hunger to fullness and abundance. It is the feeling of sitting at a table where the supply exceeds the need. The word picture is one of a cup overflowing or a stomach completely filled. In ancient Near Eastern culture, where famine and food insecurity were constant threats, being "satisfied" was a tangible sign of divine favor, safety, and peace.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb saba means to eat one's fill, to be sated, or to be satisfied. In the Qal stem, it means to be sated with food or drink. In the Hiphil (causative) stem, it means to satisfy or fill to the full. Lexically, it denotes reaching a state of complete satiety where there is no longer any desire or need for more. It is used both literally of physical appetite and metaphorically of spiritual or emotional desires, such as being satisfied with days (long life), favor, or goodness.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• saba occurs frequently in the Old Testament to contrast the emptiness of human striving with the fullness of God's provision.

• Physical Satiety: It describes eating until one wants no more, often contrasted with hunger.

• Spiritual and Emotional Contentment: It is used of being satisfied with God's mercy or the goodness of His house.

• Satiety of Life: It describes living a full, complete life, as seen in patriarchs like Abraham who died "old and full of days."

• The negative counterpart is the inability of wealth or wicked desires to satisfy the human soul.

### 4. Theological Implications

• saba reveals the character of God as a lavish Provider. God does not merely give His people enough to survive; He gives until they are satisfied.

• Satisfaction is a covenantal promise. When Israel obeys, God satisfies them with bread. When they turn away, they eat but are not satisfied.

• True satisfaction is ultimately found only in God Himself. Earthly food satisfies the body temporarily, but God's presence satisfies the longing soul eternally.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:14, Ruth sits with the reapers at Boaz’s invitation. He offers her parched grain. She eats, is "satisfied" (saba), and has leftovers ("kept some back").

• The context highlights the transition of Ruth from a destitute, hungry foreigner to a highly favored guest receiving abundant, overflowing grace.

• Her satisfaction (saba) contrasts sharply with the famine that initiated the book of Ruth. The return to Bethlehem ("House of Bread") is now fully realized in Ruth's personal experience of satiety at the table of the kinsman-redeemer.

• The fact that she was satisfied and had leftovers highlights the superabundant nature of Boaz's (and by extension, God's) grace. It foreshadows the feeding miracles of Jesus, where the hungry are satisfied and baskets of fragments remain.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 107:9 - "For He satisfies the longing soul, and fills the hungry soul with goodness."

• Psalm 22:26 - "The poor shall eat and be satisfied; Those who seek Him will praise the LORD. Let your heart live forever!"

• Psalm 103:5 - "Who satisfies your mouth with good things, So that your youth is renewed like the eagle’s."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The leftover portion ("kept some back") that Ruth saved from her satisfying meal was not wasted; she brought it back to Naomi. This demonstrates how God's abundant satisfying of one person overflows to bless others in their family or community.

• The Hebrew verb saba shares its root letters (s-b-a) with the word for "seven" (sheba), which in Hebrew thought represents completion, perfection, and wholeness. This linguistic relationship reinforces the idea that to be satisfied is to be made complete and whole.
