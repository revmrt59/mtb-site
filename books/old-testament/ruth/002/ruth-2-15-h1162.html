BOAZ - ( Bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

Boaz is a prominent Bethlehemite of the tribe of Judah, characterized as a "man of great wealth" or noble standing, who serves as the kinsman-redeemer for Ruth and Naomi. His name carries the meaning of "in him is strength," representing the security, protection, and redemptive grace he provides. In Ruth 2:15, Boaz exhibits this strength not through harsh dominance, but through active, generous protection and provision for the vulnerable foreign widow, foreshadowing the ultimate redemptive grace of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Boaz appears in the context of the period of the Judges, a turbulent era of Israel's history marked by spiritual compromise, social chaos, and famine. Against this dark backdrop, Boaz stands out as a beacon of covenant loyalty (chesed). He is a wealthy landowner in Bethlehem, an agricultural community whose name means "House of Bread." The word picture associated with his name is one of structural support, stability, and defensive strength. This imagery of strength is so profound that centuries later, King Solomon names the left bronze pillar at the entrance of the temple vestibule "Boaz" (1 Kings 7:21), signifying that God is the source of Israel's strength and stability.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name Boaz is derived from a compound of the preposition be (in / by) and the noun oz (strength). Thus, the literal lexical meaning of the name is "in him is strength" or "by strength." It represents a state of robust power, security, and the capacity to deliver. An alternative etymological suggestion links the name to an Arabic root meaning "alacrity" or "quickness," yet the overwhelming biblical and historical usage emphasizes strength, security, and the capability to act as a protector.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name occurs 24 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in the Book of Ruth, with additional mentions in the genealogies of 1 Chronicles, and in the architectural descriptions of Solomon's temple.

• The Living Pillar: In the Book of Ruth, Boaz is the living embodiment of his name. He is described as a gibbor chayil (a man of great wealth, valor, and standing), using his social and physical strength to uphold the weak and vulnerable.

• The Temple Pillar: In the temple architecture, the pillar named Boaz stands alongside Jachin ("He will establish"), visually proclaiming to all worshippers that God establishes His people and that in Him alone is their strength.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Boaz serves as one of the clearest Old Testament types of Jesus Christ. He acts as the go'el (kinsman-redeemer), a role requiring three distinct elements that Boaz fulfills:

• Kinship: He must be a blood relative of the family in need.

• Ability: He must possess the financial and social strength to pay the redemption price.

• Willingness: He must voluntarily choose to redeem.

Through his willing redemption of Ruth, Boaz demonstrates how God's strength is channeled through covenant love to bring outsiders into the family of faith. His strength does not exploit the vulnerable; rather, it redeems them from poverty, gives them an inheritance, and secures their future.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:15, Ruth rises to glean in the fields, and Boaz commands his young men, "Let her glean even among the sheaves, and do not reproach her." This specific instruction reveals how Boaz's strength operates in daily practice. Normally, gleaners were restricted to the edges of the fields and could only gather what fell to the ground after the harvesters had finished. By allowing Ruth to glean "among the sheaves"—the bundled grain ready for transport—Boaz bypasses standard social barriers. He uses his authoritative strength to issue a protective decree, ensuring she is not humiliated ("do not reproach her") or harmed. His strength becomes a shield of grace, transforming a place of potential danger into a sanctuary of provision.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between the book of Judges and the story of Boaz is striking. While Judges concludes with "everyone did what was right in his own eyes," Boaz does what is right in the eyes of Yahweh. His interactions with his workers are marked by mutual blessings in the Lord's name (Ruth 2:4). Furthermore, the genealogies highlight that Boaz was the son of Rahab, the Canaanite resident of Jericho who trusted in Yahweh. This lineage explains his readiness to show extraordinary grace to Ruth, a Moabitess, acknowledging that faith in Yahweh transcends ethnic boundaries.
