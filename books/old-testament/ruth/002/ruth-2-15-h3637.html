REPROACH - ( kalam , Strong's H3637 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb kalam represents the act of wounding someone's honor, causing public humiliation, or treating them with insulting and shaming behavior. In the ancient Near East, where honor and shame were paramount to social survival, to reproach or shame someone was to deal a severe blow to their dignity and standing in the community. In Ruth 2:15, Boaz's command to his young men not to "reproach" Ruth is a powerful demonstration of protective grace. He orders them not to humiliate her as she gleans close to the grain-cutters, transforming her experience from vulnerable marginalization into one of dignity, safety, and abundant provision.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, social life was structured around the preservation of family honor and the avoidance of shame. To be an outsider, a widow, and a foreigner—as Ruth was—placed a person at the very bottom of the social hierarchy. Gleaners were typically the poorest of the poor, operating on the dangerous fringes of harvest fields where they were highly vulnerable to verbal abuse, physical exploitation, and social exclusion.

The Hebrew word kalam evokes the visible effect of shame—often associated with a blushing face, downcast eyes, and a crushed spirit. It is the public stripping away of a person's dignity. When Boaz commands his workers not to "reproach" Ruth, he is erecting a protective wall around her. He forbids them from treating her with the condescension, mockery, or harshness that a destitute foreign widow would normally expect to receive.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary verb kalam means to humiliate, insult, put to shame, dishonor, or cause to blush.

• In the Hiphil stem (as used in Ruth 2:15), the verb denotes active shaming, insulting, or mistreating someone so as to cause them humiliation or embarrassment.

• In the Niphal stem, it means to be humiliated, put to shame, or feel disgraced.

• The core semantic range spans from causing emotional embarrassment and social disgrace to inflicting active insult and verbal abuse. It stands in direct contrast to showing favor, honor, and respect.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses kalam to describe the painful experience of public disgrace.

• The physical manifestation of kalam is often associated with the face turning red or being covered in confusion (as seen in the prophetic descriptions of shame and disgrace).

• It is frequently paired with other words for shame, such as bosh (to be disappointed or delayed in hope), showing a progressive loss of confidence and social standing.

• In military and historical narratives, kalam refers to treating envoys or guests with shameful abuse, causing them deep humiliation.

• In wisdom literature, it depicts the painful sting of verbal rebukes or unjust accusations that wound a person's character.

### 4. Theological Implications

Shame is one of the primary consequences of the Fall, representing a broken relationship with God and others. The theological arc of Scripture shows God as the one who removes the shame of His people and places it upon His enemies.

Boaz, acting as a type of the ultimate Redeemer (Jesus Christ), models God’s heart toward the vulnerable and marginalized. By forbidding his workers from shaming Ruth, Boaz demonstrates how godliness actively works to alleviate the social and psychological shame of the weak. This reflects the character of God, who raises the poor out of the dust and promises that those who trust in Him will never be put to shame. In the kingdom of God, grace does not merely feed the hungry; it restores their dignity and shields them from reproach.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:15, Ruth has just received Boaz's permission to glean even "among the sheaves"—an unprecedented privilege, as gleaners were normally restricted to the outer edges of the field and could only gather what fell far behind the harvesters.

Gleaning among the sheaves put Ruth in direct proximity to the male workers and the piled grain, exposing her to potential resentment, accusations of theft, or harassment. By commanding his workers, "do not reproach her," Boaz removes the threat of verbal abuse and social degradation. He ensures that her presence is not questioned or mocked. Combined with his command in verse 16 not to "rebuke" her, Boaz establishes an environment of absolute safety and abundant grace, allowing her to gather food with her head held high.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Job 19:3 - "These ten times you have reproached me; You are not ashamed that you treat me insolently." (Here, Job uses kalam to describe the relentless, crushing verbal insults of his friends that compound his physical suffering.)

• Proverbs 25:8 - "Do not go hastily to court; For what will you do in the end, When your neighbor has put you to shame?" (This wisdom warning highlights the social embarrassment and public humiliation of losing a legal dispute.)

• Psalm 44:9 - "But You have cast us off and put us to shame, And You do not go out with our armies." (The psalmist laments a national defeat, using kalam to describe the loss of honor and the public disgrace before surrounding nations.)

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates kalam in Ruth 2:15 using the Greek verb kataischyno, which means to dishonor, disgrace, or put to shame. This same Greek word is used in the New Testament to describe how God will never let those who believe in Christ be put to shame (Romans 9:33; 10:11; 1 Peter 2:6). Boaz’s protective care foreshadows the redemptive work of Christ, who endured the ultimate reproach and shame of the cross to rescue believers from eternal disgrace and clothe them in His righteousness.
