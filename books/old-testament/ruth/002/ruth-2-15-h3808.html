NOT - ( lo , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo is the absolute negation in the Old Testament, expressing a permanent, objective, and definitive "no" or "not." In Ruth 2:15, this small but powerful word functions as a strict prohibition issued by Boaz to his young men, commanding them "do not reproach her." By utilizing this absolute negative, Boaz establishes a protective hedge around Ruth, transforming her status from a vulnerable, foreign gleaner into a highly favored guest under his personal guardianship. This prohibition reveals the heart of redemption, where grace actively restrains the harmful, natural inclinations of others to ensure the safety and provision of the needy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The negative particle lo serves as the primary and most frequent means of absolute negation in the Hebrew Bible. While Hebrew possesses other negative particles that carry subjective, temporary, or deprecatory nuances, lo is objective, unconditional, and permanent.

• To understand its force, one can picture a boundary stone or an unyielding wall. It does not suggest a temporary pause or express a mere wish; it establishes an absolute boundary that cannot be crossed.

• In ancient Near Eastern harvest fields, impoverished foreign women like Ruth were highly susceptible to physical abuse, social exclusion, and verbal humiliation. Under the Law of Moses, landowners were commanded to leave the corners of their fields for the poor and the stranger, but this law was frequently ignored or grudgingly accommodated. By commanding his workers with this absolute negative, Boaz establishes a sanctuary of safety and dignity for Ruth, effectively drawing a line in the dust that none of his laborers dare cross.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The particle lo functions as an absolute adverb of negation, translated in English as "not," "no," or "neither."

• When combined with an imperfect verb, as seen in the phrase lo taklimuha ("do not reproach her"), it expresses a categorical, permanent prohibition. It demands the complete and total avoidance of the action.

• The semantic range of lo spans across absolute declarations of fact, eternal moral laws, and unconditional prohibitions. It is the linguistic tool used when an action must be utterly ruled out, leaving no room for negotiation, modification, or exception.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Category of absolute law: This particle is the exact word used throughout the Decalogue (Ten Commandments) to forbid actions such as murder, theft, and adultery. It sets an immutable standard of righteousness.

• Covenantal assurance: It represents a linguistic seal of security. When God uses lo to describe His protection or presence, He is assuring His people that under no circumstances will He fail them.

• A protective hedge: In the context of the harvest, Boaz's command serves as a protective wall around Ruth. He does not merely ask his men to be polite; he places an absolute prohibition on their behavior, ensuring that Ruth's gathering is entirely free from hostility.

4. Theological Implications

• Actively restraining grace: Grace is not merely passive kindness; it is active, protective, and commanding. Covenant love (hesed) is demonstrated when those with power use their authority to restrain the harmful impulses of others, creating a safe space for the vulnerable to flourish.

• The protective character of the Redeemer: Boaz, as a kinsman-redeemer (goel), prefigures Jesus Christ. The absolute prohibition issued by Boaz highlights the role of the Redeemer as a shield. Christ does not merely comfort the broken; He actively forbids their condemnation and silences their accusers.

• Security in the presence of the Lord: Just as Ruth found safety and abundance under the wings of the God of Israel and the direct command of Boaz, the believer finds absolute security under the sovereign, protective declarations of Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:15, Boaz issues a highly unusual directive: "Let her glean even among the sheaves, and do not reproach her." Typically, gleaners were restricted to the edges of the field and were forced to wait until the harvesters had moved far ahead. To glean "among the sheaves" was to gather from the freshly cut, high-yield bundles—a privilege reserved solely for hired servants.

• This extraordinary privilege would naturally provoke jealousy, resentment, and hostility from the hired workers. They would be tempted to mock her, shout at her, or demand that she leave.

• Recognizing this danger, Boaz utilizes the absolute negation of lo combined with the verb kalam ("to humiliate, insult, or put to shame"). By commanding "do not reproach her," Boaz completely shuts down any potential verbal or social mistreatment. He changes the social dynamics of the field by decreeing that Ruth is to be treated with the same respect as his own household, signaling to his workers that any insult directed at her is an insult to his own authority.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 20:13 - "You shall not murder." (The absolute, permanent prohibition of murder).

• Joshua 1:5 - "No man shall be able to stand before you all the days of your life; as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not leave you nor forsake you." (The unconditional assurance of God's covenantal presence).

• Psalm 23:1 - "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want." (The definitive declaration of the soul's total security and lack of lack under the Shepherd's care).

• Isaiah 42:3 - "A bruised reed He will not break, and smoking flax He will not quench; He will bring forth justice for truth." (The messianic promise of Christ's gentle, protective restraint toward the weak).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between lo and al: While al is the subjective negative particle used for immediate or temporary appeals (often translated "do not" in a momentary sense), lo is used here to establish a lasting, objective rule for the duration of the harvest. This was not a temporary warning for the afternoon, but a permanent law of Boaz's field.

• Progressive protection: In Ruth 2:9, Boaz tells Ruth that he has charged the young men "not (bility, a related negative particle) to touch you." Now, in verse 15, he expands this protection by commanding them "do not (lo) reproach her." He moves from protecting her physical safety to aggressively guarding her emotional, social, and psychological dignity.
