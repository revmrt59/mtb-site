YOUNG MEN - ( na'ar , Strong's H5288 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'ar primarily denotes a young, marriageable, but dependent male, ranging from infancy to early adulthood. In administrative, domestic, and military contexts, it transitions from indicating age to indicating social status, frequently designating a servant, squire, or trusted attendant. In the book of Ruth, particularly in Boaz’s field, the term represents the young male agricultural laborers under the supervision of a foreman. Boaz's command to these young men to protect and accommodate Ruth highlights his role as a righteous protector, establishing a safe environment for a vulnerable foreigner and demonstrating how covenant faithfulness (chesed) filters down from leadership to the hands of everyday laborers.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun na'ar originates from a cultural context where age, family status, and labor were deeply intertwined. In ancient Israelite society, a na'ar was a young male who had not yet become the head of his own patriarchal household. He remained under the authority of a father or a master.

• This status naturally lent itself to roles of service, military assistance (such as an armor-bearer), or agricultural labor. The background of the word reflects a position of strength and vigor, yet one bound by accountability and submission to authority.

• In an agricultural setting like Boaz's estate, these young men formed the backbone of the harvesting workforce, operating under a designated supervisor—who is himself referred to as the na'ar set over the reapers in Ruth 2:6.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The semantic range of na'ar spans several distinct categories of age and status:

• An infant, babe, or toddler (such as Moses in the basket in Exodus 2:6, or Samson in Judges 13:5).

• A young boy or youth who is old enough to perform tasks but is not yet fully grown (such as Ishmael in Genesis 21:12, or Joseph at seventeen in Genesis 37:2).

• An unmarried young man of marriageable age who is active in physical labor or military service (such as David when he fought Goliath in 1 Samuel 17:33).

• A servant, steward, or personal attendant, irrespective of chronological age, indicating a subordinate relationship to a master (such as Gehazi, the servant of Elisha, or Ziba, the servant of Saul's house).

• In the context of Ruth 2:15, the word carries the dual nuance of young, vigorous male laborers and hired servants under the authority of Boaz.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In the Old Testament, na'ar is frequently contrasted with the elders (zaqen) or the master (adon).

• When used in a military or royal household, the na'ar is the trusted helper who carries weapons or runs errands, demonstrating that the term implies loyalty and responsiveness to commands.

• In agricultural contexts, the "young men" (ne'arim, plural) represent the active force in the field. They harvest, bind sheaves, and carry out the physical demands of the estate. They are the ones whose behavior dictates the safety and environment of the field for marginalized gleaners like Ruth.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of na'ar in Ruth 2:15 serves as a profound illustration of covenant righteousness (chesed) in action. Boaz, as a worthy man of standing (gibbor chayil), does not merely possess personal virtue; he actively directs his household and laborers (ne'arim) to walk in that same righteousness.

• The vulnerability of a young, foreign widow like Ruth in an open harvest field was immense. Unrestrained young men in the ancient Near East could easily abuse or exploit such a woman. By commanding his "young men" not to reproach, insult, or touch her (Ruth 2:9, 15), Boaz establishes a sanctuary of safety.

• This reveals a theological truth: godliness in leadership must sanctify the environment of those under authority. The master’s obedience to God’s law regarding the poor and the stranger directly constrains and guides the actions of his young servants.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:15, Boaz issues a direct order to his ne'arim ("young men") to let Ruth glean "even among the sheaves" and "do not reproach her."

• Normally, gleaners were restricted to the edges of the field or were only allowed to pick up what fell behind the reapers after the sheaves had been gathered and carted away. Boaz breaks social conventions by allowing Ruth to glean among the gathered sheaves themselves—where the grain is thickest and easiest to gather.

• For the ne'arim, this command required active cooperation. They had to intentionally drop extra grain for her (v. 16) and refrain from treating her as an intruder. The compliance of the young men reflects the absolute authority of Boaz and his success in cultivating a culture of generosity and respect on his estate.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The feminine equivalent is na'arah (young woman), which is used extensively in the same chapter to describe Ruth herself (Ruth 2:5, 6) and the young female workers of Boaz (Ruth 2:8, 23).

• The interaction between the ne'arim (young men) and the ne'arot (young women) in the harvest fields was a standard part of ancient agricultural life. Boaz deliberately positions Ruth among his young women for her safety, while strictly commanding his young men to treat her with dignity.
