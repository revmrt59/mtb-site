SAYING - ( 'amar, Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, fundamentally meaning to say, speak, command, or declare. In Ruth 2:15, translated as "saying," it functions as an infinitive construct (lemor) that introduces direct speech, transitioning the internal protective kindness of Boaz into an explicit, authoritative command. By speaking directly to his workers, Boaz establishes a safe environment that preserves Ruth's physical safety and human dignity while she gleans in his fields.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb 'amar represents the primary vehicle of communication, intent, and authority in the Hebrew scriptures. It is not passive or silent thought; it is the active externalization of what is conceived in the mind and heart. In the agrarian, patriarchal culture of the ancient Near East, the spoken word of a landholder or patriarch carried the weight of law. When Boaz speaks, his words create a social and legal boundary. The background of 'amar in this passage highlights that Boaz’s grace is not merely a passive feeling of pity, but an active, spoken intervention that fundamentally alters Ruth’s standing in the field.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'amar is to say, speak, utter, or tell. Depending on the context, its semantic range includes:

• To declare, announce, or proclaim.

• To command, order, or charge (especially when spoken by a superior to subordinates, as in Ruth 2:15).

• To think or say to oneself (when paired with "in the heart").

• To promise, intend, or resolve.

In the specific form found in Ruth 2:15 (lemor), the word functions grammatically as a transition particle equivalent to "saying" or "to the effect that," introducing the precise direct quote of the speaker.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, 'amar acts as a creative and governing force. In Genesis 1, God "said" ('amar) and creation responded, demonstrating that spoken words in Hebrew thought are agents of reality. When a king or master uses 'amar, it establishes administrative policy. In Ruth 2:15, the word picture is that of a master setting the boundaries of his estate by his voice. Boaz’s verbal instruction establishes a canopy of safety over a vulnerable foreign widow, transforming a potentially hostile workspace into a sanctuary of provision.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'amar reveals a God who speaks and whose spoken words redeem. In the Book of Ruth, Boaz serves as a historical type of the Kinsman-Redeemer (Goel), foreshadowing Jesus Christ. His spoken words to his young men reflect the heart of God toward the marginalized. This word teaches that covenant love (chesed) is vocal; it does not remain silent in the face of need. True redemption expresses itself in clear, spoken instructions that protect and provide for the defenseless.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:15, the text reads: "And when she rose up to glean, Boaz commanded his young men, saying..." The writer uses two verbs of speech: tsavah (commanded) and 'amar (saying). This double emphasis underscores the deliberateness of Boaz's intervention. He does not drop hints; he issues a formal, spoken directive. He commands his workers to let Ruth glean among the sheaves and forbids them from reproaching her. The contextual nuance of 'amar here is one of protective authority. It translates Boaz’s private compassion into public, binding policy for his entire harvest crew.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Under the Mosaic Law, the poor and the stranger had a legal right to glean the corners of the fields (Leviticus 19:9-10). However, in practice, poor gleaners—especially foreign women—were frequently subjected to abuse, insults, and physical danger. By using 'amar to command his workers not to "reproach" or humiliate Ruth, Boaz goes far beyond the letter of the law. He uses his authority to protect her emotional and social dignity, demonstrating that biblical righteousness actively speaks up to protect the vulnerable from social shame.
