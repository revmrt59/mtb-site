SHEAVES - ( omer , Strong's H6016 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word omer refers to a bundle of freshly cut grain stalks bound together after being reaped. In the ancient near-eastern agricultural context, these sheaves represented the tangible fruit of labor and God’s physical provision for His covenant people. In the narrative of Ruth 2:15, Boaz’s command to allow Ruth to glean "even among the sheaves" is an act of extraordinary favor and protective grace. Rather than leaving her to forage on the vulnerable fringes of the field, Boaz welcomes her into the secure, abundant center of the harvest, illustrating how God's redeeming grace provides for the needy far beyond the letter of the law.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun omer derives from the root verb amar, which means to bind together, gather, or manipulate grain. In the ancient world, harvesting was a labor-intensive, multi-step process. Reapers used sickles to cut standing stalks of barley or wheat. Behind them, other workers gathered these stalks and tied them into bundles, or sheaves (omer), so they could be transported to the threshing floor.

Additionally, omer serves as a dry unit of measurement, representing approximately one-tenth of an ephah (roughly two quarts). This dual identity creates a beautiful biblical word picture: the omer is both the physical bundle of the harvest and the precise daily measure of manna that God provided to feed each Israelite in the wilderness (Exodus 16:16). Thus, the sheaf is fundamentally a symbol of God's direct, daily, and life-sustaining provision.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term denotes:

• A bundle of cut stalks of grain bound together (a sheaf).

• A dry measure of capacity, equivalent to one-tenth of an ephah.

In agricultural passages, it refers exclusively to the bound bundles of grain. In the context of the book of Ruth, it designates the fully harvested stalks belonging to the landowner before they are threshed and winnowed.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, the sheaf represents the concrete reality of harvest blessings and the reward of diligent labor.

• The Reward of Labor: To bind and carry sheaves is the ultimate joy of the agriculturalist, representing the successful culmination of a growing season.

• The Firstfruits offering: The "sheaf of the firstfruits" was waved before the Lord as an act of thanksgiving and dedication, recognizing that the entire harvest belonged to Yahweh.

• The Provision for the Marginalized: Under the Mosaic Law, any sheaf accidentally left behind in the field was not to be retrieved by the owner. It was divinely designated as property for the poor, the widow, and the stranger (Deuteronomy 24:19).

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of omer in the text carries profound theological weight:

• Covenant Kindness (Hesed): The law required landowners to leave forgotten sheaves for the poor. Boaz, however, goes far beyond the letter of the law. He does not wait for a sheaf to be forgotten; he actively commands his young men to let Ruth glean directly among the standing sheaves. This reflects the heart of God's covenant mercy, which does not merely offer begrudging survival to the foreigner but invites them into abundant blessing.

• A Foretaste of Redemption: The sheaf is the physical evidence of God "visiting His people of Bethlehem to give them bread" (Ruth 1:6). By bringing Ruth into the midst of the sheaves, Boaz acts as a human agent of God's protective wings, demonstrating how God shelters the vulnerable.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:15, Boaz issues a highly unusual command: "Let her glean even among the sheaves, and do not reproach her."

According to standard harvest customs, gleaners were strictly restricted to the perimeter of the field, picking up only the single, stray stalks dropped by the reapers. They were absolutely forbidden from approaching the bound sheaves, as this would make it incredibly easy to steal high-quality grain directly from the owner’s gathered bundles. Furthermore, young female gleaners were highly vulnerable to harassment by the male harvesters on the open field.

By granting Ruth permission to glean among the sheaves, Boaz completely alters her status. She is protected from "reproach" (insult, shame, or physical harm) and is positioned where the grain is thickest and most accessible. She is treated not as a desperate scavenger, but as an honored guest of the household.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 24:19: "When you reap your harvest in your field, and forget a sheaf in the field, you shall not go back to get it; it shall be for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow, that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands."

• Leviticus 23:10: "Speak to the children of Israel, and say to them: 'When you come into the land which I give to you, and reap its harvest, then you shall bring a sheaf of the firstfruits of your harvest to the priest.'"

• Leviticus 23:15: "And you shall count for yourselves from the day after the Sabbath, from the day that you brought the sheaf of the wave offering: seven Sabbaths shall be completed."

• Job 24:10: "They go about naked, without clothing, and hungry they carry the sheaves;"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), omer in Ruth 2:15 is translated using the word dragmaton (the genitive plural of dragma), which refers to a handful or sheaf of grain.

In the New Testament, the spiritual principle of the "firstfruits" sheaf (omer) finds its ultimate fulfillment in Jesus Christ. In 1 Corinthians 15:20, Christ is called the "firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep." Just as the presentation of the first sheaf of the harvest sanctified and guaranteed the rest of the coming crop, the resurrection of Jesus guarantees the future resurrection harvest of all believers.
