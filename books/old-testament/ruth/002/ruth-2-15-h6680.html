COMMANDED - ( tsavah , Strong's H6680 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb tsavah refers to the act of giving a charge, order, or command, establishing a binding instruction backed by authority. In Ruth 2:15, it describes Boaz issuing a protective decree to his young men concerning Ruth. This act of "commanding" shifts Ruth's status from a vulnerable, foreign gleaner on the margins to a protected individual under the canopy of Boaz's authority and covenant-keeping kindness. It demonstrates how authority can be wielded to shield the vulnerable and extend grace far beyond the letter of the law.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near Eastern agrarian world, landowners possessed absolute authority over their estates, fields, and laborers. A master’s word was law within his domain. Harvest fields were busy, highly competitive, and often dangerous places, especially for foreign women who had no male protector. Vulnerable gleaners faced harassment, abuse, and exploitation. Against this background, the word tsavah functions as a protective shield. When an authoritative figure like Boaz "commanded" his workers, he established an inviolable boundary. His word redrew the social lines of the field, transforming a potentially hostile workspace into a sanctuary of safety and provision for Ruth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb tsavah is a primary root occurring over 500 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in the intensive Pi'el stem. It signifies to lay a charge upon, give orders to, commission, or decree. Unlike a mere request, suggestion, or invitation, tsavah carries legal weight and demands obedience based on the hierarchical status of the speaker. It denotes the formal exercise of authority to organize, direct, or restrict behavior. The word is used of kings issuing decrees, fathers giving dying charges to their households, and most frequently, God commanding His covenant people.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, tsavah is the standard term for divine and human mandates.

• The Divine Charge: It is the word used when God "commanded" the light to shine, when He "commanded" Noah concerning the ark, and when He gave the commandments on Mount Sinai. The word picture is one of bringing order out of chaos through authoritative speech.

• The Covenant Directive: When used by parents or leaders (such as Abraham, Jacob, or Moses), tsavah refers to transmitting a sacred legacy or moral boundary to the next generation. It pictures a life-ordering boundary marker that preserves the family's spiritual path.

• The Sovereign Decree: In military or administrative contexts, it depicts a superior appointing a subordinate to a specific post or duty, leaving no room for negotiation.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, tsavah reveals the nature of true biblical authority. In the biblical worldview, authority is not given for self-aggrandizement but for the stewardship of order, justice, and mercy.

• Reflecting the Character of God: Boaz's command in Ruth 2:15 mirrors the heart of Yahweh. Just as God commands the clouds, the blessings, and the laws of hospitality to care for the poor, Boaz uses his human authority to mandate kindness and protection.

• The Active Nature of Hesed (Lovingkindness): Hesed is not merely a warm feeling; it is covenant love in action. By issuing a formal command (tsavah), Boaz translates his compassionate feelings into an institutional reality for his household. He legally binds his young men to act graciously toward Ruth, demonstrating that righteousness actively creates environments where the vulnerable can thrive.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:15, the context highlights the extraordinary generosity of Boaz. Ruth has just received permission to glean in the field. When she rises to continue working, Boaz privately commands (tsavah) his young men: "Let her glean even among the sheaves, and do not reproach her."

• Exceeding the Law: Under the Law of Moses (Leviticus 19:9-10), landowners were required to leave the corners of the fields and the fallen drops for the poor. However, gleaners were strictly forbidden from gathering "among the sheaves"—the bundled grain ready for the threshing floor. By commanding his men to allow this, Boaz overrides standard protocol to grant her privileged access.

• Securing Her Dignity: The phrase "do not reproach her" (or do not humiliate/shame her) indicates that Boaz's command was a preventative measure. He knew the young men might resent a foreign woman taking the premium grain. His command proactively neutralizes any potential hostility, preserving both her safety and her dignity.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The noun form of this root is mitzvah, which is the standard Hebrew word for a "commandment" or "precept." In post-biblical Jewish tradition, a mitzvah came to refer not only to a legal commandment but also to any act of kindness or charity. This semantic development is beautifully illustrated in Ruth 2:15. Boaz’s command (tsavah) to his servants is both a strict house rule and a profound act of charity (mitzvah). It bridges the gap between law and love, showing that the ultimate goal of any command is the preservation of life and the expression of grace.
