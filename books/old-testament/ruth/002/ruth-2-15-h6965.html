ROSE UP - ( qum , Strong's H6965 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qum means to arise, stand up, or establish oneself. In the context of Ruth 2:15, it describes Ruth rising from her meal to resume her laborious task of gleaning in Boaz's field. Far from a mere physical movement, her rising represents a pivotal step of faith, diligence, and obedience under God's providential care. It marks a transition from receiving immediate grace at Boaz's table to actively cooperating with that grace through hard work.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• qum is a foundational Hebrew root signifying movement from a lower physical position to a higher one, such as sitting, kneeling, or lying down to standing.

• It evokes images of resurrection, mobilization, preparation for action, and the validation of a covenant or promise.

• In the ancient Near Eastern world, rising up was the necessary precursor to labor, battle, worship, or speaking with authority. It denotes readiness, intent, and agency.

• Historically, the posture of standing represents readiness to serve. By rising up, Ruth positions herself as a humble and active servant ready to gather the provision God has made available.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of qum is to arise, stand up, stand, rise, or establish.

• In the Qal stem—the verbal stem used in Ruth 2:15—it expresses the physical action of getting up from a state of rest, sleep, or sitting.

• In other verbal stems, such as the Hiphil (causative), it carries the meaning of raising up, erecting, performing, or confirming (such as establishing a covenant or raising up descendants).

• The semantic range includes physical rising, rising to testify in a judicial setting, rising up in hostility, or rising to assume an office of leadership.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Movement: qum is often used to transition a narrative from preparation to execution. For example, Abraham rose (qum) early in the morning to obey God's command regarding Isaac.

• Spiritual and Emotional Restoration: The term is used metaphorically to describe rising out of a state of grief, depression, or spiritual despondency. Abraham rose (qum) from mourning Sarah to find her a burial place.

• Divine Intervention: The word frequently describes God "arising" to defend the helpless, execute judgment, or fulfill His covenant promises.

• National and Political Rise: It describes the rising of historical figures, prophets, or kings to power, showing that human authorities only stand because of divine appointment.

4. Theological Implications

• Synergy of Grace and Effort: qum highlights the beautiful harmony between divine sovereignty and human responsibility. God provides the protection and the abundance in the field, but Ruth must physically rise up to gather it. Grace does not produce passivity; it empowers activity.

• Theology of Restoration: In the book of Ruth, Naomi and Ruth begin in a state of physical, emotional, and economic prostration. The frequent use of qum in the narrative signals the beginning of their restoration—rising out of despair, poverty, and widowhood into a new standing before God.

• Foreshadowing Redemption: Ruth's rising points to the ultimate "raising up" of a Redeemer. Just as Boaz raises up the name of the dead through inheritance, God raises up Jesus Christ (the ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer) to bring life out of death.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:15, the action occurs immediately after Boaz has shown immense favor to Ruth, inviting her to eat bread, dip her morsel in the vinegar, and eat until she is satisfied (Ruth 2:14).

• Having received this extraordinary grace, Ruth does not become complacent, demanding, or lazy. She does not take her seat at the table as a permanent entitlement. Instead, she "rose up to glean."

• Her immediate rising demonstrates her humility, character, and work ethic. She views Boaz’s kindness not as an excuse to stop working, but as the strength-giving nourishment needed to work harder.

• This act of rising up triggers further generosity. Because she rises to work, Boaz commands his young men to let her glean even among the standing sheaves and to purposely pull out grain from the bundles for her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:3: "So Abraham rose early in the morning and saddled his donkey..." (The use of qum here highlights prompt, unquestioning obedience to God).

• Joshua 1:2: "Moses My servant is dead. Now therefore, arise, go over this Jordan..." (God commands Joshua to qum to step into leadership and inherit the land).

• Judges 5:7: "Village life ceased, it ceased in Israel, until I, Deborah, arose, arose a mother in Israel." (Indicates rising to a position of leadership, courage, and deliverance).

• Micah 7:8: "Do not rejoice over me, my enemy; when I fall, I will arise; when I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light to me." (A powerful expression of hope, resilience, and ultimate restoration).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between sitting (to eat) in verse 14 and rising (to work) in verse 15 underscores a healthy, biblical rhythm of receiving grace and executing duty.

• This pattern mirrors the spiritual life of a believer: we sit at the Lord's table to be fed, nourished, and comforted by His presence, and then we rise up to serve Him diligently in the harvest field of the world.
