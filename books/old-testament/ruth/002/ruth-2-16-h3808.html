NOT - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' expresses absolute negation, permanent prohibition, or unconditional denial. In Ruth 2:16, Boaz uses this particle to issue a strict, authoritative command to his young men, forbidding them from rebuking, humiliating, or embarrassing Ruth as she gleans even among the sheaves. This small but powerful word showcases Boaz’s protective care, setting a structural boundary of grace that ensures Ruth is treated with dignity rather than the hostility typically shown to foreign gleaners.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The particle lo' is the primary and most common adverb of negation in Biblical Hebrew, occurring over 5,000 times across the Old Testament. Unlike the subjective negative particle 'al, which typically expresses a temporary, immediate, or emotional appeal ("do not do this right now"), lo' represents an objective, permanent, and absolute "no." It is the word of ultimate boundaries, most famously used in the negative prohibitions of the Ten Commandments.

In the Ancient Near Eastern agricultural context, foreign gleaners—especially young, vulnerable widows like Ruth—faced severe social hazards. They were frequently harassed, driven off, or insulted by field workers who viewed them as nuisances or thieves. By placing lo' before the verb "rebuke" (ga'ar), Boaz establishes an unyielding wall of protection around Ruth, treating her safety and dignity as an absolute, non-negotiable law in his fields.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• lo' functions as the absolute negative adverb. When paired with an imperfect verb, it expresses an absolute command or a permanent prohibition ("you shall not"). It signifies a complete reversal or total absence of the action described by the accompanying verb.

In the specific context of Ruth 2:16, lo' is coupled with ga'ar (to rebuke, chide, corrupt, or drive away with harsh words), creating an absolute injunction: "You must under no circumstances rebuke her." It indicates that any form of reproach, verbal warning, or attempt to restrict her access is completely forbidden.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The particle lo' does not carry a visual root graphic of its own, but its usage paints a stark boundary line. It functions like a locked gate or an impenetrable shield.

• In legal and covenantal texts, lo' establishes the absolute limits of moral and ethical behavior. It represents the objective standard of God's will.

• In narrative literature, when spoken by a master of the estate (like Boaz), lo' carries the full weight of legal authority. To cross a lo' issued by the master is to invite immediate dismissal or judgment. It turns a personal preference into an inviolable house decree.

4. Theological Implications

The use of lo' in Ruth 2:16 reflects the heart of covenantal kindness (chesed). Boaz's prohibition is not a casual suggestion but a structural defense of the vulnerable. It mirrors God's own absolute prohibitions designed to protect the poor, the fatherless, and the widow.

This absolute negation highlights the transition of Ruth from a helpless outcast to one under the protective wings of the God of Israel. Boaz becomes the human instrument of God's protective "not"—ensuring that no harm, shame, or reproach touches her. It illustrates how grace actively restrains the natural hostility of the world, silencing the voice of condemnation and opening the floodgates of abundant provision.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:15-16, Boaz gives specific instructions to his young men. He permits Ruth to glean even "among the sheaves"—a privilege normally forbidden, as it allowed direct access to the harvested grain before it was processed.

Knowing this counter-cultural permission might provoke resentment or harsh corrections from his workers, Boaz preemptively uses lo' with ga'ar ("do not rebuke/humiliate her"). The nuance is crucial: Boaz does not merely tolerate Ruth's presence; he commands his workers to actively facilitate her gleaning (dropping handfuls on purpose) while strictly forbidding any discouraging words. The absolute "not" removes any loophole for passive-aggressive exclusion or verbal abuse by his staff.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 20:13: "You shall not murder."

• Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

• Leviticus 19:9: "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful contrast exists between lo' and the other Hebrew negative particle, 'al. While 'al is often used for immediate, situational, or emotional appeals (such as Ruth saying "Do not entreat me to leave you" in Ruth 1:16), Boaz’s use of lo' in Ruth 2:16 establishes a firm, objective, and permanent policy for his field. It signals to his workers that this is a settled rule of conduct, not a passing request.
