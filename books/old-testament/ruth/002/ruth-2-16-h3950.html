GLEAN - ( laqat , Strong's H3950 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb laqat refers to the action of picking up, gathering, or collecting items from the ground one by one, most notably leftover grain after an agricultural harvest. In the context of Ruth 2:16, it describes the practice of gleaning—a divinely mandated social safety net in Israel designed to sustain the poor, the widow, and the stranger. In this passage, the word transcends a mere survival mechanism, becoming a vivid portrait of God's active grace and generous providence channeled through the kindness of Boaz toward Ruth.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite agriculture, harvesting was done by hand. Reapers would cut the standing stalks of grain with sickles, and others would follow behind to bind the stalks into sheaves. Inevitably, some stalks of grain would fall to the ground.

Under the Mosaic Law, landowners were explicitly forbidden from going back to pick up these fallen pieces or harvesting the very corners of their fields. Instead, these leftovers were to be abandoned to the poor, the widow, the fatherless, and the resident foreigner. This practice of picking up the leftovers was known as gleaning (laqat). It was a unique welfare system that preserved the dignity of the needy by requiring physical labor rather than passive begging, while fostering a culture of generosity among landowners.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb laqat basically means "to gather, collect, or pick up." It is used for gathering food, stones, money, or even people. The term implies a diligent, piece-by-piece collection from the ground.

In a harvest context, laqat means to glean—to systematically walk behind the harvesters to gather what they left behind. The noun form of the word, leqet, refers to the actual gleanings or the leftover portion of the crop. The word does not suggest receiving a free handout; it demands active, physical effort to seek out, bend down, and gather the provision.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb laqat appears frequently in the Old Testament to illustrate different aspects of gathering:

• The Manna in the Wilderness: In Exodus 16, God rained bread from heaven, and the Israelites were commanded to go out and gather (laqat) a daily portion. This established laqat as an act of daily dependence on God's faithful provision.

• Gathering of Stones: In Genesis 31:46, Jacob’s relatives gathered (laqat) stones to build a memorial heap representing a covenant of peace.

• A Portrait of Humiliation: In Judges 1:7, defeated kings were forced to gather (laqat) scraps under the table of Adoni-Bezek, representing extreme loss of status and dependency.

• The Diligent Labor of Ruth: In Ruth 2, the word is used repeatedly (twelve times in this chapter alone) to depict Ruth’s tireless work in the fields to provide for Naomi and herself.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theology of laqat centers on the character of God as a protector of the vulnerable and the source of all provision.

• Providential Grace: God does not merely provide for the wealthy landowner; He establishes legal rights for the marginalized. Gleaning shows that God cares for the sustenance of those who have no land inheritance.

• The Dignity of Work: God’s provision through gleaning requires human agency. Ruth had to work hard from morning until evening, showing that faith in God's provision operates hand-in-hand with diligent labor.

• Grace Overcoming the Law: While the law mandated that landowners leave the fallen grain, Boaz went far beyond the legal requirements. He commanded his reapers to purposefully drop handfuls of grain for Ruth. This illustrates how divine grace exceeds the letter of the law to abundantly bless the needy.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:16, Boaz commands his young men: "Also let grain fall from the bundles for her; leave it that she may glean, and do not rebuke her."

Normally, a gleaner had to search carefully for accidental droppings and was often looked down upon or harassed by the workers. Boaz radically alters this dynamic. By commanding his workers to pull stalks out of the bundles on purpose (shalal) and leave them, he transforms Ruth's labor from a stressful search for scraps into a joyful gathering of abundance. The nuance of laqat here shifts from a desperate struggle for survival to an experience of overwhelming, unmerited favor. Ruth still performs the physical act of gathering, but the ground she walks on is saturated with deliberate grace.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 16:4 – "Then the LORD said to Moses, 'Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you. And the people shall go out and gather a certain quota every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in My law or not.'"

• Leviticus 19:9 – "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest."

• Ruth 2:2 – "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

• Ruth 2:7 – "And she said, 'Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves.' So she came and has continued from morning until now, except for a short stay in the house."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The book of Ruth showcases the movement from emptiness to fullness. The repetitive use of laqat highlights this transition. Ruth begins by hoping to glean just enough to survive, but through the covenant-keeping kindness (chesed) of Boaz, she gleans an ephah of barley in a single day—roughly half a bushel, an immense amount for a single gleaner.

This serves as a beautiful picture of the believer's relationship with Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer. We come to His fields empty and foreign, hoping only for scraps of mercy. Yet, He commands His blessings to be dropped in our path, turning our meager efforts into spiritual abundance.
