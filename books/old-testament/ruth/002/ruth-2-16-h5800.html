LEAVE - ( azab , Strong's H5800 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb azab primarily means to leave, forsake, loose, or abandon. In the pastoral and agricultural setting of Ruth 2:16, it describes Boaz instructing his young men to intentionally "leave behind" grain from the bundles for Ruth to glean. Rather than representing abandonment or neglect, this act of leaving represents an intentional, grace-filled provision that preserves the dignity of the recipient while reflecting the generous heart of the provider.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete root of azab carries the physical sense of letting go, loosening, or releasing something from one's hand, control, or possession. In ancient Near Eastern pastoral life, it was used to describe untying an animal, leaving land uncultivated during a Sabbath year, or releasing someone from an obligation. In an agricultural context, it refers to leaving portions of a harvest behind in the field. Under Mosaic Law, landowners were commanded to azab (leave) the corners of their fields and the gleanings for the poor, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow. It is a word that transitions from a physical act of relinquishing possession to a profound moral and spiritual act of provision.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb azab occurs over 200 times in the Old Testament, spanning several distinct nuances determined by context:

• To leave behind, depart from, or relinquish control of a physical object, place, or person.

• To forsake, abandon, or desert a covenant, commandment, or relationship (frequently used to describe Israel's faithlessness to Yahweh).

• To commit, entrust, or hand over someone or something to another's care or safekeeping.

• To loose, release, or set free from a burden or restraint.

In the Qal stem in Ruth 2:16, the verb functions as an imperative instruction: "and you shall leave [it]," commanding the harvesters to deliberately relinquish ownership of the grain so that Ruth may gather it.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes azab to paint vivid pictures of relational and spiritual dynamics:

• The Pain of Desertion: It depicts the deep emotional wound of a wife forsaken in her youth or a child abandoned by parents.

• Covenantal Treachery: It serves as the primary theological term for turning away from God's law or worshiping idols, portraying a deliberate walkout on a sacred relationship.

• Sovereign Protection: Conversely, it highlights God's faithful promise never to abandon His people. Even when earthly parents azab their children, Yahweh takes them up.

• Grace in Relinquishment: In agricultural laws, it represents a willing hands-off approach to wealth, where landowners do not squeeze out every drop of profit but loose their grip on their resources so that the vulnerable can survive.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of azab in the context of Ruth 2:16 shines when viewed against the backdrop of God's covenantal character. The Torah required Israelites to leave (azab) the gleanings of their harvests for the poor (Leviticus 19:10). This law was designed to prevent systemic poverty and ensure that the marginalized were not reduced to begging; they worked for their sustenance, but the path was cleared for them.

When Boaz orders his men to azab extra grain, he elevates the law from a cold legal obligation to an instrument of extravagant grace. Boaz does not merely tolerate Ruth's presence; he actively engineers her abundance. This reflects the character of God, who does not merely meet our bare survival needs but leaves us "handfuls of purpose" along our path, ensuring we are sustained by His abundant mercy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:16, Boaz commands his young men, "Also let some from the bundles fall purposely for her; leave (azab) it that she may glean, and do not rebuke her."

The context here is crucial. Ruth is a destitute foreign widow, working under the hot sun to keep herself and Naomi alive. Boaz, acting as a redeemer-figure, takes notice of her faith and diligence. His use of azab is highly unusual and deeply compassionate. Normally, harvesters would carefully guard the bundles to prevent loss. Boaz, however, commands his workers to pull grain purposely from the bundles and leave (azab) it behind.

This creates a beautiful, dignified form of charity. Ruth is not handed a patronizing welfare check; she still has to work, bend down, and glean. Yet her basket is filled because of a hidden, deliberate grace. The "leaving" of the grain is not an act of carelessness by negligent workers, but the intentional, gracious provision of the master of the harvest.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:24 - "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

• Leviticus 19:10 - "And you shall not glean your vineyard, nor shall you gather every grape of your vineyard; you shall leave them for the poor and the stranger: I am the LORD your God."

• Deuteronomy 31:6 - "Be strong and of good courage, do not fear nor be afraid of them; for the LORD your God, He is the One who goes with you. He will not leave you nor forsake you."

• Psalm 22:1 - "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me? Why are You so far from helping Me, And from the words of My groaning?"

• Proverbs 28:13 - "He who covers his sins will not prosper, But whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy."

7. Other Relevant Insights

For preaching and teaching, the use of azab in Ruth 2:16 offers a powerful template for pastoral ministry and Christian charity. True biblical charity often looks like "leaving" resources, opportunities, and encouragement in the paths of others so they can gather them with their dignity intact.

Furthermore, this passage preaches the quiet, providential grace of God. Often in our lives, we look back and see unexpected provisions, open doors, and sudden blessings. We may think we simply "stumbled" upon them through our own hard work. In reality, our great Redeemer, Jesus Christ, has gone before us, commanding His blessings to be dropped "purposely" on our path, instructing the circumstances of life to "leave" them for us to gather.
