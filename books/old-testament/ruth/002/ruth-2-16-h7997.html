LET FALL ON PURPOSE - ( shalal , Strong's 7997 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shalal fundamentally refers to the physical act of drawing out, pulling out, or stripping. While the term is predominantly employed in military and historical narratives to describe the seizing of plunder or taking of spoil, its single occurrence in the agricultural setting of Ruth 2:16 carries a beautifully distinct, peaceful, and gracious nuance. In this passage, Boaz instructs his young men to deliberately pull out grain from the harvested sheaves and leave it behind for Ruth to glean. This intentional act of kindness far exceeds the basic requirements of the Mosaic Law, serving as a powerful illustration of proactive grace, intentional generosity, and the covert ways in which divine providence ensures the care of the vulnerable.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, harvesting was a highly structured, labor-intensive process. Reapers cut the standing grain with sickles, while others gathered the stalks and bound them into bundles or sheaves. According to Mosaic Law, landowners were commanded to leave the corners of their fields unharvested and were forbidden from gathering the leftover grain that fell accidentally during the harvest. This practice of "gleaning" was a legal safety net designed to sustain the poor, the widow, and the stranger (Leviticus 19:9-10; Deuteronomy 24:19). Under normal circumstances, a gleaner like Ruth would follow at a distance behind the reapers, gathering only the sparse, accidental droppings.

The word picture of shalal in Ruth 2:16 dramatically alters this dynamic. Boaz instructs his reapers to actively reach into the already-bound bundles and pull out stalks of grain, deliberately dropping them in Ruth’s path. The verb evokes the visual of a reaper intentionally extracting wealth from his own storage to place it on the ground for a poor foreigner, transforming a passive allowance of legal gleaning into an active, aggressive demonstration of grace.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shalal occurs 17 times in the Hebrew Scriptures. Its primary lexical definition is to draw out, pull out, or strip. In approximately 16 of its 17 occurrences, it is used in a military context to denote the stripping of a defeated enemy or the taking of booty, spoil, and plunder.

In Ruth 2:16, the verb appears in an emphatic, double-intensity grammatical construction. It utilizes both the infinitive absolute shal and the imperfect verb tashollu (shal-tashollu). In Hebrew grammar, this repetition intensifies the action, signaling an absolute, unavoidable obligation. It translates to "surely pull out," "deliberately draw out," or "let fall on purpose." Boaz is not merely suggesting that his workers be careless; he is giving a direct, emphatic command to actively deplete their own gathered bundles for Ruth’s benefit.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Spoils of War: In the wider Old Testament, shalal is almost exclusively associated with the violent stripping of the conquered. It describes victorious armies taking the valuable possessions, livestock, and clothing of their enemies. The physical motion is one of taking by force—drawing out or stripping away what belongs to another.

• The Spoils of Grace: In Ruth, this word picture is radically inverted. Instead of a soldier stripping an enemy to impoverish them and enrich himself, a wealthy landowner "strips" his own harvest to enrich a vulnerable, destitute widow. The motion of drawing out remains the same, but the motivation is entirely transformed from greed and conquest to mercy and covenant love (chesed).

4. Theological Implications

• Grace Beyond the Law: The Mosaic Law provided for the marginalized by allowing them to work for accidental leftovers. Boaz’s command to shalal grain shows that true godliness does not stop at the boundaries of legal obligation. Grace does not merely permit the poor to survive; it actively and intentionally ensures that they overflow with abundance.

• God's Hidden Providence: This word provides a profound theological metaphor for the believer’s relationship with God. Ruth went to the field hoping to find favor, expecting a hard day of meager gathering. She was unaware of Boaz's private command to his reapers. Similarly, God operates behind the scenes, commanding blessings to be dropped "on purpose" along the paths of His people, turning their desperate labor into encounters with divine abundance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The context of Ruth 2:16 is critical to understanding the depth of Boaz's character. Boaz issues this command secretly to his young men, out of Ruth's hearing. This discreet instruction preserves Ruth's dignity. She is not handed a patronizing handout; she is still allowed to work, sweat, and gather. Yet, her efforts are supernaturally blessed because the reapers are dropping prime grain on purpose. This illustrates the biblical model of charity: preserving the recipient's dignity while ensuring their needs are met with overwhelming generosity. Furthermore, by framing this command as an absolute requirement (shal-tashollu), Boaz protects Ruth from any resentment or rebuke from his workers, who might otherwise begrudge a foreigner taking from their master's profits.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 2:16: "Also let fall some from the bundles on purpose for her; leave them, that she may glean, and do not rebuke her."

• Isaiah 10:6: "I will send him against an ungodly nation, and against the people of My wrath I will give him charge, to seize the spoil, to take the prey, and to tread them down like the mire of the streets."

• Ezekiel 26:12: "They will plunder your riches and pillage your merchandise; they will break down your walls and destroy your pleasant houses, and they will lay your stones, your timber, and your soil in the midst of the water."

• Ezekiel 38:12: "to take plunder and to take booty, to stretch out your hand against the waste places that are again inhabited, and against a people gathered from the nations, who have acquired livestock and goods, who dwell in the middle of the land."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between the typical warlike use of shalal and its peaceful use in Ruth highlights the theme of redemption that runs throughout the book. Ruth's life had been "plundered" by famine, death, and poverty in Moab. When she arrives in Bethlehem, she is empty. Through Boaz, who acts as a type of the Kinsman-Redeemer (Goel), God reverses this plunder. By commanding his men to shalal grain for her, Boaz begins the process of filling her emptiness, demonstrating that redemptive love actively restores what life and tragedy have stripped away.
