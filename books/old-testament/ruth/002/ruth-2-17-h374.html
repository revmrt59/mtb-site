EPHAH - ( 'ephah , Strong's H0374 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ephah designates a standard dry unit of measurement in the ancient Near East, equivalent to approximately three-fifths of a bushel, or about 22 liters (roughly 30 to 40 pounds of grain). In Ruth 2:17, Ruth's gleaning of an entire "ephah" of barley in a single day serves as a powerful, concrete sign of God's abundant, overflowing provision and Boaz's extraordinary generosity, far exceeding the typical handfuls gathered by a destitute widow.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

• The 'ephah was one of the most common dry measures used in ancient Israel for grain, flour, and other dry goods.

• Historically, the term is an Egyptian loanword referring to a basket or a large measuring container. Over time, this physical vessel became standardized in Israelite commerce.

• An ephah represents ten omers (Exodus 16:36). In the wilderness, an omer was the daily portion of manna allotted to a single person. Therefore, an ephah represents a ten-day supply of food for one individual.

• For an impoverished widow to glean an entire ephah of barley in a single day was a physical impossibility under normal circumstances. It speaks of a heavy, overflowing basket that required significant physical strength to carry home.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word 'ephah refers specifically to a dry measure of capacity and the physical vessel used for measuring it.

• The exact modern equivalent is between 20 to 22 liters (approximately 5.3 to 5.8 US gallons, or 0.6 bushels). In weight, an ephah of barley would weigh approximately 30 to 40 pounds (14 to 18 kilograms).

• In legal and prophetic texts, the word also symbolizes commercial honesty and societal justice, often paired with weights and other liquid measures to demand fair business transactions.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Household Abundance: In daily life, an ephah was a substantial wholesale or storage quantity, not a standard daily kitchen measure (which was typically measured in much smaller omers or seahs).

• Sacrificial Portions: An ephah, or fractions of it, is frequently prescribed for grain offerings, showing that God requires substantial, measurable devotion from His people.

• Standard of Integrity: The physical container itself represents societal justice. A "shrunk" or "wicked" ephah represents systemic greed, while a "just ephah" represents a community walking in covenant faithfulness.

### 4. Theological Implications

• Providential Abundance: The presence of an entire ephah in Ruth's hands underscores the theme of hesed (covenant love and kindness). God does not merely provide survival-level sustenance; He provides in superabundance.

• Grace Over Merits: Ruth went out hoping to find a little favor and gather leftovers. She returned with a massive bounty. This mirrors the economy of divine grace, where God does exceedingly abundantly above all that His people ask or think.

• The Character of the Redeemer: Boaz, acting as a type of Christ, instructs his young men to purposely let grain fall from the bundles. His generosity is measured not by how little he can give to satisfy the letter of the law, but by how much he can pour out to bless the needy.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:17, Ruth "gleaned in the field until evening, and beat out what she had gleaned, and it was about an ephah of barley."

• Gleaning was a survival mechanism for the poorest of society (Leviticus 19:9-10). A typical gleaner might gather enough grain for a single meal or, at best, a couple of pounds of grain after a long, exhausting day of labor.

• The mention of "about an ephah" acts as a narrative climax in chapter 2. When Naomi sees the sheer volume of grain Ruth brought home, she immediately knows someone of great wealth and kindness has looked upon her. Naomi's question, "Where have you gleaned today?" (Ruth 2:19), is a direct reaction to the shocking physical evidence of the ephah of barley.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Levitus 19:36 – "You shall have honest scales, honest weights, a just ephah, and a just hin: I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt."

• Deuteronomy 25:15 – "You shall have a perfect and just weight, a perfect and just ephah, that your days may be lengthened in the land which the LORD your God is giving you."

• Judges 6:19 – "So Gideon went in and prepared a young goat, and unleavened bread from an ephah of flour..."

• 1 Samuel 17:17 – "Then Jesse said to his son David, 'Take now for your brothers an ephah of this dried grain and these ten loaves, and run to the camp to your brothers.'"

• Proverbs 20:10 – "Diverse weights and diverse measures, They are both alike an abomination to the LORD." (The Hebrew text literally reads "an ephah and an ephah," referring to using a large measure for buying and a small measure for selling).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Labor of Ruth: While the abundance was a gift, Ruth still had to "beat out" (thresh) the grain herself. The passage emphasizes the beautiful synergy between diligent human labor and divine provision. God provided the field, the generous redeemer, and the protection, but Ruth worked hard until evening.

• Nutritional Value: A single person requires about 1 to 2 pounds of bread per day. An ephah of barley would easily feed Ruth and Naomi for several weeks, demonstrating that one day in the field of the redeemer secured their survival for nearly a month.
