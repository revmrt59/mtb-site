GLEANED - ( laqat , Strong's H3950 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb laqat means to gather, collect, or pick up items from the ground. In the context of ancient Israelite agricultural law, it refers specifically to the practice of "gleaning"—picking up the leftover grain dropped by harvesters. This practice was a God-ordained welfare system designed to provide for the poor, the stranger, the widow, and the orphan, allowing them to maintain their dignity through labor while ensuring their physical survival. In the story of Ruth, her gleaning in the field of Boaz highlights both her diligent resourcefulness and God's providential care.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The agricultural landscape of ancient Israel was governed by laws that protected the vulnerable. Under the Mosaic Covenant, landowners were forbidden from reaping to the very edges of their fields or gathering the droppings (the gleanings) of their harvest. Instead, these leftovers were to be left for the poor and the resident alien. The word laqat paints a picture of a humble, manual labor process. It describes bending down to the earth, searching through the stalks, and picking up individual heads of grain one by one. It was tedious, physically demanding work that required endurance and humility, as those who gleaned depended entirely on what was left behind by others.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb laqat refers to the action of picking up or gathering scattered items from the ground. It is used in several distinct contexts in the Old Testament:

• Agricultural Gleaning: The gathering of grain, olives, or grapes left behind after the primary harvest.

• Gathering Food: The collection of daily provisions, most notably the gathering of manna in the wilderness.

• General Collection: The picking up of objects, such as stones for a memorial, arrows, or money.

The core semantic concept is the manual, piece-by-piece accumulation of scattered objects, transforming what is scattered and lost into a gathered resource.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word laqat is rich with practical and spiritual imagery. In the wilderness narrative of Exodus 16, the Israelites were commanded to "gather" (laqat) a daily portion of manna. Here, the word illustrates complete dependence on divine provision; the food was freely given by God, but the people still had to actively go out and gather it. In the context of the harvest laws in Leviticus and Deuteronomy, laqat represents the intersection of divine charity and human dignity. God did not simply hand food to the impoverished; He provided the opportunity for them to work and gather (laqat) for themselves, preserving their agency and self-respect.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, laqat reveals the heart of God for the marginalized and the economically vulnerable. The laws of gleaning show that God claims ultimate ownership of the land and its yield. Landowners were merely stewards; they did not have the right to maximize their profits at the expense of the poor. By requiring landowners to leave the leket (the gleanings) behind, God institutionalized compassion. Furthermore, laqat illustrates the cooperative relationship between divine sovereignty and human responsibility. God provides the harvest and the opportunities, but mankind must labor to gather the blessings.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:17, the word laqat is used twice: "So she gleaned (laqat) in the field until evening, and beat out what she had gleaned (laqat)." The context of Ruth 2 highlights Ruth's extraordinary character and Boaz's exemplary righteousness. As a destitute Moabite widow, Ruth took the initiative to go into the fields to glean, exposing herself to potential danger and exhaustion. The double use of the word in this verse emphasizes her industriousness. She did not merely look for an easy handout; she labored "until evening." Her diligent gleaning resulted in an ephah of barley (about 30 to 50 pounds), an exceptionally large amount for a single day's gleaning, which testifies both to her hard work and to Boaz's deliberate generosity in telling his young men to let grain fall purposely for her.

• Psalm 104:28: "What You give them they gather; You open Your hand, they are filled with good."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Ancient Near East, welfare systems were virtually non-existent outside of Israel. The practice of laqat was unique because it was legally mandated by the sovereign God of Israel as a structural right for the poor, rather than a voluntary, sporadic act of personal charity. Additionally, the spiritual parallel in the New Testament is striking: just as Ruth gathered the physical grain left behind, believers are encouraged to diligently gather the spiritual bread of God's Word, which has been abundantly provided for our spiritual sustenance.
