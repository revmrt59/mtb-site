EVENING - ( ereb , Strong's H6153 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ereb denotes the twilight hours, sunset, or the closing of the day. In the narrative of Ruth, "evening" marks the boundary between grueling labor and the reception of provision. It symbolizes the completion of a faithful day's work and the transition to rest, safety, and the abundance provided by Boaz's grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word ereb is closely tied to the natural rhythms of light and darkness. It depicts the daily transition when the sun dips below the horizon, shadows stretch, and the intense heat of the day gives way to the cool night air.

• In the ancient Near East, life was strictly governed by daylight. The arrival of evening meant that outdoor work had to cease because of darkness and safety concerns. It was the designated hour for laborers to gather their tools, secure their earnings, and seek the safety of their homes.

• Figuratively, evening represents the natural boundary of human strength. It is the time when humanity acknowledges its limits, stops striving, and rests under the watch of the Creator.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The noun ereb refers to sunset, evening, or twilight. It signifies the period of declining light just before darkness fully sets in.

• It can designate the transition from the late afternoon to nightfall, often marked by the cooling of the day.

• In some contexts, it indicates the beginning of the biblical day, which commences at sunset rather than midnight.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word occurs over 130 times in the Hebrew Bible.

• It is frequently paired with "morning" (boker) to define a full day, most famously in the creation account of Genesis 1, where the evening and the morning mark the passage of each creative day.

• In the Mosaic Law, "until evening" is a standard temporal boundary. A person who became ceremonially unclean remained so "until evening," at which point they washed and were declared clean. Thus, evening represents a boundary of purification and transition.

• Evening is also depicted as a time of vulnerability, when wild beasts roam, enemies plan ambushes, and travelers must find lodging, making shelter and hospitality essential during this hour.

4. Theological Implications

• Evening serves as a physical reminder of God’s sovereignty over time and creation. He established the day and the night, and He sustains both.

• The evening sacrifice (minchah) was offered daily at twilight, indicating that God's people were to close their day by seeking atonement, expressing gratitude, and dedicating their night to Him.

• Spiritually, the transition to evening reminds believers that God's faithfulness endures through the darkness. Even when the light of day fades and the night brings uncertainty, the Lord remains a constant shield.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:17, Ruth gleans in the field "until evening." This detail highlights her extraordinary work ethic, determination, and physical endurance. As a vulnerable foreign widow, she did not cut her workday short or shrink from the intense heat; she labored until the daylight completely failed.

• The evening is also the moment of transition from labor to evaluation. Only when evening came did she "beat out" what she had gleaned. This process revealed the miraculous extent of her day's work: an ephah of barley (roughly 30 to 50 pounds).

• The mention of evening underscores the shift from Ruth's exhausting toil under the sun to the joyful discovery of Boaz's overwhelming grace and provision, which she would soon share with Naomi.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:5: "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. So the evening and the morning were the first day."

• Joshua 10:26: "And afterward Joshua struck them and killed them, and hanged them on five trees; and they were hanging on the trees until evening."

• 1 Kings 17:6: "The ravens brought him bread and meat in the morning, and bread and meat in the evening; and he drank from the brook."

• Psalm 104:23: "Man goes out to his work And to his labor until evening."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In Jewish tradition, the day begins at sunset ("evening"). Therefore, when Ruth finishes her labor at evening, she is not merely ending a hard day; she is stepping into a brand-new day of hope, redemption, and covenant relationship.

• The evening represents the turning point in the Book of Ruth. It marks the end of the season of bitter emptiness and the beginning of a season of harvest and restoration.
