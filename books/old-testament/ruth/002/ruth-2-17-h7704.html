FIELD - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to open country, cultivated land, or a specific plot of ground owned by an individual. In the book of Ruth, the "field" is not merely a geographic location but the providential stage where God’s grace, protection, and redeeming love unfold. As Ruth gleans in the field of Boaz, this ordinary place of agricultural labor becomes a sanctuary of divine provision, illustrating how God uses the commonplace circumstances of life to bring about His sovereign purposes and covenant redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, the sadeh was the lifeblood of agrarian society. Unlike modern farms with clearly defined fences, ancient Israelite fields were vast, open expanses of arable land. Individual holdings within these fields were divided only by narrow paths or small boundary stones. Because these fields were open and remote from the safety of walled cities, they were places of intense labor, vulnerability, and dependence on God. For a destitute foreigner and widow like Ruth, entering an open field to glean was a courageous act of faith, exposing her to potential harassment from workers and the elements.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term sadeh primarily denotes an open field, cultivated ground, pastureland, or the territory of a specific nation. It stands in contrast to the walled city (iyr) and the barren wilderness (midbar). In legal and agricultural contexts, it refers to a designated portion of land belonging to an individual. The word represents the soil that produces food, requiring human cultivation while remaining entirely dependent on the early and latter rains provided by God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sadeh is used in several distinct ways:

• The Place of Sustenance: It is the source of grain, wine, and oil, symbolizing God's physical provision for His covenant people.

• The Place of Vulnerability and Covenant Law: The open field is the specific location where God's welfare laws are put to the test, such as the commandment to leave the corners of the field unreaped for the poor and the stranger.

• A Metaphor for Spiritual Condition: A fertile, well-watered field represents blessing, obedience, and divine favor, while a wild, briar-choked field represents spiritual laziness and judgment.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the sadeh is the theater of God's quiet providence. In the book of Ruth, God does not speak from a burning bush or part a sea; instead, He works through the daily, mundane routines of the harvest field. The field reveals the heart of the covenant community. Boaz’s management of his field reflects his deep commitment to chesed (lovingkindness) as he exceeds the letter of the Mosaic law to protect and feed a vulnerable foreigner. Thus, the agricultural field becomes a picture of God's kingdom, where the marginalized find safety, abundance, and inclusion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:17, the text notes that Ruth "gleaned in the field until evening." The context of this specific sadeh is crucial:

• Providential Placement: Ruth "happened" to light upon the portion of the field belonging to Boaz. What seemed like chance was the guiding hand of God.

• A Safe Haven: Under the protection of Boaz, this field is transformed from a place of danger for a foreign woman into a sanctuary of dignity and generous provision.

• Abundant Reward: That she gleaned until evening and beat out an ephah of barley (about five gallons) demonstrates that this was no ordinary field. It was a place of superabundant grace, foreshadowing the spiritual harvest of redemption that would come through her lineage.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Israelite law, the redemption of land (sadeh) was directly linked to the redemption of families (Leviticus 25). When Naomi and Ruth returned from Moab, they were forced to sell or yield the family plot of land. The kinsman-redeemer's duty was not only to buy back the physical sadeh to keep it within the family tribe but also to marry the widow of the deceased landowner to carry on his name. Therefore, the physical harvest field where Ruth labored is dynamically connected to the legal and redemptive transaction Boaz carries out at the city gate. The field of labor ultimately leads to the home of rest.
