GLEANED - ( laqat , Strong's H3950 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb laqat means to gather, pick up, or collect. In the agrarian context of ancient Israel, it refers specifically to the practice of gleaning—collecting the leftover grain, olives, or grapes after the primary harvest has been completed. This action was legally protected by God's covenant law as a welfare provision for the poor, widows, orphans, and strangers. In Ruth 2:18, laqat describes Ruth’s diligent labor in the field of Boaz, which yielded an astonishingly abundant supply of barley. This word highlights both the physical effort required of the impoverished and the beautiful mechanism of divine providence that preserves human dignity.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The background of laqat is rooted in the agricultural life and social security system of ancient Israel. According to the Torah, land ownership was a stewardship under God, and landowners were forbidden to harvest their fields to the very edges or to go over their vines and fields a second time. The dropped or missed crops were legally reserved for those without land. Gleaning was not a handout; it required the marginalized to enter the fields, endure the heat of the day, and physically bend down to pick up individual stalks of grain. The word paints a picture of humble, strenuous work and daily dependence on God’s mercy and the generosity of landowners.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb laqat denotes the physical act of picking up or gathering objects from the ground. Its primary definitions include:

• To gather or collect natural items (such as stones, wood, or manna).

• To glean or pick up leftover produce from a harvested field, orchard, or vineyard.

• To collect or gather together people or items in a figurative or metaphoric sense.

In the context of the harvest, it carries the technical legal and agricultural sense of collecting the leftovers that the primary harvesters dropped or missed.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term occurs 37 times in the Hebrew Bible, conveying powerful word pictures of provision and collection:

• The Manna in the Wilderness (Exodus 16): The word is used repeatedly to describe the daily collection of manna. Israel had to go out each morning and gather (laqat) their daily bread. This established a word picture of daily obedience, physical effort, and direct trust in God’s ongoing provision.

• The Law of the Harvest (Leviticus 19 and 23): The law prohibited landowners from gathering (laqat) the fallen remnants of their own fields, leaving them for the poor. The word picture here is one of community responsibility and structural compassion.

• Ruth’s Exhaustive Labor (Ruth 2): The verb occurs 12 times in this single chapter, emphasizing Ruth’s active role. She is not a passive beggar; she is a gatherer, embodying the virtuous and hardworking woman who actively works to sustain her household.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, laqat illustrates the delicate balance between divine sovereignty and human responsibility. God provided the grain through the rain and soil, and He provided the legal right to glean through His law. However, Ruth still had to go out and gather it. God’s provision does not bypass human agency; rather, it dignifies it. Furthermore, the practice of laqat reveals God's deep concern for the poor. It establishes a welfare system that preserves the dignity of the recipient, who earns their bread through honest labor rather than dependency on arbitrary charity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:18, the word laqat is used when Ruth returns to Naomi and her mother-in-law sees what she "had gleaned." The context reveals an extraordinary nuance: the sheer volume of what Ruth gathered. Ruth returned with an ephah of barley (approximately 30 to 50 pounds), which was an immense yield for a single day of gleaning. Normally, a gleaner would gather only a fraction of this amount. The massive pile of gleaned grain instantly signaled to Naomi that Ruth had not merely picked up random scraps on the margins, but had been given special favor to gather even among the rich sheaves of Boaz's field.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation of the Old Testament, laqat is frequently translated by the Greek verb sullego, which means to gather, collect, or crop. This Greek term is utilized by Jesus in the New Testament, notably in the Parable of the Tares (Matthew 13), where the angels are described as gathering out the weeds at the final harvest. This connects the earthly agrarian act of gathering leftovers to the ultimate, divine harvest at the end of the age.
