TOOK UP - ( nasa' , Strong's H5375 )

Summary:

In the narrative of Ruth, the action of taking up a burden represents the physical manifestation of covenant faithfulness and the reception of divine favor. When Ruth lifts up the heavy ephah of barley she gleaned in the fields of Boaz, she is not merely transporting grain; she is carrying the tangible proof of God’s abundant grace and Boaz’s protection back to her destitute mother-in-law. This simple act of manual labor bridges the gap between God's sovereign provision and the practical daily sustenance of a vulnerable family, illustrating how faith expresses itself through diligent, loving service.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew verb nasa' is a rich, dynamic word that conveys the physical action of lifting, hoisting, carrying, or bearing a load. The underlying imagery is that of bending down to grasp a heavy object, exerting strength to raise it from the ground, and sustaining its weight to transport it elsewhere. In the ancient agricultural world, this was a daily reality. Workers in the fields constantly lifted tools, sheaves, and baskets of grain.

In the context of Ruth 2:18, the word picture is incredibly vivid. Ruth had spent the entire day gleaning in the fields of Boaz, beating out what she had gathered. The yield was about an ephah of barley—approximately 30 to 50 pounds. For a young woman to lift and carry such a heavy load back to Bethlehem was an arduous physical task. The word picture emphasizes Ruth’s physical stamina, her diligent work ethic, and her unwavering commitment to provide for Naomi.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of nasa' is broad and versatile, spanning physical, figurative, and theological categories.

• To lift up or raise: Hoisting a physical object, raising one’s eyes to look, raising one's voice to speak or weep, or lifting one's hands in prayer.

• To bear or carry: Supporting a heavy burden, carrying armor, or bearing a child in one's arms.

• To take away or remove: Carrying off plunder, lifting away a burden, or taking away guilt and punishment (which forms the basis for the Old Testament concept of forgiveness).

In Ruth 2:18, the verb functions in its primary, literal sense: to lift up and carry a physical object. Ruth hoisted the heavy sack of beaten-out barley to carry it into the city.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes nasa' to depict various aspects of human and divine activity:

• The Posture of Prayer and Worship: Lifting up the hands or the soul to God as an act of dependence.

• The Burden of Leadership: Moses and the elders bearing the weight of the people's complaints and needs.

• The Work of Forgiveness: God lifting off the heavy burden of sin and carrying it away from the transgressor.

• The Shepherd’s Care: Yahweh lifting up and carrying His people through the wilderness, just as a father carries his son.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nasa' connects the physical realm of labor with the spiritual reality of God's redemption. In Ruth 2:18, the act of lifting up the barley represents the active reception of grace. God did not drop bread from heaven directly into Naomi's lap; instead, He provided the opportunity, Boaz showed favor, and Ruth labored. Ruth’s act of lifting and carrying the grain shows that divine providence operates in tandem with human faithfulness.

Furthermore, this verb foreshadows the theme of redemption and burden-bearing. Just as Ruth physically bore the heavy load of food to sustain Naomi, she would eventually ask Boaz to "spread his wings" over her—a request to lift her out of widowhood and bear the responsibility of her redemption. Ultimately, this points forward to the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ, who lifts and carries the heavy burden of our sins (Isaiah 53:4).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Within the immediate context of Ruth 2, the word nasa' highlights the shocking abundance of Boaz's generosity. A typical gleaner would go home with a small handful of grain, easily carried in a pocket or small pouch. Ruth, however, had to lift up a massive load because Boaz had commanded his young men to purposely let grain fall from the bundles for her.

When Ruth arrives in the city, the sheer weight of what she carries tells the story before she even speaks a word. Naomi sees the heavy load Ruth is bearing and instantly knows that someone of great wealth and kindness has looked upon her daughter-in-law. The physical lifting of the grain marks the turning point in the narrative, moving the characters from empty-handed grief to full-handed hope.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 19:4: "‘You have seen what I did to the Egyptians, and how I bore you on eagles’ wings and brought you to Myself."

• Numbers 6:26: "The LORD lift up His countenance upon you, And give you peace."

• Psalm 32:1: "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, Whose sin is covered."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient Near East, carrying heavy burdens was often relegated to servants, laborers, and the marginalized. By taking up this heavy load herself, Ruth embraces the role of a humble servant. She does not complain about the weight of her responsibilities. Instead, her willing endurance transforms a menial, heavy chore into a beautiful offering of love. This serves as an encouraging model for modern believers: the mundane, exhausting duties of life can become sacred acts of worship when done out of love for God and neighbor.
