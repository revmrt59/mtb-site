CITY - ( 'ir , Strong's H5892 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the Hebrew word 'ir refers to a permanent, inhabited settlement or city, typically characterized by social organization, protective boundaries, and a shared community life. In the Book of Ruth, the "city" is Bethlehem, serving not just as a geographical location, but as the covenant hub where family obligations, legal redemption, and community relationships are lived out. When Ruth returns to the city with her abundant gleanings, it marks a significant shift from isolation and famine to communal witness and divine provision.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, a city ('ir) was defined less by its population size and more by its structure, permanence, and security. Unlike nomadic encampments or open agricultural fields, a city was often fortified with walls and governed by elders who gathered at the city gates. The gates were the center of legal, social, and economic life. To enter the city was to transition from the vulnerability of the open wilderness or agricultural fields into a place of safety, order, and covenant community. In the narrative of Ruth, Bethlehem represents this place of community shelter and legal order under the God of Israel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'ir refers to a city, town, or inhabited settlement. It is used over 1,000 times in the Hebrew Bible. The term covers everything from small, fortified outposts to major royal capitals like Jerusalem or ancient empires like Nineveh. Semantically, it denotes a place of permanent dwellings as opposed to temporary tents or open country. It is fundamentally a place of human assembly and social structure.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Protection and Boundary: Cities are places of refuge and defense against enemies or wild beasts.

• The City Gate: The gate of the 'ir is the courtroom and marketplace. It represents justice, covenant transactions, and community recognition.

• Contrast with the Field: The field is the place of labor, exposure, and potential danger. The city is the place of rest, sharing, and accountability.

4. Theological Implications

The city in Scripture is a double-edged sword: it can represent human rebellion (like Babel or Sodom) or the focal point of God's dwelling and covenant faithfulness (like Jerusalem, the city of God). In the context of the historical narratives, the city is where the covenant community is expected to practice God's law. In Ruth, Bethlehem (meaning "House of Bread") is the city where God's lovingkindness is displayed through His people. The city becomes the theater of redemption, demonstrating that God's grace is not just private, but has public, communal, and legal dimensions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:18, Ruth has spent the day gleaning in the fields of Boaz. Her return to the "city" ('ir) is a pivotal moment of transition. She does not hide her blessing or consume it in isolation. By bringing her abundant grain (about an ephah of barley) back into the city, she brings hope and physical life back to Naomi, who had previously declared herself empty upon returning to Bethlehem. The city represents the watchful eyes of the community that will witness Ruth’s integrity and Boaz's righteousness. Ruth's entry into the city bridges the gap between her private labor in the fields and her public integration into the covenant community of Israel.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition between the "field" (sadeh) and the "city" ('ir) in the Book of Ruth highlights the spatial theology of the narrative. The field is a place of vulnerability where Ruth, as a foreign widow, is exposed to potential harassment. The city is the place of ultimate security and legal standing. This spatial movement culminates in Ruth 4, where the legal transaction of redemption takes place at the gate of the city ('ir), formalizing her place within the genealogy of David and ultimately Jesus Christ.
