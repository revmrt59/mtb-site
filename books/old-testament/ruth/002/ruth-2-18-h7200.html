SAW - ( ra'ah , Strong's H7200 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ra'ah goes far beyond the physical act of sight to encompass deep perception, understanding, and recognition. In Ruth 2:18, when Naomi "saw" what Ruth had gleaned, her eyes did not merely register the physical grain; her heart perceived the astonishing weight of God's grace and provision. This moment of seeing becomes a turning point in the narrative, shifting Naomi's perspective from bitter emptiness to hopeful recognition of the Lord's enduring kindness.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, physical sight is dynamic and active rather than passive. To see is to connect with the object of sight, often leading to an emotional, intellectual, or volitional response. The eye is viewed as the window of understanding and the indicator of the heart's condition. Thus, seeing is closely linked to experiencing, evaluating, and acting. When a person "sees," they do not merely absorb light waves; they grasp the significance of what is before them and position themselves to respond.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ra'ah primarily means to perceive with the eyes, look at, observe, or gaze upon. Beyond physical sight, it denotes intellectual and spiritual perception: to understand, consider, experience, or find out. In the causative stems, it means to present to view or show. When applied to God, it often refers to His caring oversight, observation of human suffering, and subsequent intervention. When applied to humans, it often describes the transition from ignorance to sudden, profound realization.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Visual Observation: The basic act of looking at an object or person to gather information.

• Spiritual and Cognitive Perception: Discerning the true nature of a situation, recognizing God’s hand at work, or gaining sudden insight.

• Experiencing a Condition: "Seeing" life, death, or good fortune, meaning to fully participate in or undergo those realities.

• Providential Care: Looking upon someone with compassion, favor, or concern, which leads to active deliverance and provision.

### 4. Theological Implications

The concept of ra'ah is foundational to the biblical doctrine of providence. God is the one who sees human affliction and acts on behalf of His people. Human seeing, when aligned with faith, recognizes God's invisible hand operating behind visible circumstances. In the book of Ruth, God's providence is quiet and hidden behind human actions. When Naomi sees the physical fruit of Boaz’s kindness, her spiritual vision is restored, allowing her to see that Yahweh has not abandoned His covenant loyalty (chesed).

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:18, Ruth returns from her first day of gleaning in Boaz's field with an ephah of barley, which is roughly 30 to 50 pounds. Naomi’s physical observation of this massive bounty triggers immediate cognitive comprehension. She does not just see barley; she recognizes an extraordinary act of grace. The physical sight of the grain serves as a catalyst for Naomi's transition from spiritual blindness and bitter despair to a renewed perception of God's active goodness.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:4: "And God saw the light, that it was good; and God divided the light from the darkness."

• Genesis 29:32: "So Leah conceived and bore a son, and she called his name Reuben; for she said, 'The LORD has surely looked on my affliction. Now therefore, my husband will love me.'"

• Exodus 3:7: "And the LORD said: 'I have surely seen the oppression of My people who are in Egypt, and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows;'"

• 1 Samuel 16:7: "But the LORD said to Samuel, 'Do not look at his appearance or at his physical stature, because I have refused him. For the LORD does not see as man sees; for man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The Latin root of the English word "providence" is providere, which literally means "to see ahead" or "to foresee." This aligns beautifully with the Hebrew usage of ra'ah in contexts of divine provision. Naomi’s return of sight contrasts sharply with her earlier declaration in Ruth 1:21, where she claimed she went out full and the Lord brought her home empty. Her eyes are now opened to the reality that she was never truly empty; God was working in the dark to provide.
