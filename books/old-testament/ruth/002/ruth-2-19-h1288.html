BLESSED - ( barak , Strong's H1288 )

Summary:

In Ruth 2:19, the word "blessed" (barak) functions as an earnest invocation of divine favor, protection, and prosperity upon an unnamed benefactor. Expressed as a passive participle (baruk), it is Naomi’s spontaneous prayer of thanksgiving when she witnesses the extraordinary abundance of grain Ruth brought home. Rather than offering a simple human thank-you, Naomi calls upon Yahweh, the covenant-keeping God of Israel, to actively reward the person who showed such unexpected grace to her widowed daughter-in-law. The term signifies a transfer of beneficial power and favor, recognizing that true blessing originates from God but is often channeled through human hands.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew root barak is rich with physical and relational imagery. The root is identical to the Hebrew noun for "knee" (berek). This visual association points to the posture of kneeling—either in adoration and worship of God, or in the act of bending down to present a gift or bestow favor upon someone of lower status. When God blesses humanity, He stoops down in grace to enrich, sustain, and empower them. Conversely, when humans bless God, they bow the knee in humble adoration and praise. In the ancient Near East, a blessing was not a mere sentiment or empty wish; it was understood as a powerful, spoken reality that carried the weight of divine backing. Once spoken, a blessing set in motion the active favor and life-giving power of God to bring about prosperity, safety, and fruitfulness.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb barak means to bless, praise, salute, or invoke favor. In its passive participle form (baruk), as used in Ruth 2:19, it denotes someone who is "blessed"—rendered recipient of divine favor and endowed with beneficial power.

• To bless a human means to wish or declare that God’s life-giving presence, protection, and material abundance will rest upon them.

• To bless God means to declare His praises, acknowledge His sovereign goodness, and honor His holy character.

• The term carries the core meaning of enduing someone with the capacity for abundant life, fruitfulness, success, and harmonious relationships within the covenant community.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, barak operates in three primary directional dimensions:

• Vertical Downward (God to Man): This is the most common usage, where God bestows life, fertility, and prosperity upon His creation. His blessing enables human beings to multiply, inherit land, and experience security (e.g., Genesis 1:28, Deuteronomy 28:2-6).

• Vertical Upward (Man to God): Here, the creature "blesses" the Creator. It does not imply that humans can add anything to God's inherent fullness; rather, it is an act of knees-bent worship, thanksgiving, and public praise for His wonderful deeds (e.g., Psalm 103:1).

• Horizontal (Man to Man): This is the invocation of God’s favor by one person upon another, often spoken by a parent, priest, or king. In Ruth 2:19, Naomi engages in this horizontal dimension, calling down vertical blessing from God onto the unknown man who helped Ruth.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, barak is intimately tied to the concept of covenant. In the Pentateuch, blessing is the natural overflow of a right relationship with Yahweh, while the curse (arar) is the consequence of breaking covenant.

• The Channel of Grace: Blessing is the tangible outworking of God's chesed (steadfast love and loyalty). Naomi’s prayer of blessing in Ruth 2:19 highlights that God's covenant blessings are often mediated through the acts of righteous human beings. Boaz's kindness is the practical means of God's provision, which Naomi immediately recognizes and connects to the source of all blessing.

• Redemption from Emptiness: In the broader narrative arc of Ruth, Naomi had previously declared herself "empty" and "bitter" due to the loss of her husband and sons (Ruth 1:20-21). The sudden pronouncement of barak in Ruth 2:19 marks a critical theological turning point. The language of blessing replaces the language of bitterness, signaling the return of hope, life, and redemption to the household of Elimelech.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:19, Ruth returns to Naomi with an astonishing amount of grain—about an ephah of barley (roughly 30 to 50 pounds)—after a single day of gleaning. Naomi instantly recognizes that such an abundance could not be the result of ordinary gleaning; someone must have gone out of their way to show extraordinary favor. Before she even knows the identity of the benefactor, she cries out, "Blessed be the one who took notice of you."

• The context dictates that barak here is a prayer of high gratitude. It is an appeal to the sovereign Lord to recompense a human instrument of mercy.

• By pronouncing this blessing, Naomi invokes God’s supernatural reward upon Boaz.

• The phrase "took notice of you" (nakar) highlights the contrast between the vulnerability of a foreign widow and the unexpected grace of an influential redeemer. The blessing is Naomi's immediate, spiritual response to this life-altering act of kindness.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:2 - "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you And make your name great; And you shall be a blessing."

• Genesis 14:19 - "And he blessed him and said: 'Blessed be Abram of God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth;'"

• Ruth 2:20 - "Then Naomi said to her daughter-in-law, 'Blessed be he of the LORD, who has not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead!' And Naomi said to her, 'This man is a relation of ours, one of our close relatives.'"

• Psalm 103:1 - "Bless the LORD, O my soul; And all that is within me, bless His holy name!"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the book of Ruth, the verb barak is spoken by various characters at pivotal moments, forming a literary pattern of reciprocal blessing. Boaz blesses his reapers (Ruth 2:4); Naomi blesses Boaz (Ruth 2:19-20); Boaz blesses Ruth for her loyalty (Ruth 3:10); the elders and neighbors bless the marriage of Boaz and Ruth (Ruth 4:11-12); and the women bless Yahweh at the birth of Obed (Ruth 4:14). This repetitive use of barak underscores the theme that a community living under the lordship of Yahweh becomes an ecosystem of mutual blessing, where God's favor is continuously circulated and celebrated.
