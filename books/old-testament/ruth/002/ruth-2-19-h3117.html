TODAY - ( yôm , Strong's H3117 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yôm most fundamentally denotes a warm period of time in contrast to the night, a literal twenty-four-hour day, or a generic, point-in-time concept translated as "today" when paired with the definite article (hayyôm). In the context of Ruth 2:19, it highlights a specific, providential day of labor and grace where Ruth’s encounters transition from survival to redemption. It anchors the theological reality of God's daily provision and sovereign guidance in the ordinary, routine moments of human life.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary root of yôm is associated with the concept of heat, referring to the warm, daylight hours when the sun is at its height, distinguishing it from the cool of the night. In the Ancient Near East, time was understood not merely through abstract, mechanical increments but through concrete activities, agricultural rhythms, and relational interactions. A "day" was the basic unit of human labor, a cycle of waking, working, and resting under the watchful eye of God. In a pastoral or agricultural setting like the book of Ruth, a single day represented the difference between hunger and satisfaction, safety and danger.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun yôm has a broad semantic range that depends heavily on its grammatical construction and context. It denotes a literal daylight period (from sunrise to sunset), a full twenty-four-hour cycle, a generic point in time, or an extended, indefinite period (such as "the day of the Lord" or "in the day of"). When prefixed with the Hebrew definite article, hayyôm literally means "the day" and is routinely translated as "today" or "this day." It points to the immediate, present moment, emphasizing the current reality and the necessity of immediate action, recognition, or decision.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yôm is used in several figurative and literal ways:

• The Division of Light and Darkness: Established in Genesis 1:5, where God called the light "day" (yôm), marking the rhythm of creation.

• A Point of Decision or Covenant: Used to challenge Israel to choose "this day" (hayyôm) whom they will serve, emphasizing the urgency of covenant loyalty (Joshua 24:15).

• A Period of Judgment or Salvation: Prophetic literature utilizes "the day of the Lord" to describe a future epoch of divine intervention, reckoning, and ultimate restoration.

• The Daily Portion: In historical narratives, it frequently emphasizes the daily, step-by-step unfolding of human life under divine providence, where each day brings its own labor and its own measure of grace.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yôm reminds readers that God works within the constraints of human history and time. He is not a distant deity of abstract philosophy but the Lord of "today." The use of yôm reinforces the biblical theme of daily dependence on Yahweh. Just as the Israelites received manna day by day, characters in the Old Testament experience God's mercy renewed every morning. Furthermore, "today" represents the intersection of human responsibility—gleaning in the fields, working diligently—and divine sovereignty, where God directs a person’s steps to the exact field of redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:19, the word yôm appears twice as hayyôm ("today") in the dialogue between Naomi and Ruth. Ruth returns from her first day of gleaning in the fields of Boaz with an extraordinary amount of barley (about an ephah). Naomi, astonished by this abundance, asks, "Where have you gleaned today? And where did you work?" Later in the verse, Ruth answers, "The man’s name with whom I worked today is Boaz." The repetition of "today" underscores the immediacy of God’s intervention. What began as a routine, grueling day of survival for a destitute foreign widow ended as a day of shocking favor and hope. The term anchors the narrative in historical reality; this was not a vague, mythical event, but a specific day where God’s covenant faithfulness (hesed) became tangible through the kindness of Boaz.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:5: "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. So the evening and the morning were the first day."

• Deuteronomy 30:15: "See, I have set before you today life and good, death and evil."

• Joshua 24:15: "And if it seems evil to you to serve the Lord, choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve..."

• Psalm 118:24: "This is the day the Lord has made; We will rejoice and be glad in it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint or LXX), yôm in Ruth 2:19 is translated using the Greek word semeron, which also means "today" or "this day." This Greek equivalent is the same word used by Jesus in the Lord's Prayer ("Give us this day our daily bread," Matthew 6:11) and in His declaration of salvation ("Today salvation has come to this house," Luke 19:9). This linguistic connection highlights the continuity of God's pattern of providing immediate, daily grace and redemption to His people across both Testaments.
