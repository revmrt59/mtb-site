GLEANED - ( laqat , Strong's H3950 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb laqat means to gather, collect, or glean. In the agricultural context of the ancient Near East, it refers specifically to the practice of picking up leftover grain or produce after the primary harvest has passed. God’s law mandated that landowners leave these remnants for the poor, the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless. Thus, gleaning represents both a physical act of survival for the marginalized and a tangible demonstration of God's providential care and communal responsibility.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The background of laqat lies in the agrarian society of ancient Israel. Harvesting was a rapid, labor-intensive process where reapers cut stalks of grain and binders tied them into sheaves. Inevitably, stalks were dropped or missed. Under normal circumstances, these leftovers would be collected by the owner's servants. However, the Mosaic Law established a social safety net. Landowners were forbidden from stripping their fields bare or going back to collect dropped grain. This leftover portion was designated for the vulnerable.

Gleaning was not a handout that fostered dependency; it required hard, physical labor under the hot sun. The poor had to walk behind the reapers, bending down repeatedly to gather individual stalks one by one. This preserved the dignity of the poor while ensuring they did not starve.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb laqat is a primitive root that means to pick up, gather, or collect. In the intensive stem (Piel), which is its most common verbal form, it means to gather up, glean, or collect systematically. The lexical focus is on the action of selecting and gathering individual items from the ground.

While it is frequently used for agricultural gleaning, the term also applies to gathering other physical objects, such as manna in the wilderness, stones for a pillar, or arrows shot by an archer. The core idea is the deliberate, piece-by-piece collection of scattered items.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Daily Sustenance and Trust: In the wilderness, the Israelites were commanded to gather (laqat) the manna daily. This usage paints a picture of complete reliance on God's provision. One could not gather more than a day's portion without it spoiling, emphasizing daily obedience and trust.

• The Remnant of Grace: In the context of the harvest, the word represents the margin of grace left by the wealthy for the destitute. It illustrates how God's law integrates economic enterprise with compassionate charity.

• Scattered Judgment and Restoration: Metaphorically, the term is occasionally used to describe the scattered gathering of people, either in judgment (being picked off one by one) or in restoration (God gathering His scattered flock back to the land).

### 4. Theological Implications

• God as the Provider for the Vulnerable: The law of gleaning reveals the heart of God. He is the ultimate owner of the land, and He demands that His people mimic His generosity. By letting the poor gather, God provides for them through the abundance of the land He blessed.

• The Intersection of Grace and Industry: Gleaning models the biblical balance between charity and labor. The wealthy show grace by leaving the grain, and the poor show industry by gathering it. God provides the opportunity, but humans must exert the effort.

• A Pathway to Redemption: In the book of Ruth, gleaning is the divine catalyst that brings a destitute Gentile widow into contact with Boaz, her kinsman-redeemer. The physical act of gathering grain becomes the very mechanism of her social and spiritual redemption.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:19, Naomi asks Ruth, "Where have you gleaned today?" Naomi’s question is prompted by the extraordinary amount of barley Ruth brought home—about an ephah (roughly 30 to 50 pounds). A typical gleaner would be fortunate to gather a fraction of that amount after a grueling day of work.

Ruth's massive haul was not the result of ordinary gleaning; it was the result of Boaz’s deliberate favor. He had commanded his reapers to purposefully pull out grain from the bundles and leave it for her to gather. Naomi immediately recognizes that such abundance could not be accidental. The sheer volume of the "gleaned" grain points directly to the extraordinary grace of a benefactor, shifting the narrative from mere survival to the hope of redemption.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 16:4 - "Then the LORD said to Moses, 'Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you. And the people shall go out and gather a certain quota every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in My law or not.'"

• Leviticus 19:9 - "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest."

• Judges 1:7 - "And Adoni-Bezek said, 'Seventy kings, with their thumbs and big toes cut off, used to gather their food under my table; as I have done, so God has repaid me.' Then they brought him to Jerusalem, and there he died."

• Ruth 2:2 - "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast with Ingathering: The verb laqat stands in contrast to asap (another common Hebrew word for "gather"). While asap often refers to bringing in the main harvest or assembling a large group in triumph, laqat is the humble, tedious action of picking up leftovers.

• Character through Labor: Ruth's willingness to perform the lowliest task of gleaning demonstrates her humility, work ethic, and devotion to Naomi. She did not wait for charity to find her; she actively went out to work, positioning herself in the place of God's sovereign provision.
