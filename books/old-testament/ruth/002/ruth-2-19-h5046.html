TOLD - ( nagad , Strong's H5046 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nagad fundamentally means to declare, announce, make known, or bring information to light in a conspicuous manner. Used over 370 times in the Old Testament, it denotes a clear, face-to-face communication that unveils hidden or unknown facts. In Ruth 2:19, this word marks a vital pivot in the narrative, where Ruth's joyful report to Naomi concerning her labor in the field of Boaz initiates a sequence of events leading to their ultimate redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root of nagad carries the physical concept of coming to the front, placing something high, or making something stand out in plain sight.

• In the ancient Near East, information was not merely shared; it was positioned directly before a person to be evaluated and acted upon.

• To nagad is to bring what is hidden in the dark, or what is far away in another location, and lay it down directly in front of the listener's eyes. It implies a direct, face-to-face declaration that demands attention and changes the recipient's perspective or situation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Strictly occurring in the causative Hiphil stem (higgid) and the passive Hophal stem (huggad), nagad means to declare, announce, report, expound, or make conspicuously known.

• It denotes the communication of information that the listener could not otherwise know unless it were revealed to them.

• It carries the legal and relational nuance of offering a full, official testimony or report, presenting facts clearly and without reservation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Prophetic Declaration: nagad is used to describe prophetic declarations, where a prophet makes known the hidden counsels and future judgments of God to the people.

• Legal Testimony: It is used in legal settings to describe witnessing or making a formal report about an event observed.

• Relational Disclosure: In personal relationships, it represents the intimate act of revealing secrets, feelings, or one's identity to another, such as Joseph revealing his identity to his brothers.

4. Theological Implications

• Revelation and Truth: God is the ultimate speaker who tells (nagad) His people what is good, what He requires, and what is to come. Divine revelation is not abstract; it is a clear, conspicuous declaration of God’s character and plans.

• The Shift from Ignorance to Hope: In salvation history, when God "tells" or "declares" His mercy, it shifts His people from despair to hope. Human "telling" often becomes the vehicle through which God's providential grace is made known to those who are suffering.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:19, Ruth returns to Naomi after a long day of gleaning. She does not just casually mention her day; she "tells" (nagad) her mother-in-law the details of where she worked.

• This reporting is highly relational and filled with divine dramatic irony. Naomi is grieving, thinking God has abandoned them. Ruth's simple act of telling—bringing the name of Boaz into the open—instantly exposes God's invisible hand of providence.

• The verb highlights the transition from hidden grace to revealed hope. Boaz’s kindness was a hidden reality to Naomi until Ruth physically "told" her, which sparked Naomi's realization that Boaz is a close relative and redeemer.

• Notably, this echoes Ruth 2:11, where Boaz tells Ruth that her loyalty had been "fully reported" (huggad) to him. The sharing of reports links these characters in a chain of mutual recognition and covenant faithfulness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:11 - "And He said, 'Who told you that you were naked? Have you eaten from the tree of which I commanded you that you should not eat?'"

• Ruth 2:11 - "And Boaz answered and said to her, 'It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband...'"

• Psalm 19:1 - "The heavens declare the glory of God; And the firmament shows His handiwork."

• Jonah 1:8 - "Then they said to him, 'Please tell us on whose account this trouble has come upon us. What is your occupation? And where do you come from?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint translation of nagad in Ruth 2:19 uses the Greek verb anangello, which means to report, announce, or rehearse back. This underscores that Ruth was not merely chatting; she was delivering a structured, faithful report of the day's remarkable events, allowing Naomi to piece together the spiritual significance of what had occurred.
