TOOK NOTICE - ( nakar , Strong's H5234 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nakar, translated as "took notice" in Ruth 2:19, means to recognize, acknowledge, or show special regard to someone. In the ancient Near East, a destitute foreign widow like Ruth was typically ignored, avoided, or exploited. When Boaz "took notice" of her, he did not merely see her with his eyes; he actively acknowledged her dignity, extended protection, and treated her with covenant favor. This term highlights the transition from being a nameless, invisible stranger to someone who is recognized, valued, and brought into the sphere of redeeming grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root nakar carries a fascinating linguistic tension in the Hebrew language. It can mean to regard, recognize, or acknowledge (in the Hiphil stem), or it can refer to being a foreigner, stranger, or unknown person (through the related noun nekar and adjective nokri).

• This wordplay is central to the narrative of Ruth. In Ruth 2:10, Ruth asks Boaz why he has "taken notice" (nakar) of her, since she is a "foreigner" (nokriyah). The phonetic and thematic connection is intentional: she is an unrecognized outsider who has been miraculously recognized and welcomed.

• In agrarian Israel, landowners and laborers would typically ignore or abuse poor gleaners, especially foreign women. To "take notice" of someone meant to stop, look, and choose to treat them as a person of worth rather than an invisible nuisance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• In the Hiphil (causative/active) stem used in Ruth 2:19, nakar means to recognize, distinguish, acknowledge, or pay special, favorable attention to someone.

• The basic meaning is to look at someone or something with a gaze of recognition and then act favorably based on that identification.

• It stands in contrast to ignoring, forgetting, or treating someone as an outcast. It is an intentional act of the will to grant status and favor to an individual who has no legal claim to it.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Recognition: Nakar is used when Jacob recognizes Joseph's blood-stained tunic, identifying it with certainty (Genesis 37:33).

• Legal and Moral Acknowledgment: It is used when Judah recognizes his own signet, cord, and staff, admitting his responsibility toward Tamar (Genesis 38:25-26).

• Disguise and Disclosure: The word appears repeatedly during the Joseph narrative, where Joseph recognizes his brothers, but they do not recognize him (Genesis 42:8).

• In all these cases, nakar involves observing external evidence, coming to a conviction, and making a public declaration or taking responsibility. In Ruth, Boaz recognizes Ruth’s character and vulnerability, choosing to act as her protector.

4. Theological Implications

• God's grace often manifests through human agents who "take notice" of the marginalized. Ruth was empty, foreign, and unprotected, yet through Boaz’s recognition, God’s covenant mercy (chesed) became tangible.

• This word foreshadows the ultimate redemptive recognition believers receive in Christ. Just as Ruth was a foreigner who did nothing to earn Boaz's attention, believers were strangers to the covenant of promise but have been known, recognized, and adopted by God's redeeming grace.

• True biblical recognition involves action. To take notice of the poor is not merely to pity them, but to actively provide for and protect them, reflecting the character of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:19, Naomi immediately discerns that someone must have gone out of their way to bless Ruth because of the sheer abundance of food she brought home—an ephah of barley, which was an immense amount for a single day of gleaning.

• Naomi's blessing, "Blessed be the one who took notice of you," uses the Hiphil participle form of nakar (makkirek). Naomi knows that such abundance does not happen by accident or standard gleaning; it required a deliberate benefactor who saw Ruth and decided to treat her with extraordinary kindness.

• This connection back to Ruth's earlier question in Ruth 2:10 emphasizes that Boaz's recognition was the catalyst for the survival and redemption of Naomi's family line.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 37:33: "And he recognized it and said, 'It is my son’s tunic. A wild beast has devoured him. Without doubt Joseph is torn to pieces.'"

• Genesis 42:8: "So Joseph recognized his brothers, but they did not recognize him."

• Job 2:12: "And when they raised their eyes from afar, and did not recognize him, they lifted up their voices and wept; and each one tore his robe and sprinkled dust on his head toward heaven."

• Deuteronomy 33:9: "Who says of his father and mother, 'I have not seen them'; nor did he acknowledge his brothers, or know his own children; for they have observed Your word and kept Your covenant."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The word nakar also highlights the power of reputation. Boaz had observed and heard of Ruth's loyalty to Naomi before he ever met her in the field (Ruth 2:11). His "taking notice" was not a fleeting impulse but a response to her virtue, which was recognized by the entire community.

• The play on words between nakar (to recognize) and nekar (foreigner) serves as a literary bridge. It demonstrates how grace breaks down ethnic and social barriers, turning an outsider (nokriyah) into an insider who is fully recognized, redeemed, and integrated into the genealogy of the Messiah.
