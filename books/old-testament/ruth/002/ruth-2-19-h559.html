SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the primary Old Testament word meaning "to say," "to speak," or "to declare." Occurring thousands of times across the Hebrew Scriptures, it represents more than the simple physical utterance of words; it is the primary vehicle for personal self-disclosure, relationship building, and the expression of the heart's intent. In Ruth 2:19, this word marks a major emotional and spiritual turning point in the narrative. The verbal exchange between Naomi and Ruth breaks the heavy silence of their mutual grief, transforming their household from a place of bitter survival into a sanctuary of hope, thanksgiving, and realized redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, speech was understood as an active, dynamic extension of a person's inner life and authority. Words were not merely empty sounds or abstract symbols; they carried the weight of the speaker's soul, character, and intent. The verb 'amar captures this dynamic of personal expression. While other Hebrew verbs for speech, such as dabar, often emphasize the objective content or formal message being communicated, 'amar focuses heavily on the act of personal address and dialogue. It is the language of relationship, used to bridge the gap between two souls. In the patriarchal narrative and the history of Israel, when a character speaks ('amar), they are inviting another person into their perspective, revealing their desires, anxieties, or blessings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'amar primarily means to say, speak, utter, tell, command, or think. In its most common form (the Qal stem), it denotes the straightforward act of verbalizing thoughts in conversation or response.

• It can refer to internal speech, often translated as "to say in one's heart" (meaning to think or plan).

• It can carry the force of a command or decree, especially when uttered by a king or by God Himself.

• It is frequently used to introduce direct speech in dialogue, serving as the narrative bridge that allows characters to speak for themselves rather than having their thoughts summarized by a narrator.

• The semantic range does not include highly formal or technical legal pronouncements (which are more often associated with other roots), but rather spans the intimate, relational, and everyday verbal interactions of human life and divine-human communication.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament depicts several powerful word pictures and idiomatic uses of 'amar:

• The Creative Word: When God speaks in Genesis 1, the text uses 'amar ("And God said..."). This presents speech as an instrument of sovereign, creative power that brings light, life, and order out of darkness and chaos.

• The Internal Monologue: To think is to speak within oneself. The Hebrew writers frequently describe deep contemplation or scheming as "saying in the heart." This reveals that human thoughts are processed as an internal dialogue with the self.

• Relational Transparency: In dialogues, 'amar acts as a window to the heart. When a character "says" something in the text, their words expose their spiritual condition—whether it is the bitter complaint of Naomi in chapter 1 or the joyful curiosity she displays in chapter 2.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'amar underscores the communicative nature of both God and humanity. God is not a silent force but a speaking Creator who makes Himself known through verbal self-revelation. Because human beings are made in His image, human speech carries immense spiritual weight.

In the book of Ruth, speech is a barometer of spiritual restoration. Throughout chapter 1, the dialogue is heavy with grief, culminating in Naomi's request to be called "Bitter" (Mara). In chapter 2, as the providence of God begins to unfold in the fields of Boaz, the nature of the dialogue changes. The act of speaking ('amar) becomes a means of dispensing blessing, showing hospitality, and expressing gratitude. This shift shows that when God's grace visits a community, it restores wholesome, life-giving communication between people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:19, 'amar occurs twice, framing a beautifully intense, rapid-fire conversation between Naomi and her daughter-in-law.

• First, Naomi says to Ruth: "Where have you gleaned today? And where did you work? Blessed be the one who took notice of you." Naomi's speech here is energetic and breathless. Seeing the massive amount of barley Ruth brought home, Naomi's eyes are opened to the reality of God's provision. Her words are filled with eager inquiry and immediate blessing. The bitterness that silenced her in chapter 1 is broken by the goodness of God, prompting her to speak words of life.

• Second, Ruth says to Naomi: "The man’s name with whom I worked today is Boaz." This response is the climax of the scene. Ruth's speech reveals the identity of the redeemer.

The use of 'amar here demonstrates that redemption is realized and celebrated through shared testimonies. It is not enough that Boaz was kind; the story must be told, the name must be spoken, and the praise must be shared between the generations.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A critical narrative technique in Hebrew prose is the contrast between 'amar and dabar. In many narratives, dabar is used to establish the background context of a matter, while 'amar brings the reader directly into the emotional heat of the moment by recording the exact words spoken. In Ruth 2:19, the writer's choice of 'amar forces the reader to listen to the dialogue firsthand. We hear Naomi's sudden excitement and Ruth's simple, honest explanation. This technique personalizes the theological truth of God's providence. It reminds the reader that God's grand plan of redemption operates through normal, daily, loving conversations between ordinary believers.
