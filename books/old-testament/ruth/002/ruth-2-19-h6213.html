WORKED - ( asah , Strong's H6213 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb asah is a foundational Old Testament word meaning to do, make, fashion, or produce. In the context of Ruth 2:19, it describes Ruth's active, diligent labor in the fields of Boaz. Rather than remaining a passive recipient of charity, Ruth actively worked to provide for herself and Naomi, demonstrating her noble character and illustrating how divine providence cooperates with human faithfulness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb asah is one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 2,600 times. It represents the concrete action of fashioning, creating, or producing something through physical energy and labor. Unlike bara (which is used exclusively for divine creation out of nothing), asah typically refers to working with existing materials, shaping, performing, or accomplishing a task. In the agrarian culture of ancient Israel, asah frequently referred to physical labor in the fields, preparing food, or executing daily duties. The word picture is one of hands-on, energetic engagement with the material world to bring about a practical, beneficial result.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb means to do, make, fashion, accomplish, or produce. It carries the primary sense of completing an action, executing a task, or engaging in active labor. In contexts involving employment or daily labor, it denotes working, performing a service, or carrying out a specific assignment. The semantic range includes:

• To produce or yield (such as a tree producing fruit or land yielding crops).

• To fashion or manufacture (such as building an altar or making garments).

• To perform or execute (such as doing justice, performing a ritual, or carrying out a duty).

• To spend time or labor in a specific place (as in working a field).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The usage of asah in Scripture spans from God's cosmic work of creation to the simplest human chores. It is used to describe:

• Divine craftsmanship: God making the firmament, the sun, the moon, and humanity.

• Human industry: Artisans fashioning the Tabernacle, builders constructing the walls of Jerusalem, and laborers working the soil.

• Character expression: In wisdom literature, asah describes the active lifestyle of the righteous (who "do" justice) versus the wicked (who "do" iniquity). What a person does (asah) is the ultimate indicator of who they are.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of asah in the context of human labor is profound:

• Dignity of Work: Because God Himself is one who "works" (asah), human labor is not a curse but a reflection of the divine image. When Ruth worked, she was exercising a God-given dignity.

• Divine-Human Synergy: The Book of Ruth beautifully illustrates the intersection of divine sovereignty and human responsibility. God provided the grain and directed Ruth to the field of Boaz, but Ruth had to actively gather and process the grain. God's grace does not make human effort unnecessary; rather, it empowers and rewards it.

• Redemptive Orchestration: God's grand plan of redemption (bringing about the lineage of King David and eventually the Messiah) unfolded through the quiet, ordinary, daily faithfulness of a foreign widow working diligently in a barley field.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:19, the word asah is used three times in the dialogue between Naomi and Ruth: "Where did you work? ... with whom she had worked ... the man's name with whom I worked today is Boaz."

• Naomi's astonishment was triggered by the sheer volume of barley Ruth brought home (an ephah, roughly 30 to 50 pounds). This massive yield was far beyond what a typical gleaner could gather in a day. Naomi immediately knew this was the result of both Ruth's extraordinary labor (asah) and someone's extraordinary generosity.

• The repetition of worked emphasizes Ruth's active agency. She did not beg; she labored. She did not wait for hand-outs; she went to the field.

• There is a beautiful transition from independent labor to communal connection. Ruth worked "with" Boaz. The physical labor of asah became the staging ground for a covenant relationship that would redeem both women.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:2: "And on the seventh day God ended His work which He had done, and He rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had done."

• Nehemiah 4:6: "So we built the wall, and the entire wall was joined together up to half its height, for the people had a mind to work."

• Psalm 103:20: "Bless the Lord, you His angels, Who excel in strength, who do His word, Heeding the voice of His word."

• Ecclesiastes 3:12: "I know that nothing is better for them than to rejoice, and to do good in their lives,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), asah in Ruth 2:19 is translated with the Greek verb poieo (to do, make, or practice). This is the same root from which we get the English word poem (a creation or workmanship). In Ephesians 2:10, believers are called God's "workmanship" (poiema), created in Christ Jesus for good works. Thus, Ruth's physical labor (asah) serves as a beautiful Old Testament type of the active, fruitful lives believers are called to live as God's own workmanship.
