NAME - ( shem , Strong's H8034 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, a person’s name represents far more than a vocal label; it encapsulates their character, reputation, authority, and ultimate legacy. In Ruth 2:19, when Ruth reveals that the benefactor’s name is Boaz, the mention of this specific name serves as the dramatic turning point of the entire book. It transitions the narrative from a story of mere survival to one of divine providence and covenant redemption, signaling to Naomi that God has not abandoned His covenant loyalty to her family.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near Eastern world, a name was inextricably linked to the essence of the person or thing named. To have no name was to not exist, while to know someone's name was to possess relational access to them. Names also carried the weight of a person’s reputation, authority, and destiny.

Throughout the book of Ruth, names carry significant thematic weight. Elimelech means "My God is King," yet he leaves the Promised Land. Mahlon ("sickly") and Chilion ("pining") die young in Moab. Naomi ("pleasant") renames herself Mara ("bitter") to reflect her grief. When Boaz ("in him is strength") enters the narrative, his name perfectly mirrors his role as a strong, wealthy, and righteous redeemer. The naming of individuals throughout this historical account is not accidental; it serves as a literary and theological compass pointing toward God's unfolding plan.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun shem signifies a name, reputation, fame, memorial, or monument. At its most basic level, it designates an individual person or place. However, the term quickly extends beyond identification to encompass the renown, standing, and honor of an individual within a community.

In legal and familial contexts, shem carries the heavy implication of memory and legacy, particularly regarding the preservation of a family line through inheritance. To "cut off" a name is to completely obliterate a person's memory and lineage from the earth, while to "raise up" a name is to ensure their legacy and property endure.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term shem is employed in several distinct ways across the Old Testament:

• Reputation and Character: A person's "name" is synonymous with their moral standing. A "good name" is a highly valued asset, representing integrity and honor within the covenant community.

• Memorial and Legacy: The word often represents the physical or familial memorial of a person. To have a name in Israel meant having land and descendants to carry that designation into future generations.

• Presence and Authority: To act in someone’s "name" means to operate with their full authority. To call upon the "name of the LORD" is to invoke His personal presence, covenant promises, and saving power.

### 4. Theological Implications

The preservation of the family shem is directly tied to God's covenant promises of land and lineage. God promised Abraham a great "name" (Genesis 12:2), a promise fulfilled through covenant faithfulness and multiplication.

The concept of the Kinsman-Redeemer (goel) exists primarily to defend the shem of a deceased relative. When a man died without an heir, his land was at risk of leaving the family, and his name was at risk of being blotted out. The redeemer stepped in to secure the property and raise up descendants, preserving the name of the dead. This foreshadows the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ, who inherits the "name above every name" and redeems His people to secure an eternal inheritance and a permanent place in His family.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:19, Ruth returns to Naomi with an abundance of grain and tells her she worked in the field of a man named Boaz. This is the structural and emotional pivot of the entire book. Up to this point, Ruth only knew Boaz as a kind landowner who took notice of her. Naomi, however, immediately recognizes the theological weight of this name.

The announcement that "the man’s name... is Boaz" transforms Naomi's perspective from despair to hope. Boaz is not a random stranger; he is a close relative with the legal right to redeem their family. The bitter widow who felt empty and abandoned by God now sees that the Lord's kindness (chesed) is active through Boaz. The simple identification of a name shifts the entire trajectory of the book from grief to redemption.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The search for a "name" is a key literary thread running through the book of Ruth. In chapter 1, Naomi loses her husband and sons, seemingly losing her family's name and future. In chapter 2, the introduction of Boaz's name introduces hope. In chapter 3, Ruth seeks a secure future under Boaz's protection. In chapter 4, the elders bless Boaz, praying that he may "be famous" (literally, "proclaim a name") in Bethlehem. The climax of the book is the birth of Obed, which restores the name of the dead and links this humble family directly to the royal name of David and ultimately to Jesus the Messiah.
