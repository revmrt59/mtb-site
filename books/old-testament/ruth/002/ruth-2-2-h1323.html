DAUGHTER - ( bat , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bat primarily denotes a female offspring, but it carries rich relational, communal, and covenantal layers of meaning throughout the Old Testament. In the book of Ruth, particularly in Ruth 2:2, Naomi’s use of "my daughter" (bitti) transcends mere biological kinship. It functions as an affectionate term of adoption, protection, and familial solidarity. By addressing Ruth this way, Naomi signals Ruth's transition from a foreign outsider to a cherished member of a Hebrew household, sheltered under the wings of the covenant God of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun bat is the feminine counterpart to the Hebrew ben (son). It is derived from the root banah, which means "to build." This linguistic connection provides a beautiful word picture: a daughter is poetically understood as one who builds up a family, a home, and a future lineage. In the ancient Near East, daughters were central to the preservation and expansion of the household. While they lived under the protective authority of their fathers—and later, their husbands—their presence represented the hope of future generations, stability, and domestic vitality.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of bat is broad and deeply contextual:

• Literal Female Offspring: A direct female child or immediate descendant.

• Descendant/Kinship: A female member of a specific family line, clan, or nation (e.g., "daughters of Canaan").

• Appellation of Affection: A warm, respectful term of address used by an elder toward a younger woman, denoting maternal or paternal care, guardianship, and acceptance.

• Collective Personification: A poetic designation for the inhabitants of a city or nation, such as "daughter of Zion" or "daughter of Babylon."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Builder of the House: Because of its connection to banah (to build), Hebrew thought links the daughter to the physical and spiritual building of the home. This is later realized in Ruth 4:11, where the elders pray that Ruth will be like Rachel and Leah, who "built the house of Israel."

• The Protected Vineyard: The Old Testament occasionally uses bat to describe a vulnerable, precious possession requiring intense care and protection. A daughter in her father’s house was a symbol of pure potential, guarded against the harsh realities of the surrounding pagan world.

• The Collective Bride: When prophets use the idiom "daughter of My people" or "daughter of Zion," they picture Israel as a young woman betrothed to Yahweh, highlighting her beauty, vulnerability, and the deep, protective jealousy of her Creator.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of bat in the narrative of Ruth is profound.

• Covenantal Adoption: Ruth is a Moabitess—a member of a nation historically excluded from the assembly of Israel. When Naomi addresses her as "my daughter," she is not merely using a polite term. She is executing a relational adoption. Ruth’s foreign identity is eclipsed by her new identity within the covenant family of Israel.

• The Grace of God (Chesed): Naomi’s words reflect the tender character of Yahweh. The marginalized widow, who felt empty and abandoned by God, is moved to extend family intimacy to a foreigner. It demonstrates how covenant love (chesed) breaks down ethnic barriers and offers belonging to the destitute.

• Redemptive Security: By receiving the title of "daughter," Ruth is granted safety. In a society where unprotected foreign women were highly vulnerable to exploitation, being recognized as Naomi’s daughter—and later as Boaz’s "daughter" in the fields—provides her with legal and social refuge.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:2, Ruth asks Naomi for permission to glean in the fields, hoping to find favor. Naomi responds, "Go, my daughter."

This short phrase marks a critical turning point in their relationship. In Chapter 1, Naomi was consumed by her own grief and bitterness, urging her daughters-in-law to return to their Moabite mothers' houses. Here, Naomi begins to look outward. By calling Ruth "my daughter," she formally accepts Ruth’s pledge of loyalty ("your people shall be my people").

Naomi’s blessing ("Go") coupled with this term of endearment validates Ruth’s role as the provider and builder of their shattered home. It shifts Ruth’s status from a tag-along foreign widow to an honored, obedient daughter of Israel.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The progression of how Ruth is addressed throughout the narrative illustrates her growing redemption. She begins as "Ruth the Moabitess" (Ruth 1:22, 2:2). When she steps into the fields, she is addressed by Boaz as "my daughter" (Ruth 2:8), signaling his protection. By the end of the narrative, she is no longer a stranger or even just a daughter-in-law, but is celebrated as the one who is "better to you than seven sons" (Ruth 4:15). The journey from a foreign widow to a beloved bat highlights the restorative power of God's providence.
