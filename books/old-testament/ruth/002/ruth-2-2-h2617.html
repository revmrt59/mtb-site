KINDNESS - ( chesed , Strong's H2617 )

Summary:

In Ruth 2:2, "kindness" translates the rich Hebrew word chesed, which denotes covenant loyalty, steadfast love, and active, undeserved faithfulness. It is not merely a warm sentimental feeling, but a deliberate, loyal commitment that acts on behalf of someone in need, reflecting God's own character in human relationships.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Rooted in the concept of loyalty, devotion, and steadfast love within a binding relationship.

• Often operates within the framework of family ties, friendship, or covenant, where one party goes beyond strict legal duty to rescue, provide for, or protect another.

• In the ancient Near East, chesed described the reliable support family members owed one another, especially in times of crisis like widowhood, poverty, or displacement.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Refers to steadfast love, unfailing loyalty, goodness, grace, and mercy.

• Encompasses actions of kindness and generosity that spring from a deep, committed relationship rather than mere obligation.

• Frequently paired with faithfulness, truth, and covenantal commitment, describing a love that refuses to let go.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Often described as "loyalty that goes beyond the call of duty."

• Acts as a bridge between emotion and action; it is love with legs, doing what is necessary to sustain the vulnerable.

• Frequently used in the Old Testament to describe God's relentless mercy toward His covenant people, and here, it describes human behavior that mirrors the divine.

4. Theological Implications

• Human chesed is a horizontal reflection of the vertical chesed of God. When people act with steadfast loyalty and mercy, they embody the character of God.

• In the Book of Ruth, chesed moves the narrative forward, transforming empty lives and bitter circumstances through unexpected acts of loyalty and grace.

• It highlights that God often accomplishes His redemption through ordinary people who choose to act righteously and compassionately in difficult times.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:2, Ruth speaks to Naomi: "Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor." The word translated as "kindness" or "favor" in this broader narrative context sets the tone for Boaz's later response to Ruth.

• Ruth is stepping out in vulnerable dependence, seeking the chesed of a landowner who is under no legal obligation to help a foreign, impoverished Moabite widow.

• This act of gleaning relies entirely on the gracious, unearned generosity permitted by the Mosaic law and practiced by a righteous landowner.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 3:10 - Then he said, "Blessed are you of the Lord, my daughter! For you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning, in that you did not go after young men, whether poor or rich."

• Exodus 34:6 - And the Lord passed before him and proclaimed, "The Lord, the Lord God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness (kindness) and truth."

• Psalm 23:6 - Surely goodness and mercy (kindness) shall follow me all the days of my life; and I will dwell in the house of the Lord forever.

• Hosea 6:6 - For I desire mercy (kindness) and not sacrifice, and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• chesed is one of the most important theological terms in the Old Testament, appearing roughly 250 times.

• Martin Luther struggled to find a single German word to capture its depth, often translating it as Lutterkeit (purity/clearness) or Huld (grace/favor).

• In Ruth, the narrative demonstrates that chesed is not just felt; it is practiced. Both Ruth and Boaz embody this virtue, moving from grief to redemption.
