GO - ( yalak , Strong's H3212 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalak (closely related to halak) fundamentally means to walk, go, or proceed. In Ruth 2:2, Ruth uses it in the cohortative form ("let me go") to express her active initiative, humble resolve, and voluntary steps to provide for herself and Naomi. Rather than remaining passive in their poverty, Ruth's request to "go" marks the critical turning point where her theoretical loyalty to Naomi and Yahweh is translated into practical, physical action in the fields of Bethlehem.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near Eastern world, walking was the primary mode of transportation and progress. Consequently, yalak is a concrete, physical verb that depicts the steady, intentional movement of a person’s feet on a path. It is never passive. The word picture behind yalak is that of a traveler setting out on a journey with a clear destination, stepping forward in faith despite potential dangers. In Hebrew thought, a person's "going" or "walking" was synonymous with their lifestyle, moral conduct, and active obedience. To "go" was to manifest one's internal convictions through external, physical steps.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yalak occurs over 1,000 times in the Old Testament. Its primary semantic range includes:

• To go, walk, or travel physically from one location to another.

• To depart or set out on a specific journey.

• To proceed, advance, or make progress.

• Figuratively, to behave, live, or follow a certain course of life (e.g., walking in God's ways).

In Ruth 2:2, the verb appears in the first-person cohortative form (elekah-na, "please let me go"). This grammatical structure expresses strong personal desire, self-motivated determination, and polite request. It signifies that Ruth is not merely wandering; she is making a deliberate, self-initiated choice to step out of the safety of her home into the fields to secure survival.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yalak is used to describe some of the most pivotal steps of faith in redemptive history:

• Abraham's departure from Haran in obedience to God's command (Genesis 12:4).

• Israel's march out of Egypt and journey through the wilderness.

• The metaphorical "walk" of the righteous who walk in the law of the Lord (Psalm 119:1).

The word picture in Ruth 2:2 connects Ruth’s physical feet with her spiritual journey. Having previously declared, "wherever you go, I will go" (Ruth 1:16), she now embodies that pledge. Her "going" to the field of Boaz is the physical manifestation of her decision to walk under the wings of the God of Israel.

4. Theological Implications

The use of yalak in Ruth 2:2 highlights several key theological truths:

• Faith is active, not passive. Real faith requires physical steps. Ruth does not wait for a miraculous supply of food to drop from heaven; she actively goes out to work.

• Divine providence partners with human responsibility. God had already sovereignly ordained that Boaz's field would be the place of redemption. However, that providence is realized through Ruth's ordinary, daily decision to go and glean. God guides the feet of those who are actively moving.

• The dignity of labor. Ruth’s willingness to "go" and perform the grueling, low-status task of gleaning demonstrates that legal rights (like the Mosaic provision for the poor) must be claimed through active, humble diligence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:2, the narrative contrasts Naomi’s passive grief with Ruth’s active devotion. The two women have returned to Bethlehem destitute. While they have physical security in the city, they have no food. Ruth takes the initiative. Her request, "Please let me go," uses the polite particle na, showing deep respect for her mother-in-law's authority.

The context is also one of immense vulnerability. As a young Moabitess (a foreign minority) in an era marked by moral lawlessness (the period of the Judges), going into a field to glean among male harvesters carried extreme risks of assault and exploitation. Ruth’s decision to "go" is therefore an act of profound courage and trust. Naomi’s response, "Go, my daughter," utilizes the imperative form of yalak (leki), granting her blessing and releasing Ruth into the hands of God's providence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:4: "So Abram departed as the LORD had spoken to him, and Lot went with him."

• Deuteronomy 8:6: "Therefore you shall keep the commandments of the LORD your God, to walk in His ways and to fear Him."

• Ruth 1:16: "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you; For wherever you go, I will go; And wherever you lodge, I will lodge; Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God.'"

• Joshua 1:9: "Have I not commanded you? Be strong and of good courage; do not be afraid, nor be dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Hebrew Scriptures, Ruth 2:2 marks the first time Ruth speaks since her famous declaration of loyalty in chapter 1. The structural parallel is intentional. In Ruth 1:16, she promised to "go" (yalak) with Naomi. In Ruth 2:2, she asks to "go" (yalak) to work for Naomi. Her character is perfectly consistent; she does not merely make verbal promises of covenant love (chesed), she actively lives them out by stepping onto the dusty paths of Bethlehem to serve.
