NAOMI - ( No'omiy , Strong's H5281 )

Summary:

Naomi is a Hebrew proper name meaning "my pleasantness" or "delight," reflecting sweetness, favor, and harmony. Throughout the book of Ruth, her name stands in stark, poignant contrast to her circumstances of famine, bereavement, and exile, culminating in her temporary desire to be called Mara ("bitter"). However, the narrative preserves her original name to underscore God's enduring covenant faithfulness, which ultimately restores her empty life to a state of abundant pleasantness and establishes her in the lineage of David and Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Naomi is rooted in the Hebrew concept of beauty, sweetness, and communal harmony. In the ancient Near Eastern world, names carried deep prophetic and characterological weight, serving as a reflection of a person’s identity or their parents' hopes. Naomi's name represents a life marked by divine favor and pleasant relations. The tragic events of her life—famine in Bethlehem, emigration to pagan Moab, and the devastating deaths of her husband Elimelech and her two sons—create a sharp literary and emotional tension with the sweetness of her name. Her life becomes outwardly the opposite of "pleasantness," leading to her crisis of faith where she seeks to reject her name in favor of "Mara" (bitter).

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper feminine name No'omiy translates directly as "my pleasantness" or "delight." It is derived from the Hebrew verb na'em, which means "to be agreeable, lovely, or pleasant," and the noun no'am, which signifies beauty, kindness, favor, or splendor. The trailing "yod" acts as a first-person pronominal suffix, rendering the literal meaning "my sweetness" or "my delight." It denotes not merely physical attractiveness, but a state of being highly favored, gracious, and bringing joy to those around her.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

While the proper noun No'omiy occurs 21 times in the Old Testament—exclusively within the Book of Ruth—its root words provide rich imagery across the Hebrew Scriptures:

• The Beauty of the Lord (Psalm 90:17): The noun no'am is translated as "beauty" or "favor" of the Lord, representing His gracious approval and the pleasantness of His presence resting upon His people.

• The Paths of Wisdom (Proverbs 3:17): Wisdom's ways are described as "ways of pleasantness" (no'am), illustrating that aligning one's life with God's design yields a deeply satisfying and harmonious existence.

• The Joy of Unity (Psalm 133:1): The related adjective na'im is used to describe how "good and pleasant" it is for brethren to dwell together in unity, capturing the fragrance of harmonious relationships.

### 4. Theological Implications

• The Reality of God’s Covenant Faithfulness (Hesed): The narrative structural tension of Ruth hinges on Naomi’s name. Although she experiences a season of extreme emptiness and declares that the Almighty has dealt bitterly with her, God’s covenant commitment to her never falters. The story demonstrates that her "pleasantness" is not determined by temporary circumstances but by the enduring grace of God, who ultimately restores her joy.

• The Preservation of Identity in Suffering: When Naomi returns to Bethlehem, she demands to be called Mara. Yet, the inspired narrator never adopts this name, continuing to refer to her as Naomi. This reveals a vital theological truth: trials may distort our perception of ourselves and God, but they do not change our true identity in God's covenant. God sees us through the lens of His ultimate, pleasant purpose, not our temporary bitterness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:2, Ruth asks Naomi for permission to glean in the fields: "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field...'"

• A Turning Point in Grief: In this verse, the name Naomi marks a critical shift. Having returned from Moab as a grieving widow, Naomi has been passive, paralyzed by her bitterness. By using her name here, the text highlights her position as the head of the household and the object of Ruth’s active devotion.

• Relational Sweetness: The continued use of No'omiy underscores the quiet, relational warmth that persists in their small household. Despite their shared tragedy, the "pleasantness" of family devotion remains intact through Ruth’s loyalty.

• The Stirring of Hope: When Naomi responds to Ruth with "Go, my daughter," she begins to step out of her passive isolation. The name Naomi serves as a subtle literary foreshadowing that the "pleasantness" of God's favor is beginning to dawn once again in Bethlehem through the faithful actions of these two women.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:2 – "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there."

• Ruth 1:20 – "But she said to them, 'Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.'"

• Ruth 2:20 – "Then Naomi said to her daughter-in-law, 'Blessed be he of the Lord, who has not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead!' And Naomi said to her, 'This man is a relation of ours, one of our close relatives.'"

• Ruth 4:17 – "Also the neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'There is a son born to Naomi.' And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Irony of Bethlehem: The book begins with Naomi ("pleasantness") leaving Bethlehem ("house of bread") due to famine. This double irony highlights the spiritual famine of the period of the Judges. However, her return to Bethlehem coincides with the barley harvest, symbolizing the return of both bread and pleasantness to her life.

• Redemption of the Lineage: In ancient Israel, legal genealogies were strictly patriarchal. Yet at the climax of the book, the neighborhood women declare, "There is a son born to Naomi" (Ruth 4:17). This unique phrasing shows that God did not merely provide for Ruth and Boaz; He specifically redeemed Naomi's line, ensuring that "pleasantness" would define her legacy, which extends directly to King David and ultimately to the Messiah.
