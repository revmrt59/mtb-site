SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the primary Old Testament word for "to say," "speak," "command," or "declare." Far more than a mere mechanical report of speech, 'amar in the context of covenant relationships—such as the one developing between Ruth and Naomi—signifies personal agency, humble initiative, and the communication of the heart's intent. In Ruth 2:2, this word marks a pivotal transition where Ruth moves from a passive sufferer of tragedy to an active, faith-filled participant in God's providential care.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root 'amar occurs over 5,000 times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most frequent verbs in the Hebrew Bible. Its basic concrete background is the externalization of internal thought. To "say" something in ancient Hebrew thought is to bring an inner reality—a thought, a desire, or a plan—into the physical realm through vocal expression. It represents the link between internal character and external action. In family and covenant settings, speech is the vehicle of relationship, building trust, establishing boundaries, and expressing devotion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'amar fundamentally means to speak, say, utter, or declare. It can also carry the nuance of "to command" (especially when spoken by a king or deity), "to promise," "to intend," or "to think" (often rendered as "to say in one's heart"). In its simplest Qal stem, it refers to direct or indirect speech where a subject communicates an idea to an object. In the context of interpersonal dialogue, it establishes a reciprocal relationship where the speaker expects to be heard, respected, and responded to.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Divine Speech as Creative Power: The first and most famous use of 'amar occurs in Genesis 1, where God "said" and creation came into being. This shows that the word carries inherent creative, life-giving, and causative weight.

• Internal Speech: Often, when the Old Testament describes someone "thinking," it translates literal phrases like "he said in his heart" using 'amar, showing that thoughts are viewed as internal words.

• Relational Entreaty: In narrative literature, 'amar is used to introduce polite requests, commands, and covenants, indicating that the speaker is initiating a relational exchange.

4. Theological Implications

In the book of Ruth, 'amar functions as the primary vehicle for expressing hesed (covenant loyalty and lovingkindness). Theology in the Old Testament is rarely delivered through abstract treatises; instead, it is lived out in everyday dialogue. When Ruth speaks, her words are not trivial; they reveal her conversion, her submission to the God of Israel, and her willingness to work. God's providence operates through the ordinary, respectful, and courageous words spoken between human beings. Ruth's speech reflects her faith in action, showing that faith speaks with humility and acts with diligence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:2, Ruth "said" ('amar) to Naomi, asking for permission to glean in the fields. This is highly significant because, as a foreign widow, Ruth could have remained passive, paralyzed by grief, or hopeless. Instead, her speech is filled with respect ("Please let me go") and hope ("in whose sight I may find favor"). The use of 'amar here marks the transition from Naomi's bitter grief to Ruth's active, respectful labor. She does not act independently or presumptuously; she speaks to her mother-in-law first, honoring the family structure even in their deep poverty.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3: Then God said, "Let there be light"; and there was light.

• Genesis 22:11: But the Angel of the Lord called to him from heaven and said, "Abraham, Abraham!" So he said, "Here I am."

• Ruth 1:16: But Ruth said: "Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you; For wherever you go, I will go; And wherever you lodge, I will lodge; Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God."

• Ruth 2:2: So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, "Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor." And she said to her, "Go, my daughter."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between Naomi’s silence or bitter speech in chapter 1 and Ruth's constructive, hopeful speech in chapter 2 is striking. Speech in the book of Ruth is redemptive. Almost every major turning point in the story is driven by a dialogue introduced by 'amar—whether it is Boaz speaking blessings over his workers, Ruth’s humble request at the threshing floor, or Boaz’s legal declarations at the city gate. Redemption is talked into existence through faithful, honorable human speech.
