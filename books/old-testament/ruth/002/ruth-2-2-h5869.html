SIGHT - ( ayin , Strong's H5869 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ayin literally refers to the physical eye, but it is frequently used idiomatically to denote mental and spiritual perception, judgment, presence, and favor. In Ruth 2:2, the term is translated "sight" in the phrase "in whose sight I may find favor." This idiom highlights the seeking of grace, validation, and protection from an authoritative figure. For Ruth, finding favor in the "sight" of an overseer or landowner was not merely about being seen physically, but about being regarded with compassion, kindness, and active goodwill.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word ayin has a dual meaning in biblical Hebrew, referring both to the physical "eye" and to a "spring" or "fountain" of water. The connection lies in the physical appearance of a spring bubbling out of the earth, resembling a weeping eye, and both being sources of flowing liquid.

• In ancient Near Eastern thought, the eye was viewed as the window of the soul and the primary organ of moral and emotional evaluation. What a person saw triggered an internal judgment, which then manifested in an outward disposition.

• To look upon someone with a "good eye" meant to show generosity, favor, and mercy, while an "evil eye" represented stinginess, envy, or malice. Thus, seeking to be in someone’s "sight" was a quest to evoke a positive, merciful response from their inner being.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Physically, ayin refers to the eye of a human or animal, representing the sense of sight or the organ itself.

• Idiomatically, when paired with the preposition bə (meaning "in"), the phrase bə‘ênê translates literally as "in the eyes of" or "in the sight of." This construct denotes:

• Personal judgment, opinion, or estimation (e.g., "right in his own eyes").

• Presence, awareness, or direct observation.

• The reception of grace and approval, most notably in the recurring covenantal idiom "to find favor (or grace) in the eyes/sight of" someone.

• In Ruth 2:2, the term functions as the spatial and relational arena where favor (chen) is granted. Ruth is seeking a landlord whose evaluation of her will lead to compassion rather than exploitation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Fountain of Favor: Just as an ayin (spring) provides life-giving water in a dry land, the ayin (eye) of a benevolent ruler or landowner provides life-giving favor to the destitute. Ruth’s hope of finding favor in someone's "sight" is a hope for a refreshing spring of grace in her desert of grief and poverty.

• The Eye of Evaluation: The eye is the seat of judgment. In the book of Ruth, human eyes are contrasted. The average harvester might look upon a foreign Moabite widow with disdain or lust. Ruth prays for an eye that evaluates her with dignity, recognizing her plight and her virtue.

• The Gaze of Protection: In Hebrew thought, to have someone's eye upon you means to be under their care and guardianship. By entering the "sight" of a godly man, Ruth seeks a canopy of safety.

4. Theological Implications

• The Divine Watchcare: Ultimately, the human "sight" that grants favor reflects the divine eye of God. The Scriptures teach that God's eyes are on the righteous and His ears are open to their cry. Ruth’s steps are guided by the invisible hand of God, who looks upon the humble with favor.

• The Character of Grace: Grace (chen) cannot be demanded; it must be freely given by the one who beholds the needy. Ruth’s search for favor "in his sight" shows her profound humility. She does not claim a right to glean; she hopes for unmerited kindness from a master who is willing to look upon her with grace.

• Redemption and Visibility: To be unseen is to be vulnerable and forgotten. By seeking to find favor in someone's sight, Ruth seeks to be recognized as a person of worth. Redemption begins when the redeemer truly "sees" the one in need.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:2, Ruth is a destitute foreigner, a Moabitess widow, entering a highly vulnerable position. The law of gleaning (Leviticus 19:9-10) allowed the poor to gather leftovers, but in the chaotic days of the Judges, young foreign women were often harassed or driven off the fields.

• Ruth's request to Naomi, "Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor," acknowledges this extreme danger. She cannot simply go to any field; she must find a landowner whose "eyes" are governed by covenant loyalty (hesed).

• The answer to her search is immediate and providential. She ends up in the field of Boaz, who notices her (Ruth 2:5) and orders his young men not to touch her. Ruth's prayer to find favor in someone's "sight" is physically realized when Boaz looks upon her with protective, redeeming grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 19:19 - "Indeed now, your servant has found favor in your sight, and you have magnified your mercy which you have shown me in saving my life..."

• Ruth 2:10 - "So she fell on her face, bowed down to the ground, and said to him, 'Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?'"

• 1 Samuel 1:18 - "And she said, 'Let your maidservant find favor in your sight.' So the woman went her way and ate, and her face was no longer sad."

• Proverbs 3:4 - "And so find favor and high esteem In the sight of God and man."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast with Judges: The story of Ruth takes place "in the days when the judges ruled" (Ruth 1:1). The hallmark of that dark era was that "everyone did what was right in his own eyes (ayin)" (Judges 21:25). In a culture where human eyes were self-centered and corrupt, Ruth seeks a different kind of "sight"—one that is aligned with God's justice and mercy.

• The Path to the Messiah: The favor Ruth finds in the sight of Boaz leads directly to their marriage, the birth of Obed, and the lineage of King David, culminating in Jesus Christ. The simple desire to find favor in the "sight" of a godly man became the catalyst for the visibility of the Savior of the world.
