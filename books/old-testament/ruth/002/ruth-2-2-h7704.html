FIELD - ( sadeh , Strong's 7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open field, cultivated land, or the surrounding countryside, distinct from fortified cities or uncultivable wilderness. In the narrative of Ruth, the sadeh represents both the place of raw physical labor and the providential stage where Ruth meets her redeemer, Boaz. It highlights God's structural provision for the marginalized through covenantal gleaning laws and serves as the physical setting where grief and emptiness begin their transformation into redemption and abundance.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, the sadeh represented the vast, open, unfenced agricultural areas surrounding a village or walled city. Unlike modern farms with defined boundaries, ancient Israelite fields were contiguous expanses of arable land divided only by narrow footpaths, small stone markers, or natural landmarks. Cultivators shared these vast stretches, though specific portions belonged to individual households. To step into the sadeh was to leave the protection and social structures of the city gates and enter a space of raw physical labor, exposure to the elements, and potential vulnerability, particularly for those without male protection or social status.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun sadeh denotes open country, wild land, or soil that is cultivated for agricultural purposes. The term is distinguished from the wilderness (midbar), which is uncultivated desert, and the city (iyr), which is inhabited and fortified. The semantic range of sadeh includes:

• Cultivated land used for growing crops such as wheat, barley, and vines.

• Pastureland or open plains where flocks graze.

• A specific plot or parcel of land owned by a family, clan, or individual.

• The open territory of a nation or region (e.g., "the country of Moab").

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs sadeh to convey several vivid pictures of life and spiritual reality:

• The Place of Wildness and Nature: It is the domain of wild beasts ("beasts of the field") and wild plants ("lilies of the field"), illustrating God's sovereign care over creation apart from human cultivation.

• The Domain of Human Labor and Productivity: It represents the sweat of man's brow, where seed is sown and harvest is gathered. A fertile field is a sign of divine favor, while a barren field indicates judgment.

• The Battlefield: It is the setting for military conflict ("the open field"), where soldiers face exposure without the defense of city walls.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of the sadeh is deeply rooted in the Torah, representing the intersection of divine ownership, human stewardship, and covenantal mercy.

• Divine Ownership: The land belongs fundamentally to Yahweh. Israelite landowners are merely tenants. Therefore, the treatment of the sadeh is a direct measure of obedience to God.

• Covenantal Mercy and Provision: The sadeh was the designated location for God's welfare system. Levitical law explicitly prohibited landowners from harvesting to the very edges of their fields or collecting the fallen grain (gleanings). This left-over harvest was legally reserved for the poor, the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless. The sadeh was thus transformed from a mere economic asset into a sanctuary of grace and dignity for the marginalized.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:2, Ruth's request to "go to the field" (hasadeh) is a step of faith, desperation, and humility. Having returned with Naomi to Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest, they have no food or income. Ruth initiates this labor, exposing herself to the heat, the physical strain, and the potential harassment of the reapers.

The use of sadeh here highlights the transition from the "fields of Moab" (where they lost everything) to the "field of Bethlehem" (where God's grace remains active). When Ruth steps into the sadeh, she does not know which specific plot she will land on. The text later notes that she "happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz." What appears as chance is the invisible hand of God guiding her footsteps into the specific portion of the sadeh where covenant love (hesed) is practiced, setting the stage for her physical provision and ultimate redemption.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 19:9 – "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest."

• Deuteronomy 24:19 – "When you reap your harvest in your field, and have forgotten a sheaf in the field, you shall not go back to get it; it shall be for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow, that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands."

• Ruth 2:3 – "Then she left, and went and gleaned in the field after the reapers. And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was of the family of Elimelech."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound spiritual contrast between the "fields of Moab" (sadeh in the plural/construct state in Ruth 1:1, 2, 6, 22) and the "field of Boaz" in Bethlehem. Elimelech led his family to the fields of Moab in search of bread during a famine, only to find death, grief, and emptiness. Ruth returns to the fields of Judah in poverty, yet through faithful labor in the sadeh under the shadow of the Almighty's wings, she finds abundant grain, a kinsman-redeemer, and a place in the lineage of the Messiah. The sadeh teaches us that true abundance is not found in fleeing God's covenant land for pagan territory, but in waiting upon and working in the place where He has promised His blessing.
