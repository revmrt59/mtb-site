BLESSED - ( barak , Strong's H1288 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb barak refers to the invocation or bestowal of divine favor, strength, peace, and abundance upon another. In the context of Ruth 2:20, Naomi’s exclamation, "Blessed be he of the LORD," represents a dramatic theological and emotional turning point. Having previously declared that the Almighty had dealt bitterly with her, Naomi now recognizes God's active, covenant-keeping kindness (chesed) through the generosity of Boaz. To bless Boaz is to acknowledge him as an instrument of Yahweh’s life-restoring grace, showing that blessing is not merely a polite greeting but a powerful recognition of God’s redemptive work operating through human relationships.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root of barak is closely associated with the Hebrew word for "knee" (berek). Historically and culturally, this suggests a physical posture of kneeling—either a superior bending down to present a gift or show favor to an inferior, or an inferior kneeling in honor, gratitude, and worship before a superior. In the ancient Near East, a blessing was not an empty sentiment or a wish; it was a spoken word filled with active power, carrying the weight of a formal transaction that set destiny and prosperity into motion. When a person blessed another in the name of Yahweh, they were positioning that person to receive the tangible, life-giving power of God’s presence, protection, and provision.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its basic verbal form, barak means to bless, praise, salute, or congratulate.

• When God is the subject blessing humans, barak refers to His active impartation of life, fertility, prosperity, and covenant security.

• When humans are the subject blessing God, barak shifts in meaning to praise, adore, or thank Him, acknowledging His supreme worth.

• When humans bless other humans, as Naomi does in Ruth 2:20, barak functions as an invocation of God's favor, asking Yahweh to reward, protect, and prosper the recipient. In Ruth 2:20, the word appears as a passive participle (baruk), meaning "endued with blessing" or "recipient of favor."

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, barak describes the transmission of life-giving power.

• The Power of the Spoken Blessing: In the patriarchal narratives, a spoken blessing could not be recalled once uttered (as seen with Isaac, Jacob, and Esau). It was a concrete reality.

• The Source of All Vitality: God’s first act after creating humanity was to bless them (barak), enabling them to be fruitful and multiply (Genesis 1:28). Thus, barak is the opposite of a curse (arar), which brings barrenness, decay, and isolation.

• Covenant Fulfillment: Barak is the mechanism through which the Abrahamic Covenant is realized. Abraham is blessed to be a blessing to all families of the earth.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, barak demonstrates that blessing is fundamentally relational and covenantal.

• Sovereign Grace: Blessing originates with Yahweh. Humans cannot bless independently of God's will; they can only invoke the blessing that God alone can perform.

• The Channel of Chesed: In the book of Ruth, barak is the verbal expression of chesed (covenant loyalty and lovingkindness). When Boaz shows extraordinary kindness to Ruth, Naomi responds by asking God to bless him. Human kindness triggers a prayer for divine blessing, showing that God's grace flows through human hands.

• Redemption and Restoration: The declaration of blessing marks the reversal of the curse of emptiness. Naomi left Bethlehem empty, but the blessing of Boaz signals that the years of famine, death, and barrenness are being overcome by God's life-giving favor.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:20, the use of barak is highly dramatic.

• In chapter 1, Naomi is filled with despair, instructing the women of Bethlehem to call her "Mara" (bitter) because the Almighty had brought her back empty and had afflicted her.

• In chapter 2, Ruth returns from the fields with an abundant harvest (an ephah of barley) and names her benefactor, Boaz.

• Instantly, Naomi’s spiritual blindness lifts. Her declaration, "Blessed be he of the LORD," is her first spoken praise and prayer in the book. It is a confession of renewed faith. She recognizes that Yahweh has "not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead."

• By blessing Boaz, Naomi recognizes that God is working behind the scenes. Boaz's field was not just a place of accidental good fortune; it was the place where God’s covenant blessing was breaking through their poverty.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:2 - "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you And make your name great; And you shall be a blessing."

• Genesis 14:19 - "And he blessed him and said: 'Blessed be Abram of God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth;'"

• Ruth 3:10 - "Then he said, 'Blessed are you of the LORD, my daughter! For you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning, in that you did not go after young men, whether poor or rich.'"

• Psalm 103:1 - "Bless the LORD, O my soul; And all that is within me, bless His holy name!"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

For pastoral preaching, barak in Ruth 2:20 offers a profound lesson on spiritual perception.

• The Transition from Bitterness to Blessing: Naomi's heart was closed by grief. It took a tangible act of human kindness (Boaz's generosity) to reopen her eyes to God's goodness. Pastors can use this to show how our acts of obedience and kindness can be the catalyst that restores someone else's faith in God.

• Speaking Blessing: Naomi does not have the material wealth to repay Boaz, but she possesses the spiritual authority to bless him. We may not always have material resources to solve every problem, but we can always invoke the blessing of God over those who do good.
