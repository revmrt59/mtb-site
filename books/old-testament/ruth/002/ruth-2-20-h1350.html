CLOSE RELATIVES - ( ga'al , Strong's H1350 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ga'al primarily means to redeem, buy back, or act as a kinsman-redeemer. In Ruth 2:20, the active participle noun form is translated as "close relatives," referring to a family member who possesses the legal right and moral obligation to rescue a destitute relative, reclaim lost family land, marry a childless widow to carry on the family name, or avenge a wrongful death. This concept highlights God’s covenantal care, demonstrating how redemption is not merely a legal transaction but an intimate, relational rescue. Boaz exemplifying this kinsman-redeemer role points forward to the ultimate redemption found in Jesus Christ, who became our brother to buy us back from sin and death.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• In ancient Israel, the preservation of the family unit, the lineage, and the ancestral land was vital for tribal survival and inheritance. When a family member fell into poverty, sold their land, or died without an heir, the family structure was threatened with extinction.

• The goel (the kinsman-redeemer) was the closest living male relative who stepped into the gap to restore what was lost. The word evokes the image of a protective shield, an advocate, and a restorer of broken lives.

• The background of this word is rooted in the Levitical laws of land redemption and the custom of levirate marriage. The goel had to meet three specific criteria to execute this role: he must be a blood relative, he must have the financial resources to pay the redemption price, and he must be willing to perform the redemption.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The root ga'al refers to the act of freeing, reclaiming, or redeeming something that has been alienated, forfeited, or enslaved.

• Lexical definitions identify two primary semantic areas for this root: legal-economic redemption (buying back property or persons from slavery) and relational-covenantal redemption (avenging blood or executing levirate marriage).

• Unlike other Hebrew words for redemption that emphasize paying a generic ransom, ga'al uniquely stresses the close family relationship between the redeemer and the redeemed. The redeemer must be a "close relative" to possess the legal right of redemption.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Land Redemption: If an Israelite was forced to sell ancestral property due to poverty, the goel was obligated to buy it back so it remained within the clan.

• Slavery Redemption: If an Israelite sold himself into slavery to pay off debts, a close relative could pay the ransom to restore his freedom.

• Blood Avenger: The goel haddam (avenger of blood) was responsible for securing justice for a murdered relative, defending the family's honor and sanctity of life.

• Levirate Marriage: Though technically distinct in legal origins, the book of Ruth blends this with the goel concept. The redeemer marries the childless widow of his deceased relative to raise up an heir and preserve the deceased's name.

4. Theological Implications

• The concept of the goel provides a vivid Old Testament shadow of the person and work of Jesus Christ.

• Jesus met all three requirements of the kinsman-redeemer. First, He became our blood relative through the Incarnation, sharing in our flesh and blood. Second, He possessed the ultimate spiritual capital—His own sinless life—to pay our debt. Third, He willingly laid down His life to redeem us.

• God Himself is frequently described as Israel's Goel (Redeemer), particularly in the prophets, where He delivers His people from exile, vindicates their cause, and restores their heritage.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:20, Naomi's eyes are opened to God’s providential care. Ruth has just returned from gleaning in the field of Boaz, and Naomi recognizes his name.

• The phrase "one of our close relatives" (mi-go'alenu) marks the turning point of the narrative. It shifts the tone of the book from grief and emptiness to hope and restoration.

• Naomi realizes Boaz is not just a wealthy landowner who showed temporary kindness, but a goel—someone who has the legal authority and capability to redeem Elimelech’s land and secure a future for Ruth and herself. The word highlights how God uses human relationships and covenant responsibilities to execute His divine grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 25:25 - "If one of your brethren becomes poor, and has sold some of his possession, and if his redeeming relative comes to redeem it, then he may redeem what his brother sold."

• Job 19:25 - "For I know that my Redeemer lives, and He shall stand at last on the earth;"

• Isaiah 43:1 - "But now, thus says the Lord, who created you, O Jacob, and He who formed you, O Israel: 'Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; You are Mine.'"

• Ruth 4:14 - "Then the women said to Naomi, 'Blessed be the Lord, who has not left you this day without a close relative; and may his name be famous in Israel!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The "nearer kinsman" obstacle in Ruth 3:12 shows that the role of goel was strictly governed by legal priority. Boaz could not act immediately because another relative had a closer right of redemption. This highlights Boaz's high moral integrity; he sought redemption through lawful and righteous means.

• The refusal of the unnamed closer relative in Ruth 4:6 ("I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance") contrasts sharply with Boaz's selfless willingness. The unnamed relative feared losing his own wealth, whereas Boaz willingly absorbed the cost out of love, mirroring Christ's sacrificial love for His bride, the Church.
