LIVING - ( chay , Strong's H2416 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chay refers to that which is alive, active, or vital, as opposed to that which is dead, stagnant, or inactive. In the Old Testament, it denotes physical life, the state of being alive, and the vibrant energy bestowed by God, who is Himself the "living God." In the context of Ruth 2:20, "the living" refers to Naomi and Ruth, the survivors of Elimelech's family. Here, the word highlights God's active, ongoing covenant faithfulness (chesed) that does not stop at the grave but continues to sustain, preserve, and restore those who remain alive to receive His mercy.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew mindset is deeply concrete and dynamic, viewing life not as a static biological category, but as active movement, breath, and relationship. The root behind chay evokes images of fresh, flowing water, active physical movement, and the literal intake of breath. Stagnant water is considered "dead" water, whereas water flowing directly from a natural spring is "living water" (mayim chayyim). This term conveys vitality, freshness, and the active presence of energy. To be alive is to possess the capacity for relational response, physical labor, and spiritual communion with God.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word chay is a Hebrew adjective and noun meaning "living," "alive," or "active." Its primary semantic definitions include:

• Physical animation or being alive, as opposed to being dead (met).

• Vitality, health, and life-giving strength.

• Freshness and flow, specifically when describing water or vegetation.

• The duration of physical life or a lifetime.

• When used in the plural form (chayyim), it serves as an abstract noun meaning "life" or "the state of being alive."

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Breath of Life: In creation, God breathes the "breath of life" (nishmat chayyim) into the dust of the ground, showing that human life is a direct, dependent participation in the vitality of the Creator.

• Living Water: Running, fresh spring water (mayim chayyim) represents sustained renewal and cleansing, contrasting with stagnant cisterns.

• The Living God: God is frequently called the "Living God" (El Chay), distinguishing Him from the dead, mute idols of the surrounding nations. He is the active, speaking, and self-existent Source of all reality.

### 4. Theological Implications

In the Old Testament, life is always a gift of God's covenant relationship. It is not merely biological existence, but existence in alignment with God's will and presence. In Ruth 2:20, Naomi links God’s covenant kindness (chesed) to both the living and the dead. This reveals that the sphere of God's faithful love is not limited by the grave. God’s care for the living is the mechanism by which He preserves the names, legacies, and inheritances of those who have died. The living are the recipients of covenant loyalty on behalf of the deceased, demonstrating the multi-generational, unbroken continuity of God's redemptive plan.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:20, Naomi’s exclamation marks the emotional and spiritual turning point of the entire book. Having left Moab in grief, feeling "empty" and functionally "dead" to hope, she suddenly recognizes the active hand of God in Boaz’s generosity. When Naomi refers to "the living," she is speaking directly of herself and Ruth. They are the physically living but socially marginalized survivors who have been preserved through famine and loss. By noting that God has not withdrawn His kindness (chesed) from "the living and to the dead," Naomi realizes that the physical survival of the living is intrinsically tied to the redemption of the deceased. The dead (Elimelech and his sons) cannot redeem their own land or continue their family line; only the living can act as agents of restoration. Through Boaz's intervention, God will provide life to the living, which in turn restores the name and inheritance of the dead.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:24: "Then God said, 'Let the earth bring forth the living creature according to its kind: cattle and creeping thing and beast of the earth, each according to its kind'; and it was so."

• Genesis 2:7: "And the Lord God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living being."

• Deuteronomy 5:26: "For who is there of all flesh who has heard the voice of the living God speaking from the midst of the fire, as we have, and lived?"

• Psalm 116:9: "I will walk before the Lord In the land of the living."

• Jeremiah 2:13: "For My people have committed two evils: They have forsaken Me, the fountain of living waters, And hewn themselves cisterns—broken cisterns that can hold no water."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The plural form chayyim represents an "intensive" plural in Hebrew grammar. Rather than simply meaning multiple lives, it denotes life in its fullest expression, completeness, and multi-faceted experiences. It emphasizes that life is not a singular, flat reality but a rich tapestry of relationships, actions, and spiritual awareness. This Hebrew foundation directly prepares the way for the New Testament understanding of "abundant life" (zoe), which is found only through union with Christ.
