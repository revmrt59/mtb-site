KINDNESS - ( chesed , Strong's H2617 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chesed describes a deep, steadfast, and covenantal love that goes far beyond mere politeness or passing emotion. In Ruth 2:20, it represents the faithful, loyal goodness shown by Boaz to Naomi and Ruth—a reflection of God's own proactive, merciful character acting on behalf of the vulnerable.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Rooted in the concept of loyalty, steadfastness, and enduring devotion within a relationship.

• Often describes acts of kindness that are morally binding and deeply personal, rather than random or obligatory gestures.

• Captures the essence of familial or covenantal obligation where one party steps in to protect, provide for, and rescue another who is in a vulnerable position.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Steadfast love, loyal love, unfailing love, or lovingkindness.

• Mercy, goodness, faithfulness, and grace put into active, practical service.

• It bridges emotion and action: it is love with its sleeves rolled up, actively meeting the needs of another based on a prior commitment or relationship.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Used to describe the unbreakable bond of loyalty between friends, family members, or a king and his subjects.

• Frequently paired with faithfulness (emet), highlighting that this is a reliable, trustworthy love that does not fail when circumstances turn difficult.

• In narrative contexts, it often marks a turning point where despair gives way to hope through the unexpected generosity of another human being.

4. Theological Implications

• Primarily describes God's covenantal loyalty and mercy toward His people, which endures forever even when they are unfaithful.

• When demonstrated by humans (such as Boaz), it is a visible, earthly reflection of divine character in action.

• Serves as the moral engine behind redemption, prompting characters to act as redeemers (goel) for the helpless.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:20, Naomi exclaims that Boaz has not left off his chesed to the living and the dead.

• Naomi recognizes that Boaz’s generosity to Ruth (and through Ruth, to the deceased Elimelech and Mahlon) is not merely accidental hospitality, but a continuation of God's covenantal care.

• This kindness signals the beginning of divine reversal, turning Naomi’s bitterness into the hope of a restored family line.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 34:6 - And the Lord passed before him and proclaimed, "The Lord, the Lord God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness (chesed) and truth..."

• Psalm 136:1 - Oh, give thanks to the Lord, for He is good! For His mercy (chesed) endures forever.

• Hosea 6:6 - For I desire mercy (chesed) and not sacrifice, and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings.

• Micah 6:8 - He has shown you, O man, what is good; and what does the Lord require of you but to do justly, to love mercy (chesed), and to walk humbly with your God?

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Chesed is notoriously difficult to translate into a single English word because it combines emotional warmth (love/mercy) with moral strength (loyalty/faithfulness).

• In the Book of Ruth, human chesed mirrors divine chesed, showing how God works through ordinary acts of human decency and radical generosity to accomplish His redemptive purposes.
