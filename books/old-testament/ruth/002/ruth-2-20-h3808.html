NOT - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' functions as the primary word for absolute negation in the Old Testament. In Ruth 2:20, it qualifies the verb "left off" ('azab), creating a powerful declaration that Yahweh has "not ceased" or "not abandoned" His covenant loyalty (chesed) to both the living and the deceased. This tiny particle marks a massive theological pivot in the book of Ruth, shifting Naomi's perspective from bitter despair to hopeful restoration by affirming the uninterrupted, active grace of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The particle lo' is the standard, absolute adverb of negation in Biblical Hebrew, appearing more than 5,000 times across the Old Testament. Unlike the subjective or deprecatory negative particle 'al (which expresses a temporary prohibition or a wish, such as "do not"), lo' represents an objective, permanent, and absolute negation. It establishes a definitive boundary, a complete and unyielding "no" or "not." In ancient Near Eastern covenant language, a negation of abandonment signifies the absolute continuation of an obligation. When paired with God's actions, lo' establishes an unshakeable truth: what God has promised, He will absolutely not fail to perform.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The adverb lo' signifies absolute negation, denying the reality, occurrence, or existence of the action or state it modifies. It functions in the following ways:

• Absolute negation of a verbal action, indicating that an event has not occurred, is not occurring, or will not occur.

• Unconditional prohibition when paired with the imperfect tense, expressing permanent laws (such as the Ten Commandments, "You shall not...").

• In Ruth 2:20, it directly negates the active participle of the verb 'azab ("to leave, forsake, abandon, or let go"). Therefore, the combination lo' 'azab translates directly as "has not abandoned" or "has not left off." This indicates the complete preservation and uninterrupted continuation of God's chesed (covenant love and kindness).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

As an adverb of negation, lo' does not carry a physical "word picture" in the way a concrete noun does, but its grammatical impact creates a vivid rhetorical boundary.

• It acts as a definitive wall blocking a negative outcome. In Hebrew thought, the negation of a negative action acts as a powerful positive affirmation. To say God has "not abandoned" is far more descriptive and emotionally resonant than simply saying He "continued." It acknowledges the threat of abandonment—which Naomi keenly felt in her grief—and definitively rules it out.

• The grammatical construction in Ruth 2:20 attaches lo' to the verb 'azab (to leave or forsake). This paints a picture of Yahweh holding fast to His covenant family. He has not let go of the rope; He has not walked away from the relationship.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of lo' in Ruth 2:20 has profound theological significance:

• Unbroken Covenant Faithfulness (Chesed): The verse links lo' with God's chesed (lovingkindness/loyalty). It asserts that God’s covenant commitment is continuous and cannot be interrupted by human tragedy, famine, or death.

• Sovereign Care in the Ordinary: Naomi recognizes God's hand in Boaz's kindness. The negation lo' reveals that even when God seemed silent or absent during the famine and deaths in Moab, He had never actually ceased His providential care.

• Redemption for Both the Living and the Dead: The continuous flow of God's kindness extends beyond the grave. By not leaving off His kindness to "the living and the dead," Yahweh preserves the lineage and name of Elimelech and Mahlon, proving that death does not terminate God’s covenantal obligations or His power to redeem.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the narrative arc of Ruth, this verse serves as the turning point of the entire book.

• In chapter 1, Naomi is filled with bitterness, claiming that the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with her and that the Lord has testified against her. She felt completely abandoned.

• In Ruth 2:20, upon hearing that the man who showed Ruth kindness was Boaz, Naomi's eyes are opened to God's silent providence. The word lo' ("not") represents the sudden collapse of her bitter theology of abandonment. The reality is the exact opposite of her previous feelings: God has not forsaken them.

• The placement of lo' emphasizes the continuity of God's grace. It bridges the gap between Naomi's past sorrow and her future hope, demonstrating that her perception of abandonment was false.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Grammatical emphasis: In Hebrew syntax, placing the negative particle lo' directly before the verb 'azab creates an emphatic negation of the state. It is not a passive omission but an active denial of abandonment.

• Anthropomorphic assurance: Human relationships frequently suffer from broken promises and abandonment. By using lo' in relation to His chesed, the text contrasts God's unchanging nature with human instability.

• Pastoral application: Believers often interpret their circumstances through the lens of temporary trials, concluding that God has forgotten them. Ruth 2:20 reminds the church that our feelings of abandonment do not dictate reality. God's chesed remains actively at work behind the scenes, absolutely "not" leaving us.
