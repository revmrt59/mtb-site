DEAD - ( muth, Strong's H4191 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word muth (occurring here in its masculine plural participle form, metim) refers to those who have ceased to live physically. In the context of Ruth 2:20, Naomi uses "the dead" to refer to her deceased husband, Elimelech, and her sons, Mahlon and Chilion. The term denotes a state of physical death where earthly existence, inheritance rights, and family representation are suspended. This highlights the vulnerability of the surviving widows and emphasizes the crucial role of a kinsman-redeemer to restore the names and property of the deceased.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root muth is the standard Hebrew verb meaning "to die" or "to become dead." Its participle form describes the dead—those who have crossed the threshold from the land of the living into the realm of the grave (sheol). In ancient Israel, death was not merely the end of biological function, but a disruption in the covenant community and family lineage. To die without an heir was a profound tragedy, threatening the permanent loss of a family’s ancestral land. The "dead" in the book of Ruth represent a severed link in Israel's generational chain, requiring legal redemption so that their name and legacy do not perish from the nation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root muth carries the primary meaning of dying, expiring, or being put to death. As a substantive participle, it functions as a noun translating to "the dead."

• Physical death of human beings, marking the end of earthly life.

• The state of being deceased, where one no longer actively participates in the physical world.

• The legal status of a deceased landowner whose name must be preserved through levirate marriage or land redemption.

• In the covenant framework of Israel, the dead still retain rights to their inheritance, which close relatives are morally obligated to protect.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, a stark contrast is drawn between the "living" and the "dead." The living possess breath, praise God, and interact within the community; the dead are silent and removed from earthly relations. Yet, the biblical narrative depicts the dead as still belonging to the family unit. The concept of the goel (kinsman-redeemer) demonstrates that the deceased are not forgotten by God's law. A redeemer acts on behalf of the dead to purchase back lost property and raise up children in the deceased man's name, demonstrating that the boundaries of family responsibility extend even beyond the grave.

4. Theological Implications

The preservation of the name of the dead reflects God's covenant faithfulness (chesed). When Naomi praises the Lord for not forsaking His kindness "to the living and to the dead," she reveals that God's lovingkindness is not limited by human mortality. God's chesed reaches back to Elimelech and his sons by ensuring their lineage continues and their inheritance is redeemed. This serves as a powerful type of Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, who enters into our state of spiritual ruin and death to redeem our lost inheritance and give us eternal life.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:20, Naomi’s exclamation marks the dramatic turning point of the narrative. Having previously claimed that the Almighty had dealt bitterly with her, the discovery that Ruth gleaned in the field of Boaz changes everything. The "dead" refers specifically to Naomi’s deceased husband and sons. Naomi recognizes that Boaz’s kindness to Ruth is the providential hand of God working to restore the family. By showing favor to the living widow (Ruth), Boaz is paving the way to honor and preserve the name of the dead (Mahlon and Elimelech) through land redemption and marriage.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "kindness to the living and to the dead" shows that Hebrew thought viewed the family as a single, continuous entity spanning generations. The living had a sacred duty to honor the memory and protect the heritage of those who passed away. Boaz becomes the human instrument through whom God manifests this transgenerational chesed, proving that God's covenant promises outlast physical death.
