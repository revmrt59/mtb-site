NAOMI - ( No'omiy , Strong's H5281 )

Summary:

The Hebrew name Naomi means "my pleasantness," "delightful," or "lovely." Derived from a root denoting sweetness, beauty, and favor, her name represents her covenant identity and the joyful life God intended for her. Within the narrative of the Book of Ruth, Naomi's name serves as a vital literary and theological marker. After experiencing famine, displacement, and the devastating deaths of her husband and sons, she bitterly rejected her name, declaring herself Mara ("bitter"). However, in Ruth 2:20, upon witnessing the unexpected, abundant provision of Boaz, Naomi recognizes the active, enduring kindness of Yahweh. This pivotal moment marks the beginning of her restoration, as the bitter widow is brought back into the "pleasantness" of God’s covenantal grace and providence.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name No'omiy is built on a Hebrew root that carries the concrete idea of sweetness to the taste, physical beauty, or social agreeableness. In the ancient Near East, names were not merely labels but were deeply tied to a person's character, destiny, or the spiritual aspirations of the parents. Naomi's name suggests a life of sweetness, favor, and divine blessing.

The tragic irony of the Book of Ruth is that Naomi, whose name breathed pleasantness, experiences a series of devastating losses that directly contradict her name. She experiences famine in Bethlehem (the "House of Bread"), migrates to pagan Moab, and suffers the deaths of her husband Elimelech and her two sons, Mahlon and Chilion. Her external circumstances stood in such stark opposition to her name that she experienced an existential and spiritual crisis, leading her to demand a name change upon her return to Bethlehem.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun No'omiy literally translates to "my pleasantness" or "my delight."

• It is closely related to the noun no'am, which refers to "pleasantness," "loveliness," or "favor" (as in the "beauty/favor of the Lord" in Psalm 90:17).

• It also shares its root with the adjective na'iym, meaning "pleasant," "sweet," or "delightful."

• The final letter (yod) functions as a pronominal suffix meaning "my," or as an adjectival ending, making the name mean "the pleasant one" or "my pleasantness."

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name Naomi occurs 21 times in the Hebrew Bible, exclusively within the Book of Ruth.

The primary word picture associated with Naomi is the sharp literary contrast between "pleasantness" (Naomi) and "bitterness" (Mara). In Ruth 1:20, she states, "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me." This deliberate wordplay highlights her emotional and spiritual state.

Throughout the narrative, the reclamation of her name represents the recovery of joy, community, and divine favor. She begins as a pleasant woman, is reduced to a bitter widow, and is ultimately restored as a joyful grandmother who experiences the redeeming sweetness of God's providence.

### 4. Theological Implications

• Providence and Sovereignty: Naomi’s journey demonstrates that God’s covenantal faithfulness (hesed) is active even when His people experience bitter providences. Her name serves as a constant reminder that God's ultimate disposition toward His covenant people is one of "pleasantness" and favor, even when hidden behind painful circumstances.

• The Kinsman-Redeemer (Goel): Naomi's name is intrinsically tied to the theme of redemption. Her restoration is not individualistic; it is corporate and generational. Through Boaz, the redeemer, Naomi's empty hands are filled, showing that redemption turns bitterness back into pleasantness. This foreshadows the ultimate redemption found in Jesus Christ, who sweetens the bitter waters of human suffering and sin.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:20, the use of Naomi's name marks a pivotal turning point in the entire narrative. After Ruth returns from gleaning in the fields of Boaz with an abundance of barley, Naomi learns the identity of the landowner.

Her exclamation, "May he be blessed by the LORD, whose kindness has not forsaken the living or the dead!" reveals a dramatic shift in Naomi's posture. The woman who previously claimed "the Almighty has afflicted me" (Ruth 1:21) now recognizes the active, covenant-keeping hesed of Yahweh.

By naming Naomi here as the speaker, the narrator emphasizes that the "pleasant one" is recovering her spiritual sight. The bitterness of Mara is dissolving as she recognizes that God has not abandoned her family. Her identification of Boaz as a "close relative... one of our redeemers" sets the stage for the redemption of Elimelech's line and the birth of Obed.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:2 – "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there."

• Ruth 1:20 – "But she said to them, 'Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.'"

• Ruth 4:14 – "Then the women said to Naomi, 'Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a close relative; and may his name be famous in Israel!'"

• Ruth 4:17 – "Also the neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'There is a son born to Naomi.' And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• A Mirror of Israel: The transition from Naomi to Mara and back to Naomi mirrors the broader history of Israel. During the period of the Judges (in which this story is set), Israel repeatedly turned away from God, experienced the bitterness of oppression, and was subsequently restored to the "pleasantness" of God's presence through deliverers.

• The Full Circle of Redemption: In Ruth 4:16, we see Naomi taking Ruth's child, Obed, and laying him on her bosom, becoming a nurse to him. The community declares "a son is born to Naomi," signifying that her pleasantness has been fully restored. Her emptiness (Ruth 1:21) has been transformed into absolute fullness.
