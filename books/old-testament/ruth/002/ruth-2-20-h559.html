SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar means to say, speak, declare, or think. In the narrative of Ruth 2:20, Naomi’s utterance ("said") serves as the dramatic turning point of the entire book. After months of bitter silence and grief, her words burst forth with renewed faith, recognizing God's providence through Boaz. This simple word of speech transitions Naomi from hopeless grief to active hope, highlighting the power of spoken testimony to frame God's covenant loyalty (chesed).

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• 'amar is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Old Testament, occurring over 5,000 times.

• Unlike other Hebrew verbs for speaking that emphasize the physical act of making sound or formal proclamations (such as dabar), 'amar focuses on the content, intent, and personal expression of the speaker.

• It is often used to reveal the inner thoughts, heart posture, or deep-seated convictions of an individual.

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, speech was not merely the transmission of data but an active force that initiated relationships, established covenants, and bestowed blessings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'amar fundamentally denotes the act of verbalizing thoughts, plans, or commands. Its semantic range includes:

• To utter, say, or speak (the primary conversational use).

• To think or say to oneself (when used in contexts describing internal dialogue, often followed by "in the heart").

• To command, order, or decree (particularly when spoken by a king, authority figure, or God).

• To promise, intend, or name.

• In the Qal stem, as found in Ruth 2:20, it represents the straightforward act of speaking, yet the immediate context charges this speech with intense emotional and theological weight, turning a simple declaration into a confession of restored faith.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In the creation account of Genesis 1, 'amar is the instrument of divine power: "Then God said, 'Let there be light'..." Here, the spoken word carries the force of creative command, bringing reality out of nothingness.

• In conversational narratives, 'amar introduces direct discourse, allowing the reader to hear the heart of the character. When Naomi speaks in Ruth 2:20, her words are not a casual observation but a profound realization.

• It can express internal monologue (e.g., Genesis 20:11, "Because I thought ['amar]..."), illustrating that 'amar encompasses both the spoken word and the inner convictions that prompt it.

4. Theological Implications

The use of 'amar in the Old Testament highlights the relational nature of God and humanity. God is a speaking God who reveals Himself through words, and humanity responds through speech.

• When Naomi uses 'amar to declare "Blessed be he of the Lord," she is initiating a theological shift.

• Her speech marks the transition from bitter silence and lament (Ruth 1:20-21, where she said she was empty) to praise.

• It demonstrates that verbalizing God's goodness is central to the life of faith. Speech is the vehicle through which covenant kindness (chesed) is acknowledged and celebrated within the faith community.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:20, 'amar occurs twice: "Then Naomi said to her daughter-in-law..." and "And Naomi said to her..."

• The first 'amar introduces a passionate blessing over Boaz and a recognition of Yahweh's unbroken kindness (chesed) to both the living and the dead. This is a dramatic reversal from Naomi's previous speech in chapter 1, where she claimed the Almighty had dealt bitterly with her.

• The second 'amar introduces the crucial legal and familial revelation: "This man is a relation of ours, one of our close relatives (goel)."

• By repeating the verb 'amar, the narrator draws attention to Naomi's voice. Her speech has returned. The bitter woman who demanded to be called "Mara" is now speaking words of life, hope, and redemptive strategy. The context dictates that 'amar here is an expression of sudden, joyful enlightenment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3: "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Exodus 3:14: "And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And He said, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, "I AM has sent me to you."'"

• Ruth 1:20: "But she said to them, 'Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me;'"

• Ruth 3:5: "And she said to her, 'All that you say to me I will do.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The relationship between 'amar (to say) and dabar (to speak): While dabar often refers to the act of speaking or the word itself as a matter/thing, 'amar is almost always followed by the actual words spoken. This highlights the specificity of Naomi’s praise—she does not just talk about her feelings; she speaks specific truths about God's character.

• The literary structure of Ruth utilizes dialogue ('amar) to drive the plot forward. Over fifty-five percent of the Book of Ruth is direct speech, making 'amar the primary narrative engine that reveals the characters' faith and integrity.
