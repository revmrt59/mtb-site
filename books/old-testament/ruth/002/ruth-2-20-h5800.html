FORSAKEN - ( azab , Strong's H5800 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb azab means to leave, abandon, loose, or forsake. In the Old Testament, it describes both human desertion—such as abandoning a covenant, a spouse, or God—and the reassuring truth of God's covenant loyalty, wherein He promises never to abandon or forsake His people. In Ruth 2:20, Naomi uses this term to celebrate the Lord's unbroken loyalty, recognizing that God has not "left off" or "forsaken" His lovingkindness (chesed) toward both the living and the dead.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of azab is to release, loose, let go, or leave behind. In ancient Near Eastern pastoral and agricultural contexts, it could refer to leaving a land uncultivated, leaving flock animals unattended, or loosening a burden from a pack animal. Over time, the word developed strong relational and ethical connotations. To azab someone is to withdraw one's presence, support, or commitment. When used of God, it carries deep emotional and theological weight, representing the terrifying prospect of divine abandonment or, conversely, the comforting assurance of His perpetual presence and covenant fidelity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of azab is to leave behind, forsake, or abandon.

• It can mean to depart from a person, place, or state.

• It signifies neglecting, relinquishing, or giving up something, such as forsaking a path or a covenant.

• In passive forms, it describes that which is desolate, deserted, or completely abandoned.

• When paired with chesed (covenant faithfulness/lovingkindness), it denotes the withholding or withdrawing of loyal love. Conversely, to "not forsake" chesed means to actively and continuously demonstrate loyal love.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Forsaken Wife: Isaiah 54:6 uses azab to depict Israel as a woman "forsaken and grieved in spirit," portraying the deep emotional agony of rejection and abandonment.

• The Loosened Burden: In Exodus 23:5, the word describes helping an enemy release (azab) his donkey lying under its burden, illustrating a physical letting go or freeing from restraint.

• The Deserted Land: In prophetic literature, azab describes fields, vineyards, and cities left desolate and abandoned to wild beasts due to judgment.

• The Forsaken Covenant: Frequently in Deuteronomy and the historical books, the word describes Israel's tragic tendency to "forsake" the Lord and His commandments to serve other gods.

4. Theological Implications

• Divine Faithfulness: The grand theological theme surrounding azab is God's unwavering commitment to His people. While humans routinely forsake God, God promises never to forsake those who trust in Him. This forms the foundation of biblical hope.

• Covenant Security: God's refusal to azab His people is not based on their merit, but on His sovereign character and covenant oaths.

• The Ultimate Forsaking: The theological climax of this word occurs prophetically and redemptively. While God promises not to forsake His righteous ones, the Messiah experiences ultimate abandonment on the cross so that believers might never be eternally forsaken.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage (Ruth 2:20)

• In Ruth 2:20, Naomi exclaims, "Blessed be he of the Lord, who has not left off His kindness to the living and the dead!" The phrase translated "left off" is the Hebrew azab.

• This marks the dramatic turning point of the narrative. Having previously claimed that the Lord had dealt bitterly with her and left her empty (Ruth 1:20-21), Naomi now recognizes that God has not forsaken (azab) His chesed (loyal love/kindness).

• The grammatical structure reveals that God is the ultimate agent of this kindness. Though Boaz showed immediate favor to Ruth, Naomi sees Boaz as the instrument through whom Yahweh is actively demonstrating that He has not abandoned the family of Elimelech, even after death.

• By declaring that God has not "left off" His kindness to the living and the dead, Naomi acknowledges that the family line is still under the active, protective, and redeeming care of the covenant God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 24:27 – "And he said, 'Blessed be the Lord God of my master Abraham, who has not forsaken His mercy and His truth toward my master.'"

• Deuteronomy 31:6 – "Be strong and of good courage, do not fear nor be afraid of them; for the Lord your God, He is the One who goes with you. He will not leave you nor forsake you."

• Joshua 1:5 – "No man shall be able to stand before you all the days of your life; as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not leave you nor forsake you."

• Psalm 22:1 – "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me? Why are You so far from helping Me, And from the words of My groaning?"

• Psalm 37:25 – "I have been young, and now am old; Yet I have not seen the righteous forsaken, Nor his descendants begging bread."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In the Septuagint (LXX), azab is frequently translated by the Greek verb egkataleipo, which means to leave behind in a state of distress or abandon. This is the exact Greek word used in Matthew 27:46 and Mark 15:34 when Jesus quotes Psalm 22:1 on the cross, as well as in Hebrews 13:5, which quotes Deuteronomy 31:6 ("I will never leave you nor forsake you").

• The link between Ruth 2:20 and Genesis 24:27 is highly significant. In both instances, an unexpected encounter leads to the praise of God for not "forsaking" (azab) His mercy (chesed). Both contexts involve the providential preservation of a family line that ultimately leads to the lineage of David and Jesus Christ.
