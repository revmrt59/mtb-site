ALL - ( kol , Strong's H3605 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word kol denotes totality, the whole, entirety, or completion of a given unit or period. In Ruth 2:21, it emphasizes the absolute completion of Boaz's harvest ("all my harvest"), indicating a prolonged period of protection, provision, and close proximity for Ruth. This word underscores the comprehensive scope of Boaz’s grace, ensuring Ruth is fully cared for from the beginning of the barley harvest to the end of the wheat harvest.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun kol is derived from the root kalal, which means to complete, perfect, or make whole. The conceptual picture behind the root is that of a finished vessel or a completed circle where nothing is left out. In ancient Israelite agriculture, the harvest was a vital, multi-stage season that determined a family's survival for the coming year. By using kol, the text points to the entire cycle of gathering grain, leaving no part of the agricultural process or the calendar season excluded from Boaz's decree of safety and provision.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, kol refers to the totality of something and is translated as "all," "the whole," "every," or "entirety." When prefixed to a singular noun with a definite article, as it is in Ruth 2:21 (kāl-haq-qā-ṣîr), it denotes "the entirety of" or "the whole." It functions here to define the temporal and physical boundaries of Ruth's permission to glean. Boaz does not offer a brief, temporary, or partial allowance; his instruction covers the entire duration of the harvesting period.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, kol is used as a crucial boundary marker to establish completeness:

• It defines the parameters of covenant devotion, calling for love toward God with "all" (kol) the heart, soul, and strength.

• It describes the total scope of creation, where God saw "everything" (kol) He had made and declared it very good.

• In the book of Ruth, the word functions as a literary turning point. Ruth and Naomi began in total emptiness, but through Boaz's covenant-keeping kindness (chesed), they are brought into contact with "all" of his abundance. The word picture is one of overflowing, unchecked grace that persists until the work is fully accomplished.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, kol in this passage highlights the thoroughness of God's redemptive provision. Boaz, serving as a type of the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ, does not provide a partial salvation. His protection and provision are comprehensive. By extending his grace to cover "all" the harvest, he guarantees sustenance spanning from the barley harvest (Passover season) through the wheat harvest (Pentecost). This points to the systematic, complete care that God provides for those who take refuge under His wings.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:21, Ruth reports Boaz's words to her mother-in-law, Naomi: "He also said to me, 'You shall stay close by my young men until they have ended all my harvest.'" The pairing of kol with the verb kalah ("ended" or "completed") creates a powerful double emphasis on finality and security. This statement is so generous that it immediately evokes praise and relief from Naomi in the next verse. She realizes that staying in Boaz's fields for the entirety of the harvest prevents Ruth from being molested or vulnerable in other, less secure fields.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:31: "Then God saw everything (kol) that He had made, and indeed it was very good."

• Deuteronomy 6:5: "You shall love the LORD your God with all (kol) your heart, with all (kol) your soul, and with all (kol) your strength."

• Psalm 103:1: "Bless the LORD, O my soul; And all (kol) that is within me, bless His holy name!"

• Ruth 3:11: "And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all (kol) that you request, for all (kol) the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "all my harvest" encompasses a period of approximately seven weeks. This duration was a critical trial period for the newly returned widows. By securing Ruth's place for "all" of this time, Boaz ensures she is not just a passing recipient of charity, but a deeply integrated part of his household's daily operations. This prolonged exposure ultimately paves the way for their marriage and the preservation of the messianic line.
