MOABITESS - ( Moabiyyah , Strong's H4125 )

Summary:

The term Moabiyyah identifies a female inhabitant of the land of Moab, a nation historically born out of incest and characterized by its bitter hostility toward Israel. In the book of Ruth, this designation is repeatedly attached to Ruth's name, serving as a stark literary and theological reminder of her pagan origin, her status as an excluded outsider under the Mosaic Law, and the incredible, transforming power of God’s sovereign grace that adopts a despised foreigner into the covenant lineage of the Messiah.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The background of the Moabitess begins in Genesis 19:30–38, where the nation of Moab originates from the incestuous union between Lot and his eldest daughter. This shameful beginning set the trajectory for a nation that became a persistent spiritual snare and military enemy to Israel. During the Wilderness Wanderings, Moab refused to provide Israel with bread and water and instead hired Balaam to curse them (Numbers 22–24). Consequently, Deuteronomy 23:3–6 issued a severe legal decree: "An Ammonite or Moabite shall not enter the assembly of the Lord; even to the tenth generation none of his descendants shall enter the assembly of the Lord forever." For an Israelite audience, the word "Moabitess" carried a deep-seated stigma of moral compromise, idolatry (specifically the worship of Chemosh), and divine exclusion.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Moabiyyah is a feminine gentilic noun derived from the proper noun Moab (Strong's H4124). It translates directly as "a Moabitess" or "a woman of Moab." Lexically, the term designates ethnic and geographic origin, but in the context of the Hebrew scriptures, it functions as a spiritual and social classification. It marks the individual as an alien to the commonwealth of Israel and a stranger to the covenants of promise.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term Moabiyyah appears several times in the Old Testament, most densely concentrated in the Book of Ruth. It is also used in historical accounts to describe the foreign wives who turned the hearts of Israel's leaders away from Yahweh, most notably in the case of King Solomon (1 Kings 11:1).

In the narrative of Ruth, the persistent repetition of "Ruth the Moabitess" creates a powerful literary contrast. It sets her actual, legal status as a despised foreigner against her remarkable actions of loyalty (chesed), humility, and faith. The title acts as a word picture of "the ultimate outsider"—someone who, by all human, historical, and legal standards, should be rejected, yet is drawn into the center of God's redemptive plan.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of a Moabiyyah in the genealogy of King David and Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5) holds massive theological weight. It demonstrates that God's grace is not bound by national borders, ethnic barriers, or historical baggage. While the letter of the law in Deuteronomy excluded Moabites from the assembly, the spirit of the covenant welcomed those who, like Ruth, abandoned their false gods to seek refuge "under the wings" of Yahweh (Ruth 2:12). Her inclusion prefigures the mystery of the Gospel: that Gentiles, who were once "far off," are brought near by grace and grafted into the family of God.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:21, the title is used in a highly specific narrative moment. Ruth returns to Naomi and reports the kindness of Boaz, stating, "Ruth the Moabitess said, 'He also said to me, "You shall stay close by my young men until they have finished all my harvest."'"

The narrator's deliberate use of "the Moabitess" here heightens the shock of Boaz’s generosity. A wealthy, noble Israelite landowner has just extended extraordinary protection and provision to an impoverished, foreign widow. By emphasizing her identity as a Moabitess in the same breath that she recounts Boaz’s favor, the text underscores the radical nature of grace. She is not receiving this kindness because she has successfully blended in; she receives it while still visibly and legally recognized as "the Moabitess."

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:22: "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab."

• Ruth 2:2: "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.'"

• Ruth 4:5: "Then Boaz said, 'On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance.'"

• Ruth 4:10: "Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance..."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A beautiful and subtle shift occurs in the final chapter of the book of Ruth. After Boaz publicly redeems and marries her in Ruth 4:10, the descriptive tag "the Moabitess" completely disappears from the narrative. In Ruth 4:13, she is simply referred to as "Ruth," and in the concluding genealogy, she is integrated into the lineage of Israel. Once redemption is accomplished, her foreign identity is absorbed into her new status as a wife, mother, and ancestress of the Messiah. This mirrors the spiritual reality of the believer, who is no longer defined by their past or their alienation, but by their new identity in Christ.
