YOUNG MEN - ( na'ar , Strong's H5288 )

Summary:

In Ruth 2:21, the Hebrew word na'ar (appearing here in the masculine plural ne'arim) refers to Boaz's young male workers or harvesters. While the term fundamentally denotes a youth, lad, or servant, in the agricultural setting of Bethlehem it points to the household laborers under Boaz’s authority. By instructing Ruth to stay close to his young men, Boaz extends his protective custody and provision over her, ensuring her safety during the harvest season and signaling his growing covenantal care for this vulnerable Moabite widow.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun na'ar is deeply embedded in the social and familial structures of ancient Israel. It is derived from a root that is associated with youthfulness, active energy, and developmental transition. Historically, the word did not merely define a precise biological age but rather a social status of dependency, vigor, and service. A na'ar was someone who had not yet become the head of their own household or ancestral estate. Consequently, the word picture behind na'ar evokes the strength and utility of youth, often directed under the authority of a father, a master, or a king. In agricultural contexts, these young men performed the physically demanding labor of reaping, binding, and securing the crop.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun na'ar refers to a young man, youth, lad, or servant. It spans a semantic range that describes a male from infancy to early adulthood. In the plural form ne'arim, it designates a group of young men, agricultural laborers, or personal attendants. The term carries a dual nuance of physical youthfulness and functional subordination. It represents those who are young enough to possess physical strength for labor but are still under the direction of an authority figure.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, na'ar is employed in several distinct ways:

• An Infant or Young Child: It describes the baby Moses weeping in the basket of reeds (Exodus 2:6) and the young child Samuel when he was dedicated to the Lord (1 Samuel 1:24).

• An Adolescent or Young Man: It describes Joseph as a seventeen-year-old youth helper to his brothers (Genesis 37:2) and David as the young man who went out to face Goliath (1 Samuel 17:55).

• A Servant or Attendant: It describes Joshua as the young assistant to Moses (Exodus 33:11) and Gehazi as the personal servant of Elisha (2 Kings 4:12).

• Laborers and Soldiers: It describes the young, fit men who went out to war or worked the fields of prosperous landowners, acting as the active agents of their master's estate.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the use of na'ar underscores God’s design for order, protection, and provision within the covenant community. God often uses the vulnerable, the young, and the dependent—the ne'arim—to accomplish His sovereign purposes. Furthermore, the relationship between a master and his ne'arim reflects the biblical theme of stewardship. A righteous master like Boaz rules his household and guides his young workers in a way that honors God, transforming his workers into instruments of divine protection and hospitality for the poor and the stranger.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:21, Ruth tells Naomi that Boaz instructed her to stay close to his "young men" (ne'arim) until they finished his harvest. This instruction highlights Boaz's immediate concern for Ruth's physical safety in a highly volatile era (the period of the Judges). However, Naomi's response in verse 22 introduces a vital nuance: she tells Ruth it is better to go out with Boaz's "young women" (na'arot) so that she will not be harmed in another field.

While the masculine plural ne'arim can sometimes serve as a gender-inclusive term for "young people" or "workers" in general, Naomi’s cautious maternal advice highlights the gender distinction. Working too closely with young, unsupervised male harvesters in the open fields carried immense social and physical risks for a foreign widow. Boaz's offer of protection is fully realized as Ruth gleans alongside his female workers, while his young men are strictly commanded not to touch or harass her (Ruth 2:9).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient Near East, the harvest season was a time of great celebration, but also a time when normal social boundaries relaxed, leading to potential danger for vulnerable outsiders. By instructing his young men to leave extra grain for Ruth and forbidding them from bothering her, Boaz models godliness to his subordinates. The conduct of the ne'arim under Boaz's command serves as a direct reflection of Boaz's own character and spiritual leadership.
