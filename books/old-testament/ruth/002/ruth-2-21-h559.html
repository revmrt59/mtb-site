SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the primary Old Testament term for speech, communication, and declaration. Beyond simple vocalization, it conveys intent, command, and the disclosure of one's heart. In the context of Ruth 2:21, 'amar functions as a vital bridge of communication, where Ruth reports Boaz's protective and generous words to Naomi, highlighting the theme of covenant loyalty (chesed) expressed through speech and action.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 5,000 times. It represents the foundational concept of vocalizing thoughts, intentions, or decrees.

• Unlike other Hebrew words for speaking that emphasize the physical act of talking or whispering, 'amar focuses on the content and intent of the message being conveyed. It is the vehicle of relationship, covenant, and revelation.

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, spoken words carried substantial weight, representing a binding commitment or a disclosure of character. To say something was to put a piece of oneself into the public sphere, initiating action.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of 'amar is to say, speak, declare, command, or think.

• In its basic active form (Qal), it describes the communication of thoughts, instructions, or reports.

• In the passive form (Niphal), it means to be said, named, or called.

• In the causative form (Hiphil), it means to declare or elicit a declaration.

• The term spans a broad semantic range, from silent internal monologue ("to say in one's heart") to binding royal decrees and divine creative commands.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Divine Decree: The word is used in Genesis 1 ("And God said...") where divine speech acts as the creative power that shapes reality.

• Internal Monologue: It describes the thoughts of the heart, where a person "says to himself" or ponders a matter.

• Relational Dialogue: It serves as the primary mechanism for covenantal and familial dialogue, reflecting the intimacy, tension, or resolution between characters.

4. Theological Implications

• Speech in the Old Testament is an index of character. God's speech is creative, faithful, and life-giving. Similarly, human speech reflects the orientation of the heart.

• In the Book of Ruth, speech is a primary vehicle for chesed (covenant faithfulness and lovingkindness). The characters use 'amar to bless, protect, and make commitments to one another.

• Boaz's spoken words are not empty; they are backed by his resources, status, and willingness to act as a kinsman-redeemer. When he speaks, provision and protection follow.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:21, 'amar occurs twice: "Ruth the Moabitess said ['amar], 'He also said ['amar] to me...'"

• The double use of 'amar emphasizes the faithful transmission of Boaz's words from Ruth to Naomi. Ruth is reporting Boaz's generous invitation to remain with his workers.

• The nuance here is one of protective grace and inclusion. Boaz’s "saying" is a verbal shield over Ruth, guaranteeing her safety during the harvest.

• Ruth's report of what Boaz said reveals her humility and her excitement about the unfolding of God's providence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3 - "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Genesis 22:1 - "Now it came to pass after these things that God tested Abraham, and said to him, 'Abraham!' And he said, 'Here I am.'"

• Exodus 3:14 - "And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And He said, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, "I AM has sent me to you."'"

• Ruth 1:16 - "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you...'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In Hebrew narrative, dialogue often drives the plot more than descriptive narration. By paying close attention to what characters say ('amar), the reader discerns their true motives and spiritual posture.

• Ruth's quoting of Boaz in verse 21 slightly modifies his original instructions. In verse 8, Boaz told her to stay close to his "young women" (female servants), but in verse 21 Ruth reports that he said to stay close to his "young men" (servants). Naomi immediately refines this in verse 22, advising Ruth to stick with the young women for her safety. This subtle shift in the spoken word highlights the vulnerability of Ruth and the wise, protective maternal oversight of Naomi.
