HARVEST - ( qatsiyr , Strong's H7105 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun qatsiyr refers to the crop harvested, the act of reaping, or the harvest season itself. Derived from a root meaning "to cut down" or "to shorten," it represents the culmination of agricultural labor and God's faithful provision. In Ruth 2:21, the term highlights Ruth's transition from vulnerability to sustained protection and abundance under the care of Boaz, marking a physical and spiritual season of restoration.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun qatsiyr is closely tied to the ancient Israelite agrarian cycle, which was the heartbeat of the nation's life, economy, and worship. The word is derived from the verbal root qatsar, which means "to shorten," "to reap," or "to cut down." This yields a vivid word picture: the harvest is the moment when standing stalks of grain are cut short by the sickle, gathered in bundles, and transformed from potential food into actual, life-sustaining nourishment.

In Israel, the harvest season was not merely a period of hard work, but a time of national celebration, spiritual reflection, and joy. It was punctuated by major feasts, such as the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost), which marked the end of the grain harvest. To talk of qatsiyr was to talk of God's covenantal faithfulness keeping the earth alive.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of qatsiyr focuses on three distinct but interrelated meanings depending on the context:

• The Harvest Season: The specific time of year when crops are gathered (typically starting with the barley harvest in April/May and ending with the wheat harvest in June/July).

• The Harvest Crop: The yield or produce itself—the physical grain, grapes, or olives gathered from the fields.

• The Act of Harvesting: The process of reaping and gathering the crops.

• Poetic Extension: In a few poetic passages (such as Job 14:9 or Psalm 80:11), the word refers to the "boughs" or "branches" of a plant, illustrating the outward growth that eventually produces fruit.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, qatsiyr serves as a powerful metaphor for spiritual truths:

• Sowing and Reaping: The harvest is the inevitable consequence of what has been planted. Hosea 8:7 warns that those who sow the wind will reap (qatsiyr) the whirlwind.

• Divine Judgment: The prophets frequently use the harvest as a picture of judgment, where the wicked are "ripe" for cutting down. Jeremiah 51:33 compares Babylon to a threshing floor, warning that "the time of her harvest (qatsiyr) will come."

• Spiritual Joy: Isaiah 9:3 describes the joy of salvation and victory using the highest earthly standard of happiness: "They rejoice before You / According to the joy in harvest (qatsiyr)."

### 4. Theological Implications

The theology of qatsiyr rests on two pillars: God's provision and God's law.

• Covenant Faithfulness: In Genesis 8:22, God promised that "seedtime and harvest" would never cease. Every harvest is an ongoing testimony to God's mercy and sustenance. The book of Ruth begins during a famine (the absence of harvest) and resolves when God "visits His people in giving them bread," culminating in the abundance of qatsiyr.

• Social Justice and Grace: God’s law protected the vulnerable during the harvest. Leviticus 23:22 commanded landholders not to strip their fields bare but to leave the edges and gleanings of the qatsiyr for the poor and the stranger. The harvest was designed to be an engine of charity, ensuring that God's abundance flowed to the marginalized.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:21, Ruth tells her mother-in-law, Naomi: "He [Boaz] also said to me, 'You shall stay close by my young men until they have ended all my harvest (qatsiyr).'"

The context of this statement is vital. Ruth and Naomi had returned to Bethlehem destitute. Ruth went to glean as a poor foreigner, hoping to find mere scraps. Instead, she met Boaz, who extended extraordinary grace. By telling her to stay through the entire harvest, Boaz was offering her safe passage and guaranteed food for the next two to three months (covering both the barley and wheat harvests).

The phrase "ended all my harvest" marks a safe haven for Ruth. She is transitionally secure; she no longer has to wander from field to field exposing herself to danger. Under the wing of the kinsman-redeemer, the harvest becomes a sanctuary of provision and a prelude to her full redemption and marriage.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 8:22 - "While the earth remains, / Seedtime and harvest, / Cold and heat, / Winter and summer, / And day and night / Shall not cease."

• Leviticus 23:22 - "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field when you reap, nor shall you gather any gleaning from your harvest. You shall leave them for the poor and for the stranger: I am the LORD your God."

• Proverbs 10:5 - "He who gathers in summer is a wise son; / He who sleeps in harvest is a son who causes shame."

• Isaiah 9:3 - "You have multiplied the nation / And increased its joy; / They rejoice before You / According to the joy in harvest, / As men rejoice when they divide the spoil."

• Jeremiah 8:20 - "The harvest is past, / The summer is ended, / And we are not saved!"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Harvest Calendar: The barley harvest began around the time of Passover (April), symbolizing life out of death and redemption. The wheat harvest ended around Pentecost (June), symbolizing abundance, completion, and the gathering of the community. Ruth's presence in Bethlehem spans this exact window, moving her from the "Passover" of her initial rescue to the "Pentecost" of her full inclusion into Israel.

• The Character of the Redeemer: Boaz’s ownership of the qatsiyr represents his wealth, but his sharing of it represents his godliness. True faith is proven by how a believer manages their "harvest." Boaz used his abundance not to build bigger barns for himself, but to protect and feed the helpless.
