RUTH - ( Rut, Strong's H7327 )

Summary:

The name Ruth (H7327) is a proper noun likely derived from a Hebrew root meaning "friend," "companion," or "friendship." In the Old Testament narrative, Ruth is the Moabitess daughter-in-law of Naomi who voluntarily binds herself to Naomi, the people of Israel, and Yahweh. Her name serves as a perfect literary and thematic summary of her character. She is the quintessential loyal companion whose steadfast love (hesed) and devotion overcome the barriers of ethnicity and tragedy, ultimately placing her in the direct lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, names were not merely labels but were deeply significant, often reflecting a person’s character, destiny, or the circumstances of their birth. The name Ruth appears in the context of the period of the Judges, a dark era in Israel's history marked by spiritual apostasy, moral decay, and national instability. Against this bleak backdrop, Ruth emerges from Moab—a nation historically hostile to Israel—to shine as a beacon of faithfulness. Her transition from a foreign outsider to a beloved insider in Bethlehem is one of the most beautiful pictures of redemption in Scripture. The word picture behind her name suggests a refreshing companion, like a comforting friend who walks alongside another in times of grief and famine.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name Rut is widely understood to be a contracted form of re'ut, meaning "friendship," "association," or "companion." It shares a common root with the Hebrew word re'a, which means a friend, neighbor, companion, or fellow. Thus, the lexical core of her name is defined by relational intimacy, loyalty, and mutual alliance. Rather than signifying an abstract concept, the name designates a person characterized by active, supportive companionship. It describes one who joins herself to another in a bond of deep solidarity and love.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name Ruth occurs exactly twelve times in the Old Testament, exclusively within the four chapters of the book that bears her name.

• It is consistently paired with the epithet "the Moabitess" (Ruth 1:22; 2:2, 21; 4:5, 10). This deliberate repetition creates a striking word picture: the foreign outsider has become the ultimate "friend" and companion to Israel.

• Her name functions as a living illustration of her famous vow in Ruth 1:16–17. Where Orpah turns back to her gods and people, Ruth clings to Naomi, demonstrating the absolute essence of her name—unwavering companionship.

• In the agricultural setting of Bethlehem, she is pictured as a humble gleaner in the fields of Boaz, embodying the quiet, industrious, and loyal friend who seeks to sustain her grieving mother-in-law.

4. Theological Implications

The name Ruth carries immense theological weight.

• The Concept of Hesed: Ruth’s life is the primary human illustration of hesed—God’s covenant loyalty, lovingkindness, and grace. Her "friendship" is not sentimental; it is sacrificial and covenantal.

• Gentile Inclusion: As a Moabitess, Ruth was legally excluded from the assembly of Yahweh under the Mosaic Law (Deuteronomy 23:3). Yet, her faith and loyalty transcend ethnic barriers, proving that inclusion in God's family is based on faith and covenant relationship, not biological lineage.

• Redemptive History: Ruth’s companionship leads her to Boaz, her kinsman-redeemer (goel). This union preserves the line of Judah, leading directly to King David and, ultimately, to Jesus Christ, the ultimate Friend of sinners who redeemed humanity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:21, the text reads: "Then Ruth the Moabitess said, 'He also said to me, "You shall stay close by my young men until they have finished all my harvest."'"

Here, the use of her name "Ruth" alongside her national identity "the Moabitess" highlights the remarkable transformation taking place. Despite being an outsider from an enemy nation, Ruth is speaking of the incredible favor and protection shown to her by Boaz. Her name—"friend" or "companion"—is vividly enacted as she reports Boaz's instruction to "stay close" to his workers. She is no longer a stranger wandering the fields; she has found a place of companionable security under the wings of the God of Israel. Her name reflects her status as one who has successfully bridged the gap from foreigner to cherished companion.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Hebrew Bible, the Book of Ruth is placed in the Ketuvim (Writings) immediately following the Book of Proverbs. This placement offers a brilliant canonical connection: the Book of Proverbs ends with the description of the Eshet Chayil—the "virtuous woman" or "woman of valor" (Proverbs 31:10–31). The very next character introduced in the Hebrew canon is Ruth, whom Boaz explicitly calls a "woman of valor" (eshet chayil) in Ruth 3:11. Thus, Ruth, the "companion," is presented to God's people as the living, breathing incarnation of the wisdom and virtue described in Proverbs.
