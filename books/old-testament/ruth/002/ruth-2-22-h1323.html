DAUGHTER - ( bat , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bat primarily refers to a female offspring, but it carries deep relational, legal, and familial warmth in the Old Testament. In Ruth 2:22, Naomi uses "my daughter" (bitti) not as a biological designation, but as an affectionate, protective term of adoption and familial integration for her Moabite daughter-in-law, Ruth. This highlights the transformation of Ruth from an outsider to a cherished insider within the covenant community of Israel.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun bat is the feminine counterpart to ben (son). In ancient Semitic thought, the word is closely linked to the root banah, which means "to build." A daughter was understood to be one who builds up the household and perpetuates the family line. In the ancient Near East, a daughter's identity, protection, and legal standing were tied entirely to her father's household, and later, to her husband's family. When Naomi addresses Ruth as "my daughter," she is bridging a profound cultural gap. Ruth was a Moabite widow who had no legal or biological claim on Naomi, yet Naomi's language grants her the status, protection, and intimacy of a biological child within an Israelite home.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of bat includes:

• Biological Female Offspring: A direct female child of a parent.

• Female Descendant: A granddaughter or female ancestor further down the lineage.

• Inhabitant of a Place: Used poetically to describe the female population of a city or region (e.g., "daughter of Zion").

• Term of Affection/Endearment: An address used by an older person to a younger woman, denoting pastoral care, protection, and familial warmth.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs bat in several rich figurative and literal ways:

• Poetic Personification: The phrase "daughter of Zion" or "daughter of my people" depicts Israel or Jerusalem as a vulnerable, deeply loved young woman under God's care, or grieving under His judgment.

• Relational Adoption: In the book of Ruth, the word transcends biological boundaries. Boaz uses the term to address Ruth (Ruth 2:8; 3:10), signaling that she is safe under his protection and recognized as a virtuous woman. Naomi uses the term to finalize Ruth's transition from an foreign daughter-in-law (kallah) to a true daughter (bat) of the covenant.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of bat in the narrative of Ruth lies in the theme of redemption and inclusion. Under the Mosaic Law, Moabites were excluded from the assembly of the Lord (Deuteronomy 23:3). However, through covenant faithfulness (hesed), Ruth is integrated into Israel. Naomi's designation of Ruth as "my daughter" reflects the heart of God, who adopts the spiritual foreigner and outcast into His family. This familial language prefigures the New Testament reality of believers being adopted as sons and daughters of the Living God, joint heirs with Christ.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:22, the narrator carefully contrasts two terms. The narrator refers to Ruth as Naomi's "daughter-in-law" (kallah), highlighting her legal and historical starting point. However, when Naomi speaks directly to Ruth, she bypasses the formal title and says, "It is good, my daughter (bitti)..."

This occurs immediately after Ruth returns from gleaning in the field of Boaz with an abundant harvest. Naomi recognizes the hand of Yahweh in providing Boaz as a potential redeemer. Her use of "my daughter" signals a shift from her previous bitterness (Ruth 1:20-21) to restored hope. By calling Ruth "my daughter," Naomi takes responsibility for Ruth's future, guiding her toward security, marriage, and rest in the household of Israel.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 30:21: "Afterward she bore a daughter, and called her name Dinah."

• Ruth 2:8: "Then Boaz said to Ruth, 'Listen, my daughter, for you not to go to glean in another field, nor go from here, but stay close by my young women.'"

• Ruth 3:10: "Then he said, 'Blessed are you of the Lord, my daughter! For you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning, in that you did not go after young men, whether poor or rich.'"

• Isaiah 62:11: "Indeed the Lord has proclaimed to the end of the world: 'Say to the daughter of Zion, "Surely your salvation is coming; behold, His reward is with Him, and His work before Him."'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, Jesus uses the Greek equivalent of this affectionate Semitic term (thugater) to restore individuals who were cut off from community. In Matthew 9:22, when the woman with the flow of blood touches His garment, Jesus turns and says, "Be of good cheer, daughter; your faith has made you well." Just as Naomi's use of bat brought the Moabitess into the safety of the Israelite family, Jesus' use of "daughter" brought an unclean outcast back into the family of God.
