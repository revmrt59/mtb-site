GO OUT - ( yatsa , Strong's H3318 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yatsa means to go forth, depart, or emerge from a specific place. In the Old Testament, it serves as a foundational term for transition, deliverance, and providential movement, most famously used for Israel's exodus from Egypt. In Ruth 2:22, Naomi’s wise counsel for Ruth to "go out" with Boaz’s young women highlights a transition from vulnerability to covenant protection. This simple act of daily obedience positions Ruth directly under the wing of God's sovereign provision and safety.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root yatsa is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over a thousand times. Its core physical action is moving from an enclosed or interior space to an open or exterior space. It describes a wide range of movements: a person stepping out of a tent, a prisoner emerging from a dungeon, water flowing from a spring, or the sun rising in the sky. To "go out" is to cross a boundary, leaving behind one state or environment to enter another. In the agrarian world of the ancient Near East, leaving the safety of the city gates to "go out" into the open fields was a necessary but vulnerable act of daily labor, exposing a worker to the elements, wild animals, and potentially hostile strangers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its simple active form (Qal), yatsa means to go out, go forth, depart, or proceed. In its causative form (Hiphil), it means to bring out, lead forth, or produce. Lexically, the term covers several semantic categories:

• Spatial departure: Leaving a city, house, country, or specific boundary.

• Natural phenomena: The sun going forth (rising), stars appearing, or plants sprouting from the earth.

• Source or origin: Words proceeding from a mouth, or descendants coming from an ancestor's body.

• Official action: An army going out to battle, or a king leading his subjects.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Exodus: The ultimate theological word picture of yatsa is the redemption of Israel. God "brought out" His people from Egypt, the house of bondage. This historical act of deliverance establishes yatsa as a covenantal word associated with salvation and freedom.

• The Call of Abraham: The spiritual journey of Israel begins with a command to "go out" from Ur of the Chaldeans. Abraham's departure was an act of faith, leaving the familiar to go to a land God would show him.

• Agrarian Abundance: The verb describes the earth "bringing forth" vegetation and water "gushing out" of the rock, symbolizing life and divine provision emerging from dry, barren places.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yatsa emphasizes that God is a God of movement, deliverance, and transition. Spiritual growth and redemption almost always require an active "going out"—leaving behind old identities, false security, or past bondages to step into the territory of God’s promise. Furthermore, it demonstrates the perfect harmony between human responsibility and divine sovereignty. When God's people step out in obedience to His voice, they find that He has already gone before them to prepare their way.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:22, Naomi advises Ruth: "It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women, and that people do not meet you in any other field." In this immediate context, "going out" represents both wisdom and safety. Ruth was a foreign widow, highly vulnerable to harassment or assault in unfamiliar fields. Naomi recognizes that Boaz's field is a sanctuary of safety and grace (hesed).

By instructing Ruth to "go out" exclusively with Boaz's workers, Naomi is guiding her to remain within the boundaries of God's providential protection. Ruth’s obedience in "going out" to this specific field, day after day, is the practical vehicle through which God preserves her life, provides food for her household, and ultimately brings her into the lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 8:16: "Go out of the ark, you and your wife, and your sons and your sons’ wives with you."

• Ruth 1:7: "Therefore she went out from the place where she was, and her two daughters-in-law with her; and they went on the way to return to the land of Judah."

• Exodus 20:2: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage."

• Deuteronomy 16:3: "...that you may remember the day in which you came out of the land of Egypt all the days of your life."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew scriptures frequently couple "going out" (yatsa) with "coming in" (bo). This pairing forms a merism representing the entirety of daily life, duties, and covenant faithfulness. When Moses says he can no longer "go out and come in" (Deuteronomy 31:2), he refers to his ability to lead. In contrast, God promises to preserve His people's "going out and coming in" (Psalm 121:8), ensuring total protection in every transition of life. In the Book of Ruth, the narrative hinges on these movements: Naomi "went out" of Moab to return to God's land, and Ruth "goes out" into the fields of Bethlehem, showcasing how geographical movements mirror deep spiritual transformations.
