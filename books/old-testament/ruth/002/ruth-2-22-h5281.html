NAOMI - ( No'omiy , Strong's H5281 )

Summary:

The name Naomi means "my pleasantness" or "delightful." It represents sweetness, beauty, and favor, standing in stark contrast to her self-proclaimed name "Mara" (bitter) in Ruth 1:20. In the narrative of Ruth, Naomi's name serves as a thematic anchor, tracing her journey from pleasantness to bitterness, and ultimately back to restoration and joy under God's providential care.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Naomi is rooted in the Hebrew noun no'am, which signifies beauty, pleasantness, favor, or delight. In ancient Near Eastern culture, names were highly significant, often reflecting a person's character, destiny, or current state of life. Naomi's name reflects the initial joy and favor of her life in Bethlehem before famine and tragedy struck, making her subsequent trials and emotional shift all the more poignant. Her name carries the pastoral picture of a life intended for sweetness and divine favor, which makes her descent into grief and her eventual restoration deeply moving.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew word No'omiy is a proper feminine noun meaning "my pleasantness" or "pleasant." It is derived from the verb na'em, which means to be agreeable, pleasant, sweet, or lovely. It is used exclusively as the proper name of the wife of Elimelech and the mother-in-law of Ruth. The underlying root emphasizes a state of harmony, grace, and delight—both in personal disposition and in circumstances blessed by God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name Naomi occurs 21 times in the Old Testament, exclusively within the Book of Ruth. The literary and theological word picture associated with her name is built on the stark contrast between pleasantness and bitterness. Upon her return to Bethlehem after losing her husband and both sons, she tells the women of the city, "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (Ruth 1:20). This highlights her internal crisis: she feels that her life no longer matches her name. The narrative arc of the book is a journey of God working behind the scenes to restore "pleasantness" to a woman who had renamed herself "bitter."

4. Theological Implications

Naomi's name and narrative arc demonstrate the sovereignty and lovingkindness (hesed) of God. Even when Naomi feels "Mara" (bitter) and believes the Almighty has dealt harshly with her, God still views her and treats her according to her original name—bringing her back to a state of "pleasantness" and restoration through Ruth and Boaz. Her story shows that human bitterness does not cancel out divine favor. God's ultimate plan is to turn our "Mara" back into "Naomi," bringing sweetness out of suffering and incorporating her line into the ancestry of King David and Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:22, Ruth has just returned from her first day of gleaning in the fields of Boaz, having experienced remarkable favor and protection. Naomi says to Ruth her daughter-in-law, "It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women, and that people do not meet you in any other field." In this context, we see the transition of Naomi's heart from despair to hope. The "pleasantness" of her name is beginning to blossom again. Her instruction is protective, wise, and filled with a growing recognition of God's providence. Naomi, who had previously spoken only of loss and emptiness, now speaks of safety, goodness, and hope, guiding Ruth toward the path of redemption.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:2: "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah."

• Ruth 1:20: "But she said to them, 'Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.'"

• Ruth 4:17: "The neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'There is a son born to Naomi.' And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between Naomi ("pleasant") and Mara ("bitter") structures the literary movement of the Book of Ruth. She goes out full and returns empty (Ruth 1:21), but by the end of the narrative, the women of Bethlehem declare that Ruth is better to her than seven sons, and she nurses Obed, who restores her life and nourishes her old age (Ruth 4:14-15). Her pleasantness is fully restored, not by her own strength, but through the sacrificial love of Ruth and the redeeming grace of Boaz, pointing forward to the ultimate Redeemer.
