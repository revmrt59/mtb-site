YOUNG WOMEN - ( na'arah , Strong's H5291 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun na'arah refers to a young woman, maiden, or female servant of marriageable age. In the narrative of Ruth, it denotes the female laborers within Boaz's household. Naomi's counsel for Ruth to remain with Boaz's "young women" highlights the essential themes of safety, community, and divine providence, securing Ruth's physical protection and social integration within the covenant community of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite society, a na'arah was a young, unmarried female, typically living under the authority and protection of her father's household, or a female domestic worker under a master. The term carries connotations of youth, vitality, dependency, and the need for social protection. In agricultural settings, these young women performed essential labor alongside male harvesters, often following behind them to gather and bind the fallen grain. Because young, unattached foreign women were highly vulnerable to exploitation and abuse, a na'arah working in the fields required a secure, honorable environment to ensure her safety.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun na'arah is the feminine counterpart to the masculine na'ar (young man, servant). It designates a young girl, a maiden of marriageable age, or a female servant. The semantic range spans from an adolescent girl in her family home to a royal maiden or household attendant. It implies both a stage of life (youth) and a social status (subordinate or dependent). In legal and historical contexts, the word emphasizes either the young woman's innocence and need for parental protection or her domestic service within a larger household.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Maiden of Marriageable Age: Used to describe young women who are eligible for marriage, such as Rebekah before her betrothal to Isaac or Esther prior to her selection by the king.

• Attendant or Servant: Used to describe the female maids of Pharaoh's daughter who walked along the Nile, or the captive Israelite girl who served Naaman's wife.

• Group of Harvesters: In the Book of Ruth, the plural na'arot refers to the female workers who labor under Boaz's oversight, forming a distinct, protected class of domestic laborers.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of na'arah reflects God's covenantal care for the marginalized. Under the Mosaic Law, widows, orphans, and strangers were highly vulnerable. By providing specific gleaning laws and commands to protect these individuals, God demonstrated His role as the defender of the weak. When Boaz integrates Ruth into the circle of his na'arot, he acts as the earthly instrument of Yahweh’s protective wings. Remaining with the young women represents a shift from isolation and vulnerability to covenant safety and community belonging.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:22, Naomi strongly reinforces Boaz's instruction for Ruth to stick close to his "young women." Naomi recognizes the immense physical and moral dangers that a foreign widow would face in other fields during the chaotic era of the Judges. By staying with Boaz's female workers, Ruth is identified as part of Boaz's protected household. This prevents her from being targeted or harassed ("that people do not meet you in any other field"). The term here highlights the practical wisdom of Naomi and the redeemer-like custody that Boaz extends to Ruth even before their marriage.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural balance between the masculine na'ar (young men) and the feminine na'arah (young women) in Ruth chapter 2 illustrates a highly orderly and moral workspace. Boaz commands his ne'arim (young men) not to touch Ruth (Ruth 2:9), while inviting Ruth to join his na'arot (young women) (Ruth 2:8). This intentional gender boundaries and supervision demonstrate Boaz's righteousness as a "man of great wealth" and moral standing (gibbor chayil), ensuring that his fields are a sanctuary of safety for those seeking refuge.
