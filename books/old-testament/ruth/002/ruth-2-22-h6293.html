MEET - ( paga , Strong's H6293 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb paga represents a dynamic range of physical encounters, from a peaceful meeting or geographical boundary marker to a violent physical assault or an urgent, intercessory appeal. In Ruth 2:22, the term is translated as "meet," but in this specific cultural and literary context, it warns of danger. Naomi cautions Ruth to remain in Boaz's fields to prevent strangers from "assailing" or harassing her in an unprotected field, highlighting the physical vulnerability of the defenseless and the safety found under the wing of a redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb paga is deeply kinetic and visceral. It suggests a sudden, definite, and impactful meeting of two entities. The image behind the word is that of two paths crossing unexpectedly. Depending on who is crossing whose path, the result can be a peaceful greeting, a boundary line touching another property, a desperate cry that "strikes" the ears of a king, or a violent physical collision. In the ancient Near East, traveling outside protected boundaries meant risking an unwanted encounter. The word picture of paga inherently contains this tension: a sudden encounter can bring either life-giving assistance or destructive harm.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary semantic core of paga is "to strike," "to hit," or "to encounter." Depending on the context, this verbal root expresses several distinct nuances:

• Encounter or Meet: To come across someone or something, whether by happenstance or plan.

• Assail or Attack: To fall upon someone with hostility or physical violence.

• Reach or Touch: To make physical contact, specifically used for geographical borders touching one another.

• Entreat or Intercede: To approach someone with an earnest petition, figuratively "striking" them with persistent requests on behalf of oneself or another.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Clash of Battle and Execution: In contexts of warfare or execution, paga describes a fatal assault. When kings order executions, the agent is told to "fall upon" (paga) the condemned individual, illustrating a violent and terminal encounter.

• The Protection of Boundaries: In land distributions, paga marks where one tribal inheritance physically meets another, establishing defined borders of peace and ownership.

• The Burden of Intercession: In prophetic literature, paga takes on an intense spiritual weight. To make intercession is to run to the middle of a crisis, standing between danger and the victim to plead with God. It is also used to describe God causing the sins of humanity to "strike" or "fall upon" the Suffering Servant.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of paga rests on the concepts of safety, boundary-setting, and mediation. In a fallen world, human beings are constantly vulnerable to being "fallen upon" by enemies, sin, and divine judgment. The word demonstrates our desperate need for a safe territory and a protective shield. Ultimately, this Hebrew concept points to Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, who took the violent "strike" of our iniquities upon Himself and now lives to make "intercession" (paga) for us. What Naomi sought humanly for Ruth—a place of safety from hostile encounters—God provides eternally in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:22, Naomi hears of Boaz's kindness and instructs Ruth to stay close to his young women, warning her that she might be "met" (paga) in another field. While the English NKJV translates this as "meet," Naomi is not speaking of a casual social greeting. A young, foreign, unprotected widow gleaning in a strange field during the chaotic period of the Judges was at extreme risk of sexual harassment or physical violence. Naomi uses paga to convey the danger of being assailed, cornered, or mistreated by predatory harvesters. Within Boaz's field, she is safe under his protection; outside it, she is subject to the hostile "striking" of strangers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 5:3: "Please let us go three days’ journey into the desert and sacrifice to the LORD our God, lest He fall upon us with pestilence or with the sword."

• 1 Kings 2:25: "So King Solomon sent by the hand of Benaiah the son of Jehoiada; and he fell upon him so that he died."

• Isaiah 53:6: "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned, every one, to his own way; and the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all."

• Isaiah 53:12: "Therefore I will divide Him a portion with the great, and He shall divide the spoil with the strong, because He poured out His soul unto death, and He was numbered with the transgressors, and He bore the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors."

• Jeremiah 7:16: "Therefore do not pray for this people, nor lift up a cry or prayer for them; nor make intercession to Me; for I will not hear you."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern agricultural culture, fields during the harvest were lively but highly charged environments where lawlessness often prevailed outside the boundaries of godly households. Landowners had to explicitly charge their young men not to touch or abuse foreign gleaners (Ruth 2:9). Naomi's maternal concern highlights the reality of this environment. By staying in the field of Boaz, Ruth remained under a "canopy" of covenant protection where the violent potential of paga was replaced by the peace of Boaz's provisions.
