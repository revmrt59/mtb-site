FIELD - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open expanse of land, a cultivated plot, or a pasture. In the agrarian world of ancient Israel, the field was the vital center of life, labor, and sustenance. In the book of Ruth, and specifically in Ruth 2:22, the field of Boaz represents a sanctuary of divine provision, physical safety, and covenant redemption. Remaining within this specific field is a physical expression of abiding under the protective wings of the God of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, a sadeh was not a fenced-in, modern field. It was a vast, open tract of arable land, often marked off from neighboring properties only by small boundary stones. Because these fields lacked physical barriers, they were highly vulnerable to travelers, wild animals, and thieves. For a marginalized individual—such as a foreign widow—working in an open field carried significant physical risk. She could easily wander onto the land of an unkind owner or be accosted in isolated areas. Therefore, the field was simultaneously a place of potential life-giving harvest and a place of deep personal danger.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun sadeh denotes open country, a definite portion of territory, or land cultivated for agriculture. It stands in contrast to the walled city (ir), the wilderness (midbar), or the mountains (har). It signifies the soil that is plowed, sown, and reaped for crops like barley and wheat. Culturally, the sadeh represents the source of domestic wealth and survival, where the labor of human hands meets the rain and blessing of God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs sadeh in several distinct ways:

• Agrarian Productivity: It is the place of crops, vines, and livestock.

• Territory of a Nation: It can refer to the broader region or domain of a people group, such as the "field of Moab" or the "field of Edom."

• A Place of Vulnerability: Because it is open and removed from the safety of city walls, the field is often the setting for violence, secret actions, or sudden encounters.

• Metaphor of Divine Blessing: A fertile, rain-watered field is a picture of God's favor and covenant keeping, while a parched, ruined field represents judgment.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of the sadeh is deeply tied to the Torah’s laws of gleaning. God commanded Israelite landowners not to reap the very corners of their fields, nor to gather the fallen ears of grain, but to leave them for the poor, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19). The field was the classroom where Israel was to practice chesed (covenant loyalty and kindness). The open field belonged ultimately to Yahweh; the landowners were merely stewards. Thus, the way a man managed his sadeh revealed his heart toward God. Boaz’s field becomes a tangible expression of God's law of grace in action.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:22, Naomi tells Ruth, "It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women, and that people do not meet you in any other field." Here, Naomi recognizes that Boaz’s sadeh is a safe haven. Boaz has commanded his young men not to touch or humiliate Ruth. To leave Boaz's field for "another field" is to step out from under the canopy of his protection and provision. This advice has strong spiritual overtones: there is safety, abundance, and ultimate redemption when one abides in the place where grace is extended. Wandering to other fields represents spiritual restlessness and unnecessary danger.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:8 - "Now Cain talked with Abel his brother; and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him."

• Leviticus 19:9 - "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest."

• Proverbs 24:30 - "I went by the field of the lazy man, And by the vineyard of the man devoid of understanding;"

• Proverbs 31:16 - "She considers a field and buys it; From her profits she plants a vineyard."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful literary contrast in the Book of Ruth between the "fields of Moab" and the "field of Boaz" in Bethlehem. Elimelech’s family left Bethlehem (the "House of Bread") because of famine and sought life in the fields of Moab, which resulted in death and emptiness. When Ruth accompanies Naomi back to Bethlehem, she enters the sadeh of Boaz. The narrative comes full circle: seeking life in pagan fields brings ruin, but laboring in the field of the redeemer brings safety, abundance, a family, and a royal lineage leading to the Messiah.
