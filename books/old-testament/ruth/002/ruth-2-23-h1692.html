CLOSE - ( dabaq , Strong's H1692 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb dabaq conveys the rich concept of clinging, cleaving, or remaining closely joined to someone or something. In Ruth 2:23, it denotes Ruth's loyal adherence to Boaz's young women, reflecting her committed choice to remain in the place of safety, provision, covenant faithfulness, and community.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of dabaq is that of two surfaces glued, welded, or stuck together so tightly that they cannot be separated without damage. It represents a state of hard adhesion, such as a tongue sticking to the roof of the mouth from extreme thirst, or scale armor plates clinging tightly to one another. When applied to human relationships, this word picture transitions from physical adhesion to covenantal cohesion. It describes a bond of intense loyalty, affection, and proximity, where one deliberately links their destiny to another.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb dabaq means to cling, cleave, keep close, adhere, catch, or follow close. In its basic stem, it expresses physical contact and proximity. In its intensive and causative forms, it denotes pursuing closely, overtaking, or causing to cling. Lexically, it spans both physical realities (clinging to the soil, bones sticking to skin) and relational or spiritual realities (cleaving to a spouse, holding fast to God). It signifies a persistent, active clinging rather than a static state.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Relational Cleaving: It describes the marital bond where a man leaves his parents and cleaves to his wife, creating an inseparable union.

• Physical Adhesion: It describes a soldier's hand freezing or clinging to his sword in the heat of battle, representing exhaustion combined with unyielding determination.

• Spiritual Devotion: It represents Israel's covenantal obligation to hold fast to Yahweh, remaining loyal to Him alone in the midst of idolatrous nations.

• Persistent Pursuit: It depicts army units closely pursuing or overtaking an enemy in battle, refusing to let them escape.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, dabaq is the practical expression of covenant love (hesed). Love in the Old Testament is not merely an emotion; it is an act of the will that manifests as physical and relational closeness. When God's people are commanded to "hold fast" to Him, dabaq indicates that loyalty requires a deliberate refusal to wander, a refusal to create distance, and an active pursuit of closeness with the covenant Lord. In the book of Ruth, human dabaq reflects divine faithfulness. Ruth's clinging to Naomi and her decision to stay close to Boaz's workers are concrete expressions of her integration into the covenant community of Israel and her trust under the wings of the Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:23, the use of dabaq ("stayed close") marks the successful completion of a crucial phase in Ruth's journey. Boaz had previously charged her to "stay close by my young women" for her protection and sustenance (Ruth 2:8). Ruth's obedience to this instruction throughout both the barley and wheat harvests demonstrates her wisdom, diligence, and humility. By staying close to the young women, she remained within the boundary of Boaz's field—a place of redemption, safety, and abundant grace. The verb emphasizes her steadfastness; she was not distracted by other fields or other opportunities, but remained tightly bound to the place where grace was extended to her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:24 - "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

• Deuteronomy 10:20 - "You shall fear the Lord your God; you shall serve Him, and to Him you shall hold fast, and take oaths in His name."

• Joshua 23:8 - "but you shall hold fast to the Lord your God, as you have done to this day."

• Ruth 1:14 - "Then they lifted up their voices and wept again; and Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her."

• 2 Samuel 23:10 - "He arose and attacked the Philistines until his hand was weary, and his hand clung to the sword."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A powerful literary and spiritual contrast exists in the book of Ruth using this very word. In Ruth 1:14, Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye, but Ruth "clung" (dabaq) to her. This same Hebrew word is then used in Ruth 2:8 and 2:23 to describe Ruth staying close to Boaz's workers. This repetition highlights that Ruth's character is defined by dabaq—she is a clinger. Her initial decision to cling to Naomi (and thus to Naomi's God) translates practically into her daily work ethic of staying close to the provision and safety provided by the redeemer, Boaz.
