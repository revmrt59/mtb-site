WHEAT - ( chittah , Strong's H2406 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chittah refers to wheat, the premier and most valuable agricultural crop in ancient Israel. While barley was harvested first and often associated with poverty or basic survival, the wheat harvest marked the peak of the agricultural season, representing abundance, divine blessing, and sustained life. In the book of Ruth, the mention of the wheat harvest marks a critical period of transition. It highlights how God's abundant provision kept Ruth and Naomi safe and sustained over several months under the protective care of Boaz, setting the stage for the ultimate redemption of their family line.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, agriculture was the heartbeat of daily life, and chittah (wheat) was the most prized of all grain crops. Unlike barley, which could grow in poorer soils and colder climates, wheat required rich, well-prepared soil, adequate rainfall, and careful cultivation. It was sown in the late autumn, grew through the winter rains, and was harvested in the late spring, specifically during the month of Sivan (May–June). The wheat harvest concluded the grain harvesting season and culminated in the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost), where firstfruits of the wheat crop were presented to God in thanksgiving. Because of its superior gluten content, wheat produced the high-quality, light bread preferred for daily consumption and sacred offerings, making it a universal symbol of prosperity, nourishment, and divine favor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun chittah refers specifically to the wheat plant (Triticum) or the harvested grain itself. In the plural form (chittim), it denotes wheat crops or quantities of wheat grain. The term represents one of the primary food staples of the ancient world. It is distinguished lexicographically from barley (seorah), which was cheaper and harvested earlier. Chittah is classified as one of the seven premier agricultural blessings of the Promised Land, representing agricultural completion and culinary excellence.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, chittah is used both literally and metaphorically to convey several spiritual realities:

• Divine Favor and Prosperity: Finest wheat is a metaphor for the highest quality of God's physical and spiritual provisions. To be fed with wheat is to experience the fullness of God's goodness.

• Judgment and Devastation: The loss or destruction of the wheat crop is used as an image of divine judgment, signaling a breakdown in the covenant relationship between God and His people.

• The Sifting and Separation: The process of threshing and winnowing wheat represents divine sorting, separating the substantial, valuable grain from the weightless, useless chaff.

• Covenant Loyalty: Giving the firstfruits of the wheat harvest to God was an act of worship, demonstrating trust that the Lord was the ultimate provider of the rain and soil.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of chittah lies in its connection to God's covenantal promises. In the Pentateuch, the abundance of wheat is explicitly tied to Israel's obedience to God’s commands. When Israel was faithful, God promised to bless their grain, wine, and oil. Conversely, a failure of the wheat crop indicated a spiritual drought. Additionally, the transition from the barley harvest to the wheat harvest mirrors the progression of God's grace. Barley provides immediate relief from starvation, but wheat represents long-term, abundant security. This progression reminds the believer that God does not merely provide the bare minimum for survival; He desires to bring His people into a state of spiritual abundance and rest.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:23, Ruth gleans "until the end of barley harvest and wheat harvest." This brief temporal note carries immense pastoral and narrative weight.

• A Season of Sustained Grace: The barley harvest began around Passover (April), and the wheat harvest ended around Pentecost (June). This means Ruth gleaned in Boaz's fields for approximately two to three months. This was not a one-time stroke of luck; it was a daily, sustained season of provision and safety.

• Character and Integrity: For several months, Ruth proved her diligence, and Boaz proved his honorable character. Their virtue was not a fleeting impulse but a consistent lifestyle.

• The Quiet Transition: The end of the wheat harvest meant that the field work was drawing to a close. The season of daily gleaning was ending, forcing Naomi and Ruth to look to the future. It acts as the perfect literary bridge to chapter 3, where Naomi seeks a permanent "rest" or home for Ruth through Boaz's role as the kinsman-redeemer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 30:14: "Now Reuben went in the days of wheat harvest and found mandrakes in the field, and brought them to his mother Leah."

• Exodus 34:22: "And you shall observe the Feast of Weeks, of the firstfruits of wheat harvest, and the Feast of Ingathering at the year’s end."

• Deuteronomy 8:8: "a land of wheat and barley, of vines and fig trees and pomegranates, a land of olive oil and honey;"

• Judges 6:11: "Now the Angel of the Lord came and sat under the terebinth tree which was in Ophrah, which belonged to Joash the Abiezrite, while his son Gideon threshed wheat in the winepress, in order to hide it from the Midianites."

• Psalm 147:14: "He makes peace in your borders, And fills you with the finest wheat."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, the spiritual legacy of the wheat harvest (chittah) is fully realized. Pentecost, the festival celebrating the end of the wheat harvest, became the historical day when the Holy Spirit was poured out on the early Church, gathering the firstfruits of the global spiritual harvest. Furthermore, Jesus built upon the agricultural imagery of wheat to explain His own redemptive mission, stating that a grain of wheat must fall into the ground and die in order to produce much grain (John 12:24). The bread of life, ultimately, is born from the crushed grain of the wheat, pointing directly to Christ's sacrifice which feeds the hungry souls of all who believe.
