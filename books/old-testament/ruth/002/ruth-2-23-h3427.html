DWELT - ( yashab , Strong's H3427 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yashab carries the rich dual meaning of sitting down and dwelling or remaining in a place. In the narrative of Ruth 2:23, it denotes not merely a physical residence, but a settled, loyal relationship of devotion and rest. Having completed her grueling labor in the fields of Boaz, Ruth returns to "dwell" with Naomi, signifying her steadfast commitment to her mother-in-law, her integration into the covenant community of Israel, and her quiet anticipation of God's unfolding redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical action behind the root yashab is to sit down, take a seat, or settle in a specific location. It stands in direct contrast to wandering, journeying, or standing in a state of preparation. In the Ancient Near East, where life was often nomadic or subject to sudden displacement due to famine and war, the ability to "sit" or "dwell" in peace was highly valued. It is a word picture of stability, safety, and permanence. When someone "sat" or "dwelt" with another, it implied a shared life, mutual protection, and social alignment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of yashab is to sit, remain, abide, or inhabit. Its semantic range covers several distinct nuances depending on the context:

• Physical posture: To sit down on a chair, the ground, or a throne.

• State of residence: To dwell, live, or reside in a house, city, or land as an inhabitant.

• Remaining: To stay behind, tarry, or wait in a specific place while others depart.

• Enthronement: To sit as a king, judge, or ruler in authority.

In Ruth 2:23, the aspect of remaining and establishing a stable home is paramount. The verb is in the Qal imperfect form, indicating an ongoing, continuous state of dwelling.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes yashab in various figurative and literal ways to convey deep theological truths:

• The posture of authority: Kings sit (yashab) on thrones to judge, and God is depicted as the King who sits enthroned forever over the flood (Psalm 29:10).

• Peace and security: A classic picture of national blessing is when every man can sit (yashab) under his own vine and fig tree without fear (Micah 4:4).

• Spiritual fellowship: To dwell in the house of the Lord represents the ultimate desire of the believer for unbroken fellowship and safety in the presence of God (Psalm 27:4).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yashab points to the theme of covenant rest. God's ultimate desire for His people is that they would dwell securely in the land He promised them, living in peaceful relationship with Him and one another.

In the book of Ruth, the theme of "dwelling" undergoes a significant spiritual journey. Elimelech and his family left Bethlehem to "sojourn" (gur) in Moab, but they eventually settled and "dwelt" (yashab) there (Ruth 1:4), which resulted in tragedy and loss. In contrast, Ruth makes the active decision to leave Moab and "dwell" in Bethlehem under the wings of Yahweh. Her choice to dwell with Naomi represents her covenant loyalty (hesed) not only to her family but also to the Lord God of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:23, the barley and wheat harvests have officially ended. This marks a major transition in the narrative. For months, Ruth’s life had been characterized by grueling, daily movement—going out early to glean in the fields of Boaz and returning late. Now, the physical labor of the harvest is finished.

The text states that "she dwelt with her mother-in-law." Rather than using this transition to seek out her own path, return to her homeland, or isolate herself, Ruth remains steadfastly in the home of Naomi. Her "dwelling" is an expression of quiet faith and contented submission. She does not force her own way but waits patiently. This period of quiet dwelling sets the stage for Chapter 3, where Naomi will seek a permanent "rest" (a home and husband) for Ruth.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 13:12: "Abram dwelt in the land of Canaan, and Lot dwelt in the cities of the plain and pitched his tent even as far as Sodom."

• Genesis 47:27: "So Israel dwelt in the land of Egypt, in the country of Goshen; and they had possessions there and grew and multiplied exceedingly."

• 1 Kings 4:25: "And Judah and Israel dwelt safely, each man under his vine and his fig tree, from Dan as far as Beersheba, all the days of Solomon."

• Psalm 133:1: "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is For brethren to dwell together in unity!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound literary contrast in Hebrew narrative between "sojourning" (gur) and "dwelling" (yashab). A sojourner is a temporary resident with no permanent rights or roots. Ruth began her journey in Israel as a foreign sojourner, but by choosing to yashab with Naomi, she is woven into the very fabric of the community. This Hebrew word marks her transition from an outsider looking in to a settled insider who will ultimately become an ancestress of King David and Jesus Christ.
