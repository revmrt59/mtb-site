HARVEST - ( qatsir , Strong's H7105 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun qatsir refers to the act of harvesting, the specific season of the harvest, or the gathered crop itself. Derived from a verbal root meaning to shorten, cut off, or reap, it represents the culmination of a season of labor, a time of gathering the fruit of the earth, and a crucial period of physical provision. In the narrative of Ruth, qatsir functions as more than a seasonal marker; it serves as the literary and theological backdrop for God's providential care, transitioning the characters from famine and emptiness to abundance and redemption.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word qatsir comes from the verbal root qatsar, which means to cut short, reap, or harvest. In the ancient Near Eastern agrarian society, the harvest was the most critical season of the year. Survival depended entirely on the crop yield. The process involved manually cutting the stalks of grain with sickles, binding them into sheaves, transporting them to the threshing floor, and winnowing them in the wind. The annual harvest began with the barley crop in the spring (around April) and concluded with the wheat crop (around June), spanning approximately seven weeks. Because this season required intensive community labor and determined the nation's food security, it was marked by hard physical work, intense vigilance, and profound communal joy.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of qatsir is the harvest, both as a process and as a season. The semantic range of the noun includes:

• The act or process of reaping grain.

• The time or season of harvesting (e.g., "at the time of harvest").

• The harvested crop itself, standing or gathered.

• The foliage, boughs, or branches of a tree (a distinct, metaphorical usage found in poetic texts like Job and the Prophets).

In the historical narrative of Ruth, the term refers specifically to the agricultural harvest of cereal grains (barley and wheat).

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently employs qatsir to communicate profound spiritual and relational truths:

• Joy and Gladness: The harvest represents the ultimate expression of joy. Scripture contrasts the weeping of sowing with the singing of harvesting (Psalm 126:5) and uses the phrase "the joy of harvest" to describe supreme rejoicing (Isaiah 9:3).

• Judgment: Because a harvest occurs when a crop is fully ripe, it is used metaphorically for divine judgment. When a nation's wickedness is "ripe," God swings the sickle of judgment (Joel 3:13).

• Sowing and Reaping: The harvest is the natural and moral consequence of previous actions. Those who sow wickedness will reap a harvest of trouble (Job 4:8).

• Opportunity: The harvest represents a limited window of opportunity. Sleeping or idling during this time is a mark of foolishness and brings shame (Proverbs 10:5).

### 4. Theological Implications

The theology of qatsir is deeply rooted in God’s covenant relationship with Israel.

• God as Provider: The harvest is never viewed merely as the result of human labor or natural processes. It is a direct gift from God, who sends the rain in its season and blesses the soil.

• Covenant Law and Compassion: God established specific laws regarding qatsir to protect the vulnerable. Landowners were commanded not to reap the very corners of their fields or gather the gleanings of the harvest (Leviticus 23:22). These leftovers were legally reserved for the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the stranger. Therefore, the harvest season was designed to be a visible manifestation of covenant mercy, love, and community welfare.

• Sovereign Restoration: In the book of Ruth, the return of the harvest signifies that God has visited His people to give them bread (Ruth 1:6), reversing the curse of the famine.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:23, qatsir appears twice: "the end of barley harvest and wheat harvest." This verse serves as a crucial literary transition and a testimony of sustained grace.

• Sustained Grace: Ruth's gleaning began at the "beginning of barley harvest" (Ruth 1:22) and continued uninterrupted through the "wheat harvest." This means Ruth and Naomi received daily, miraculous provision and protection in the fields of Boaz for nearly two months.

• Security and Integration: By staying close to Boaz’s young women throughout both harvests, Ruth is protected from harassment and integrated into the community.

• The Transition of Seasons: The completion of the harvest (qatsir) marks the end of the agricultural cycle and sets the stage for the next phase of redemption. With the granaries full, Naomi shifts her focus from physical survival to securing a permanent home and redeemer for Ruth.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 8:22 - "While the earth remains, Seedtime and harvest, Cold and heat, Winter and summer, And day and night Shall not cease."

• Exodus 23:16 - "and the Feast of Harvest, the firstfruits of your labors which you have sown in the field; and the Feast of Ingathering at the end of the year, when you have gathered in the fruit of your labors from the field."

• Proverbs 10:5 - "He who gathers in summer is a wise son; He who sleeps in harvest is a son who causes shame."

• Isaiah 9:3 - "You have multiplied the nation And increased its joy; They rejoice before You According to the joy of harvest, As men rejoice when they divide the spoil."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The timeline of the barley and wheat harvests in Israel aligns with the liturgical calendar. The barley harvest began around the Feast of Firstfruits (Passover season), and the wheat harvest concluded around the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost). This period of seven weeks represents a time of anticipation, thanksgiving, and spiritual preparation. Furthermore, the physical harvest of grain in Bethlehem (which means "House of Bread") prefigures the spiritual harvest of souls. Ruth, a Gentile Moabite, is gathered into the covenant community of Israel during the harvest, illustrating the global scope of God's redemptive plan.
