BARLEY - ( se'orah , Strong's H8184 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, barley represents the firstfruits of the spring harvest, signifying provision, hope, and the end of famine. In the narrative of Ruth, the barley harvest is not merely an agricultural season but a divinely orchestrated backdrop for redemption. As Ruth gleans in the fields of Boaz, this hardy, humble grain becomes a tangible symbol of God’s grace returning to Naomi’s empty home, transforming their bitter loss into abundant life.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun se'orah (plural se'orim) is derived from a root word meaning "to bristle" or "to stand on end," which also yields the Hebrew word for hair (se'ar). This refers to the long, hair-like bristly fibers (awns) that project from the head of the barley ear. Barley was one of the staple crops of ancient Israel, prized for its ability to grow in poor soils and withstand drier, harsher conditions than wheat. The barley harvest took place in early spring (Abib/Nisan, corresponding to March/April), marking the very beginning of the agricultural cycle and the celebration of Passover and the Feast of Firstfruits. Wheat harvest followed several weeks later, concluding around Pentecost.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Se'orah designates the barley plant, the standing crop, or the harvested grain. In the ancient Near East, barley had several distinct associations:

• The Food of the Common People: Because it was cheaper and easier to grow than wheat, barley was the primary grain used to make bread for the poor and working classes.

• Animal Feed: It was widely cultivated as fodder for beasts of burden, particularly horses and donkeys.

• A Standard of Value: Before the introduction of minted coinage, specified measures of barley served as a currency and a legal standard for commercial transactions and valuations.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Loaf of Gideon (Judges 7:13): A tumbling cake of barley bread represents the humble, low-status Israelite army under Gideon. The midianite soldier's dream identifies this cheap, coarse bread as the unexpected instrument of victory over a powerful oppressor, demonstrating how God uses the weak things of the world to shame the strong.

• The Firstfruits of Resurrection (Leviticus 23:10-11): Because the barley harvest was the first crop to ripen, the waving of the first barley sheaf (the omer) before the Lord represented the dedication of the entire coming harvest. It is a powerful picture of hope, resurrection, and reliance on God’s ongoing provision.

• Elisha’s Multiplication (2 Kings 4:42): The multiplication of twenty loaves of barley bread to feed a hundred men prefigures Jesus' feeding of the five thousand with five barley loaves (John 6:9), emphasizing God's supernatural power to multiply humble offerings to meet the needs of His people.

### 4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Faithfulness (Chesed): The availability of barley in Bethlehem ("House of Bread") signifies that God has visited His people to give them food, bringing an end to the famine that drove Elimelech's family away.

• Sovereign Provision: God establishes agricultural laws—such as leaving the corners of the fields for the poor and stranger (Leviticus 19:9-10)—to ensure that the abundance of the barley harvest reaches the most vulnerable. The harvest is a physical manifestation of God's grace in action.

• From Emptiness to Fullness: Naomi left Bethlehem full and returned empty (Ruth 1:21). The abundant grain Ruth brings home daily from Boaz’s field is the physical proof that God is actively filling their empty lives.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:23, Ruth "stayed close by the young women of Boaz to glean toward the end of barley harvest and wheat harvest."

• The Span of Grace: The mention of both the barley and wheat harvests indicates a period of about two to three months (from late April to June). This reveals that Boaz’s provision and protection were not fleeting, one-time gestures, but a sustained, daily shelter.

• Virtuous Diligence: Staying close to Boaz’s workers highlights Ruth's obedience, wisdom, and moral integrity. She avoided the dangers of wandering into unknown fields and committed herself to hard labor to feed her mother-in-law.

• The Bridge to Redemption: This prolonged season of labor served as the transitional bridge. It allowed Naomi to observe the outstanding character of Boaz and formulate the plan of redemption that would culminate at the threshing floor, where the harvested grain was processed.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 9:31: "Now the flax and the barley were struck, for the barley was in the head and the flax was in bud."

• Leviticus 27:16: "If a man dedicates to the LORD part of a field of his possession, then your valuation shall be according to the seed for it. A homer of barley seed shall be valued at fifty shekels of silver."

• Judges 7:13: "And when Gideon had come, there was a man telling a dream to his companion. He said, 'I have had a dream: To my surprise, a loaf of barley bread tumbled into the camp of Midian; it came to a tent and struck it so that it fell and overturned, and the tent collapsed.'"

• 2 Kings 4:42: "Then a man came from Baal-Shalisha, and brought the man of God bread of the firstfruits, twenty loaves of barley bread, and newly ripened grain in his knapsack. And he said, 'Give it to the people, that they may eat.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The chronological alignment of the barley harvest with Passover and the wheat harvest with Pentecost is deeply significant. The Book of Ruth is traditionally read in synagogues during Shavuot (Pentecost). Just as Israel received the Law at Sinai during the wheat harvest, Ruth is brought into the covenant community of Israel and experiences the fullness of redemption under Boaz during this same agricultural season.
