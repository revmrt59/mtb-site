BOAZ - ( Bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

Boaz is a prominent character in the Book of Ruth whose name likely means "in him is strength" or "fleetness." As a wealthy, God-fearing landowner of Bethlehem and a close relative of Elimelech, Boaz serves as the quintessential "kinsman-redeemer" (goel). His character, exemplifying covenant faithfulness (chesed), integrity, and grace, points forward to Jesus Christ as the ultimate Redeemer of those who are destitute and marginalized.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Historical Context: The account of Boaz takes place during the turbulent period of the Judges, an era marked by moral decay, spiritual compromise, and social instability, where "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). Boaz stands in stark contrast to this cultural decay as a man of righteousness, honor, and loyalty to God's law.

• Bethlehem and Agricultural Setting: The setting is Bethlehem during the barley harvest. Ruth, a destitute Moabitess widow, seeks to survive by gleaning in the fields, a provision ordained by Mosaic law for the poor, the stranger, and the widow.

• Social Standing: Boaz is introduced as a gibbor chayil, a phrase indicating both substantial financial resources and noble character, high social standing, and moral integrity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Name Meaning: Boaz is a proper noun meaning "in him is strength" or "alacrity/swiftness."

• Derivation: The most widely accepted etymology combines the prepositional prefix be ("in") and the noun oz ("strength"), yielding "in him is strength." A secondary derivation links it to an unused root meaning "alacrity" or "fleetness."

• Double Usage: Beyond its usage as a proper name for the Bethlehemite landowner, the name was also given to the left-hand bronze pillar erected in the porch of Solomon's Temple, representing strength and divine stability (1 Kings 7:21).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Protector and Provider: Boaz behaves as a protective guardian. When he encounters Ruth, he instructs his young men not to touch her and allows her to drink from the water vessels of his workers. This provides a vivid word picture of refuge and security.

• The Wings of God: In Ruth 2:12, Boaz blesses Ruth, praying that she receive a full reward from the Lord God of Israel, "under whose wings you have come for refuge." Later, in Ruth 3:9, Ruth asks Boaz to "spread your wing over your maidservant, for you are a close relative." This wordplay shows Boaz acting as the physical expression of God's protective wings.

• Exemplary Employer: Boaz's greeting to his harvesters, "The LORD be with you!" and their response, "The LORD bless you!" portrays a workplace governed by mutual respect, covenant faithfulness, and spiritual devotion.

4. Theological Implications

• The Kinsman-Redeemer (Goel): The primary theological significance of Boaz lies in his role as a goel (kinsman-redeemer). According to the Law of Moses, a redeemer had to be a close relative who was both able and willing to buy back land lost by a poor relative and to marry the childless widow to carry on the family name.

• Requirements of Redemption: Boaz perfectly satisfies the three criteria of a redeemer:

• Relationship: He was a near kinsman of Elimelech's family.

• Resources: He possessed the necessary wealth to redeem the estate.

• Resolve: He was willing to make the personal sacrifice, risking his own inheritance to redeem Ruth and Naomi.

• Christological Typology: Boaz is a profound type of Jesus Christ. Just as Ruth was a destitute Gentile stranger who had no legal claim on Boaz but received his grace, so also believers are redeemed by Christ, who is our elder brother (relationship), infinitely rich in grace (resources), and laid down His life voluntarily to redeem His bride (resolve).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:3, Ruth "happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz." The Hebrew writer uses the word miqreh, meaning "hap," "chance," or "accidental occurrence."

• While humanly viewed as "good luck," this is a literary device highlighting the invisible hand of God's providence. God guided her steps to the field of the very man who had the power and character to redeem her.

• Boaz’s field serves as a sanctuary. While the rest of Israel was experiencing chaos and lawlessness, Boaz's estate was a microcosm of the Kingdom of God, characterized by order, generosity, protection, and the fulfillment of the Torah.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 2:1 - "There was a relative of Naomi’s husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech; his name was Boaz."

• Ruth 3:2 - "Now Boaz, whose young women you were with, is he not our relative? In fact, he is winnowing barley tonight on the threshing floor."

• Ruth 4:9 - "And Boaz said to the elders and all the people, 'You are witnesses this day that I have bought all that was Elimelech’s, and all that was Chilion’s and Mahlon’s, from the hand of Naomi.'"

• 1 Kings 7:21 - "Then he set up the pillars by the vestibule of the temple; he set up the pillar on the right and called its name Jachin, and he set up the pillar on the left and called its name Boaz."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Heritage of Grace: According to the genealogy in Matthew 1:5, Boaz was the son of Salmon and Rahab, the Canaanite harlot of Jericho. Having a mother who was a foreign outsider who found grace in Israel likely shaped Boaz's compassionate, non-judgmental attitude toward Ruth, who was also a foreign outsider from Moab.

• Royal Ancestry: Boaz and Ruth became the parents of Obed, who fathered Jesse, the father of David. Thus, Boaz is the great-grandfather of Israel's greatest king and a direct ancestor of Jesus the Messiah, proving that God weaves unexpected threads of grace into the lineage of redemption.
