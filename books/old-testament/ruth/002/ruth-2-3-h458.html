ELIMELECH - ( 'Elimelek , Strong's H458 )

Summary:

The name Elimelech means "My God is King." It is a compound Hebrew name rich with theological significance, representing a confession of Yahweh's absolute sovereignty. In the book of Ruth, the name serves as a poignant irony: despite bearing a name that proclaimed God's kingship and provision, Elimelech abandoned the Promised Land during a famine to seek refuge in pagan Moab, where he ultimately died. In Ruth 2:3, the mention of Elimelech's family connects the destitute widow Ruth to Boaz, her potential kinsman-redeemer, demonstrating that even when human faith fails, the ultimate King remains sovereignly faithful to His covenant promises.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Elimelech is a compound of two Hebrew elements: 'El (the singular form of the word for God, pointing to God's power and might) with the first-person singular pronominal suffix î ("my"), and melek (the Hebrew word for "king").

• In the Ancient Near East, names were not merely labels but declarations of character, destiny, or parentage. A name like Elimelech was a living confession of faith. It declared to everyone who spoke it that the God of Israel was the true ruler and sustainer of His people.

• This name arose during the period of the Judges, a chaotic era characterized by the spiritual refrain: "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). Against this backdrop of spiritual anarchy, Elimelech’s name stood as a vital theological counter-testimony: God is King.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The name means "My God is King" or "God is my King."

• The initial root 'alap refers to strength or power, from which 'El is derived, designating the strong and mighty God.

• The second root malak means to reign, rule, or take counsel as a king. The noun melek designates a royal ruler or sovereign.

• Combined, the name functions as a personal nominal sentence: "My God is King."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The name Elimelech appears only in the book of Ruth (6 times).

• Literary foil: The name acts as a literary foil for the actions of the man himself. Facing a famine in Bethlehem ("House of Bread"), the man whose name declared "My God is King" decided that his King could not or would not provide for him. He emigrated to Moab, a nation born of incest and historically hostile to Israel.

• This movement from the land of promise to the plains of Moab represents a failure to live up to the name's confession. Instead of submitting to the sovereign rule of his King within the covenant land, Elimelech sought survival under his own terms in a foreign, pagan land, resulting in the tragic loss of his life and his sons' lives.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty and Providence: The central theme of the name is the kingship of God. Even when His people are faithless, God remains faithful to His royal covenant. Elimelech dies in exile, but the King he abandoned does not abandon Elimelech’s widow, Naomi, or his daughter-in-law, Ruth.

• The Irony of Unbelief: Elimelech’s life illustrates the tragedy of naming God as King with one's lips while denying His sovereignty with one's actions. True covenant faith trusts the King's provision even in times of famine.

• The King's Redemption: The reference to Elimelech in Ruth 2:3 sets the stage for the redemption of his estate. God, acting as the true King, works behind the scenes through the kinsman-redeemer (Boaz) to preserve Elimelech’s name and inheritance, ultimately leading to the birth of David and, eventually, Jesus Christ—the ultimate King.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:3, Ruth "happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was of the family of Elimelech."

• The narrator uses the phrase "happened to come" (literally, "her chance chanced upon") to describe Ruth's arrival at Boaz’s field. This is a literary device highlighting divine providence. What seems like random chance to human eyes is actually the sovereign hand of the King (Elimelech's namesake) directing Ruth's steps.

• By identifying Boaz as being "of the family of Elimelech," the text alerts the reader that the rights of redemption are active. Elimelech’s name, though representing a deceased husband and father, still carries legal weight in Israel. The deceased "King's" family line will be restored by the sovereign grace of the true King.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:2: "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there."

• Ruth 1:3: "Then Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died; and she was left, and her two sons."

• Ruth 2:1: "There was a relative of Naomi’s husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech. His name was Boaz."

• Ruth 4:3: "Then he said to the close relative, 'Naomi, who has come back from the country of Moab, sells the piece of land which belonged to our brother Elimelech.'"

• Ruth 4:9: "And Boaz said to the elders and all the people, 'You are witnesses this day that I have bought all that was Elimelech’s, and all that was Chilion’s and Mahlon’s, from the hand of Naomi.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The name Elimelech may also reflect the political atmosphere of Israel during the period of the Judges. Gideon famously refused the kingship, declaring, "the LORD shall rule over you" (Judges 8:23). Elimelech’s parents likely named him as a protest against human kingship, affirming that Yahweh alone was Israel's monarch.

• The tragic irony of Elimelech's death in Moab is balanced by the glorious resolution in Ruth 4. Through the levirate-like redemption of Elimelech’s property and line, his name is not blotted out from Israel. Instead, it is preserved in the genealogy of the Messiah, proving that the King always preserves His own.
