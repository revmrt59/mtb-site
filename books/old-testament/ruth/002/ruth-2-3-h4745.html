HAPPENED - ( miqreh , Strong's H4745 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word miqreh refers to a chance occurrence, hap, fortune, or event that happens without human planning. In Ruth 2:3, the phrase "she happened to come" (literally, "her hap happened") describes what appeared to be a random coincidence from a human perspective, but was actually the invisible, sovereign hand of God steering Ruth to the field of her kinsman-redeemer, Boaz.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun miqreh comes from the verbal root qarah, which means to meet, encounter, or happen.

• The underlying word picture is an unplanned meeting or an event that crosses one's path unexpectedly. It represents an occurrence that is entirely outside of human foresight, control, or design.

• In the ancient Near East, life was filled with unpredictable circumstances. When an event occurred without an obvious human cause or orchestration, it was described as a miqreh—a "chance" or "hap."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Miqreh denotes an accident, chance, fortune, fate, or a sudden event.

• It signifies a circumstance that occurs by happenstance, or an event that unexpectedly befalls someone.

• In theological contexts, it highlights the contrast between human ignorance of the future and divine administration of all events. What humans perceive as "luck" or "coincidence" is the unfolding of a larger, divinely ordered reality.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In historical narratives, miqreh describes events that seem completely accidental, such as Ruth wandering into the field of Boaz or the Philistines wondering if their plagues were merely a "chance" that happened to them.

• In wisdom literature, particularly Ecclesiastes, the word is often translated as "event," "fate," or "befalls," describing the common destiny of death that awaits both the wise and the fool, or humans and animals. Here, it emphasizes the temporal limitations of human life and the unpredictability of earthly existence.

4. Theological Implications

• The word miqreh serves as a literary and theological foil for divine providence. By framing an event as "chance," the biblical author highlights that while humans cannot see the connection between events, God is actively working behind the scenes.

• Scripture rejects absolute, blind chance. God's sovereignty encompasses even the "coincidences" of life. The lot cast into the lap is governed by the Lord, and the "hap" of Ruth is the deliberate guidance of Yahweh fulfilling His covenant faithfulness.

• For the believer, "chance" is simply the human term for the invisible, preparatory work of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:3, the Hebrew text employs a striking cognate accusative: wayyiqer miqreha, literally meaning "and her hap happened" or "her chance chanced."

• Ruth did not possess a map of Bethlehem's fields, nor did she know which plot belonged to Boaz. She was simply looking for a place to glean to survive.

• The author uses this double emphasis on "chance" with deep irony. To the casual observer, Ruth's arrival at Boaz’s field was a stroke of pure luck. However, this "chance" encounter is the exact turning point of the narrative, revealing that God's silent providence was leading this grieving Moabite widow directly to her redeemer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 1 Samuel 6:9 - "but if not, then we shall know that it is not His hand that struck us—it was a chance that happened to us."

• 1 Samuel 20:26 - "Nevertheless Saul did not say anything that day, for he thought, 'Something has happened to him; he is unclean, surely he is unclean.'"

• Ecclesiastes 2:14 - "The wise man’s eyes are in his head, but the fool walks in darkness. Yet I myself perceived that the same event happens to them all."

• Ecclesiastes 3:19 - "For what happens to the sons of men also happens to beasts; one thing befalls them: as one dies, so dies the other."

• Ecclesiastes 9:2 - "All things come alike to all: One event falls to the righteous and the wicked..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The concept of miqreh reminds pastors and teachers that God's most profound works are often packaged in the mundane, seemingly accidental moments of daily life.

• It contrasts sharply with pagan fatalism. In pagan thought, "fate" or "chance" is an impersonal, capricious force. In the biblical worldview, what appears to be "chance" is entirely subject to a personal, loving, and covenant-keeping God.

• Ruth's "happening" upon the field of Boaz illustrates Romans 8:28 in action centuries before it was written. God orchestrates the steps of those who walk in obedience, even when they are unaware of His immediate direction.
