FAMILY - ( mishpachah , Strong's H4940 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mishpachah represents a foundational unit of ancient Israelite society, situated between the immediate household (bet-ab) and the larger tribe. Translated most frequently as "family" or "clan," it denotes a group of relatives bound by ancestral bloodlines, shared land inheritances, and mutual covenant obligations. In the Book of Ruth, particularly in Ruth 2:3, mishpachah is not merely a genealogical label but a structural vehicle of God's providence. It identifies Boaz as belonging to the clan of Elimelech, thereby legally and relationally qualifying him to act as the family's kinsman-redeemer (goel). This word underscores how God uses established family bonds and covenant loyalties to bring about redemption, restoration, and ultimate hope.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• In ancient Israel, social structure was concentric. The largest unit was the nation, followed by the tribe (shevet or matteh), the clan or family (mishpachah), and finally the ancestral household (bet-ab).

• The mishpachah served as the primary protective, judicial, and economic unit of society. It was responsible for keeping land within the family line, redeeming members who fell into slavery, and continuing the lineage of deceased relatives.

• The background of mishpachah is rooted in agrarian life and covenantal inheritance. Land was gifted by God to specific tribes and subdivided among the mishpachot (plural of mishpachah). Therefore, maintaining the integrity of the mishpachah was synonymous with preserving God's covenantal distribution of the Promised Land.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Mishpachah refers to a clan, family, or subdivision of a tribe. It is derived from a root that relates to association, class, or kind.

• In legal and historical texts, it designates a group of families associated by blood, geography, and shared inheritance.

• The word can also refer broadly to a "class" or "species" of animals or nations, but its primary theological and narrative usage designates the Israelite clan.

• The term emphasizes solidarity, shared responsibility, and kinship obligations, particularly the duty of redemption (geullah).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Old Testament uses mishpachah over 300 times, predominantly in historical narratives, genealogies, and legal codes (such as Leviticus, Numbers, and Joshua) describing the division of the Promised Land.

• A powerful word picture associated with mishpachah is that of a protective hedge. In a harsh world of famine, war, and economic vulnerability, the clan was an individual's safety net. To be separated from one's mishpachah was to be utterly exposed to poverty and foreign exploitation.

• In the context of the goel (kinsman-redeemer) laws, the mishpachah acts as a reservoir of restoration. If a member of the clan loses his land or freedom, the nearest capable relative within the mishpachah has the right and duty to step in and buy it back, restoring wholeness to the family line.

4. Theological Implications

• Providence through Covenant Structures: God does not work in a vacuum; He often chooses to manifest His sovereign grace through the existing legal, social, and relational structures He established. The mishpachah is the vehicle for redeeming Ruth and Naomi.

• The Shadow of the Ultimate Redeemer: The obligations of the mishpachah point directly to Jesus Christ. To redeem humanity, Jesus had to become part of our "family" (the human race, Heb. 2:11-17), making Himself our near kinsman who possesses both the right and the resources to redeem us.

• Relational Holiness: God's covenant with Israel was not merely individualistic. Holiness and restoration were communal realities worked out through daily family and clan relationships.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:3, Ruth "happens" to glean in the portion of the field belonging to Boaz, "who was of the family (mishpachah) of Elimelech."

• The narrator uses "happened" (miqreh) with literary irony. What seemed like chance to Ruth was the sovereign coordination of God. The mention of mishpachah immediately alerts the reader to the legal possibilities: Boaz is not a random stranger; he is a clan member.

• This connection shifts the narrative from a story of mere survival (gleaning to eat) to a story of potential redemption. The word mishpachah acts as a bridge between the hopelessness of Naomi’s empty household and the hope of restoration through Boaz's kinsman-redeemer role.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:3: "I will bless those who bless you, and I will curse him who curses you; and in you all the families [mishpachah] of the earth shall be blessed."

• Leviticus 25:10: "And you shall consecrate the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land to all its inhabitants. It shall be a Jubilee for you; and each of you shall return to his possession, and each of you shall return to his family [mishpachah]."

• Leviticus 25:49: "either his uncle or his uncle’s son may redeem him, or anyone who is a near relation to him in his family [mishpachah] may redeem him; or if he is able, he may redeem himself."

• Joshua 13:15: "And Moses had given to the tribe of the children of Reuben an inheritance according to their families [mishpachah]."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between shevet (tribe) and mishpachah (family/clan) is vital. While a tribe provided political and military identity, the mishpachah provided practical, daily economic survival and legal protection.

• In the ancient Near East, a family's name and inheritance were considered an extension of their very soul. If a mishpachah died out, it was viewed as a spiritual and physical tragedy. Thus, Boaz's willingness to redeem Ruth and raise up an heir for the deceased Elimelech was an act of profound covenant love (chesed) that saved a failing branch of his mishpachah.
