HAPPENED TO COME - ( qarah , Strong's H7136 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qarah expresses the occurrence of an event that, from a human standpoint, is accidental, unplanned, or coincidental. In Ruth 2:3, the phrase "happened to come" highlights a turning point where Ruth’s steps are guided to the field of Boaz without her prior planning or knowledge. While the word denotes "chance" or "hap" to human observers, the broader narrative reveals it as the silent, sovereign orchestration of God's providence, working behind the scenes to care for the vulnerable and fulfill His redemptive purposes.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of qarah relates to meeting, encountering, or building a framework where elements come together. In architectural contexts, a closely related form refers to laying beams or constructing a framework where different parts meet to form a supportive structure. Metaphorically, when applied to human events, qarah pictures the sudden, unplanned intersection of two distinct pathways. It describes the moment where human journeys or circumstances cross paths without prior coordination by the participants.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qarah means to encounter, meet, befall, happen, or bring about. The semantic range spans several distinct nuances depending on the grammatical stem:

• Qal (Active): To meet, encounter, or happen to someone. It describes events that simply occur or overtake an individual.

• Hiphil (Causative): To cause to meet, to make something happen, or to grant success. In this stem, the verb is used to ask God to make a journey successful by causing the right person or event to cross one's path.

• Niphal (Passive): To meet unexpectedly or to be met by someone.

In the context of Ruth 2:3, the verb appears in the Qal stem (wayyiqer) alongside its cognate noun miqreh (hap, chance, or fortune), creating a literary wordplay that translates literally to "and her hap happened" or "her chance chanced."

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses qarah to describe moments where human control is entirely absent, leaving the outcome to what appears to be sheer coincidence.

• The Unplanned Meeting: It describes a traveler walking down a road and encountering someone unexpected, emphasizing the lack of human scheduling.

• The Divine Setup: In the causative stem, it is a prayer for God to orchestrate a meeting. Abraham's servant prays for qarah when seeking a bride for Isaac, asking God to "cause it to happen" that the right woman appears at the well.

• The Shared Destiny: The term also describes events that "befall" all people regardless of their moral choices, such as physical death, illustrating that certain earthly realities are unavoidable encounters.

### 4. Theological Implications

The usage of qarah in Ruth 2:3 serves as a primary theological vehicle for the doctrine of divine providence. The book of Ruth contains no spectacular miracles, open visions, or direct angelic interventions. Instead, God works silently through the ordinary decisions of faithful people.

By describing Ruth’s arrival at Boaz’s field as a "chance happening," the text deliberately highlights the limitations of human foresight. Ruth did not plan to go to Boaz's field; she was simply looking for a place to glean. Boaz did not plan for her to be there. What is "chance" (qarah) to Ruth is the sovereign, covenant-keeping hand of God (hesed) directing her steps. The term teaches that there are no random coincidences in the life of the believer; what appears to be "luck" is the execution of God's unseen, benevolent plan.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:3, the context is one of desperate survival. Naomi and Ruth have returned to Bethlehem destitute. Ruth goes out to glean in the fields, exposing herself to potential harassment and physical danger as a foreign widow.

The author writes, "And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz." The narrator uses qarah to emphasize the sheer improbability of this event from a human perspective. Bethlehem had many fields, and Ruth was a stranger who knew nothing of the family lines or land holdings. Her entrance into the exact field of a wealthy, righteous relative who could serve as a family redeemer was, humanly speaking, a stroke of incredible luck. The literary placement of qarah sets up the immediate entrance of Boaz in the very next verse, heightening the reader's awareness that God is actively coordinating this rescue.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 24:12 - "Then he said, 'O LORD God of my master Abraham, please give me success this day, and show kindness to my master Abraham.'" (The phrase "give me success" translates the causative form of qarah, petitioning God to cause the right encounter to happen).

• Genesis 27:20 - "But Isaac said to his son, 'How is it that you have found it so quickly, my son?' And he said, 'Because the LORD your God brought it to me.'" (The phrase "brought it to me" translates the causative form of qarah, meaning God caused the game to cross his path).

• Numbers 23:3 - "Then Balaam said to Balak, 'Stand by your burnt offering, and I will go; perhaps the LORD will come to meet me, and whatever He shows me I will tell you.' So he went to a desolate height." ("come to meet me" is a usage of qarah as a divine encounter).

• Ecclesiastes 2:14 - "The wise man’s eyes are in his head, / But the fool walks in darkness. / Yet I myself perceived / That the same event happens to them all." (Here, "happens" uses qarah to describe the common encounter of death that overtakes everyone).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew noun counterpart in Ruth 2:3 is miqreh (Strong's H4745), translated as "hap," "chance," "fortune," or "fate." When the author combines the verb qarah with the noun miqreh, it creates an intense emphasis on the accidental nature of the event. It is a deliberate rhetorical device. The writer uses the language of extreme chance to force the reader to look beyond the natural world to the supernatural director of the drama.

This contrasts sharply with pagan worldviews, which viewed "chance" or "fortune" as a blind, capricious deity (such as the Canaanite ideas of fate). In the Hebrew Scriptures, "chance" is never independent of Yahweh; it is merely a human term for the unexpected moves of a sovereign God.
