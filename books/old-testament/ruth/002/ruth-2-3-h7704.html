FIELD - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to open country, cultivated land, or a specific plot of ground owned by an individual. In the narrative of Ruth, the "field" represents both the place of grueling labor for survival and the providential stage where God orchestrates redemption. It is the physical space where Ruth’s faithfulness meets Boaz’s generosity, transforming her status from a destitute foreign gleaner to a favored bride.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In agrarian ancient Israel, the sadeh was the lifeblood of the community. It represents the arable, cultivated land surrounding a city or village, standing in contrast to the walled city (iyr) and the barren wilderness (midbar). The sadeh was the place of planting, tending, grazing, and harvesting. It was an open, vulnerable space, devoid of city walls, meaning those who worked there—especially poor gleaners—were exposed to the elements, wild animals, and potential human exploitation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Sadeh denotes open land, a defined plot of agricultural ground, or the territory belonging to a specific nation or clan. It refers to land that can be plowed, sown, and harvested, as well as pastureland for livestock. In some contexts, it refers to the broader, uncultivated countryside where wild beasts roam. In the book of Ruth, it is used to describe the agricultural fields of Bethlehem where barley and wheat were cultivated.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Field of Vulnerability: In the ancient Near East, the open field was a place where a person was exposed to danger. It was the setting where Cain attacked Abel (Genesis 4:8), away from the protective eyes of family.

• The Field of Covenant Provision: The law of Moses designated the sadeh as a place where the rich were to express charity. The corners of the field were to be left unharvested for the poor, the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless (Leviticus 19:9).

• The Field of Inheritance: Land ownership in Israel was deeply tied to family covenant lines. A family's sadeh was their permanent inheritance, which could not be permanently sold, but had to be redeemed if lost due to poverty (Leviticus 25:23-28).

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of sadeh in the Old Testament is tied to God's ownership of the land. The land belongs to Yahweh, and Israel holds it as stewards. Therefore, the sadeh is the primary testing ground of a landowner’s covenant faithfulness. In the book of Ruth, the sadeh serves as the stage for divine providence. God does not speak through spectacular visions or thunderous voices in this book; instead, He works silently through the daily, mundane tasks of working and gleaning in the field. The field of Boaz becomes a sanctuary of grace where the marginalized find shelter under the wings of the God of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:3, the word sadeh is used twice in a way that highlights the intersection of human initiative and divine sovereignty. Ruth goes to glean in "the field" (sadeh), which refers to the vast, open agricultural area outside the gates of Bethlehem. This area was not divided by fences but by small stone markers.

As she gleans, the text notes that she "happened to come to the part of the field (sadeh) belonging to Boaz." While from a human perspective it was a mere coincidence—an accidental choice of direction—the repetition of sadeh emphasizes that her steps were guided by God. She went to the general, communal field of the town, but landed on the specific plot of land owned by the kinsman-redeemer. What was a place of survival and toil became the place of her redemption.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:8 - "Now Cain talked with Abel his brother; and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him."

• Leviticus 19:9 - "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest."

• Ruth 2:2 - "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.'"

• Proverbs 24:30 - "I went by the field of the lazy man, and by the vineyard of the man devoid of understanding;"

• Jeremiah 32:15 - "For thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel: 'Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, the Greek equivalent agros is used in Jesus’ parables to convey similar spiritual truths. In the Parable of the Sower, the field represents the world or the human heart (Matthew 13:38). In the Parable of the Hidden Treasure, the field is the place where a man finds a treasure of supreme value and joyfully sells all he has to buy that field (Matthew 13:44). This mirrors the story of Ruth, where Boaz acquires the field of Elimelech, and in doing so, joyfully acquires Ruth as his bride.
