WENT - ( bo' , Strong's H0935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo' (primarily meaning "to come" or "to enter") is a common yet highly dynamic word of motion in the Old Testament. In Ruth 2:3, translated as "went," it describes Ruth’s arrival and entry into the fields of Bethlehem to glean. Rather than depicting mere aimless wandering, bo' signifies purposeful movement toward a destination, serving as a literary pivot that moves Ruth from the isolation of her home into the sphere of God's providential care in the field of Boaz.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical action of bo' conveys the action of entering a specific space, coming home, or reaching a goal. It contrasts with halak (to walk, depart, or go away). In ancient Near Eastern life, "entering" a city, a tent, or a field carried legal and relational weight. To enter someone's field was to step into their domain of protection or vulnerability. For a destitute foreign widow like Ruth, entering a public field was a step of faith, leaving the safety of her home and crossing a threshold into the unknown, reliant entirely on the grace of the landowner and the protection of Yahweh.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bo' is used extensively in the Old Testament, appearing over 2,500 times. Its primary meaning is "to come in," "to enter," or "to go in." Depending on the direction of the movement relative to the speaker or narrator, it translates as "come," "go," "arrive," "bring," or "enter." In the context of spatial movement, it denotes the completion of a journey or the crossing of a boundary into a new location. It stands in contrast to yatsa' (to go out). When paired with another verb of motion, it often indicates a two-stage action: setting out and arriving or entering.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Spatial Entry: Used for entering a tent, house, or city (e.g., entering the ark, entering the Promised Land).

• Relational Association: "Going in" to a person can refer to coming into another's presence for petition or, in other contexts, intimate relational union.

• Temporal Progression: Used metaphorically for the setting of the sun ("the sun went down") or the coming of days and seasons.

• Divine Intervention: Used to describe God "coming" to judge, deliver, or dwell among His people.

4. Theological Implications

The verb bo' underscores the active, physical nature of faith and providence. Faith is not static; it moves. In the redemptive history of Israel, God’s people are constantly "coming" and "going" under His direction. Ruth's "coming" to the field represents the intersection of human obedience and divine sovereignty. Her physical arrival in the field of Boaz was not accidental, though it seemed so to her. Under God's sovereign guidance, her physical movement was the exact path through which the Messianic line would be preserved.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:3, the Hebrew text employs two verbs of motion back-to-back: wattelekh (and she went/walked) and wattabo' (and she entered/came), followed by wattelaqqet (and she gleaned). The NKJV translates this sequence as "Then she left, and went and gleaned."

The use of bo' emphasizes her arrival and entry into the field. She did not just walk around aimlessly; she arrived and entered a specific sphere of activity. The transition from leaving her home to entering the field marks the moment she places herself under the laws of gleaning (Leviticus 19:9-10). It highlights her vulnerability as a stranger entering foreign territory, which immediately sets up the introduction of Boaz, the lord of the field, who will provide her ultimate refuge under the wings of the Lord God of Israel.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural symmetry in the book of Ruth often hinges on this word. In chapter 1, Naomi and Ruth "come" (bo') to Bethlehem empty. In chapter 2, Ruth "comes" (bo') to the field of Boaz and "comes" (bo') back to Naomi with abundant grain. In chapter 3, Ruth "comes" (bo') secretly to the threshing floor of Boaz. In chapter 4, Boaz "comes" (bo') to the gate, and eventually, he takes Ruth and she "comes" to him as his wife (Ruth 4:13). The theological movement from emptiness to fullness is mapped onto the physical geographical "comings" and "goings" of the characters.
