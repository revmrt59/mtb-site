BOAZ - ( bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

Boaz is a Hebrew proper noun meaning "in him is strength" or "alacrity." In the Old Testament, it refers primarily to the wealthy, godly Bethlehemite landowner who becomes the kinsman-redeemer of Ruth, as well as to the northern bronze pillar erected at the vestibule of Solomon's temple. In the narrative context of Ruth 2:4, the introduction of Boaz marks a dramatic turning point in the history of Naomi's family, shifting the narrative from a tone of grief and destitution to one of hope, divine providence, and covenantal restoration.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Boaz is traditionally understood as a compound of the preposition be ("in") and the noun oz ("strength"), signifying "in him is strength" or "in strength." In ancient Israelite culture, names were deeply significant, often reflecting the character, destiny, or spiritual identity of the bearer. Boaz’s name serves as a literary and theological signpost. He is introduced as a "man of great wealth" (Ruth 2:1), but his true "strength" lies in his moral integrity, his generous spirit, and his unswerving commitment to the Torah's commands regarding the marginalized. He stands as a pillar of strength for two vulnerable, widowed foreign women in a period of Israel's history characterized by spiritual and societal decay.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Boaz is a proper masculine noun. The semantic weight of the word denotes:

• "In him is strength" or "by his strength."

• "Fleetness" or "quickness" (derived from root variations associated with energetic movement).

In the biblical text, the name is used to identify:

• The Bethlehemite of the tribe of Judah who married Ruth and became an ancestor of King David and Jesus Christ.

• The left-hand (northern) pillar of the two monumental bronze columns built by Solomon at the entrance of the temple in Jerusalem.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Book of Ruth, the name Boaz becomes synonymous with the concept of the goel—the kinsman-redeemer. He is the living embodiment of God's chesed (covenant loyalty and lovingkindness).

• The Protective Wing: In Ruth 2, Boaz invites Ruth to find refuge under his protection, a physical gesture that mirrors the spiritual reality of taking refuge under the wings of the God of Israel.

• The Pillar of the Temple: In 1 Kings 7:21, Solomon names one of the temple pillars Boaz. Paired with Jachin ("He will establish"), the name Boaz ("In Him is strength") forms a complete theological statement: "God will establish [the temple/dynasty], and in Him is its strength." This word picture reminds the worshiper that security and endurance come only from the Lord.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Boaz serves as one of the Old Testament's most vivid types of Jesus Christ.

• The Kinsman-Redeemer: To qualify as a kinsman-redeemer, a man had to be a near kinsman, possess the financial means to redeem the lost inheritance, and be willing to do so. Boaz met all three criteria. In the same way, Jesus became our near kinsman through the Incarnation, possessed the infinite spiritual resources to pay our debt, and willingly laid down His life to redeem us.

• Sovereign Grace in the Lineage of Promise: Boaz’s willingness to marry a Moabite widow shows that God's grace transcends national and ethnic boundaries. This union secured the line of David, showing that human strength surrendered to God's law becomes the vehicle for bringing the Messiah into the world.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:4, the narrator shifts the scene to the harvest field. The arrival of Boaz is marked by his speech: "The LORD be with you!" and the response of his workers: "The LORD bless you!" This greeting is highly significant in the context of the era of the Judges, a time when "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). Boaz’s language reveals that his estate is run on the principles of covenant faith. He does not treat his laborers or the poor gleaners with contempt. His presence immediately brings order, safety, and blessing. The text emphasizes that Boaz's strength is not tyrannical, but is instead characterized by godly leadership, dignity, and pastoral care for the needy.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The location of Boaz's fields in Bethlehem ("House of Bread") provides a profound geographic link. During the period of the famine, Bethlehem had no bread, but when Boaz—the man of strength—arrives, he restores abundance. Centuries later, in this same town of Bethlehem, Jesus Christ, the Bread of Life and our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, was born to provide spiritual sustenance and eternal redemption to all who glean in His fields of grace.
