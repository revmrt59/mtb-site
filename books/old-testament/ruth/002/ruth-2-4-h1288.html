BLESS - ( barak , Strong's H1288 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb barak fundamentally means to kneel, salute, praise, or invoke divine favor. In the Old Testament, it describes the transfer of life-giving benefit, strength, and holiness from God to humanity, or the responsive offering of praise and adoration from humanity back to God. Within the context of everyday interactions in ancient Israel, as seen in Boaz’s greeting to his workers, barak functions as a holy invocation of God’s active favor, signaling covenant loyalty, relational goodwill, and the presence of God’s peace within a community.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root of barak is closely associated with the Hebrew noun berek, which means "knee." From this physical root comes the concrete word picture of bending the knee, kneeling down, or presenting a gift on bended knee. In the ancient Near East, kneeling was an act of profound respect, submission, and adoration before a superior, a king, or God Himself. When applied to God blessing humans, the word picture shifts from the posture of the recipient to the generous condescension of the Giver, who stoops down to bestow vitalizing strength, abundance, and grace upon His creation. To bless is not merely to speak well of someone; it is to actively impart spiritual and physical vitality that leads to fruitfulness and well-being.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb barak is used extensively throughout the Old Testament to convey several interrelated concepts depending on the grammatical stem and context:

• To invoke divine favor: To pray or speak a blessing over someone, asking God to grant them success, protection, and prosperity.

• To endow with power and fruitfulness: God’s act of sovereignly granting life, fertility, growth, and success to people, animals, and the land.

• To praise or adore: Human expressions of gratitude and worship directed toward God, acknowledging Him as the source of all goodness.

• To salute or greet: A common form of polite, spiritually-minded greeting or departure, recognizing the worth of the other person under God’s covenant.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament depicts blessing in three primary directional movements:

• Vertical (Downward): God blessing humanity. When God blesses, His word is creative and performative. His blessing produces physical fruitfulness (as seen in the creation account), material prosperity (as seen in the lives of the patriarchs), and spiritual peace. It is the opposite of the curse (arar), which brings barrenness, decay, and isolation.

• Vertical (Upward): Humanity blessing God. When humans bless the Lord, they are not adding to His strength or conferring favor upon Him. Rather, they are kneeling in heart and posture, magnifying His attributes, and offering Him praise and Thanksgiving.

• Horizontal: Humanity blessing humanity. This occurs when one person invokes God's name and favor upon another. It is a prayerful wish that carries spiritual authority, particularly when spoken by parents, priests, or leaders. In these exchanges, the blesser acts as an instrument of God's grace, pointing the recipient to Yahweh as the true source of all life.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, barak is deeply tied to the covenant relationship between Yahweh and Israel. In the patriarchal narratives, the blessing is a tangible inheritance passed down through generations, carrying the promise of land, descendants, and divine presence. The ultimate trajectory of God’s blessing is missional: God blesses Abraham so that "all the families of the earth" will be blessed through him.

Furthermore, blessing is a sign of the restoration of creation. While sin brought the curse, God’s covenant initiative aims to restore His blessing. To live under God's blessing is to experience shalom—complete wholeness, harmony, and well-being in every dimension of life.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:4, Boaz arrives at his harvest field and greets his reapers, saying, "The LORD be with you!" They respond, "The LORD bless you!" (yebarekeka).

• This exchange highlights the spiritual and ethical climate of Boaz's estate during the dark spiritual era of the Judges. While the book of Judges is characterized by moral decay and social exploitation, Boaz's field is characterized by mutual respect, piety, and covenant love (hesed).

• The blessing here is a horizontal invocation of God's favor. The workers do not merely offer a polite, empty greeting; they pray that Yahweh will actively bestow His life-giving favor, prosperity, and protection upon their master.

• This setting of mutual blessing prepares the reader for the unfolding redemption of Ruth and Naomi. Boaz, a man who lives under and invokes God's blessing, will become the very channel of that blessing to a destitute Moabite widow.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:2 - "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great; and you shall be a blessing."

• Numbers 6:24 - "The LORD bless you and keep you;"

• Psalm 103:1 - "Bless the LORD, O my soul; and all that is within me, bless His holy name!"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

For preaching and teaching, the interaction in Ruth 2:4 serves as a powerful model for the workplace. The relationship between Boaz (the employer) and his reapers (the employees) is sanctified by the recognition of God's presence. When leaders and workers invoke the blessing of God upon one another, it transforms the daily grind of labor into an act of worship. It reminds believers that every sphere of life—including business and manual labor—is to be lived under the canopy of God's covenant grace and mutual blessing.
