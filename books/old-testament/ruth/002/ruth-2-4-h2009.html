BEHOLD - ( hinneh , Strong's H2009 )

Summary:

The Hebrew interjection hinneh serves as a literary and conversational pointer, drawing immediate, vivid attention to a person, event, or sudden development. It functions like a theatrical spotlight, signaling the reader or listener to focus closely on what is about to be revealed. In narrative contexts, it often introduces an unexpected turn of events, a divine intervention, or a crucial character entrance. In Ruth 2:4, "behold" marks the arrival of Boaz at a providential moment, transitioning the story from Ruth's quiet labor to the unfolding of God’s redemptive provision.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word hinneh is a demonstrative particle derived from the shorter form hen. It acts as a verbal gesture, the linguistic equivalent of pointing a finger to say, "Look right here!" It is designed to capture the senses, shifting the listener from passive hearing to active, visual anticipation. In the ancient near eastern mind, narrative was experienced dramatically and orally; hinneh served as the cue that a scene was changing, a new actor was entering, or an essential truth was being unveiled. It creates a sense of presence and immediacy, inviting the reader to step into the narrative as an eyewitness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word is an interjection meaning "behold," "lo," "see," or "here is."

• It is used to assert the existence, presence, or sudden appearance of a person or thing.

• When joined with pronominal suffixes (such as hineni), it translates as "Here I am," expressing complete availability, focus, and submission to a superior, particularly when an individual answers God's call.

• It serves to arrest attention, focus the mind, introduce a new and significant fact, and highlight a vital turning point in a narrative.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• hinneh occurs over a thousand times in the Old Testament, demonstrating its vital role in Hebrew storytelling and poetry.

• It frequently introduces dreams or prophetic visions, signaling that the imagery about to be described carries deep spiritual weight.

• The word is often used in sudden, surprising discoveries, such as waking up to find an unexpected reality or stumbling upon a vital resource in the wilderness.

• It acts as a literary exclamation point. Rather than simply stating facts chronologically, biblical writers used this word to disrupt the flow of the text, demanding that the audience stop and ponder the spiritual import of the specific moment.

4. Theological Implications

• hinneh is deeply tied to the doctrine of divine providence. When human beings act or plan, and hinneh introduces the next event, it reveals that what appears to be a human coincidence is actually an orchestrated, sovereign act of God.

• It highlights God's active, immediate involvement in the ordinary, mundane aspects of life.

• The word underscores the suddenness of divine intervention. When God acts, He often does so in a way that catches human onlookers by surprise, prompting the exclamation of "behold!"

• It calls for human attentiveness. When God commands His people to "behold," He is calling them to look away from their anxieties and focus entirely on His character, His promises, and His incoming deliverance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:4, the narrative shifts from Ruth’s perspective of "happening" to glean in the field of Boaz to the grander, providential reality. The phrase "Now behold, Boaz came from Bethlehem" marks the precise moment God's grace begins to manifest visibly in Ruth's life.

• The arrival of Boaz is not a mere chronological detail; it is the climax of Ruth's desperate search for favor. Just as Ruth begins her hard labor, the master of the field—the worthy man and potential kinsman-redeemer—arrives on the scene.

• The use of hinneh here invites the reader to marvel at God's perfect timing. It highlights the transition from Ruth's vulnerability to the security she will find under Boaz’s protection, framing Boaz's entrance as the answer to Naomi's empty grief.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 28:12 - "Then he dreamed, and behold, a ladder was set up on the earth, and its top reached to heaven; and there the angels of God were ascending and descending on it."

• Genesis 29:25 - "So it came to pass in the morning, that behold, it was Leah. And he said to Laban, 'What is this you have done to me? Was it not for Rachel that I served you? Why then have you deceived me?'"

• Isaiah 7:14 - "Therefore the Lord Himself will give you a sign: Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a Son, and shall call His name Immanuel."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• hinneh shifts the narrative perspective from a detached, third-person overview to the subjective, eye-level view of a character in the story. In Ruth 2:4, it places the reader right there in the dusty field with the harvesters, watching Boaz crest the hill from Bethlehem.

• The immediate dialogue following this "behold" reveals the character of the man who has just arrived. Boaz greets his workers with "The LORD be with you!" and they reply, "The LORD bless you!" The dramatic introduction of Boaz via hinneh is immediately validated by his godly leadership, confirming to the reader that he is indeed the provider Ruth needs.
