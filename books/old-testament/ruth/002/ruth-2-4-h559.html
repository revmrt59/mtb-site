SAID - ( amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, primarily meaning "to say," "speak," "tell," or "command." In Ruth 2:4, it introduces Boaz’s warm, faith-filled greeting to his harvest workers, revealing his character, spiritual leadership, and the godly atmosphere of his estate. This simple word of speech serves as the catalyst for a relationship defined by covenant loyalty (hesed) and divine providence, showcasing how godly language fosters a righteous community.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb amar is a fundamental Semitic root found across ancient Near Eastern languages, serving as a primary verb of communication. Unlike words that emphasize the physical act of making a sound or the heavy declaration of a formal message, amar focuses on the expression of thoughts, intentions, and the establishment of relationship through spoken words. In the ancient agrarian world, the speech of a landowner or master set the entire tone of the household and estate. Boaz's speech immediately distinguishes him as a righteous man of standing who uses his voice not to demand, exploit, or demean, but to bless and acknowledge his workers under the covenant of Yahweh.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb amar carries a broad semantic range depending on its grammatical stem and context:

• To say, speak, or utter a statement.

• To think or say in one's heart (when followed by "in the heart").

• To command, declare, or decree (often used of royal or divine commands).

• To promise, intend, or name.

In the Qal stem, which is used in Ruth 2:4, it denotes the direct act of speaking to someone. It is a highly relational verb that connects the speaker to the listener, establishing a bond of communication and mutual recognition.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Creation and Power: In the opening chapter of Genesis, amar is the primary vehicle for divine revelation and creation ("And God said..."). This associates the word with creative power, authority, and life-giving order.

• The Mirror of the Heart: The word is used throughout Hebrew narrative to reveal the inner life of characters. In biblical Hebrew thought, what a person "says" reflects the abundance and moral condition of their heart.

• Reciprocal Community: In household or administrative settings, when a master "says" something to his servants, it typically carries the weight of a unilateral direction. In Ruth 2:4, however, the word introduces a reciprocal blessing, creating a word picture of a harmonious, God-fearing community working together under a benevolent provider.

4. Theological Implications

• The Weight of Words: In Hebrew thought, words are not merely empty sounds; they carry substance, intent, and active force. When Boaz speaks a blessing, he is actively invoking the presence and favor of Yahweh over his workers.

• Living the Covenant: The dialogue introduced by amar in Ruth 2:4 showcases how the Law of Moses is lived out practically. Instead of tension between labor and capital, there is mutual respect and shared devotion to Yahweh, illustrating the beauty of a community yielded to God's covenant.

• Reflection of Divine Character: Just as God speaks (amar) to bless, guide, and covenant with His people, Boaz—acting as a type of the ultimate Redeemer—uses his speech to initiate a relationship of grace, protection, and provision.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:4, amar is used twice: once for Boaz's greeting ("said to the reapers") and once for their response ("And they answered [literally, 'said' to] him").

• This exchange of blessing ("The LORD be with you!" and "The LORD bless you!") stands in stark contrast to the dark spiritual climate of the period of the Judges, characterized by moral chaos, spiritual decay, and self-interest.

• The context of amar here shows that Boaz's first recorded words in the narrative are words of blessing and faith. This immediately establishes his character as a worthy man of wealth (gibbor chayil) who honors God in his daily work. It sets the stage for how he will speak to and protect Ruth later in the chapter.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3 - "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light." (The prime example of amar as the vehicle of divine, creative command).

• Genesis 22:1 - "Now it came to pass after these things that God tested Abraham, and said to him, 'Abraham!' And he said, 'Here I am.'" (Demonstrates the relational, testing dialogue between God and His servant).

• Exodus 3:14 - "And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And He said, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, "I AM has sent me to you."'" (Multiple uses of amar emphasizing divine self-revelation and commissioned speaking).

• 1 Samuel 3:9 - "Therefore Eli said to Samuel, 'Go, lie down; and it shall be, if He calls you, that you must say, "Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears."'" (Instruction on responsive speech in the presence of God).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between amar (to say/speak) and dabar (to speak/word) is worth noting. While dabar often emphasizes the substance, matter, or objective message of the speech, amar focuses on the personal act of saying and the direct address to another.

• In the narrative structure of Ruth, dialogue is highly crafted and dominant. Over fifty percent of the Book of Ruth consists of direct speech introduced by amar. This literary device draws the reader directly into the relationships, motives, and hearts of the characters, making the unfolding story of redemption feel intimate and immediate.
