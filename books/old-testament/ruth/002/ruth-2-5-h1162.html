BOAZ - ( Bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

Boaz is a prominent Bethlehemite of the clan of Elimelech, whose name historically denotes "in strength" or "fleetness." In the narrative of Ruth, Boaz serves as the quintessential "kinsman-redeemer" (goel), representing Christ's redeeming love, provision, and strength. In Ruth 2:5, his introduction to the scene marks a pivotal shift from Naomi and Ruth's emptiness to Yahweh's providential fullness, as this "man of great wealth" steps forward to show grace to a foreign widow.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The historical and cultural backdrop of Ruth 2:5 is set during the dark and chaotic era of the Judges, a time characterized by moral decay and spiritual compromise. Against this bleak horizon, Boaz emerges as a breath of fresh air—a man of integrity, godliness, and immense responsibility. When he enters his harvest field in Bethlehem, his first words to his workers are a blessing ("The LORD be with you!"), and they respond in kind. Boaz is introduced as a gibbor hayil, a phrase often translated as "a man of great wealth," but one that also carries the connotation of valor, noble character, and capability. He represents the ideal covenant-keeping Israelite who protects the vulnerable, respects the Torah's provisions for the poor, and acts with deliberate, quiet strength.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun Bo'az is derived from the prepositional prefix be ("in") and the noun oz ("strength"), yielding the primary meaning of "in him is strength" or "by strength." An alternative, less common etymological suggestion links the name to an Arabic root meaning "fleetness" or "alacrity." In the biblical text, the name represents both the historical person of Bethlehem who marries Ruth and the northern bronze pillar erected at the vestibule of Solomon's temple, symbolizing the enduring strength of Yahweh's covenant with the house of David.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Book of Ruth, Boaz acts as a vivid word picture of the Goel, the Kinsman-Redeemer. Under Mosaic law, the kinsman-redeemer was a close relative who had the right and responsibility to buy back land sold during times of poverty, redeem relatives sold into slavery, and marry the widow of a deceased relative to preserve the family line.

• The Shielding Wing: In Ruth 2:12, Boaz prays that Ruth will receive a full reward from the Lord God of Israel, "under whose wings you have come for refuge." Later, in Ruth 3:9, Ruth asks Boaz to "spread your wing over your maidservant, for you are a close relative." The word picture comes full circle: Boaz becomes the hands and feet of God, physically extending his own cloak (wing) to shelter and redeem her.

• The Temple Pillar: Centuries later, when Solomon builds the temple, he names one of the two massive bronze pillars "Boaz" (1 Kings 7:21). Positioned alongside "Jachin" ("He will establish"), the naming of Boaz declares that God's house is "established in strength," a beautiful retrospective nod to the Bethlehemite through whom the Davidic dynasty was anchored.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Boaz serves as one of the Old Testament's most beautiful types of Jesus Christ.

• He is the Redeemer who possesses the necessary resources: Boaz was a man of great wealth; Christ possesses the unsearchable riches of grace.

• He is the Redeemer who has the right to redeem: Boaz was a near kinsman to Elimelech; Christ became a man, taking on flesh and blood, so that He might be our elder brother and near kinsman (Hebrews 2:11-17).

• He is the Redeemer who has the willingness to redeem: Boaz acted out of love and covenant faithfulness (hesed) rather than mere duty, just as Christ willingly laid down His life to redeem His bride, the Church.

• He is the Redeemer of the Gentile: Boaz gladly brought Ruth, a Moabitess and a stranger to the covenant, into the family of God, foreshadowing the inclusion of the Gentiles into the household of faith.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:5, Boaz asks his servant in charge of the reapers, "Whose young woman is this?" Coming immediately after Ruth "happened" to glean in his field, Boaz's arrival and subsequent question highlight the unseen hand of God's providence. This inquiry is not born of cold suspicion, but of careful, compassionate stewardship. As the master of the estate, he notices the new, vulnerable face among the gleaners. His question initiates a sequence of grace, showing that he does not view the poor as invisible nuisances, but as individuals worthy of notice and protection. His strength is immediately channeled into hospitality and safety for the foreign widow.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The biological lineage of Boaz provides profound insight into his character. According to Matthew 1:5, Boaz's mother was Rahab, the Canaanite harlot of Jericho who was saved by faith. Having grown up with a mother who was a foreign outsider redeemed by the grace of Yahweh, Boaz would have possessed a unique, firsthand empathy for Ruth. He understood that faith in Yahweh transcends national boundaries, making him uniquely prepared by God to welcome, protect, and ultimately marry the Moabitess Ruth.
