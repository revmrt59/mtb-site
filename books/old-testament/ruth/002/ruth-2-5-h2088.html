THIS - ( zeh , Strong's H2088 )

Summary:

The Hebrew demonstrative pronoun zeh (with its feminine form zot appearing in Ruth 2:5) functions as a direct grammatical pointer to identify a specific person, place, or object in immediate proximity. In the book of Ruth, this word is far more than a spatial locator; it serves as a critical literary and theological hinge. When Boaz asks, "Whose young woman is this?", the pronoun singles Ruth out from an anonymous crowd of gleaners. This moment of recognition initiates her journey from a marginalized, foreign outsider to a protected insider, illustrating how God's particular grace rescues the vulnerable and places them into the lineage of redemption.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The pronoun zeh (along with its feminine counterpart zot and plural elleh) operates like a pointing finger in biblical Hebrew. In ancient Near Eastern culture, which was highly relational and oral, using a demonstrative pronoun drew concrete, sensory attention to something physically present or recently spoken of. It establishes a boundary between what is distant and what is intimately close. In biblical narratives, it frequently marks a sudden shift from general observation to highly focused, personal engagement, turning an abstract subject into an immediate reality.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The pronoun designates proximity, presence, and specificity.

• As a demonstrative adjective, it modifies a definite noun (e.g., "this young woman") and agrees in gender and number, intensifying the focus on that specific object.

• As a demonstrative pronoun, it stands alone to mean "this one" or "this person."

• It denotes immediate spatial or temporal presence, demanding that the listener recognize, decide upon, or react to the subject being pointed out.

• Depending on the narrative context, it can convey deep honor and choice, or it can be used pejoratively to indicate distance or contempt (e.g., "this fellow").

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, zeh is used to ground spiritual truths in physical realities.

• It identifies sacred space and encounters, such as Jacob's exclamation at Bethel: "How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of God" (Genesis 28:17).

• It establishes covenantal memorial times, marking specific days of deliverance to be observed by future generations.

• It highlights personal intimacy and chosenness, as seen in the bride's declaration: "This is my beloved, and this is my friend" (Song of Solomon 5:16).

• Physically pointing to an individual with zeh acts as a literary spotlight, pulling a single character out of the background of a crowded scene to make them the focus of the reader's attention.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, zeh underscores the highly personal, particular nature of God's providence. God does not deal with humanity merely in vague, corporate generalities; He acts toward specific people in precise times and places.

• Covenantal Specificity: God's redemptive actions are aimed at "this" land, "this" covenant people, and "this" family line.

• Election and Grace: The transition of a person from being unnoticed to being singled out as "this one" reflects the mechanics of divine grace. Ruth was a destitute Moabitess, socially and legally invisible. When she is singled out, it mirrors how God beholds, chooses, and elevates the lowly.

• The Particularity of Redemption: Salvation history is built on specific choices. The spotlight on "this young woman" eventually leads to the birth of David, and ultimately to the presentation of the Messiah: "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:17).

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:5, Boaz arrives at his field from Bethlehem and asks his foreman, "Whose young woman is this (zot)?"

• Ruth has arrived in Bethlehem as an impoverished foreigner, seeking survival by gleaning behind the reapers. To the laborers, she is simply another nameless, desperate widow clinging to the margins of the field.

• Boaz's inquiry immediately changes her status. The pronoun zot indicates physical proximity; she is close enough to be pointed out directly.

• By asking "Whose young woman is this?", Boaz is not merely asking for her identity, but is inquiring about her legal status, her family connections, and her place of belonging.

• This simple word marks the precise moment of transition in the story. Ruth is pulled from anonymity into the light of Boaz's favor, setting in motion the entire process of her legal and relational redemption.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• Grammatical Concord: In Hebrew, when zeh (or zot) modifies a definite noun, it must also take the definite article (ha-zot), literally translating to "the young woman, the this-one." This double determination intensifies the specificity, leaving no ambiguity about which person is being identified.

• Literary Contrast: The book of Ruth operates on deliberate contrasts. While Boaz immediately notices Ruth and asks about "this" specific woman, the closer relative in Ruth 4:1 cannot even bring himself to say her name, addressing Boaz's kinsman as "such a one." Boaz sees Ruth as a highly valued individual, while others see her only as an anonymous legal complication.
