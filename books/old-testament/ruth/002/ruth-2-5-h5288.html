SERVANT - ( na'ar , Strong's H5288 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'ar designates a young man, youth, or subordinate personal attendant. In the narrative of Ruth 2:5, it refers specifically to the foreman overseeing Boaz's harvest workers. This term highlights both the youth and physical vigor required for field management, as well as the position of trust and stewardship granted to him within the estate.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun na'ar is deeply rooted in the developmental stages of life and the social hierarchies of the ancient Near East. It derives from a root associated with youth, early life, or the act of shaking off. The word picture portrays someone who has progressed past early childhood but has not yet attained the status of an independent family patriarch or elder.

Historically, this word was used to describe individuals characterized by physical strength, adaptability, and dependency on a higher authority. Within ancient households and military structures, a na'ar was frequently an active agent—such as a personal squire, an armor-bearer, a domestic servant, or an agricultural foreman. This position required vigor, reliability, and close alignment with the master's interests.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term signifies a boy, youth, young man, or servant. Its semantic range is broad, defined primarily by age and social status:

• An infant or young child: Denoting a male child from infancy up to early childhood.

• A youth or young man: Describing an unmarried male who has reached puberty but is not yet fully recognized as a tribal head or elder.

• A personal attendant or servant: Indicating a subordinate assistant, steward, or deputy who acts on behalf of a master, regardless of his exact chronological age.

The term represents status and relationship as much as physical age. When used in a domestic or agricultural context, it designates an authorized subordinate who carries out the direct instructions of a superior.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Scriptures, na'ar is employed in several distinct ways to illustrate various roles of service and youth:

• The dependent youth: Used for Isaac during the binding of Isaac, and for the young Joseph when he was feeding the flock with his brothers.

• The trusted military assistant: Applied to Jonathan's armor-bearer, who acted with immense courage and absolute loyalty to his master.

• The domestic steward: Used for Ziba, the servant of the house of Saul, who managed substantial properties and agricultural assets.

• The infant: Used to describe the three-month-old baby Moses weeping in the basket among the reeds.

### 4. Theological Implications

The usage of na'ar underscores the biblical theme of stewardship and delegated authority. In the economy of God's covenant community, authority is not concentrated solely in one individual; it is distributed through relationships of trust. The na'ar represents the hands and eyes of the master in the field, illustrating how God delegates earthly responsibilities to human agents.

Furthermore, this word shows that God frequently uses the young, the subordinate, or those in positions of service to accomplish His grand purposes. The servant's faithfulness in small, daily administrative tasks (such as managing a harvest field) sets the stage for God's larger redemptive plans—in this case, bringing Ruth and Boaz together, which ultimately preserves the messianic lineage.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:5, the na'ar is the supervisor "who was in charge of the reapers." In this context, the term denotes a highly responsible foreman rather than a menial domestic slave. Boaz does not address a nameless laborer; he speaks to his trusted manager to inquire about the identity of the young woman gleaning in the field.

The response of this na'ar in verses 6-7 reveals his observational skills, fair-mindedness, and respect for Ruth. He recognizes her connection to Naomi, details her request to glean, and commends her extraordinary work ethic. The character of this servant reflects the righteous, orderly, and compassionate household of Boaz himself. It shows that under a godly master, even the subordinates act with dignity and kindness toward the marginalized.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

It is valuable to contrast na'ar with ebed (the more common Hebrew word for servant or slave). While ebed often emphasizes the legal status of servitude, subjection, or lifelong bondservice, na'ar retains a sense of youthfulness, vigor, and potential for growth. A na'ar is frequently positioned to learn from the master, functioning in an apprenticeship role. The relationship between Elisha and his na'ar Gehazi, or Joshua acting as Moses' na'ar (Exodus 33:11), demonstrates that this role was a stepping stone to future leadership, emphasizing mentorship and spiritual development.
