YOUNG WOMAN - ( na'arah , Strong's H5291 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'arah refers to a young, unmarried woman of marriageable age, a maiden, or a young female servant. In the context of Ruth 2:5, it denotes Ruth’s status as an unattached, vulnerable foreigner. Boaz’s question, "Whose young woman is this?" is a query about her social and legal alignment, initiating her journey from a defenseless outsider to a protected member of his household, reflecting God's grace to the needy.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The term na'arah is the feminine counterpart to the masculine na'ar, which means a boy, youth, or young servant. The word carries a dual sense of age and social status. It designates a female who has reached physical maturity but is not yet settled as a mother or matriarch of her own household. Historically, a na'arah lived under the authority and protection of her father. If she was widowed without children and left without patriarchal protection, she occupied a highly vulnerable position in ancient Israelite society. The word evokes a picture of youth, physical capability (often associated with working in fields or fetching water), and social dependency.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of na'arah includes:

• A young woman of marriageable age.

• A newly married young woman.

• A girl or maiden.

• A female servant or personal maid.

The core of the word is not strictly biological but social. It describes a transition stage of life where a young female is active and capable, yet relies on the protection of a male figure (father, husband, or master) to navigate the community safely.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, na'arah is used to paint vivid pictures of young women in transitional or vulnerable moments:

• Rebekah at the Well: She is described as a na'arah when Abraham’s servant meets her. She is beautiful, energetic, and helpful, drawing water for the camels—a picture of industrious youth.

• Dinah: She is called a na'arah when Shechem falls in love with her, highlighting her vulnerability and the protective outrage of her brothers.

• Pharaoh’s Daughter’s Maidens: The attendants who walked along the Nile are na'arot (plural), illustrating the term’s application to young female servants.

• In all these cases, the term carries an expectation of protection, virtue, and social custody.

### 4. Theological Implications

The usage of na'arah in the Old Testament underscores the vulnerability of young women in a fallen world and God's specialized care for them. Laws regarding the na'arah in the Torah protect her from exploitation, rape, and economic ruin. Theologically, the na'arah represents those who lack institutional power. When God steps in to redeem a na'arah—as He does with Ruth—it demonstrates His character as the defender of the weak. This foreshadows the gospel, where Christ searches out, protects, and redeems those who are spiritually vulnerable and without a covering.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:5, Boaz asks his foreman, "Whose young woman is this?" Boaz does not merely ask "Who is she?" but "To whom does she belong?" In the ancient Near East, an unattached woman gleaning in a harvest field was exposed to severe danger, harassment, and assault. Boaz's inquiry is a protective and administrative one. By identifying her as a na'arah, the text sets up the contrast between her current state of vulnerability and her future state of security. When the servant responds in the next verse that she is the "Moabite young woman," her extreme vulnerability is exposed: she is young, widowed, poor, and a foreigner. Boaz's response to this na'arah is a beautiful picture of grace, as he immediately extends his wings of protection over her.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 24:16 - "Now the young woman was very beautiful to behold, a virgin; no man had known her. And she went down to the well, filled her pitcher, and came up."

• Genesis 34:3 - "His soul clung to Dinah the daughter of Jacob, and he loved the young woman and spoke kindly to the young woman."

• Ruth 2:6 - "So the servant who was in charge of the reapers answered and said, 'It is the Moabite young woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

It is valuable to contrast na'arah with other Hebrew words for women. While almah refers specifically to a young woman of childbearing age (with an emphasis on her youth and potential virginity) and bethulah refers strictly to a virgin in a physiological sense, na'arah is broader. Ruth is no longer a virgin (bethulah) because she had been married to Mahlon, yet she is still called a na'arah. This proves that the term emphasizes her youthful status and her need for family integration and protection rather than her physical history.
