SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar refers to the act of speaking, declaring, or commanding, representing the external expression of one's internal thoughts and intentions. In Ruth 2:5, this word initiates the vital encounter between Boaz and his foreman regarding Ruth. Rather than a casual inquiry, Boaz’s spoken words reveal his active, hands-on oversight of his estate and his immediate attentiveness to the vulnerable stranger gleaning in his field, ultimately setting the stage for covenantal redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root 'amar is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Hebrew Bible. At its most basic level, it describes the outward expression of an inner thought or intent. Unlike other Hebrew words for speaking that focus on the physical mechanics of speech or the formal proclamation of a message, 'amar focuses on the relational and volitional nature of communication. It is the primary vehicle through which a person's character, desires, and identity are made known to others. In the agrarian context of ancient Israel, the spoken word of a landowner carried legal weight, protective power, and moral authority. When Boaz arrives at his field and speaks, his words establish the tone and boundary of his entire estate.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary lexical definition of 'amar is to say, speak, utter, command, or think. The verb possesses a broad semantic range that depends heavily on context:

• To declare or state information directly to an auditor.

• To command or instruct subordinates, where the saying carries the force of an order.

• To intend or "say in one's heart," which denotes thinking, planning, or reflecting internally.

• To call or name, designating the identity or status of a person or object.

In Ruth 2:5, the verb is used in the Qal imperfect with a waw consecutive, functioning as a narrative past-tense declaration ("Then Boaz said"). It denotes a direct, verbal inquiry addressed to his foreman.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The usage of 'amar spans several vital categories in the Old Testament:

• Divine Decree: The word is the instrument of creation in Genesis 1, where God speaks ('amar) light and life into existence. His saying is an act of sovereign power that creates reality.

• Internal Deliberation: Characters often "say in their heart," indicating their secret motivations and plans.

• Relational Connection: It is the standard term used to initiate dialogue, showing that Hebrew narrative progresses primarily through direct speech rather than passive description.

In the Book of Ruth, 'amar is used repeatedly to drive the plot forward. The dialogue between characters—what they say to one another—unveils their righteousness, loyalty (chesed), and faith in Yahweh.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'amar highlights the power of the spoken word to reflect the heart of the speaker. Just as God's words reveal His holy character and creative power, a human being's words reveal their spiritual state. Boaz’s first recorded words in the narrative are a blessing to his reapers, and his immediate next words are an inquiry about the identity of the young woman. This reveals a theology of active compassion. Speech in the ancient world was a tool of power; Boaz uses his speech not to exploit or dismiss, but to inquire, protect, and bless. The use of this verb underscores that redemption begins with active, caring communication.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:5, the context is highly specific. Boaz has just arrived from Bethlehem, greeted his workers, and noticed a new face among the gleaners behind the reapers. When Boaz says to his servant, "Whose young woman is this?", his speech is both protective and legal. As the owner of the field and a "man of great wealth" (Ruth 2:1), he has a legal obligation under the Torah to allow the poor to glean, but he also possesses the authority to protect them from harassment. His question is not born of suspicion but of observant care. By speaking, Boaz initiates a chain of redemption that transitions Ruth from a foreign outsider to a protected guest, and eventually to his wife.

7. Other Relevant Insights

For the pastor and teacher, 'amar in this passage illustrates the power of attentive leadership. Boaz does not remain isolated in his wealth; he visits his workers and speaks to them directly. His verbal engagement with his foreman is an act of spiritual and physical oversight. It reminds us that godly leaders use their voice to inquire about the vulnerable, to establish safety, and to set a culture of dignity within their sphere of influence.
