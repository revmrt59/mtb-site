MOAB - ( Mo'ab , Strong's H4124 )

Summary:

The name "Moab" refers both to the historical progenitor of the Moabite people—the son of Lot's incestuous union with his eldest daughter—and the geographic region east of the Dead Sea where his descendants settled. In the biblical narrative, Moab represents a complex spiritual and historical reality: a nation born of dysfunction, a frequent adversary of Israel, and a place of pagan idolatry. Yet, in the book of Ruth, "the country of Moab" serves as the backdrop of sovereign redemption. It is the place of exile where Elimelech's family sought temporary physical relief but found tragedy, and it is the origin of Ruth, the Moabitess, who would forsake her homeland to seek refuge under the wings of the God of Israel, ultimately entering the lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The origin of the name Moab is rooted in the tragic and shameful aftermath of the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah. Sheltered in a cave, Lot’s oldest daughter conspired to preserve their family line through her father. The resulting son was named Mo'ab, a name that serves as a permanent linguistic and historical marker of this incestuous origin.

Geographically, Moab was a fertile plateau situated east of the Dead Sea, characterized by rich pastureland and natural mountainous defenses. This high tableland, bounded by the Arnon gorge to the north and the Zered brook to the south, enjoyed more stable rainfall than the hill country of Judah. This environmental reality made it a tempting destination for Israelites seeking relief during times of famine, despite the spiritual dangers it posed.

Historically and spiritually, Moab was a constant source of trouble for Israel. The Moabites hired Balaam to curse Israel, seduced Israelite men into idolatry and sexual immorality at Shittim, and oppressed Israel during the era of the Judges. Because of their hostility, God commanded that no Moabite could enter the assembly of the Lord to the tenth generation. Thus, Moab represents a place of physical abundance but deep spiritual hazard.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun Mo'ab is derived from a combination of the prepositional prefix mi (from) and the noun ab (father), literally translating to "from a father" or "of a father."

The semantic range of the term includes:

• The individual patriarch, the son of Lot.

• The ethnic group descended from him (the Moabites).

• The territory they inhabited, frequently referred to in the Hebrew text as the "field of Moab" (sedeh mo'ab) or the "country of Moab."

In Ruth 2:6, the word designates the geographical region (misdeh mo'ab—"from the country of Moab") from which Ruth and Naomi returned to Bethlehem.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, Moab is used as a multifaceted symbol:

• A Place of Compromise: In the historical books, turning to the "fields of Moab" represents leaving the place of God's covenant blessing (the Promised Land) to seek worldly security. Elimelech's move to Moab is a picture of choosing sight over faith, trading the spiritual inheritance of Bethlehem for the physical survival of a pagan land.

• Complacency and Judgment: In prophetic literature, Moab symbolizes arrogant security. Because they lived in a well-protected, fertile region, they became complacent. Jeremiah compares Moab to wine that has settled on its dregs, never poured from vessel to vessel, remaining tasteless and proud until judgment comes.

• A Catalyst for Grace: In the narrative of Ruth, Moab is the dark canvas upon which God paints His brightest masterpiece of redeeming love. It is the place of death and loss that prepares Naomi and Ruth to receive the fullness of God's restorative chesed (lovingkindness) back in Bethlehem.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of Moab in Ruth 2:6 lies in the shocking reality of sovereign grace. The law of Deuteronomy 23:3 explicitly excluded Moabites from the congregation of Israel. This exclusion was not merely ethnic; it was spiritual, aimed at protecting Israel from the abominable worship of Chemosh, the chief deity of Moab, whose worship included child sacrifice.

When Ruth, a woman of Moab, is brought into the covenant community of Israel, it demonstrates that God’s redemptive grace is greater than the legal barriers of the law and the shameful heritage of her ancestry. Her inclusion in the lineage of David and ultimately Jesus Christ shows that faith in Yahweh transcends national, cultural, and historical barriers. God delights in taking those from "Moab"—the place of shame, idolatry, and exclusion—and making them central to His plan of salvation.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:6, the servant in charge of the reapers identifies Ruth to Boaz: "It is the Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab."

The repetition of "Moabite" and "Moab" in this single verse is highly intentional. To the workers in the field and the community of Bethlehem, Ruth’s identity was entirely bound up in her origin. She was "the outsider," the representative of a historic enemy, and a reminder of the pagan territory where Naomi's family suffered ruin.

The servant’s description carries a tone of caution, highlighting her vulnerability and low social standing. However, this heavy emphasis on her Moabite origin sets up the dramatic contrast of what follows. Boaz does not see a cursed Moabite; he sees a virtuous woman who has taken refuge under the wings of the God of Israel. The very label that marked her for exclusion becomes the background that highlights Boaz’s grace and foreshadows her inclusion in Israel's royal line.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The tension between Israel and Moab is further illuminated by archaeological discovery. The Mesha Stele (also known as the Moabite Stone), dating to approximately 840 BC, contains an inscription by King Mesha of Moab. Written in a language closely related to biblical Hebrew, it details Moab's subjugation by Israel under King Omri and credits their god Chemosh with their eventual liberation. This historical artifact confirms the distinct national and religious identity of Moab as a powerful, organized kingdom that stood in direct opposition to Israel and Yahweh, underscoring just how radical Ruth's conversion and acceptance truly was.
