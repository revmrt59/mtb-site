SERVANT - ( na'ar , Strong's H5288 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'ar primarily denotes a young man, youth, or servant, reflecting both an age group (from infancy to early manhood) and a social status of subordination or service. In Ruth 2:6, it designates the young man placed in charge of Boaz's reapers. This term highlights not only his youth and vigor but also his trusted position of oversight within Boaz's estate. His testimony regarding Ruth's industriousness and character serves as a pivotal bridge that introduces Ruth's virtue to Boaz, illustrating how God uses ordinary, faithful servants in subordinate roles to advance His providential purposes.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite society, a na'ar occupied a unique social space. The term derives from a root associated with youth and status, describing a male from infancy to marriageable age who is not yet the head of his own household. Culturally, it denotes a position of dependence, helper, or apprentice. In domestic, agricultural, and military settings, a na'ar was a personal assistant, armor-bearer, or supervisor of field labor. The word picture is one of youthful strength, adaptability, and active service. While the word can refer to a very young child, when applied to a servant in a wealthy household like Boaz's, it depicts a capable young man entrusted with operational leadership over other workers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun na'ar refers to a boy, youth, young man, or servant. It has a broad semantic range that is defined by context rather than a rigid age limit:

• A male infant or child, emphasizing helplessness or early development.

• An adolescent or young unmarried man, emphasizing youth, strength, and life stage.

• A personal attendant, squire, or domestic servant, emphasizing functional subordination and loyalty to a superior, regardless of chronological age.

• A young warrior or military assistant, emphasizing vigor and readiness for combat.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term is utilized across the Old Testament to highlight different facets of youth and service:

• Youthful innocence and development: Used of the infant Moses weeping in the basket, highlighting his vulnerability, and of the young child Samuel serving in the tabernacle.

• Dependence and apprenticeship: Used of Joshua during his years of preparation under Moses, demonstrating that service is the pathway to spiritual maturity.

• Loyalty and practical assistance: Used of personal attendants, such as Jonathan’s armor-bearer or Elisha’s servant Gehazi, who acted as extensions of their masters' authority.

• Stewardship: In agricultural contexts, it depicts a trusted agent who manages resources, coordinates laborers, and protects the master's interests.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of the na'ar in the narrative of Ruth illustrates the doctrine of divine providence operating through ordinary, quiet faithfulness. God frequently uses nameless or socially subordinate individuals to accomplish key turning points in redemptive history. The servant in Ruth 2:6 does not possess a grand title, yet his fair-mindedness and accurate reporting of Ruth’s diligence protect her and draw Boaz's favorable attention to her. This underscores the theological truth that every level of stewardship matters in the kingdom of God. Furthermore, the servant's kindness reflects the character of his master, Boaz, showing how a godly leader's integrity influences his entire household.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:6, the word is used in the construct state to describe "the servant who was in charge of the reapers." This specific na'ar has been elevated to a position of foreman. When Boaz asks about the identity of the young woman gleaning in his field, this servant provides an objective, highly favorable character reference. He notes her origin ("the Moabite woman who came back with Naomi") and her work ethic ("she has continued from morning until now"). The servant's willingness to allow her to rest briefly in the shelter and his positive report to Boaz show that he was not abusive or suspicious, but rather shared the generous, covenant-keeping spirit of Boaz. The term here highlights a young man of character who uses his delegated authority with justice and mercy.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:5: "And Abraham said to his young men, 'Stay here with the donkey; the lad and I will go yonder and worship, and we will come back to you.'" (Here, "lad" refers to Isaac as a young man).

• Exodus 33:11: "So the Lord spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend. And he would return to the camp, but his servant Joshua the son of Nun, a young man, did not depart from the tabernacle." (Here, "young man" highlights Joshua's status as a devoted assistant).

• 1 Samuel 3:1: "Now the boy Samuel ministered to the Lord before Eli. And the word of the Lord was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation." (Here, "boy" indicates Samuel’s youth during his early priestly service).

• 2 Kings 4:12: "Then he said to Gehazi his servant, 'Call this Shunammite.' When he had called her, she stood before him." (Here, "servant" denotes a personal domestic attendant).

7. Other Relevant Insights

A comparison between the na'ar (servant) and the shiphhah (maidservant) in the book of Ruth reveals the social hierarchy of the ancient near eastern estate. When Ruth refers to herself as a maidservant, she places herself below the status of Boaz's young men. The servant in charge of the reapers bridges this social gap by validating her presence, ensuring she is treated with dignity. Additionally, the transition of key biblical figures (such as Joseph, Joshua, David, and Samuel) from a na'ar to a national leader demonstrates that in God's economy, humble, obedient service in youth is the essential training ground for spiritual authority and leadership in maturity.
