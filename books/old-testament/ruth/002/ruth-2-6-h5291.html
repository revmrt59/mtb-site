YOUNG WOMAN - ( na'arah , Strong's H5291 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'arah designates a young, marriageable woman, highlighting her youth, vitality, dependency, or status as a maiden or servant. In the book of Ruth, particularly in Ruth 2:6, it identifies Ruth not merely by her gender, but by her stage of life, vulnerability, and foreign origin as a "young Moabite woman." This term underscores both her need for protection in a male-dominated agrarian society and her potential for marriage and redemption under Israel's covenantal laws.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word na'arah is the feminine counterpart to the masculine noun na'ar, which refers to a boy, youth, or young servant. Sociologically, a na'arah is a female who has reached physical maturity but has not yet fully transitioned into the established role of an ishah (wife or mature woman of a household). It depicts a person in a transitional phase of life, characterized by physical capability, beauty, and social dependency.

In ancient Near Eastern culture, a na'arah was typically under the authority and protection of either her father, a husband, or, in the case of a servant, a household master. The term brings to mind images of youthful energy, household service, or labor in the fields. However, because she lacked the protection of a husband or a father, a widowed na'arah like Ruth was highly vulnerable to exploitation, poverty, and physical danger.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term na'arah refers to a young woman, a maiden, or a young female servant. The semantic range of the word includes several specific nuances:

• A young, unmarried woman of marriageable age who is still living in her father's house.

• A young woman whose virginity and purity are highlighted as she prepares for marriage.

• A female attendant, maidservant, or domestic worker who serves a mistress or works under a supervisor.

• A female laborer or gleaner who works alongside male reapers during the harvest seasons.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, na'arah is used to paint pictures of youth, beauty, service, and vulnerability.

• As an image of youth and beauty: It is used to describe Rebekah when she first meets Abraham’s servant at the well, emphasizing her youth, physical capability to draw water, and her purity.

• As an image of service and royalty: It describes the maidens who attended Pharaoh’s daughter at the Nile and the young female attendants provided to Queen Esther in the Persian palace. Here, the word emphasizes their helper role and youth.

• As an image of vulnerability and labor: In harvest contexts, the plural form na'arot refers to the young female workers who gleaned behind the male reapers. These women were at the bottom of the social hierarchy, requiring protective boundaries established by the landowner.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the use of na'arah in the narrative of Ruth highlights God's profound care for the vulnerable and marginalized.

• God’s Law and the Marginalized: The Law of Moses contains specific provisions to protect the fatherless, the widow, and the stranger. Ruth embodies all three: she is a widow, a foreigner, and a young woman (na'arah) with no male advocate in Bethlehem until she meets Boaz. Her presence in the field tests the obedience of Israel to God's humanitarian laws.

• The Covenantal Plan of Redemption: By identifying Ruth as a na'arah, the text emphasizes her potential for marriage. She is not an aged widow like Naomi, whose childbearing years are past; she is a young woman who can still bear children to carry on the family name of Elimelech. This sets the stage for the kinsman-redeemer (goel) to step in, demonstrating God's covenant faithfulness in restoring broken families.

• Grace to the Lowly: God routinely bypasses the powerful and self-sufficient to work His redemption through the weak. Ruth is introduced at the lowest tier of society—a foreign, destitute na'arah gleaning in the dirt—yet she is chosen to become the great-grandmother of King David and an ancestor of Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:6, Boaz notices Ruth and asks his foreman, "Whose young woman is this?" The overseer replies, "It is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi."

The servant’s answer combines na'arah (young woman) with Moabiyyah (Moabite). This double identifier marks her social status. As a na'arah, she is young and capable of hard work, but also vulnerable. As a Moabite, she is an outsider.

The servant's description balances her foreign identity with her exceptional character. He notes her humility and industry, explaining that she has worked diligently from morning until now. This description prompts Boaz's protective and generous response. He recognizes her vulnerability as a na'arah and immediately instructs his young men not to touch her, while encouraging her to stay close to his own na'arot (young women) for safety.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A powerful contrast exists in the dialogue of Ruth 2. While the foreman and Boaz refer to Ruth as a na'arah (a young woman of status, albeit vulnerable), Ruth refers to herself in Ruth 2:13 as a shiphhah (a low-ranking maidservant or slave), noting that she does not even rise to the status of Boaz's own na'arot (young female workers). This linguistic contrast highlights Ruth's deep humility and Boaz's elevation of her dignity. Though she views herself as the lowest of the low, Boaz sees her as a young woman worthy of protection, respect, and ultimately, redemption.
