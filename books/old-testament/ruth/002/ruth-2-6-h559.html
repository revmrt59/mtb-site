SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, primarily meaning "to say," "to speak," "to command," or "to think." In Ruth 2:6, it introduces the direct response of Boaz's foreman, who identifies Ruth not merely by her physical appearance but by her foreign origin and her loyal association with Naomi. This word functions as a key narrative vehicle that drives the dialogue, unveils character motives, and advances the unfolding of God's providential care in the lives of Ruth and Naomi.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb ’amar is a primary Hebrew root found over 5,000 times in the Old Testament. Unlike dabar (which often emphasizes the formal act of speaking or the substance of the word spoken), 'amar focuses on the communication of thoughts, intentions, or direct discourse. It brings the inner thoughts of a person into the audibility of relationships. In the ancient Near Eastern context, oral communication was the primary means of establishing relationships, making covenants, and declaring status. The spoken word carried immense weight, embodying the character and authority of the speaker.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'amar is "to say," "to speak," or "to utter." Depending on the grammatical stem and context, its semantic range includes:

• To declare or proclaim.

• To command or bid (especially when spoken by a superior, such as a king or God).

• To think or say to oneself (when followed by the phrase "in his heart").

• To call or name.

In Ruth 2:6, it is used in the Qal imperfect with a waw consecutive (wayyo'mer), serving as the standard narrative past tense ("and he said") to introduce direct speech in response to a question.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The verb often functions as a bridge between internal intent and external reality. For example, when God "said" in Genesis, creation came into being.

• It is frequently paired with 'anah ("to answer") to denote responsive dialogue, as seen here in Ruth 2:6 ("answered and said"). This pairing emphasizes the relational and cooperative nature of human interaction under divine providence.

• When used of human speech in historical narratives, 'amar reveals the heart of the speaker. Because the mouth speaks out of the abundance of the heart, the verb exposes the speaker's underlying attitude toward others.

### 4. Theological Implications

• The use of 'amar underscores the personal and relational nature of the God of Israel. God is not a silent force but one who speaks, revealing His covenant will.

• In the book of Ruth, human speech often mirrors divine grace. Though God’s voice is not directly heard through prophets in this book, His will is worked out through the responsible, kind, and faithful words spoken by His people, such as Boaz, his servant, and Ruth.

• Words spoken in this narrative carry weight; they are not idle. They set in motion acts of redemption, protection, and covenant loyalty (chesed).

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:5, Boaz asks his servant, "Whose young woman is this?" The servant’s response in Ruth 2:6 uses 'amar to introduce a critical designation: "It is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi."

• The servant's speech is respectful, clear, and objective. By saying this, he immediately contextualizes Ruth’s identity for Boaz. He highlights her foreign origin ("Moabite") but also her loyalty ("who came back with Naomi").

• This verbal identification is pivotal. It bridges Ruth's vulnerability as a foreign gleaner with Boaz’s responsibility as a potential kinsman-redeemer. The servant’s words catalyze Boaz’s subsequent actions of extraordinary generosity and protection.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3 – "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Genesis 22:7 – "But Isaac spoke to Abraham his father and said, 'My father!' And he said, 'Here I am, my son.' Then he said, 'Look, the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?'"

• Exodus 3:14 – "And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And He said, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, "I AM has sent me to you."'"

• Ruth 2:2 – "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The repetition of dialogue in the Book of Ruth is a deliberate literary device. Over 50 percent of the book consists of direct speech introduced by 'amar. This highlights that the redemption of Naomi's family is accomplished not by silent fate, but through active, verbalized commitments and covenantal conversations.

• In Hebrew narrative, "answered and said" is a common idiom ('anah + 'amar) that signals the formal, significant nature of the reply. The servant was not merely chatting; he was giving an official report to his master regarding a newcomer in the field.
