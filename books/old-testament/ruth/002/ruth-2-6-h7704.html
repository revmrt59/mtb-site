COUNTRY - ( sadeh, Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open field, cultivated land, or a specific regional territory. In Ruth 2:6, it is translated as "country" and points to the foreign, pagan territory of Moab. The term serves as both a geographical and a spiritual boundary marker, contrasting the fields of famine and foreign exile with the fertile fields of Bethlehem, where Yahweh provides redemption, grace, and bread for His people.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun sadeh derives from an unused root meaning to spread out, paint, or level. It evokes the mental picture of an expansive, open space. Unlike a walled city (iyr) or a domestic pasture (migrash), sadeh represents the vast agricultural fields where crops are sown, the wild open country where beasts roam, or the broad plains where military battles are fought. In the ancient Near East, the "fields" or "country" of a nation defined its arable borders and regional sovereignty. To leave one's home city for the sadeh of a foreign nation meant stepping outside of familial protection, covenant boundaries, and familiar civil structures into a vulnerable, dependent existence.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term sadeh denotes open fields, wild country, cultivated agricultural land, or the territory and domain of a specific nation. It encompasses several distinct nuances depending on the context:

• Cultivated land: A specific plot of ground owned for agricultural purposes or crop production.

• The open field: Wild, uncultivated territory in contrast to walled towns, often associated with hunting or wild animals.

• Territory/Region: The sovereign borders or general geographic country of a specific group of people, such as the "country of Edom" or the "country of Moab." In this sense, it describes the entire regional landscape of a foreign nation.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sadeh is used to draw vivid theological contrasts:

• A Place of Labor and Vulnerability: The field is where mankind labors in the sweat of his brow. Cain slew Abel in the sadeh (Genesis 4:8), representing a place away from the protective eyes of the family unit.

• A Place of Wildness versus Cultivation: Esau is described as a "man of the field" (sadeh), highlighting his wild, nomadic, and uncultivated nature, in contrast to Jacob’s domestic lifestyle (Genesis 25:27).

• A Place of Divine Blessing or Judgment: Fertile fields are physical representations of God's covenant blessing, while dry, desolate, or devoured fields represent His divine judgment and the withdrawal of His favor.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of sadeh in the narrative of Ruth is found in the geography of redemption. Elimelech and his family abandoned the land of covenant promise during a famine to find sustenance in the sadeh of Moab. This movement represents a tragic irony: leaving the "House of Bread" (Bethlehem) to seek life in a foreign country known for idolatry and hostility toward Israel.

Instead of life, the "country of Moab" becomes a graveyard for Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion. The foreign field cannot sustain covenant life. Conversely, when Naomi and Ruth return to Bethlehem, Ruth immediately goes to work in another sadeh—the cultivated harvest field of Boaz. The transition from the foreign sadeh of spiritual death to the covenant sadeh of harvest and redemption highlights the sovereign grace of God. It shows that true provision is found not in fleeing God's territory, but in returning to His presence and placing oneself under His wings.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:6, the servant in charge of the reapers identifies Ruth to Boaz: "It is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab." Here, sadeh (translated as "country") functions as a social and spiritual label. It emphasizes Ruth’s status as an outsider, a destitute foreigner from a pagan territory.

By identifying her with the sadeh of Moab, the servant highlights the immense cultural and religious distance Ruth has traveled. Moab was a place of historical enmity with Israel. Yet, Ruth has forsaken that foreign country to glean in the fields of Judah. The contrast is stark: she who was defined by the distant, pagan "country of Moab" is now standing in the intimate, life-giving "field" of Boaz, poised to receive the covenant mercy of God.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew narrative, word repetitions are highly deliberate. The book of Ruth plays on the word sadeh to transition the reader's focus. In chapters 1 and 2, the scene shifts rapidly from the "country (sadeh) of Moab" where hope was lost, to the "field (sadeh) of Boaz" where hope is restored. Furthermore, the law of gleaning (Leviticus 19:9-10) commanded Israelite landowners not to reap the very corners of their sadeh, leaving grain for the poor and the stranger. Thus, the physical boundaries of the Israelite sadeh were designed by God to be instruments of charity, welcoming the needy foreigner who left their own country to seek refuge in Israel.
