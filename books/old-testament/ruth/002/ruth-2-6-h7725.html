CAME BACK - ( shuv , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shuv is a foundational covenantal term meaning to return, turn back, or repent. In Ruth 2:6, it is translated as "came back," highlighting Ruth's physical relocation and her deep spiritual alignment with the God of Israel. Although Ruth was a foreigner who had never previously lived in Bethlehem, her decision to accompany Naomi is described as a homecoming—a returning to the land of promise and the community of faith. This word underscores the themes of redemption, conversion, and the gracious scope of God's covenant family.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of shuv conveys a physical movement: to turn back, turn around, or reverse direction. It implies a departure from a current path and a deliberate return to a starting point, home, or original state. In the ancient world, returning home was not merely about changing locations; it was about restoring relationship, safety, and identity. Physically turning around serves as the primary Old Testament metaphor for spiritual repentance—turning away from sin and turning back to Yahweh.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shuv is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, occurring over 1,000 times. It possesses a broad semantic range:

• Physical movement: to go back, return to a place of origin, or go home.

• Relational restoration: to turn back to a person, reconcile, or be restored to fellowship.

• Spiritual conversion: to repent, turn away from evil, or return to covenant loyalty.

• In the Qal stem, as used in Ruth 2:6, the verb denotes the simple action of returning, coming back, or turning back.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The book of Ruth employs shuv as a vital thematic thread (a leitword), using it twelve times in the first chapter alone to trace the movement of Naomi and her daughters-in-law. Orpah chooses to "turn back" to her people and her false gods, while Ruth refuses to turn back from following Naomi. When Ruth "returns" with Naomi, she is not merely traveling; she is reversing the tragic departure of Elimelech's family from the Promised Land. Her physical return mirrors her spiritual arrival under the protective wings of the Lord God of Israel.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shuv represents the essence of biblical repentance. True faith involves a decisive change of direction. For a Gentile like Ruth, "returning" to Bethlehem signifies inclusion in the covenant community. It demonstrates that God's grace extends beyond national boundaries to receive those who turn to Him in faith and loyalty. It also reflects God's heart for restoration: when His people or those who seek Him turn back, they find refuge, sustenance, and redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:6, the servant in charge of the reapers identifies Ruth to Boaz as "the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab." The phrase "came back" (the feminine singular participle hashabah, "the one returning") functions as Ruth's primary identifier in the community. She is known in Bethlehem not simply as a foreigner, but as the one who actively chose to return with Naomi. This presents a beautiful paradox: Ruth is a Moabite who had never actually lived in Israel, yet her journey is called a "return." By pledging herself to Naomi's God, she entered her true spiritual home. She is treated not as an outsider, but as one who has returned to the covenant fold.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:16 - "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you...'"

• Ruth 1:22 - "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab."

• Psalm 51:13 - "Then I will teach transgressors Your ways, And sinners shall be converted to You."

• Isaiah 55:7 - "Let the wicked forsake his way, And the unrighteous man his thoughts; Let him return to the LORD, And He will have mercy on him; And to our God, For He will abundantly pardon."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The repetition of shuv in the narrative highlights the contrast between emptiness and fullness. Naomi left the land full and "returned" empty, yet God uses the "returning" of Ruth to bring ultimate fullness, culminating in the birth of Obed and the lineage of King David and Jesus Christ. This term challenges Israel's self-righteousness by showing that a Moabite woman displayed a more genuine "returning" (covenant loyalty and faith) than many native Israelites during the chaotic period of the Judges.
