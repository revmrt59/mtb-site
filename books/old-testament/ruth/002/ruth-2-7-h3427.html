RESTED - ( yashab , Strong's H3427 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yashab primarily means to sit, dwell, remain, or abide. In the context of Ruth 2:7, it depicts a brief period of sitting down or tarrying to rest from exhausting physical labor. Rather than indicating laziness, this temporary pause underscores Ruth's remarkable industry; she worked tirelessly from morning until afternoon, taking only a short, necessary rest in the shelter before returning to the fields.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical posture of yashab implies a transition from active movement (walking, standing, working) to a stationary state of rest, dwelling, or occupying a seat. In ancient Near Eastern culture, sitting down denoted taking a break from labor, establishing a home, or even sitting in authority (like a king on a throne or a judge at the city gate). In the context of agricultural labor, yashab represents the vital moments of physical recovery under a temporary shelter erected in the fields to protect workers from the blazing Middle Eastern sun. It paints a picture of a weary worker seeking refuge and quiet strength before resuming their task.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yashab carries the basic meaning of to sit down, remain, dwell, or abide. It occurs over 1,000 times in the Old Testament, representing a highly versatile semantic range:

• To sit down (for a meal, rest, or court session).

• To dwell or reside in a place (as a permanent or semi-permanent inhabitant).

• To remain, tarry, or stay behind in a specific location.

In Ruth 2:7, the specific form is the infinitive construct with a pronominal suffix (shibtah, meaning "her sitting"), which is translated contextually as "she rested." It refers to a brief period of sitting down to recuperate.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, yashab is used in several figurative and literal ways:

• Sitting as an act of waiting or abiding: It describes sitting at the door of a tent or waiting expectantly.

• Dwelling in safety and security: It is used to describe sitting under one's own vine and fig tree, representing peace and prosperous rest.

• Enthronement and rule: It depicts God sitting enthroned forever, signifying His sovereign, undisturbed reign over the universe.

• In the Book of Ruth, the word picture is a physical pause. Ruth's "sitting" in the shelter is contrasted with her continuous standing and walking to glean. It highlights the physical toll of her devotion to Naomi and the sheer effort required to survive.

4. Theological Implications

The use of yashab in Ruth 2:7 illustrates the biblical balance between diligent labor and necessary rest. God-honoring work is characterized by strenuous effort, but it acknowledges human limitations through rest. Additionally, it reflects God’s providential care. Even in her brief moments of "sitting" or resting, Ruth is positioned within the sphere of Boaz's protection and favor. The concept of yashab eventually shifts in the narrative from a temporary, weary "resting" in the field to a permanent "dwelling" in security and rest as Boaz’s wife, mirroring the believer’s transition from striving under the weight of life to resting in the redemption of Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:7, the overseer of the reapers is reporting Ruth's work ethic to Boaz. He notes that she arrived early in the morning and worked continuously, with only a very brief pause ("she rested a little in the house"). The "house" (bayit) refers to a temporary shade shelter or hut built in the field for the reapers to escape the intense heat of the sun. The phrase "rested a little" is a crucial detail. It defends Ruth against any potential accusation of laziness or entitlement. She is not a professional gleaner looking for easy hand-outs; she is a driven, respectful, and incredibly hard-working woman whose brief rest only emphasizes her immense endurance and quiet dignity.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) often translates yashab with Greek verbs such as kathezomai (to sit down), kathemai (to sit), or katoikeo (to dwell). In Ruth 2:7, the Greek translation emphasizes her pause from labor. There is a slight textual difficulty in the Masoretic Text of Ruth 2:7 regarding the exact construction of "her sitting the house a little," but the overall sense of a brief pause in the shelter remains the most contextually sound and widely accepted rendering. This brief rest also highlights the cultural hospitality of the harvest field, where even a foreign gleaner was permitted to use the shelter, showing the subtle hand of God's grace preparing her way.
