GLEAN - ( laqat , Strong's H3950 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb laqat refers to the physical act of picking up, collecting, or gathering scattered items from the ground, most notably leftover ears of grain during a harvest. In the ancient biblical world, gleaning was far more than an agricultural task; it was a divinely instituted welfare system designed to provide food for the poor, the widow, the stranger, and the orphan while preserving their dignity through personal labor. In Ruth 2:7, Ruth’s request to glean highlights her humility, exceptional work ethic, and reliance on God’s covenant provision, positioning her directly in the path of Boaz, her kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The agricultural life of ancient Israel provides the background for this word. During the harvest, reapers cut standing grain with sickles, and other workers bound the stalks into sheaves. Inevitably, some stalks fell to the ground. God's law strictly forbade landowners from going back to pick up these dropped stalks or from harvesting the very corners of their fields. Instead, the vulnerable members of society—those without land of their own—would follow behind the harvesters to gather the leftovers. The word laqat depicts a posture of humility and physical labor, requiring the gleaner to bend down repeatedly, scan the earth, and gather individual stalks of grain by hand. It represents the meeting point of God's sovereign provision and human diligence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Laqat is a primitive root verb meaning to gather, collect, pick up, or glean. In its basic verbal form, it describes the manual collection of objects scattered on the ground. Depending on the context, this can refer to harvesting food, collecting stones, or gathering scattered objects. The word denotes an active, systematic effort to gather what has been left behind or distributed across an area. It stands in contrast to the wholesale reaping of a field, focusing instead on the painstaking collection of individual remnants.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Daily Provision: In the wilderness, Israel was commanded to gather (laqat) the manna each morning. Every individual had to go out and collect their own portion, picturing daily faith and active obedience in response to divine sustenance.

• Covenantal Memorials: The word is used for gathering stones to build a heap or altar, symbolizing the concrete gathering of testimonies of God's faithfulness.

• Complete Humiliation: In a darker sense, the word is used to describe defeated kings gathering scraps under a conqueror's table, indicating complete subjugation and dependency.

• Spiritual Sifting: The word pictures the deliberate, careful collection of valuable truths or remnants out of a larger, chaotic environment.

4. Theological Implications

The practice of gleaning reveals the heart of God's law, balancing the rights of private property with a profound responsibility toward the poor. God declared that the land belonged to Him, and landowners were merely stewards. By requiring landowners to leave the margins and the dropped grain, God ensured that the wealthy did not exploit the land to its absolute limit. Simultaneously, because the poor had to physically gather (laqat) the grain themselves, the law protected their dignity, preventing the dependency of handouts and encouraging industriousness. Ruth’s reliance on this law demonstrates her integration into the covenant community of Israel and her trust in the character of Israel's God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:7, the supervisor of the reapers reports Ruth’s request: "Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves." This request contains an important nuance. Ordinarily, gleaners were restricted to the outer boundaries of the field or had to wait until the primary harvest was completely finished and the sheaves removed. Ruth, however, asks to glean "among the sheaves"—directly behind the active reapers. This was a request for exceptional favor, protection, and access to the areas of highest yield. Her request, followed by the supervisor’s observation that she worked tirelessly "from morning until now," showcases both her extraordinary boldness born of necessity and her noble character, which immediately caught the eye of Boaz.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 2:2: "And Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

• Leviticus 19:9: "‘When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest."

• Exodus 16:21: "So they gathered it every morning, every man according to his need. And when the sun became hot, it melted."

• Judges 1:7: "And Adoni-Bezek said, 'Seventy kings, with their thumbs and big toes cut off, used to gather their food under my table; as I have done, so God has repaid me.' Then they brought him to Jerusalem, and there he died."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The interaction between Ruth and Boaz highlights the beauty of grace surpassing the letter of the law. While the law of gleaning merely required Boaz to tolerate Ruth's presence on the edges of his property, his covenant kindness (chesed) led him to command his young men to deliberately pull out grain from the bundles and leave it for her to gather (laqat). This illustrates a profound spiritual principle: God's grace does not merely leave behind accidental crumbs for the seeker; He actively and intentionally drops blessings along our path. For the modern believer, gleaning serves as an excellent metaphor for studying the Scriptures—diligently searching, bending down in study, and gathering life-giving truths word by word.
