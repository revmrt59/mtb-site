PLEASE - ( na , Strong's H4994 )

Summary:

The Hebrew particle na is an enclitic particle of entreaty, exhortation, or polite request, most often translated in the NKJV as "please," "now," or "I pray." Rather than expressing a demanding command, it softens imperative and cohortative verbs, signaling respect, humility, and earnest appeal. In Ruth 2:7, Ruth uses this particle when addressing Boaz's foreman, demonstrating her remarkable humility, adherence to social propriety, and gentle spirit as a foreign widow seeking favor rather than demanding rights.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The particle na does not derive from a concrete noun that yields a physical image, but its usage in ancient Near Eastern dialogue functions as a linguistic gesture of bowing. Just as a petitioner would physically prostrate themselves before a person of superior rank, appending na to a verbal request is an oral expression of deference. It establishes that the speaker recognizes the hearer's authority and acknowledges that the success of the request relies entirely on the hearer's favor and goodwill.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word na is an uninflected particle used to urge, entreat, or exhort. It is attached to:

• Imperatives, transforming a command into a polite petition (e.g., "hear, please").

• Cohortatives, expressing a polite, self-encouraging desire while seeking consent (e.g., "let me go, please").

• Jussives, expressing a respectful wish concerning a third party.

The primary semantic function of na is to add courtesy, urgency, or earnest appeal to a clause. It alters the pragmatic force of an utterance from an assertive demand to a respectful request, meaning "please," "now," "pray," or "I pray you."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The particle occurs over several hundred times across the Old Testament and serves several rhetorical purposes:

• Relational Buffer: It acts as a relational lubricant in hierarchical social structures, preventing friction when speaking to superiors, strangers, or angels.

• Expression of Urgency: In desperate situations, it elevates a polite request to an earnest, passionate plea for rescue or mercy.

• Conditional Softening: It frequently appears alongside the phrase "if I have found favor in your eyes," reinforcing the petitioner's dependence on the giver's grace.

4. Theological Implications

Though brief, na carries rich theological significance regarding grace, humility, and human-divine interactions.

• Human Deference to God: When humans address God using na, it highlights the vast distinction between the Creator and the creature. It underscores that God owes humanity nothing and that all petitions are made on the basis of His covenant mercy (chesed).

• Divine Condescension: In rare and profound instances, God employs na when speaking to humans (such as in Isaiah 1:18). This demonstrates His astonishing grace, where the sovereign Lord invites and entreats His people rather than merely issuing coercive decrees.

• Interpersonal Meekness: In human relationships, na models a biblical ethic of meekness. It demonstrates that the people of God should operate through humility, honoring authority structures and recognizing that earthly blessings are gifts to be received with gratitude.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:7, the foreman reports Ruth's request to Boaz: "Please let me glean and gather after the reapers." Under the Mosaic Law (Leviticus 19:9-10; Deuteronomy 24:19), the poor, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow possessed a legal right to glean the corners and leftovers of the harvests. Ruth met all of these qualifications.

However, Ruth does not enter the field aggressively demanding her legal rights. Instead, she approaches the foreman with deep deference, appending the particle na to her cohortative verb ("let me glean, please"). This linguistic choice reveals her awareness of her status as a foreign outsider (nokriyah) with no social capital. She does not presume upon the landowner's compliance. Her polite appeal wins the foreman's favor, prompting him to grant her access and later commend her work ethic and character to Boaz.

• Genesis 18:3 - "and said, 'My Lord, if I have now found favor in Your sight, do not pass by Your servant.'"

• Genesis 22:2 - "Then He said, 'Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains of which I shall tell you.'"

• Psalm 118:25 - "Save now, I pray, O LORD; O LORD, I pray, send now prosperity."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint frequently translates the particle na using the Greek postpositive particle de (which can carry an encouraging or pleading tone when paired with imperatives) or leaves the particle untranslated, allowing the subjunctive or imperative mood of the main verb to carry the polite force of the request. In New Testament Greek, the spirit of this particle is captured by the verb parakaleo ("I urge, beseech, or entreat"). Recognizing the presence of na helps readers avoid misinterpreting ancient texts through modern, individualistic lenses of entitlement, highlighting instead a biblical pattern of gentleness and respect.
