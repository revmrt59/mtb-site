SHEAVES - ( omer , Strong's H6016 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word omer refers to a bundle of freshly cut stalks of grain bound together after harvesting, and it also serves as a biblical unit of dry measurement equivalent to about two quarts. In the agrarian context of ancient Israel, sheaves represented the tangible fruit of a farmer's labor, the preservation of life through food, and the physical manifestation of God's covenantal provision. In the book of Ruth, the sheaves serve as a critical boundary line of grace: gleaners were typically forbidden from gathering directly "among the sheaves" where the harvest was already secured, making Boaz’s permission for Ruth to do so an extraordinary demonstration of favor, protection, and redemptive kindness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word omer comes from a root associated with binding together, gathering, or dominating. In the ancient Near East, harvesting was a highly collaborative, labor-intensive process. Reapers would cut the standing grain with sickles, and others would follow closely behind to gather the fallen stalks and bind them into secure bundles, or sheaves.

• These sheaves were then collected, transported to the threshing floor, and crushed to separate the edible grain from the useless chaff.

• A sheaf represents completed work and gathered provision. It signifies the transition from vulnerable, standing crops to a secured, life-sustaining food supply.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The term omer possesses two primary definitions in biblical Hebrew. First, it refers to a concrete bundle of cut grain stalks, typically barley or wheat, tied together in the field.

• Second, it designates a dry measure of capacity, specifically one-tenth of an ephah (roughly equivalent to two dry quarts or 2.2 liters).

• In the legal and ceremonial codes of Israel, this specific measure was used to quantify the daily portion of manna provided in the wilderness and the firstfruits offering brought to the tabernacle. In the narrative of Ruth, the word functions in its primary physical sense as the bundled harvest lying in the field.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In scripture, sheaves are frequently used as symbols of harvest joy, divine blessing, and labor rewarded. The pulling together of scattered stalks into a unified, valuable bundle represents restoration and productivity after a season of trial.

• Conversely, the withholding or taking away of sheaves is a picture of poverty, judgment, and severe oppression, as seen in the wisdom literature where the needy carry sheaves but remain hungry.

• The ritual of the "wave sheaf" (omer) in the spring festival of Firstfruits served as a visual dedication of the entire harvest to Yahweh, acknowledging Him as the ultimate source of life and sustenance.

4. Theological Implications

• Providential Care and Generosity: The Old Testament law explicitly commanded landowners not to glean their fields to the very edges, nor to return to retrieve a forgotten sheaf (Deuteronomy 24:19). The dropped or forgotten omer was divinely designated as the property of the marginalized—the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow. This reveals God's heart for the vulnerable, transforming a simple agricultural byproduct into an instrument of systemic social welfare.

• Firstfruits and Resurrection: The presentation of the omer of firstfruits in Leviticus 23 prefigures the ultimate harvest of redemption. This theological thread connects directly to the New Testament reality of Christ's resurrection as the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep, marking the guarantee of a greater harvest to come.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:7, Ruth requests permission from Boaz's foreman to "glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves." Under normal circumstances, a poor gleaner was restricted to the edges of the field, picking up only what the harvesters accidentally left behind far from the main workspace. To walk "among the sheaves"—where the high-quality grain had already been neatly piled and bound—was highly unusual and technically an intrusion onto the landowner's secured property.

• Ruth’s request demonstrates her desperate diligence, while Boaz’s subsequent command in Ruth 2:15 ("Let her glean even among the sheaves, and do not reproach her") represents an astounding act of grace. Boaz breaches standard legal boundaries to grant Ruth access to the inner circle of his wealth and protection, transforming her status from a vulnerable, distant scavenger to an honored guest eating from the heart of his abundance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 23:10 - "Speak to the children of Israel, and say to them: 'When you come into the land which I give to you, and reap its harvest, then you shall bring a sheaf of the firstfruits of your harvest to the priest.'"

• Deuteronomy 24:19 - "When you reap your harvest in your field, and forget a sheaf in the field, you shall not go back to get it; it shall be for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow, that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands."

• Ruth 2:15 - "And when she rose up to glean, Boaz commanded his young men, saying, 'Let her glean even among the sheaves, and do not reproach her.'"

• Job 24:10 - "They go about naked, without clothing, And hungry they carry the sheaves."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The transition of the word omer from a literal agricultural bundle to a standardized dry unit of measure (as seen in the wilderness manna accounts of Exodus 16) highlights how deeply Israel's economic and spiritual language was rooted in the soil.

• A single omer of grain was considered the precise daily food requirement for an individual. Therefore, when Boaz allowed Ruth to gather freely among the sheaves, he was not merely offering a workplace courtesy; he was guaranteeing that her daily omer of provision would be overflowing, far exceeding the bare survival rations of a typical widow.
