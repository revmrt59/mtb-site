BOAZ - ( Bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

Boaz is a proper noun meaning "in him is strength" or "fleetness." In the Old Testament, Boaz is a wealthy Bethlehemite of the tribe of Judah who becomes the kinsman-redeemer (goel) of Ruth, ultimately marrying her and becoming an ancestor of King David and Jesus Christ. His name also designates the left-hand bronze pillar erected at the vestibule of Solomon's temple, symbolizing the strength and stability God establishes. In the book of Ruth, Boaz serves as a beautiful type of Christ, demonstrating grace, protection, and provision for the vulnerable and marginalized.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Boaz is composed of two Hebrew elements: be (in, with) and oz (strength). The name translates to "in him is strength." In the patriarchal, agrarian culture of ancient Israel, names carried prophetic significance and reflected the character and destiny of the bearer. Boaz lives up to his name as a "man of great wealth" or "mighty man of valor" (gibbor chayil), representing the strength of character, social standing, and spiritual integrity needed to rescue a destitute family.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun Boaz refers to:

• The Bethlehemite redeemer in the book of Ruth, who was a close kinsman of Elimelech's family.

• The northern or left-hand bronze pillar set up at the portico of King Solomon's Temple in Jerusalem.

Lexically, the name derives from an unused root meaning to be strong or swift. The theological weight of the name emphasizes that strength is not inherent in human achievement but resides in the Lord, who infuses His servants with the strength to act righteously and redeem the broken.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name Boaz appears 24 times in the Hebrew Bible, primarily in the Book of Ruth, with genealogical and architectural mentions elsewhere.

• The Kinsman-Redeemer: In Ruth, Boaz is the living picture of the kinsman-redeemer (goel). The law of levirate marriage and land redemption required a close relative to buy back lost property and raise up an heir for a deceased relative. Boaz represents the willing, capable, and loving redeemer who steps in when all hope is lost.

• The Temple Pillar: In the accounts of Solomon's temple, Boaz is the name given to the left pillar, paired with Jachin ("He will establish"). Together, they stood as a visual sermon to Israel: "He will establish, in Him is strength." Every worshiper entering the temple was reminded that the covenant relationship with God was established by His sovereign will and sustained by His almighty strength.

4. Theological Implications

Boaz serves as one of the clearest Old Testament types of Jesus Christ.

• Grace to Outsiders: Just as Boaz extends favor, protection, and provision to Ruth—a destitute Moabite widow—Jesus extends saving grace to Gentiles and outcasts who seek refuge under His wings.

• The Willingness to Redeem: Boaz was not the closest kinsman, but he was the one willing to pay the price and endure the social risk to redeem Ruth. Similarly, Christ willingly laid down His life to redeem humanity from the debt of sin.

• The Source of Strength: The name Boaz points to the ultimate truth that redemption requires divine strength. No human can redeem themselves; strength must come from another who is capable, worthy, and willing.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:8, Boaz speaks directly to Ruth for the first time: "Then Boaz said to Ruth, 'Listen, my daughter, do you not hear? Do not go to glean in another field, nor go from here, but stay close by my young women.'"

In this immediate context, Boaz acts with extraordinary kindness that transcends the bare minimum of the Mosaic law. While the law permitted the poor to glean the corners of the fields, it did not guarantee safety, abundant provision, or personal welcome. By commanding Ruth to stay in his field and calling her "my daughter," Boaz acts as her protector. His name, "in him is strength," shines brightly here; he uses his social influence, material wealth, and physical protection to shield a vulnerable foreign woman, demonstrating that his strength is coupled with deep compassion and covenant faithfulness (chesed).

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the genealogy of Jesus Christ recorded in Matthew 1:5, Boaz is listed as the son of Rahab, the Canaanite woman of Jericho. This lineage is incredibly rich with theological significance. Boaz, the son of a redeemed Gentile woman (Rahab), becomes the redeemer of another Gentile woman (Ruth the Moabite). His heritage prepared his heart to show mercy to outsiders. This foreshadows the Gospel of Jesus Christ, which tears down the dividing wall of hostility to make Jews and Gentiles one new man.
