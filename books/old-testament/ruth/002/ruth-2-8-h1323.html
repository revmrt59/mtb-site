DAUGHTER - ( bath , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bath primarily denotes a female child, descendant, or citizen, but it frequently functions as a tender term of address from an elder to a younger woman. In Ruth 2:8, Boaz uses this word to address Ruth not out of biological relation, but as a pastoral expression of protection, respect, and familial care. By addressing her as "my daughter," Boaz removes the social distance of her foreign, widowed status and establishes a safe refuge for her within his household and fields.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the patriarchal structure of ancient Israel, the family (bet ab, or "house of the father") was the primary unit of identity, security, and economic survival. The word bath is the feminine counterpart to ben (son) and is derived from a root associated with building, reflecting the understanding that daughters build up and extend the household. Within this cultural framework, a daughter remained under the legal custody and protection of her father until marriage. A foreign woman who was also a widow possessed no patriarchal protector, leaving her highly vulnerable to exploitation, abuse, and destitution. When Boaz steps into this narrative, his verbal adoption of Ruth as a "daughter" signals that he is extending the protective umbrella of his household to cover her vulnerability.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of bath includes:

• A literal female offspring or immediate biological daughter.

• A female descendant, granddaughter, or distant female relative.

• A female inhabitant of a specific city or region, often used in poetic personification (e.g., "daughter of Zion").

• A designation of age or classification (e.g., "a daughter of fifty years").

• A title of respectful, affectionate, and protective address used by an older person of authority toward a younger woman.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs bath in diverse contexts to paint vivid theological and social pictures:

• The Relationship of Belonging: In familial lineages, daughters represent the continuity of legacy and the domestic strength of the community.

• The Personification of Nations: The phrase "daughter of..." (such as the daughter of Zion in Isaiah 1-8 or the daughter of Babylon in Psalm 137:8) represents the collective population of a city or nation as a vulnerable, beloved, or chastised woman.

• The Language of Adoption and Authority: When used vocatively by an older authority figure, the word transforms from a mere biological label into an instrument of social and spiritual guardianship. It communicates that the speaker assumes responsibility for the recipient's welfare.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of bath in the book of Ruth carries profound covenantal and redemptive weight. By addressing a Moabite foreigner as "my daughter," Boaz demonstrates the heart of the Torah regarding the treatment of the stranger, the widow, and the poor.

• Inclusion of the Gentile: Ruth, once an outsider excluded from the assembly of Israel, is drawn into the familial language of the covenant community. This prefigures the New Testament reality where Gentiles are adopted into the family of God.

• The Character of the Redeemer: Boaz acts as a type of Christ, the ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer. His first spoken words to Ruth do not treat her as cheap labor or an object of suspicion, but rather elevate her to the status of a cherished daughter. The Redeemer's voice brings identity, safety, and belonging before he performs the physical act of redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:8, Ruth has arrived in the field of Boaz to glean among the sheaves—a dangerous task for an unprotected foreign woman. Boaz, recognizing her devotion to Naomi, speaks to her directly: "Listen, my daughter, will you not go to glean in another field...?"

• By adding the possessive suffix to create the phrase "my daughter" (bitti), Boaz establishes a paternal and protective relationship.

• This title alters her status instantly in the eyes of the other workers in the field. She is no longer an alien scavenger; she is now a woman under the personal protection of the master of the estate.

• The term establishes a boundary of respect and honor, preventing the young male workers from harassing her, and assuring Ruth that her presence is welcome.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The age differential between Boaz and Ruth is underscored by this word. While Ruth is a young widow, Boaz is an established, older man of wealth. His use of "my daughter" frames his initial interest in her as purely honorable, protective, and pastoral. It removes any immediate romantic pressure, creating a foundation of trust, safety, and mutual respect before their relationship transitions into marriage.
