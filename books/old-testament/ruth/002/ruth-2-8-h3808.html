NOT - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' represents the absolute, objective, and permanent negation "no" or "not." Unlike subjective or temporary prohibitions, lo' establishes a definitive boundary, an unyielding factual reality, or an enduring commandment. In Ruth 2:8, Boaz uses this strong negation to instruct Ruth "nor go from here," establishing a protective, permanent boundary that ensures her safety and constant provision under his care, illustrating the secure refuge found under the wings of the Redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew language utilizes two primary negative adverbs to establish boundaries and prohibitions: al and lo'. The word al functions as a subjective, immediate, and temporary negative—essentially conveying "do not do this right now." In contrast, lo' functions as an objective, absolute, and permanent negative—conveying "you shall not ever." It acts as an unyielding wall. This is the word used to construct the Decalogue (the Ten Commandments), drawing absolute lines that must not be crossed. In the agricultural context of the Ancient Near East, where land boundaries were marked by loose stones and safety outside one's community was highly volatile, lo' served as a verbal fence. When Boaz speaks this word to Ruth, he is drawing a permanent boundary of protection and provision around her, declaring that she has no need to step outside his care.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The particle lo' is the primary adverb of negation in biblical Hebrew, occurring over 5,000 times. It expresses absolute negation and is translated variously as "not," "no," "neither," "nor," or "by no means."

• When paired with a verb in the imperfect tense, it functions as an absolute, permanent prohibition or a statement of objective impossibility.

• It denies a fact, asserts a permanent state of non-existence, or establishes an enduring law.

• In Ruth 2:8, the phrase lo' ta'abori ("nor go" or "you shall not pass over") uses this absolute negative with the imperfect verb, upgrading a friendly piece of advice into an absolute, binding protective decree for the duration of the harvest.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The biblical usage of lo' centers on establishing absolute categories, leaving no room for negotiation or gray areas.

• The Inviolable Boundary: It is used to set limits that cannot be breached, such as the boundaries of the sea or the decrees of God that cannot be annulled.

• The Covenant Promise: It is used in divine oaths where God guarantees that He will not fail, not forget, and not forsake His people, transforming a negative particle into a foundation of absolute positive security.

• The Shield of Prohibition: In legal contexts, lo' acts as a shield to protect the vulnerable (the poor, the widow, the stranger) by absolutely forbidding their exploitation.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, lo' reveals the binary nature of holiness, covenant, and redemption. God’s holiness requires absolute boundaries; there is a realm of life and a realm of death, a realm of blessing and a realm of cursing. Boaz, acting as a type of Christ (the Ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer), uses this absolute negative to bring Ruth into a covenant-like sphere of safety. By telling her "nor go from here," Boaz makes her safety an absolute reality. Within the borders of the redeemer's field, there is abundant life; outside of it, there is spiritual and physical vulnerability. The absolute "not" highlights the exclusivity of redemption—the believer does not need to wander to other fields because the provision within the Redeemer's boundary is absolute, total, and secure.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:8, Boaz addresses Ruth with remarkable tenderness and authority: "Do not go [al, temporary/immediate prohibition] to glean in another field, nor [lo', absolute/permanent negation] go from here..."

• The movement from al to lo' is critical. Boaz starts by telling her not to go to another field today (al-teleki), but immediately strengthens his language to an absolute, permanent command: "you shall not pass over from here" (lo' ta'abori).

• By using lo', Boaz removes any ambiguity about her future. He is not merely giving her a daily tip; he is adopting her into his household's protective care for the entire harvest season.

• This absolute negation serves to relieve Ruth's anxiety. As a foreign widow, she was highly vulnerable to assault and starvation. Boaz’s lo' acts as a sovereign decree that blocks her from entering harm's way and guarantees her ongoing place of privilege at his table.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 20:13: "You shall not murder."

• Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

• Joshua 1:5: "I will not leave you nor forsake you."

• Psalm 121:3: "He will not allow your foot to be moved; He who keeps you will not slumber."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew thought, the absolute negative lo' does not merely restrict freedom; it creates a safe haven where true freedom can flourish. When Boaz tells Ruth "do not go from here," he is freeing her from the exhausting, dangerous task of searching for grace in hostile fields. The absolute boundary of the Redeemer is the ultimate source of the believer's rest.
