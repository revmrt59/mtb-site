YOUNG WOMEN - ( na'arah , Strong's H5291 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'arah refers to a young, marriageable woman, a maiden, or a young female servant. In the narrative of Ruth, it denotes the female domestic workers employed in Boaz's harvest fields. By instructing Ruth to remain close to these young women, Boaz provides her with a secure social and physical environment, shielding her from potential exploitation while integrating her into his community of workers.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite society, a na'arah occupied a transitional and dependent social status. The term designates a young female who is no longer a child but has not yet married or become the mistress of her own household. It carries connotations of youth, physical vitality, and vulnerability. When used in a domestic or agricultural context, a na'arah was a female servant or attendant under the authority and protection of a master or household head.

In the setting of an agricultural harvest, these young women performed essential labor alongside the men. While the male workers cut the standing grain, the ne'arot (plural of na'arah) followed closely behind to bind the fallen stalks into sheaves and manage the gathered crop. This cooperative environment required physical stamina and close proximity, making the presence of protective boundaries vital for the young women's safety.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun na'arah is the feminine counterpart of the masculine na'ar (meaning a boy, youth, or young servant). The semantic range of na'arah includes:

• A girl or young child.

• A young, unmarried woman of marriageable age, emphasizing her youth and purity.

• A female servant, maid, or personal attendant assigned to a specific household or superior.

In Ruth 2:8, the word appears in its plural form with a pronominal suffix (ne'arotay, "my young women"). Here, the term functions double-duty: it describes both the youth and gender of these workers, as well as their economic and social status as hired servants under Boaz’s care and authority.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, na'arah paints vivid pictures of youthful service, beauty, and vulnerability:

• Youthful Beauty and Purity: The word describes Rebekah when Abraham's servant first encounters her at the well, highlighting her readiness for marriage and her virtue.

• Royal Attendants: It designates the maidens who accompanied Pharaoh’s daughter along the Nile when she discovered the infant Moses, depicting a retinue of trusted young female helpers.

• Captive Service: It is used for the little Israelite girl captured by Syrian raiders who became a servant to Naaman’s wife, illustrating how God can use the most vulnerable servant to direct kings and generals to His healing power.

• Wisdom’s Messengers: In wisdom literature, Lady Wisdom sends out her ne'arot to invite those lacking understanding to her banquet, representing faithful, urgent messengers of life-giving truth.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence and treatment of the ne'arot in the book of Ruth highlight several profound theological themes:

• God’s Provision for the Vulnerable: The Old Testament law repeatedly commands Israel to care for the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless. By instructing Ruth to join his young women, Boaz acts as the physical hands of God's covenant mercy, ensuring that a destitute foreign widow receives both sustenance and protection.

• The Sanctuary of God's Community: The young women of Boaz's household represent a safe, sanctified community. For Ruth, a Moabitess who has recently declared that Yahweh is her God, being welcomed into the company of these Israelite women is a practical step of assimilation into the covenant family of faith.

• Dignity in Labor: By placing Ruth alongside his personal workers, Boaz elevates her from a desperate scavenger to an associate of his respected household staff, demonstrating how God's grace restores dignity to the marginalized.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The context of Ruth 2:8 is crucial. Ruth has entered the field of Boaz as a poor foreigner trying to glean leftovers to keep herself and Naomi alive. In the ancient Near East, harvest fields could be dangerous places for unattached, foreign women, who were highly susceptible to verbal and physical abuse from male harvesters.

Boaz, recognizing Ruth's vulnerability, steps in with paternal and pastoral care. His command, "stay close by my young women," is a strategic act of protection. By grouping Ruth with his female servants, Boaz makes her a part of his protected circle. He effectively tells the male reapers that Ruth is off-limits to harassment, as she is now counted among his household. This transition from the dangerous open margins of the field to the safe company of Boaz’s ne'arot marks the turning point in Ruth's experience in Bethlehem.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A striking literary and moral contrast exists between the ne'arim (young men) and the ne'arot (young women) in Ruth 2. Boaz addresses both groups to secure Ruth's welfare. He charges the young men not to touch or molest her (Ruth 2:9), while simultaneously charging Ruth to bind herself closely to his young women.

This dual command demonstrates Boaz's realistic understanding of human nature and his godly administrative wisdom. He does not simply hope for the best; he actively structures his workforce to ensure that the vulnerable are protected. For pastors and leaders today, this serves as a timeless model of how to establish safe, honorable, and protective environments within the household of faith.
