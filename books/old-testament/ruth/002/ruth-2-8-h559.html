SAID - ( amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb amar is the primary Old Testament term for speaking, saying, or declaring. In Ruth 2:8, where Boaz addresses Ruth as "my daughter" and invites her to remain in his fields, this word goes beyond mere vocalization. It marks a critical turning point in the narrative, signifying a shift from isolation to protection and covenant-aligned kindness. When Boaz speaks, his words carry authority, initiative, and redemptive grace, reflecting the heart of God toward the vulnerable.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The verb amar is one of the most frequently used terms in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over 5,000 times. At its core, the word represents the external expression of internal thought. It is the basic mechanism of communication, used for commands, promises, declarations, and conversational exchanges.

• In the ancient Near East, spoken words carried substantial weight; they were not merely passing sounds but actions that set events in motion. When a person of status and authority spoke, their words established reality, obligation, and social boundaries.

• In the agricultural and patriarchal context of Ruth, the spoken word of a landowner to a foreign gleaner carries legal, protective, and relational significance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of amar is to say, speak, utter, or command.

• Depending on the context, its semantic range includes declaring or proclaiming a matter, thinking or saying in one's heart (when used reflexively), commanding or directing an action, and promising or assuring.

• In the Qal stem, as seen in Ruth 2:8, it denotes the straightforward act of speaking or addressing someone directly.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Voice of Creation: The word is used in Genesis 1 when God speaks creation into existence, where speech is the vehicle of creative power.

• The Voice of Covenant: It is used when Yahweh speaks to the patriarchs, establishing covenants and giving assurances of His presence.

• Heart-Speech: In several passages, amar is coupled with "in the heart" to picture internal contemplation or reasoning.

• Social Hierarchy: When a superior speaks to an inferior, amar often carries the weight of a benevolent directive or protective decree, as seen in Boaz's address to Ruth.

4. Theological Implications

• Theologically, amar underscores a communicating God and a covenant community that mirrors His character. God is not silent; He speaks to reveal His will and redeem His people.

• Similarly, human speech in the covenant community is meant to bring life, order, and protection.

• When Boaz speaks to Ruth, his words are not empty talk; they are the physical manifestation of covenant faithfulness (chesed). His speech acts as a shield over the vulnerable foreigner, demonstrating how God's law of gleaning and care for the stranger is actualized through righteous human speech and action.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:8, amar introduces Boaz's first direct speech to Ruth: "Listen, my daughter, do you not hear? Do not go to glean in another field..."

• The context is highly charged. Ruth is a Moabitess, a destitute widow vulnerable to abuse in the fields. Boaz's speech immediately alters her status from an exposed outsider to a protected guest. His words establish a boundary of safety around her.

• By addressing her as "my daughter," his speech conveys familial warmth and patriarchal protection. The nuance of amar here is one of gracious initiative; Boaz does not wait for Ruth to petition him, but speaks first to bestow favor and security.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3: "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Genesis 12:1: "Now the Lord had said to Abram: 'Get out of your country...'"

• Exodus 3:14: "And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And He said, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, "I AM has sent me to you."'"

• Ruth 2:21: "Ruth the Moabitess said, 'He also said to me, "You shall stay close by my young men until they have finished all my harvest."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In the structure of the Book of Ruth, dialogue drives the theology. Very little direct narrative commentary is given about God's actions; instead, God's providence is seen through the words and deeds of characters like Boaz.

• Boaz's speech in Ruth 2:8 is the first time Ruth is directly addressed by anyone in Bethlehem other than Naomi. This word marks the transition of Ruth from a silent, hard-working laborer to a recognized and valued member of the community.
