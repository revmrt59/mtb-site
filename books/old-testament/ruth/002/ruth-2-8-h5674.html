GO - ( abar , Strong's H5674 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb abar fundamentally means to "cross over," "pass through," or "go beyond" a boundary. In Ruth 2:8, Boaz uses this word to instruct Ruth not to "go from" (literally, "pass over" or "cross beyond") his field. This command functions as a protective hedge, offering her safety, provision, and community within the boundaries of his land, while warning her against crossing over into the vulnerability and danger of unfamiliar fields.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb abar evokes a vivid, concrete physical action in the ancient Near Eastern world. It is the standard Hebrew term for crossing a river, passing through a gateway, or stepping over a boundary line. Because fields in ancient Israel were not typically divided by high walls or fences, but rather by simple stone markers, a person could easily step from one person's property to another without realizing it. To abar was to make a transition from one physical space—and therefore one sphere of authority, safety, or ownership—to another.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of abar is to pass over, cross over, or pass through. Depending on the context, its semantic range includes:

• To cross a physical barrier (such as a river, valley, or boundary).

• To pass by or pass through a specific location.

• To pass away or disappear (such as time, shadow, or life).

• To transgress, bypass, or go beyond a command, law, or covenant.

In Ruth 2:8, the verb is used in the Qal stem as a negative prohibition (lo ta'aburi), meaning "do not cross over," "do not pass beyond," or "do not depart from" the designated boundaries of Boaz's fields.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, abar is used to describe momentous transitions and crossings:

• It is the word used for Abraham "passing through" the land of Canaan (Genesis 12:6) and Jacob "crossing over" the Jabbok (Genesis 32:22).

• It is used of Israel "crossing over" the Jordan River on dry ground to enter the Promised Land (Joshua 3:17).

• In a metaphorical sense, the word describes "transgressing" (crossing over) the Lord's covenant or commandments (Deuteronomy 17:2).

The word picture is always one of movement across a threshold. When one crosses over, they leave behind one condition or territory and enter into another.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, abar in Ruth 2:8 highlights the safety and sufficiency of the Redeemer's provision. Boaz, a type of Christ (our Kinsman-Redeemer), establishes a safe boundary for the vulnerable foreign widow.

• The Boundary of Grace: God establishes boundaries not to restrict our joy, but to preserve our lives. Staying within the boundary of the Redeemer's field is the place of security and blessing.

• Sufficiency of Provision: Ruth does not need to wander or "cross over" into other fields in search of sustenance. The Redeemer's field contains more than enough.

• Community and Fellowship: Boaz commands Ruth to stay close to his young women. The instruction not to cross over is an invitation to remain in covenant community rather than returning to isolated vulnerability.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:8, Boaz uses two different verbs translated as "go" in the NKJV. First, he says, "Do not go (halak) to glean in another field." Halak is the general verb for walking or going. Second, he says, "nor go (abar) from here."

By switching to abar, Boaz intensifies his instructions. He is not merely telling her not to choose another field to walk in; he is telling her not to even step across the boundary lines of his estate. In the lawless era of the Judges, a young, foreign woman gleaning alone was highly susceptible to physical assault and exploitation. By instructing her not to cross over his boundary, Boaz sets up a protective sanctuary. Her safety is directly tied to her remaining within the spatial limits of his grace and ownership.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Christian life, there is a constant temptation to "cross over" the boundaries of God's clear Word and provision in search of security, acceptance, or pleasure. Like the prodigal son who crossed over the boundary of his father's house into a "far country," believers often find that crossing God's boundaries leads to spiritual famine and danger. Boaz’s exhortation to Ruth is a beautiful Old Testament picture of Christ's New Testament command to "abide" in Him (John 15:4). When we remain within the "field" of His will and Word, we find absolute protection and superabundant grace.
