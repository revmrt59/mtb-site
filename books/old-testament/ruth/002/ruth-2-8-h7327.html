RUTH - ( Re'uth , Strong's H7327 )

Summary:

The name Ruth (Re'uth) signifies "friendship," "companion," or "desirable," perfectly encapsulating her character and her redemptive journey. In Ruth 2:8, when Boaz addresses her by name, it highlights her transition from an isolated foreign widow to a cherished companion under the wings of the God of Israel. Her name reflects the covenant loyalty (chesed) she extended to Naomi, showing that true companionship is rooted in self-sacrificing faith.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The name Re'uth is closely linked to the Hebrew root ra'ah, which means to associate with, to shepherd, or to act as a companion or friend. It is also related to the noun re'a, meaning a friend, neighbor, or companion.

• Historically and culturally, names in the ancient Near East carried profound predictive or descriptive significance. "Ruth" represents the quintessential companion—one who clings to another not out of obligation or legal contract, but out of covenant loyalty. This is vivid against the historical backdrop of Moab, a nation traditionally hostile to Israel, showing how a faithful "friend" can arise from an unexpected place.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Re'uth primarily means "friendship," "companion," "association," or "female companion."

• In the context of Hebrew onomastics (the study of names), it functions as a proper noun denoting the Moabite daughter-in-law of Naomi.

• The semantic root points to relational warmth, mutual support, and a bond that is chosen rather than merely biological.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The verbal root ra'ah depicts a shepherd tending a flock, emphasizing care, guidance, and provision. The noun form re'a depicts a close friend with whom one shares life and secrets.

• Ruth's character is a living word picture of her name. When Orpah kissed Naomi and returned to her gods, Ruth "clung" (dabaq) to Naomi (Ruth 1:14). This physical action of "clinging" is the outward manifestation of her name, Re'uth (companion/friend).

• In Ruth 2:8, when Boaz addresses her by name, he acknowledges this established reputation of companionship and loyalty to Naomi.

4. Theological Implications

• Ruth’s name and character illustrate the expansive scope of God’s covenant mercy (chesed). A Moabitess, excluded by the law from the assembly of Yahweh to the tenth generation (Deuteronomy 23:3), becomes the ultimate "friend" and companion to an Israelite widow, and ultimately a mother in the line of David and Jesus Christ.

• This highlights the theological truth that faith and covenant loyalty transcend national and ethnic boundaries. It prefigures the inclusion of the Gentiles into the household of God, transforming former enemies into "friends" and companions of the covenant.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:8, Boaz meets Ruth for the first time in his field. By addressing her as "Ruth," he treats her not as an anonymous, disposable foreign laborer, but as an individual of dignity whose reputation of self-sacrificing friendship has preceded her.

• The juxtaposition of Boaz calling her "Ruth" (friend/companion) and then "my daughter" signals a transition from her status as an outcast alien to a protected, welcomed member of the community.

• Boaz's command to "stay close by my young women" echoes the meaning of her name; she is to find companionship and safety among his household, reflecting how God provides companionship to the lonely.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:14 - "Then they lifted up their voices and wept again; and Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her."

• Ruth 1:16 - "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, or to turn back from following after you...'"

• Ruth 2:22 - "And Naomi said to Ruth her daughter-in-law, 'It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women...'"

• Ruth 4:13 - "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The book of Ruth is read annually by the Jewish community during Shavuot (Pentecost), celebrating both the harvest and the giving of the Torah. The story of Ruth the "friend" teaches that the Torah is ultimately fulfilled through acts of lovingkindness (chesed).

• Ruth's companionship was not passive. It was active, initiative-taking, and hardworking. Her friendship to Naomi was demonstrated through laboring in the hot sun of the barley harvest, turning her name into a verb of active devotion.
