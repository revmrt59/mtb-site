FIELD - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open field, cultivated land, or a defined plot of ground used for agricultural purposes such as crops or pasture. In the agrarian society of ancient Israel, the sadeh was the primary source of life, sustenance, and labor. In the context of Ruth 2:8, Boaz commands Ruth not to glean in any other sadeh, marking his specific field as a place of divine appointment, physical safety, and abundant grace under the wings of the God of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• In the ancient Near East, a sadeh was not merely empty acreage, but the vital boundary of life and survival for a household or clan.

• Cultivated fields in ancient Israel were often large, contiguous tracts of fertile land. Instead of fences, individual family plots (chelqah) were demarcated by simple boundary stones.

• Because these fields lacked physical barriers, laborers, travelers, and animals could easily pass from one person's property to another. This open layout made vulnerable people—such as widows, orphans, and foreigners—highly susceptible to harassment or exploitation when wandering searchingly across different properties.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word sadeh denotes open country, a cultivated field, or a specific territory.

• It refers to land that is tilled for growing grains (like wheat and barley), vines, or vegetables, contrasting with the wilderness or walled cities.

• It designates pastureland where flocks are kept, or the hunting grounds of wild beasts.

• It represents a regional territory belonging to a specific nation or people group, such as the "field of Moab."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• A Place of Vulnerability or Safety: The open field is depicted in Scripture as a place where one is exposed to either great danger (such as Cain attacking Abel) or exceptional favor and protection.

• The Soil of Blessing or Judgment: The condition of the sadeh reflects the spiritual state of the nation. Under God's blessing, the fields yield abundantly; under covenant disobedience, the fields wither, suffer drought, or are devoured by locusts.

• Inheritance and Identity: A family's sadeh was their permanent covenant holding in the Promised Land. It was never to be permanently sold, representing their ongoing legacy and portion within Yahweh's land.

4. Theological Implications

• The Sanctuary of the Poor: God instituted the law of gleaning (Leviticus 19:9-10; 23:22), which restricted landowners from harvesting the very edges of their sadeh. This command transformed private property into a venue for public mercy, showcasing God's care for the marginalized.

• The Stage of Divine Providence: The sadeh is where human helplessness meets divine sovereignty. Ruth "happened" to find her way to the field of Boaz. What appeared to be a random choice of location was God's silent orchestration to bring the widow of Mahlon to her redeemer.

• The Soil of Redemption: The physical field of Boaz becomes the ground where the legal, spiritual, and marital redemption of Ruth is initiated.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:8, Boaz speaks with fatherly care, telling Ruth, "do you not go to glean in another field (sadeh)."

• By instructing her to stay in his sadeh, Boaz is offering her more than just barley; he is offering her sanctuary. The open fields of other landowners carried the threat of physical abuse, verbal assault, or rejection for a foreign Moabitess.

• Boaz's sadeh represents a domain of covenant faithfulness (chesed). Here, the law of gleaning is not performed grudgingly but is exceeded by generous hospitality, where Ruth is welcomed to drink from the water jars and eat with the harvesters.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:8: "Now Cain talked with Abel his brother; and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him."

• Levitus 19:9: "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, nor shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest."

• Ruth 2:3: "Then she left, and went and gleaned in the field after the reapers. And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was of the family of Elimelech."

• Proverbs 24:30: "I went by the field of the lazy man, and by the vineyard of the man devoid of understanding;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The literal sadeh where Ruth gleans in chapter 2 anticipates the legal transaction involving the sadeh in chapter 4. When Naomi must sell the "piece of land" (sadeh) belonging to Elimelech, Boaz steps forward as the kinsman-redeemer (goel) to acquire the field and, in doing so, acquires Ruth as his wife to perpetuate the name of the dead. The agricultural field of labor thus becomes the redeemed inheritance of grace.
