LISTEN - ( shama , Strong's H8085 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shama goes far beyond the mere physical sensation of sound waves hitting the eardrum. It encompasses a holistic response of active attention, internalization, and obedient action. To "listen" or "hear" in the Old Testament is to heed the speaker’s voice with a heart ready to comply. In the context of Ruth 2:8, Boaz uses this word to arrest Ruth's attention, inviting her not just to hear his words but to accept his protection, provision, and pastoral care. It marks a critical turning point where the hearer is drawn into a covenantal relationship of safety under the wings of the Redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, hearing and doing are functionally inseparable. There is no unique, abstract Hebrew word that translates simply to "obey" as a separate mental construct; rather, obedience is shama in action. The ear is viewed as the organ of cognitive reception, intellectual processing, and volitional submission. When a superior speaks to an inferior, or when God speaks to His people, to shama is to incline the heart, receive the instruction, and walk in alignment with it. The word picture is one of bowing the ear to receive the words of a master, recognizing that true listening manifests in the movement of the feet and hands.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shama means to hear, listen to, pay attention, understand, and obey. It carries the primary sense of perceiving sound, but transitions rapidly into cognitive comprehension and behavioral response. In grammatical constructions, when paired with the preposition b (literally, "to listen to the voice of"), it functions as the standard term for "to obey." In Ruth 2:8, the verb appears in the QAAL stem as a gentle but firm rhetorical question, meaning "Have you not heard?" or "You will listen, will you not?" This construction serves as an earnest appeal for undivided attention, signifying that the words to follow are of vital importance to the listener's well-being.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

As one of the most frequently used verbs in the Old Testament, occurring over 1,100 times, shama establishes communication as the primary basis of relationship between God and humanity.

• The Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4): "Hear, O Israel..." is the foundational confession of the covenant community, demanding a lifestyle of attentive obedience.

• God's Hearing: When God is the subject of the verb, it indicates He does not merely register sound, but actively responds to the cries of His people (Exodus 2:24).

• The Opened Ear: Scripture uses the picture of God "digging" or "opening" the ear to describe a heart made ready to receive divine instruction (Psalm 40:6).

• The Closed Ear: Refusing to listen is depicted as stiffening the neck or stopping the ears, which leads to spiritual ruin (Zechariah 7:11-12).

4. Theological Implications

The theology of hearing stands in stark contrast to the visual idolatry of the ancient Near East. While pagan religions focused on seeing the divine through physical images, biblical faith is rooted in hearing the spoken Word of God.

• Faith and Obedience: True worship requires the surrender of the will through hearing. Sacrifice without shama is empty and rejected by God (1 Samuel 15:22).

• Covenant Security: Hearing is the gateway to covenantal blessing. To listen to the voice of the Lord is to enter into His sphere of protection and provision.

• Christological Type: In the book of Ruth, Boaz acts as the kinsman-redeemer (goel), a vivid type of Jesus Christ. When Boaz commands Ruth to "listen," he models the Savior who calls the weary, vulnerable stranger to find permanent refuge and spiritual sustenance exclusively within His fields of grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:8, Boaz addresses Ruth: "You will listen, my daughter, will you not? Do not go to glean in another field..."

Ruth is a foreign Moabite, highly vulnerable, destitute, and working in a potentially dangerous environment. Boaz speaks to her with fatherly tenderness, calling her "my daughter," and issues a protective instruction. By using shama, Boaz is not merely making a polite suggestion. He is commanding her attention for her own safety.

To "listen" to Boaz means she must cease looking for other sources of provision, remain in his fields, and trust his oversight. It marks her transition from an exposed outsider to a protected member of his household. Her willing obedience to his voice secures her physical survival and paves the way for her redemption.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:18: "In your seed all the nations of the earth shall be blessed, because you have obeyed My voice."

• Exodus 19:5: "Now therefore, if you will indeed obey My voice and keep My covenant, then you shall be a special treasure to Me above all people; for all the earth is Mine."

• 1 Samuel 15:22: "So Samuel said: 'Has the Lord as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, As in obeying the voice of the Lord? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, And to heed than the fat of rams.'"

• Jeremiah 7:23: "But this is what I commanded them, saying, 'Obey My voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be My people. And walk in all the ways that I have commanded you, that it may be well with you.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) translates shama in Ruth 2:8 using the Greek verb akouo, which also carries the double meaning of hearing and obeying.

For the gospel teacher, this passage illustrates the necessity of exclusive devotion to the Redeemer's voice. Just as Ruth was tempted to seek grain in other fields, believers are often tempted to seek satisfaction, safety, and validation in the "fields" of the world. Hearing the voice of Jesus Christ demands that we remain close to His presence, walk alongside His servants, and refuse to wander into environments that threaten our spiritual life.
