GO - ( halak , Strong's H1980 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb halak fundamentally means to walk, go, or proceed, serving as a foundational biblical metaphor for one's lifestyle, conduct, and relationship with God. In Ruth 2:9, Boaz uses this word to extend extraordinary protection and provision to Ruth, instructing her to "go" after his female workers and to "go" freely to the water jars drawn by his young men. This simple verb of movement transitions from a physical action to a profound demonstration of covenant-keeping grace, showing how God provides safe passage and refreshment for those who take refuge under His wings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root halak is one of the most frequent verbs in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over 1,500 times. It paints a concrete, kinetic picture of physical movement—putting one foot in front of the other to traverse physical space.

• In the Ancient Near East, walking was the primary mode of travel, making the verb deeply experiential. It describes the physical movement of people, animals, flowing water, and even the wind.

• Culturally, "walking" or "going" with someone implied fellowship, alignment, and shared destiny. Thus, the physical act of walking naturally became the primary Hebrew idiom for a person’s ethical conduct, spiritual direction, and lifestyle.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb halak carries a broad semantic range centered on physical and metaphorical progression:

• To walk, go, travel, or journey physically.

• To depart, go away, or vanish.

• To behave, live, or conduct oneself (metaphorically walking in truth, law, or wickedness).

• To bring, carry, or lead (in causative stems).

• In Ruth 2:9, the word is used in the Qal aspect as an imperative and a perfect with waw consecutive (functioning as an instruction and an invitation), directing Ruth to physical locations of safety ("go after them") and provision ("go to the vessels").

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Progression: It describes everyday movement, such as Abraham going to the place God would show him (Genesis 12:1).

• Spiritual Walk: It characterizes Enoch and Noah "walking with God," representing intimate, loyal fellowship (Genesis 5:24; 6:9).

• The Walk of Faith: God commands Abraham to "walk before Me and be blameless" (Genesis 17:1), representing a life lived under the constant, conscious gaze of Yahweh.

• Flowing Water: It describes the continuous movement of rivers (Genesis 2:14) or the spreading of a sweet aroma (Hosea 14:6).

4. Theological Implications

• In the Old Testament, faith is not a static mental assent but an active, physical walk. God's people do not merely contemplate the truth; they walk in it.

• The command to "go" is frequently connected to God's covenantal guidance and protection. When God commands someone to go, He promises His presence (e.g., Moses in Exodus 3:12; Joshua in Joshua 1:9).

• In the context of the theology of redemption, halak highlights the shift from aimless wandering or walking in darkness to purposeful movement under the safety of divine covenantal care. Boaz, acting as a type of the Kinsman-Redeemer, mirrors God’s heart by directing Ruth’s going away from vulnerability and toward absolute security.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:9, Boaz uses halak twice. First, he commands Ruth, "go after them" (referring to his female young workers), ensuring her integration into a safe working environment where she is protected from harassment. Second, he tells her, "when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink."

• This second use is culturally staggering. Gleaners were marginalized, impoverished outsiders who had to wait for everyone else or gather water themselves under grueling heat. For Boaz to invite a foreign Moabite widow to "go" and drink from the water drawn by his own young men was an act of lavish, barrier-breaking grace.

• The word halak here bridges the gap between Ruth’s status as a vulnerable foreigner and her new status as a protected beneficiary of Israel's covenant. Her physical steps to the water jars represent her active reception of grace and safety.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:1 - "Now the LORD had said to Abram: 'Get out of your country, from your family and from your father’s house, to a land that I will show you.'" ("Get out" is halak)

• Deuteronomy 8:6 - "Therefore you shall keep the commandments of the LORD your God, to walk in His ways and to fear Him." ("walk" is halak)

• Psalm 1:1 - "Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the ungodly, nor stands in the path of sinners, nor sits in the seat of the scornful;" ("walks" is halak)

• Psalm 23:4 - "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; for You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me." ("walk" is halak)

• Proverbs 10:9 - "He who walks with integrity walks securely, but he who perverts his ways will become known." ("walks... walks" are both forms of halak)

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The grammatical construction in Ruth 2:9 (wehalakt - "and you shall go") functions as a strong permission that carries the weight of a warm invitation. Boaz is removing every social and cultural obstacle that would prevent Ruth from accessing refreshment.

• In Jewish tradition, the body of Jewish law is called Halakha (derived from halak), meaning "the way of walking." It denotes how a person translates the Torah into daily physical steps and moral actions.
