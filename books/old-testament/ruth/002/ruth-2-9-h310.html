AFTER - ( 'achar , Strong's H310 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'achar primarily functions as a preposition, adverb, or conjunction meaning "behind," "after," or "afterwards." In spatial contexts, it indicates following behind someone or something, while in temporal contexts, it refers to events occurring after a specific point in time. In Ruth 2:9, Boaz uses 'achar to instruct Ruth to follow closely behind his young female workers as they harvest. This directive is not merely locational; it is a pastoral provision of safety, community, and divine favor, positioning Ruth within the protective custody of Boaz's household.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb behind 'achar means to remain behind, to delay, or to tarry. The concrete, physical image of 'achar is the posture of being at the back of something or following in the footsteps of another. It evokes the picture of a journey where one party leads and another follows in close proximity, tracing the path already trodden. In the agrarian culture of the ancient Near East, this word was woven into the daily rhythm of harvesting. Reapers cut the grain, binders tied the sheaves, and the poor and vulnerable walked behind them—at their heels—to gather what remained.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

As a spatial preposition, 'achar denotes the physical position of being behind, at the rear of, or following after. When applied to movement, it expresses the act of pursuing, accompanying, or following a leader. As a temporal preposition, it means after, afterwards, or in the future, marking a chronological sequence of events. In its plural construct form ('achare), it literally translates to "the backs of," emphasizing a close, protective, and submissive alignment with the one leading.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Relational Devotion: The term is frequently used in spiritual contexts to describe a person's allegiance. To "walk after" Yahweh is a standard Old Testament idiom for obedience, covenant loyalty, and worship. Conversely, to "walk after" other gods represents spiritual adultery and disobedience.

• Physical Proximity and Protection: It describes soldiers marching behind a commander, or sheep following a shepherd. In these settings, being "after" the leader ensures that the follower remains under the leader’s authority and protective shield.

• Historical Succession: In historical narratives, 'achar marks transitions of power or life stages, indicating what took place after the death of a patriarch, king, or leader.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of 'achar in the Old Testament rests on the concepts of discipleship, submission, and divine order.

• The Path of Grace: God commands His people to walk behind Him, indicating that He is the pathfinder, protector, and provider. The follower does not forge the path but trusts the footprints of the Leader.

• The Law of Provision: Under the Mosaic Covenant, God structured the harvests so that the marginalized could find sustenance by walking behind the harvesters. The physical position of being "after" became the very place where the poor experienced the grace and provision of God.

• Humility and Exaltation: To go "after" others requires a posture of humility. Ruth willingly accepted the lowliest position—walking behind the servants—yet this path of humility was the exact avenue God used to bring her into redemption and the lineage of the Messiah.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, Boaz tells Ruth, "Let your eyes be on the field which they reap, and go after them."

• A Safe Boundary: The feminine plural pronoun "them" refers specifically to Boaz’s young female workers. By telling Ruth to go "after" them, Boaz is establishing a safe zone. Ruth is not to wander into other fields or walk behind the male harvesters where she might be vulnerable to mistreatment.

• Covenant Inclusion: In the ancient world, gleaners were often treated as social outcasts. By anchoring Ruth "after" his own workers, Boaz elevates her status from a vulnerable foreigner to an protected associate of his household.

• Providential Leading: Ruth’s obedience to this instruction keeps her in the very field where she will meet her kinsman-redeemer. Her physical positioning "after" the maidens preserves her safety and aligns her with the unfolding plan of redemption.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:1 - "After these things the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward.'"

• Deuteronomy 13:4 - "You shall walk after the LORD your God and fear Him, and keep His commandments and obey His voice; you shall serve Him and hold fast to Him."

• Ruth 2:2 - "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The spatial use of 'achar in Ruth 2:9 highlights the beauty of biblical community. Ruth did not glean in isolation; she was joined to a group. For the pastor or teacher, this highlights that God’s provision is rarely solitary. He places the vulnerable in community, instructing them to follow those who are already established under His care. Walking "after" others in the faith provides mentorship, accountability, and physical protection.
