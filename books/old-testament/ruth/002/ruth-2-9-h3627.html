VESSELS - ( keli , Strong's H3627 )

Summary:

In Ruth 2:9, the word "vessels" refers to the water jars or storage containers used by Boaz's harvest workers. By granting Ruth, a foreign outsider, free access to these jars, Boaz extends an extraordinary level of grace and protection. This simple act of hospitality represents the abundant, unmerited provision of God for those who seek refuge under His wings.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun keli derives from the root kalah, which means to end, finish, complete, or prepare. A keli is a broad term for something completed or prepared for a specific practical task. It represents any physical object fashioned by human hands to serve as a tool, utensil, container, or weapon.

In the agricultural setting of ancient Israel, the word typically referred to large clay pottery jars used for transporting and storing water, wine, or grain. Drawing water in the arid climate of Judah was grueling, labor-intensive work, usually requiring trips to a deep communal well. To have a filled keli at the edge of the field was a vital resource for survival during the hot harvest season.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of keli is incredibly broad, encompassing:

• An article, utensil, or vessel for domestic use (pottery, jars, plates).

• A tool or implement used for work (farming equipment, building tools).

• A weapon or armor used in warfare (swords, shields, quivers).

• An instrument of music (harps, lyres).

• A piece of furniture or sacred equipment used in temple worship.

In the specific context of Ruth 2:9, the word functions as a concrete noun denoting water jars or drinking vessels holding fresh water drawn from the local well by Boaz's hired servants.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Because keli is a general term for an instrument or container, the Old Testament employs it in several vivid word pictures:

• The Potter's Clay: The vessel is a picture of human vulnerability and divine sovereignty. A clay vessel is fragile, easily broken, but entirely subject to the design of the artisan who fashions it.

• Instruments of War: The term describes the physical weapons used to carry out battle, illustrating human agency in spiritual or physical conflict.

• Holy Utensils: In the tabernacle and temple, keli refers to the dedicated gold and bronze vessels used to transport and offer sacrifices, symbolizing objects set apart exclusively for divine service.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of the keli in Ruth 2:9 highlights the theology of covenant kindness (chesed).

• Unmerited Grace: Ruth was a Moabitess, an outsider with no legal right to the fruits of the harvest, let alone the water drawn by Boaz’s workers. By telling her to drink from the vessels, Boaz bypasses social and ethnic barriers to offer her life-sustaining refreshment.

• The Providential Provider: Boaz acts as a redeemer-figure (a type of Christ), who provides the vessels of refreshment for the weary traveler. The believer, like Ruth, comes to the field of the Lord empty-handed and is invited to drink freely from provisions prepared by others.

• Earthen Vessels: The physical jars were ordinary, breakable clay pots, yet they held the water that sustained life. This prefigures the New Testament reality where God deposits His spiritual treasures into fragile, human vessels.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, Boaz says, "And when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink what the young men have drawn."

During the ancient harvest, gleaners were at the very bottom of the social ladder. They were vulnerable to abuse and were typically forced to wait until all the landowner's workers had finished drinking before they could access water—or they had to draw their own under the midday sun.

Boaz reverses this social order. He commands his young men not to touch Ruth, protecting her from harassment, and then grants her immediate access to the communal water jars. The phrase "what the young men have drawn" emphasizes that Ruth is benefiting from labor she did not perform. She is treated not as a beggar, but as an honored guest of the household.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 2 Kings 4:3: "Then he said, 'Go, borrow vessels from everywhere, from all your neighbors—empty vessels; do not gather just a few.'"

• Jeremiah 18:4: "And the vessel that he made of clay was marred in the hand of the potter; so he made it again into another vessel, as it seemed good to the potter to make."

• Psalm 31:12: "I am forgotten like a dead man, out of mind; I am like a broken vessel."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), keli is regularly translated by the Greek word skeuos.

This connects directly to the New Testament usage where Paul describes believers as "earthen vessels" (skeuos) in 2 Corinthians 4:7: "But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellence of the power may be of God and not of us."

Just as the agricultural workers in Ruth's day relied on ordinary clay jars to hold the water that kept them alive in the fields, the Lord places the life-giving treasure of the Gospel into fragile, ordinary human beings to display His sovereign power and grace.
