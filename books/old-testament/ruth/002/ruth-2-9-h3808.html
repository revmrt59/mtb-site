NOT - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' represents an absolute, permanent, and objective negation. Unlike subjective or temporary negatives, lo' establishes an unyielding boundary or an objective fact. In Ruth 2:9, this small but powerful word is used twice: first, to establish an indisputable fact ("Have I not commanded..."), and second, to issue an absolute prohibition to protect Ruth ("...not to touch you"). Through this word, Boaz establishes an inviolable hedge of protection around Ruth, transforming her status from a vulnerable, foreign gleaner to a highly guarded member of his household, reflecting God’s ultimate covenantal protection over those who take refuge under His wings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The adverb lo' is the primary negative particle in Biblical Hebrew. In the ancient Near East, the agricultural landscape was filled with physical and social dangers, especially for vulnerable, foreign widows like Ruth who had no male protector. In such a context, a simple request or situational guideline would not suffice to ensure her safety. Biblical Hebrew makes a sharp distinction between temporary, situational prohibitions and absolute, permanent prohibitions. By using lo', the language establishes a permanent boundary line—a verbal hedge that cannot be crossed without severe consequences. It functions like an iron wall, guaranteeing protection.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The particle lo' functions as the absolute negative adverb. It expresses objective negation, denial, or absolute prohibition. Unlike the subjective negative al, which is used for temporary, immediate, or emotional appeals, lo' denotes an unchanging and objective state of affairs.

• When paired with an imperfect verb, it forms an absolute prohibition, meaning "you shall never" or "it is absolutely forbidden."

• When combined with the interrogative particle (halo'), it creates a rhetorical question that demands an emphatic, positive answer, meaning "is it not an established, undeniable fact?"

• In its essence, it denotes complete exclusion, leaving no room for negotiation, compromise, or exception.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Unalterable Law: The primary word picture of lo' is that of a boundary stone or a locked gate. Just as a boundary stone cannot be moved, a command negated by lo' cannot be altered or set aside.

• The Rhetorical Shield: In its interrogative form (halo'), it serves as a shield of truth. It brings an undeniable reality into the light, forcing the listeners to acknowledge a binding verbal decree that has already been established.

• Absolute Safety: By commanding the young men lo' to touch her, Boaz turns his field into a sanctuary. The verb "touch" (naga') often carries the connotation of striking, harming, or molesting. The negation lo' completely forbids any form of physical harm or harassment, setting Ruth apart as untouchable.

4. Theological Implications

The absolute negation of lo' in the book of Ruth mirrors the covenantal protection of Yahweh. In the Old Testament, God’s most solemn promises and moral boundaries are framed with this absolute negative (such as the Ten Commandments). When Boaz uses this word to protect Ruth, he acts as a human agent of divine providence. This demonstrates that under the wings of the Redeemer, safety is not tentative, partial, or conditional; it is absolute. The theological reality of lo' is that when God prohibits harm against His chosen ones, the prohibition is permanent and absolute. It points forward to the security of the believer, who is kept by a divine decree that cannot be broken.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, the double occurrence of lo' defines the atmosphere of Ruth's safety. First, Boaz asks, "Have I not (halo') commanded the young men...?" Boaz appeals to a public, binding order he has already issued. This is not a private suggestion; it is a legally binding decree on his estate. Second, he forbids them "not (lo') to touch you." In the historical context of the period of the Judges—a lawless era characterized by social decay and the mistreatment of the weak—Boaz’s absolute prohibition stands out as an oasis of righteous order. He strips the young men of any license to mistreat her. This word elevates Ruth from an unprotected outsider to a highly guarded daughter of the household.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 20:13 - "You shall not murder." (The absolute, permanent moral prohibition of lo').

• Joshua 1:5 - "No man shall be able to stand before you all the days of your life; as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not leave you nor forsake you." (An absolute, unbreakable covenantal promise of God's presence).

• Psalm 23:1 - "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want." (The absolute assurance of complete spiritual and physical provision under the Shepherd's care).

• Psalm 121:3 - "He will not allow your foot to be moved; He who keeps you will not slumber." (The absolute, unwavering vigilance of God’s protective watch).

7. Other Relevant Insights

In pastoral exposition, contrasting lo' with the other Hebrew negative particle, al, provides profound clarity. While al represents a personal, immediate plea (often translated "please do not"), lo' represents an objective, legal, and eternal reality. When Boaz speaks to the young men, he does not say al (which would imply a temporary request), but lo', establishing an unalterable rule of conduct. This highlights the character of Boaz as a man of absolute righteousness and authority, and it assures Ruth that her protection does not depend on the shifting moods of the workers, but on an immutable decree.
