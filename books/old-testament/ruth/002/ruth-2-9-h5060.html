TOUCH - ( naga , Strong's H5060 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb naga primarily means to touch, reach, or strike. In Ruth 2:9, Boaz uses this word in a protective, legal sense, strictly commanding his young workmen not to touch, molest, or harm Ruth. This usage highlights Ruth's vulnerability as a foreign widow in the harvest fields and showcases Boaz's character as a righteous guardian who provides immediate physical safety alongside economic provision.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of naga begins with the act of laying a hand upon someone or something, establishing physical contact. In the ancient agrarian world, harvest fields were often places of intense labor and social mixing, where vulnerable individuals—particularly foreign, impoverished women gleaning on the margins—were highly susceptible to exploitation, physical harassment, and sexual assault. By utilizing naga, Boaz establishes a strict physical boundary around Ruth. The word picture is one of a protective barrier; Boaz draws a line in the dirt around this vulnerable stranger, declaring her off-limits to the physical advances or aggression of his workers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb naga carries a semantic range centered on physical contact, meaning to touch, reach, strike, or lay a hand upon. In the Qal stem, it denotes making contact with an object, person, or boundary. Depending on the context, this touch can be gentle, violent, or illicit. In contexts involving vulnerable individuals or relationships, naga frequently carries the specific nuance of touching with intent to harm, abuse, molest, or violate. In Ruth 2:9, the infinitive construct functions as a strong prohibition, legally and socially forbidding the young men from committing any act of physical harassment or violation against Ruth.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, naga paints several vivid word pictures:

• Violent Contact or Divine Striking: The word often describes God "striking" or "touching" someone with disease, plague, or calamity, such as when God touched Job’s bone and flesh, or touched the hollow of Jacob's thigh.

• Proximity and Reaching: It describes physical boundaries or objects reaching to a certain height or distance, such as the wings of the cherubim touching the walls of the temple.

• Illicit or Sexual Violation: In interpersonal contexts, the word serves as a euphemism or direct term for physical violation, sexual harassment, or touching a woman in a prohibited manner. It indicates a breach of personal boundary and moral law.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, naga illustrates the concept of covenant protection and the holiness of boundaries. In the Levitical law, touching an unclean thing made a person unclean, showing that contact transfers status. Conversely, God warns foreign nations not to "touch" His anointed ones, indicating that God extends a canopy of protection over those who belong to Him. When Boaz forbids his men to touch Ruth, he acts as an instrument of divine providence. This reflects the heart of God, who protects the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless. It shows that true godliness (chesed) does not merely offer passive pity, but actively restrains the wicked to create a safe sanctuary for the vulnerable.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, the use of naga is highly specific and urgent. Ruth is an outsider—a Moabitess—with no male protector in the field. Under normal circumstances, she would be at the mercy of the field hands. Boaz’s command, "Have I not charged the young men not to touch you?" is a preemptive defense. Boaz recognizes the systemic dangers a woman in her position faces. His charge is not a casual request but a binding legal decree. The context reveals that before Boaz feeds Ruth or offers her grain, he secures her physical dignity. He transforms a potentially hostile work environment into a place of redemption and peace.

• Psalm 105:15: "Saying, 'Do not touch My anointed ones, and do My prophets no harm.'"

• Proverbs 6:29: "So is he who goes in to his neighbor's wife; whoever touches her shall not be innocent."

7. Other Relevant Insights

For pastors and teachers, the protective use of naga in Ruth 2:9 provides a powerful illustration of the church's role in the world. Just as Boaz used his authority, resources, and words to ensure that the vulnerable were not touched or harmed in his field, spiritual leaders are called to establish safe environments. This passage challenges the church to protect the weak from abuse, manipulation, and harassment. True biblical leadership, modeled by Boaz and fulfilled in Christ, secures the safety and preserves the dignity of those who seek refuge under the wings of the Lord.
