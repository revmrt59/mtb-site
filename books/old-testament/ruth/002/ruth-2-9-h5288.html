YOUNG MEN - ( na'ar , Strong's H5288 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'ar refers to a young, mobile, and active male, ranging from infancy to early manhood, often carrying the functional designation of a servant, attendant, or laborer. In the pastoral setting of Ruth 2:9, it denotes the young male agricultural laborers under Boaz's employ who reaped the harvest and drew water. Boaz’s protective command to these young men highlights his righteousness and provides a safe haven for Ruth, transforming a potentially hazardous workspace into a place of divine provision and security.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, the agricultural cycle of harvesting grain was a communal, labor-intensive effort. The ne'arim (the plural form of na'ar) represented the physical strength of the workforce. These young men were responsible for the heavy labor of cutting the standing grain, binding the sheaves, and drawing water from deep wells to sustain the workers in the intense heat. Because of their youth, physical strength, and relative lack of social oversight in the fields, they could also pose a threat to vulnerable outsiders. The harvest field was a place of social interaction, but without a strong master’s oversight, it was a place of danger for an unprotected, foreign widow like Ruth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun na'ar occurs over 230 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its primary meaning denotes a young male from infancy to the age of marriage and independence. The semantic range is broad and is determined by context:

• It can refer to an infant or young child, such as the baby Moses in the basket (Exodus 2:6).

• It can refer to a teenager or adolescent, such as Joseph at seventeen years of age (Genesis 37:2).

• It frequently designates a personal attendant, servant, or armor-bearer, representing status rather than strictly chronological age (1 Samuel 14:1).

• In agricultural contexts, it refers to young, hired laborers or domestic servants who carry out their master’s orders.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term na'ar carries a word picture of vigor, potential, and subordination.

• Vigor and Strength: The ne'arim are those in the prime of their physical strength. They are capable of enduring the grueling sun, reaping fields, and carrying heavy water jars.

• Subordination: A na'ar is always under authority. In Ruth, these young men are answerable to the foreman (the na'ar set over the reapers) and ultimately to Boaz. Their energy is harnessed for the productivity of the household.

• A Picture of Protection and Order: When a master governs his ne'arim with righteousness, they become instruments of blessing and safety rather than exploitation.

4. Theological Implications

The treatment of the vulnerable by the strong is a major theological theme in Scripture. Under the Mosaic Law, the poor, the widow, and the stranger were to be protected and allowed to glean in the corners of the fields (Leviticus 19:9-10). Boaz’s command to his ne'arim demonstrates the practical application of this law.

• The Character of God: God is the defender of the widow and the fatherless. Boaz acts as the human instrument of God's wing of protection.

• The Typology of Boaz: Boaz serves as a type of Christ (the ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer). Just as Boaz commands his young men to ensure Ruth's safety and refreshment, Christ commands His household to protect, care for, and nourish those who seek refuge in Him.

• Sanctified Labor: The workplace becomes a place of holy stewardship when the authority structure ensures the dignity and safety of the weakest worker.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, the word na'ar is used twice. Boaz tells Ruth, "Have I not commanded the young men (ne'arim) not to touch you? And when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink from what the young men (ne'arim) have drawn."

• Safety from Harassment: In that culture, a foreign woman gleaning alone was highly susceptible to assault or verbal abuse. Boaz's explicit command "not to touch you" (which carries a strong sense of physical or sexual harassment) drew a protective boundary around Ruth.

• Radical Hospitality: Drawing water from deep wells was exhausting work. Typically, a foreign gleaner would never be permitted to drink the water drawn by the estate's domestic servants. Boaz reverses the social order. The strong, laboring ne'arim are ordered to provide refreshment for a destitute Moabite widow, transforming her status from an outcast to an honored guest.

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a subtle contrast in the Book of Ruth between the ne'arim (the young working servants of Boaz) and the bachurim (young men of marriageable age). Later in the narrative, Boaz commends Ruth, saying, "You have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning, in that you did not go after young men (bachurim), whether poor or rich" (Ruth 3:10). While na'ar focuses on service, youth, and labor within the household, bachur focuses on youth in terms of physical appeal, military age, and eligibility for marriage. Ruth bypassed the younger, eligible men to seek redemption through Boaz, showing her commitment to her family's covenant line.
