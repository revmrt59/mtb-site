EYES - ( 'ayin , Strong's H5869 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ayin refers literally to the physical organ of sight, but it frequently serves as a rich metaphor for perception, attention, favor, and mental or spiritual focus. In the context of Ruth 2:9, Boaz instructs Ruth to keep her "eyes" on the field being reaped, signifying not merely passive observation but active, focused attention for the sake of her safety, guidance, and integration into his household's labor. It represents a protective gaze and a directed path of grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The concrete noun 'ayin fundamentally denotes the physical eye, one of the most vital sensory organs. In the ancient Near East, the eye was viewed not just as a passive receiver of light, but as an active projector of a person's inner disposition, intent, and character. To set one's eyes upon something or someone implies directing one's full mind, desire, and purpose toward that object.

• Additionally, 'ayin is homonymous with the word for a spring or well of water, beautifully linking the concept of physical sight with a source of life, refreshment, and outward-flowing vitality.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word 'ayin has a broad semantic range:

• The physical eye of humans or animals, used for seeing and weeping.

• Mental and spiritual faculty of perception, understanding, and judgment (cognition).

• An expression of attitude, such as favor ("finding favor in the eyes of"), pity, pride, or malice ("evil eye").

• A geographical feature denoting a spring or natural well of water.

• In Ruth 2:9, the term denotes directed attention, observation, and cognitive focus. It carries the force of keeping one's gaze fixed on a safe and purposeful path of labor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Favor and Judgment: The phrase "to find favor in the eyes of" is a common idiomatic expression where "eyes" represent the seat of judgment and estimation. To find favor in someone's eyes is to be assessed as pleasing or worthy of grace.

• The Eye as a Spring: The dual meaning of 'ayin as both "eye" and "spring" conveys a beautiful picture of the eye as a wellspring of the heart's true condition.

• Gaze of Protection: Setting one's eyes on someone can mean watching over them with protective care, mirroring God's vigilant eyes over His people.

4. Theological Implications

• In the Old Testament, the "eyes of Yahweh" represent His omniscience, sovereign care, and righteous judgment. God's eyes run to and fro throughout the earth to strengthen those whose hearts are loyal to Him.

• In the book of Ruth, human eyes often reflect divine providence. When Boaz directs Ruth’s eyes to the field, he acts as the earthly instrument of God’s protective oversight. The narrative transitions from Ruth seeking to find favor "in the eyes" of a redeemer to finding physical protection and ultimate redemption under the "wings" of the Lord, facilitated by the gaze and care of Boaz.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 2:9, Boaz tells Ruth, "Let your eyes be on the field which they reap, and go after them."

• The word 'ayin here functions as an idiom for focused attention and alignment. Ruth is a vulnerable foreign widow gleaning in a potentially dangerous environment. By telling her to keep her "eyes" on his specific field and follow his young women, Boaz provides her with a boundary of safety and a clear direction.

• Her gaze is not to wander to other fields where she might face harassment or harm. Her "eyes" are to be locked onto the sphere of Boaz's provision and protection. This physical instruction mirrors the spiritual reality of focusing on the source of grace and remaining within the bounds of covenant safety.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:8: "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD."

• Ruth 2:2: "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.'"

• Ruth 2:10: "So she fell on her face, bowed down to the ground, and said to him, 'Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?'"

• Psalm 121:1: "I will lift up my eyes to the hills—From whence comes my help?"

• Proverbs 15:3: "The eyes of the LORD are in every place, Keeping watch on the evil and the good."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The physical alignment of Ruth's eyes with Boaz's field represents the posture of discipleship and obedience. In a broader theological sense, the believer's eyes are to be fixed on the "field" of God's kingdom and harvest. Wandering eyes lead to spiritual vulnerability, whereas keeping one's eyes on the designated field of the Redeemer ensures safety, abundant provision, and ultimate joy.
