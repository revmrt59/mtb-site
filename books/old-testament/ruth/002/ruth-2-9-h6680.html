COMMANDED - ( tsavah, Strong's H6680 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb tsavah refers to an authoritative instruction, charge, or decree that establishes a binding obligation. In Ruth 2:9, Boaz uses his legal and patriarchal authority to issue a strict protective order to his young workers, ensuring that Ruth, a vulnerable Moabite widow, can glean in his fields without fear of harassment or assault. This term highlights the intersection of authority, protection, and covenant grace, illustrating how a righteous leader uses his power to establish a sanctuary for the helpless.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root of tsavah carries the physical imagery of setting something up, putting things in order, or laying a direct charge upon a person. In ancient Near Eastern culture, a command was not merely a preferred suggestion but an extension of the commander’s will and authority. When a landowner, king, or father spoke a command, it established a boundary line or a set of conditions that governed behavior. In an agricultural setting, the owner of a field possessed absolute authority over his servants. By issuing a tsavah, Boaz establishes a legal and moral hedge around Ruth, effectively placing her under his personal jurisdiction and protection.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb tsavah occurs over 500 times in the Old Testament, primarily in the intensive Piel stem. It signifies:

• To command, order, or charge an individual or group.

• To commission or appoint someone to a specific office or duty.

• To give testamentary instructions or set one's house in order.

• To decree or ordain a circumstances or blessing.

The word denotes an imperative instruction where the speaker expects complete obedience, carrying legal, moral, or divine weight depending on the status of the one speaking.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, tsavah is used in several distinct contexts:

• Divine Decrees: God commands creation into existence, commands His blessings, and issues His covenant laws (the Ten Commandments).

• Patriarchal Charges: Fathers give final charges to their households before death, ensuring the continuation of family order and inheritance.

• Military and Civil Orders: Officers and rulers command their subordinates to execute administrative or military duties.

• Prohibitive Charges: A superior issues a restrictive command to prevent harm or misconduct, establishing a boundary that must not be crossed.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, tsavah reveals the character of God's authority and His provision for His people. When God commands, He establishes order out of chaos. In the book of Ruth, Boaz acts as a type of Christ—the Kinsman-Redeemer. His authoritative command (tsavah) to his young men demonstrates how redeemer-figures use their power to enforce justice and mercy. Rather than leaving the vulnerable foreigner at the mercy of cultural prejudices or physical dangers, Boaz uses his authority to command peace and security. This mirrors how God commands His angels to protect His children, and how Christ commands the spiritual forces of darkness to leave His redeemed flock untouched.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, Boaz asks, "Have I not commanded the young men not to touch you?"

• Protection of the Vulnerable: In the ancient world, foreign women gleaning in fields were highly susceptible to physical abuse and sexual exploitation. By commanding (tsavah) his young men, Boaz removes this threat entirely.

• Absolute Prohibition: The Hebrew phrase "not to touch you" (le-vilti nog'ek) combined with tsavah indicates a strict, non-negotiable prohibition. To touch her would mean violating the direct order of the landowner, resulting in severe consequences.

• Covenantal Hospitality: Boaz's command goes beyond mere passive tolerance; it actively integrates Ruth into the safety of his household resources, allowing her to drink from the water drawn by his young men.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:16: "And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, 'Of every tree of the garden you may freely eat;'"

• Joshua 1:9: "Have I not commanded you? Be strong and of good courage; do not be afraid, nor be dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go."

• Deuteronomy 6:20: "When your son asks you in time to come, saying, 'What is the meaning of the testimonies, the statutes, and the judgments which the LORD our God has commanded you?'"

• Psalm 91:11: "For He shall give His angels charge over you, To keep you in all your ways." (Note: "charge" translates the verb tsavah as "He will command His angels").

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

For those preparing sermons or lessons, Boaz’s command provides an excellent illustration of active righteousness. Boaz does not simply feel pity for Ruth; he takes structural, authoritative action to ensure her safety. In a world full of systemic dangers for the marginalized, Christian leaders are called to emulate Boaz by using their influence, resources, and words to command protection for the helpless. Authority is given by God not for self-aggrandizement, but to create a safe harbor for those who have fled to the shadow of His wings.
