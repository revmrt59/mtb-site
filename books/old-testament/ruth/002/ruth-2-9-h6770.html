THIRSTY - ( tsame' , Strong's H6770 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb tsame' describes the physical sensation of dryness and the urgent, biological need for water. In the arid environment of the Ancient Near East, thirst was not a minor inconvenience but a life-threatening reality that underscored human fragility and dependence. Beyond its literal meaning, this word serves as a profound biblical metaphor for spiritual longing, vulnerability, and the intense desire of the human soul for God's presence and righteousness. In Ruth 2:9, Boaz’s invitation to the thirsty Moabitess to drink from the water drawn by his young men transcended ordinary social custom, representing extraordinary grace, protection, and redemptive hospitality.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient wilderness and agricultural fields of Israel, water was the ultimate symbol of life, and thirst (tsame') was a constant threat. Harvesters worked long hours under a blistering sun during the barley and wheat harvests. Securing water required significant manual labor, as it had to be drawn from deep wells and transported in heavy clay jars to the fields. Because water was scarce and hard-won, it was closely guarded. For a foreigner, a widow, and a low-status gleaner like Ruth, accessing this water without explicit permission was socially forbidden and physically dangerous. To be thirsty in a field was to be completely at the mercy of the landowner and his workers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb tsame' refers to the state of being parched, suffering from dehydration, or experiencing an intense physical craving for water. Lexically, it signifies the physiological sensation of thirst and the act of thirsting. The semantic range spans from the literal physical distress of dehydration in wilderness journeys to the psychological and spiritual craving of an individual who recognizes their ultimate insufficiency. It denotes a driving, unsatisfied need that can only be resolved by an external source of life-giving water.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term tsame' is used throughout the Old Testament to construct vivid pictures of human limitation and spiritual desire:

• Physical Vulnerability: It characterizes the desperation of Israel in the wilderness, where a lack of water threatened the survival of the nation and prompted cries for divine intervention.

• Spiritual Desperation: The poets of Israel adapted the physical sensation of thirst to describe the soul's longing for God. The parched throat of a traveler in a dry land becomes the ultimate picture of a believer seeking the presence, favor, and deliverance of Yahweh.

• Judgment and Deprivation: Prophetic literature uses the image of a thirsty land or dry throat to depict the spiritual barrenness of a nation that has rejected God, the true fountain of living waters.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, tsame' emphasizes that humanity is not self-sustaining. Physical thirst reminds people of their mortality, while spiritual thirst points to the soul's design to find its ultimate satisfaction only in God. This word highlights the transition from lack to abundance under divine care. When God's people thirst, He does not abandon them; He splits rocks in the desert to satisfy them. This Old Testament theme lays the groundwork for the New Testament fulfillment, where Jesus declares Himself to be the one who permanently quenches the spiritual thirst of those who come to Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, Boaz tells Ruth, "And when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink from what the young men have drawn." This instruction is a remarkable display of grace. Normally, a foreign gleaner would have to bring her own water or wait until the paid workers were finished, potentially receiving only leftover, warm water. Boaz reverses this hierarchy. He permits Ruth to drink from the fresh water drawn by his own young men. By removing her physical vulnerability, Boaz treats Ruth not as an outcast or a secondary laborer, but with the dignity and care reserved for members of his own household. This act of kindness foreshadows his ultimate role as her kinsman-redeemer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 17:3: "And the people thirsted (tsame') there for water, and the people complained against Moses, and said, 'Why is it you have brought us up out of Egypt, to kill us and our children and our livestock with thirst?'"

• Judges 15:18: "Then he became very thirsty (tsame'); so he cried out to the Lord and said, 'You have given this great deliverance by the hand of Your servant; and now shall I die of thirst and fall into the hands of the uncircumcised?'"

• Psalm 42:2: "My soul thirsts (tsame') for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God?"

• Psalm 63:1: "O God, You are my God; Early will I seek You; My soul thirsts (tsame') for You; My flesh longs for You In a dry and thirsty land Where there is no water."

• Isaiah 48:21: "And they did not thirst (tsame') When He led them through the deserts; He caused the waters to flow from the rock for them; He also split the rock, and the waters gushed out."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammatical form of the verb in Ruth 2:9 is a perfect with a consecutive conjunction (wə-ṣā-mit), functioning here in a conditional sense: "and should you thirst." Boaz anticipates Ruth's physical needs before she even experiences or expresses them. This pre-emptive care mirrors the prevenient grace of God, who prepares rivers of mercy and refreshment for the weary traveler long before they realize their deep spiritual poverty.
