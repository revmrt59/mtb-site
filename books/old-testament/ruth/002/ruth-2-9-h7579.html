DRAWN - ( sha'ab , Strong's H7579 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sha'ab means to draw water from a well, spring, or cistern. In the ancient Near East, drawing water was a physically demanding, daily chore often associated with domestic labor or servile status. When Boaz invites Ruth to drink the water drawn by his young men, he reverses expected social roles, offering her protection, refreshment, and a beautiful foretaste of covenantal grace.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the arid climate of ancient Israel, water was the ultimate symbol of life, and securing it required relentless, heavy labor. Wells were deep, often hand-dug through solid rock, meaning water could not simply be dipped; it had to be drawn up using ropes, buckets, and jars. This daily task fell primarily to young women or servants. It was exhausting work, carrying heavy clay vessels filled with water from the town well back to the household. Because wells were central gathering places, they were also locations of vulnerability, where foreign women or marginalized individuals could easily face harassment or exploitation.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root sha'ab means strictly to draw water. It does not refer to the drinking of the water itself, but specifically to the strenuous physical act of hauling it up from a deep source. The term is concrete and functional, describing the extraction of life-giving moisture from beneath the earth to satisfy the thirst of humans, livestock, or travelers.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb occurs throughout the Old Testament to depict various scenes of hospitality, service, and spiritual joy:

• Hospitality and Service: The term describes Rebekah’s extraordinary hospitality at the well, where she offered to draw water for Abraham's servant and all his camels—a monumental task requiring her to run back and forth to the well repeatedly.

• Servitude: In the social hierarchy, "drawers of water" represented the lowest tier of servants. When the Gibeonites deceived Israel, they were spared from death but sentenced to become perpetual woodcutters and drawers of water for the community.

• Spiritual Satisfaction: The word transitions from a physical task to a powerful spiritual metaphor in the prophets, symbolizing the active, joyful appropriation of God's saving grace.

### 4. Theological Implications

The act of drawing water carries deep theological themes of divine provision and the reversal of status. Water is a gift from God, but human effort is required to bring it to the surface. When God provides water, He provides life. Furthermore, the voluntary drawing of water for another is a premier demonstration of kindness (chesed). To receive water that has been drawn by another is to be the recipient of grace. It represents a relief from labor, a cooling of physical distress, and an admission into fellowship and safety under the care of a provider.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, Boaz tells Ruth, "and when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink from what the young men have drawn." This is a stunning display of protective grace. As an impoverished foreign widow, Ruth expected to work in the blistering heat without any rights to the landowners' resources. Normally, she would have to wait until everyone else was finished, or perform the heavy labor of drawing her own water, risking safety and exhaustion.

Boaz completely upends this dynamic. By commanding his male servants to draw water for her, Boaz elevates Ruth’s status. The young men—the powerful, established laborers—are ordered to serve the poor, foreign female gleaner. Ruth is spared the grueling labor of sha'ab and is invited to enjoy the fruits of others' labor. This act of kindness goes far beyond the letter of the Mosaic law regarding gleaning; it reflects the heart of God, who refreshes the weary soul at His own expense.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 24:13: "Behold, here I stand by the well of water, and the daughters of the men of the city are coming out to draw water."

• Joshua 9:21: "And the rulers said to them, 'Let them live, but let them be woodcutters and drawers of water for all the congregation, as the rulers had promised them.'"

• Isaiah 12:3: "Therefore with joy you will draw water From the wells of salvation."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In biblical narrative, the well is a classic "type-scene" associated with courtship and betrothal. Major covenants and marriages—such as Isaac and Rebekah, Jacob and Rachel, and Moses and Zipporah—begin with an encounter at a well involving the drawing of water. By introducing the element of drawn water in Ruth 2:9, the narrative subtly signals to the reader that Boaz is not merely a polite landowner, but a potential redeemer and husband. The provision of water foreshadows the deeper covenant of marriage that will eventually bring Ruth fully into the house of Israel.
