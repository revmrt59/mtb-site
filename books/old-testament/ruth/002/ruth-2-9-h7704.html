FIELD - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open field, cultivated land, or pastureland, representing a designated area of agricultural labor, sustenance, and ownership. In the narrative of Ruth, the "field" is not merely a physical space of agricultural harvest, but a stage of divine providence, redemption, and transition. It is the place where Ruth, a vulnerable outsider, encounters the grace of Boaz, her kinsman-redeemer, transitioning from the barrenness of Moab to the abundance of Bethlehem's harvest.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, an agrarian society, the sadeh was central to daily survival and community life. Unlike walled cities that offered security but lacked food production, or the wild wilderness (midbar) which was chaotic and dangerous, the field represented cultivated order. It was the space where human labor partnered with divine provision—where seed was sown, rain was received from heaven, and crops were harvested. For Israel, the field was also a reminder of God's covenantal blessing; a fruitful field indicated obedience and favor, while a barren field signified judgment. In a personal sense, owning a field was synonymous with having an inheritance, a legacy, and a secure future.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun sadeh has a broad semantic range that is strictly determined by its context:

• It denotes open, uncultivated country where wild beasts roam or where battles are fought.

• It refers to a specific, defined plot of land owned by an individual for the purpose of agriculture, such as growing grain or planting vineyards.

• It designates pastureland or territory associated with a specific nation or tribe (e.g., "the country/field of Moab").

In Ruth 2:9, sadeh refers specifically to Boaz’s private agricultural estate—a bounded area of cultivated land where barley and wheat were harvested by his hired laborers.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses sadeh in several highly descriptive word pictures:

• The Place of Vital Provision: The field is where God manifests His daily care. When Isaac goes out to meditate in the field at evening (Genesis 24:63), it is a place of quiet reflection and divine appointment.

• The Location of Vulnerability: Because fields were open and often far from the protective walls of the city, they were places where one could easily be taken advantage of. Cain killed Abel in the field (Genesis 4:8), and the Mosaic Law made special provisions for women assaulted in the open field, recognizing that there would be no one to hear their cries for help (Deuteronomy 22:25-27).

• The Symbol of Spiritual Condition: A field can depict spiritual fruitfulness or neglect. The "field of the lazy man" (Proverbs 24:30) is overgrown with thorns, representing spiritual apathy and moral decay. Conversely, a well-watered field represents the blessing of God upon the righteous.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of the sadeh is deeply rooted in God's ownership of the land. According to the Torah, the land belongs to Yahweh; Israel is merely His tenant. Therefore, the fields were subject to His divine laws of charity and justice.

The laws of gleaning (Leviticus 19:9-10; Deuteronomy 24:19) mandated that landowners must not harvest the very corners of their fields, nor pick up the fallen grain. These gleanings were legally reserved for the poor, the widow, and the stranger. Thus, the sadeh was designed by God to be a place of social welfare and grace, where the marginalized could work with dignity to feed themselves.

Furthermore, the field is central to the concept of the goel (kinsman-redeemer). When a family lost their land due to poverty, the redeemer's duty was to purchase the field back, keeping the inheritance within the family line. This prefigures the redemptive work of Christ, who redeems our lost inheritance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, Boaz tells Ruth, "Let your eyes be on the field which they reap, and go after them."

This instruction carries immense pastoral and protective weight. Ruth was a Moabitess, a foreign widow walking into a society that often viewed outsiders with hostility. Working in an open field put her at extreme risk of exploitation or assault.

By telling her to keep her eyes on his field, Boaz does several things:

• He Establishes Protection: He places her under his personal patronage, having already charged his young men not to touch or molest her.

• He Offers Community: He tells her to "go after" his young female workers, integrating her into his household staff rather than leaving her to wander among strangers.

• He Grants Access to Abundance: He allows her to drink from the water vessels drawn by his young men, a privilege usually reserved for contract workers.

The "field" of Boaz ceases to be a place of labor and survival; it becomes a sanctuary of grace, provision, and divine safety. Ruth’s "hap" or chance of landing in this specific field (Ruth 2:3) is revealed to be the deliberate hand of God leading her to the place of redemption.

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound literary contrast in the Book of Ruth involving the word sadeh. In Ruth 1:1, Elimelech takes his family to dwell in the "country [literally, fields / sadeh] of Moab." Moab was a place of idolatry, and for Elimelech's family, those fields became a place of death, famine, and loss.

When Naomi and Ruth return to Bethlehem ("the House of Bread"), Ruth enters the sadeh of Boaz. The transition from the fields of Moab to the fields of Bethlehem symbolizes a movement from spiritual wanderings and grief back to the place of covenant blessing, restoration, and life.
