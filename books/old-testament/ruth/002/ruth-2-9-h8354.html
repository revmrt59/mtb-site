DRINK - ( shathah , Strong's H8354 )

Summary:

In Ruth 2:9, Boaz extends an extraordinary gesture of protection and grace to Ruth, a Moabite outsider, by inviting her to drink from the water drawn by his young men. This word, representing a basic physical necessity, transcends simple hydration to symbolize radical hospitality, divine provision, and the breaking down of social barriers. It showcases how God’s covenant-keeping love (chesed) manifests in the tangible, everyday actions of His people toward those in need.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb shathah is a primary Hebrew root referring to the physical act of swallowing liquid to quench thirst. In the ancient Near East, water was a precious and hard-won commodity. Gathering water was arduous labor, typically performed by women at communal wells outside the city. Drawing water for a foreigner—especially a Moabite widow—was highly countercultural. By giving Ruth access to the water vessels drawn by his male servants, Boaz removes both her physical thirst and her social vulnerability, transforming a basic survival act into an encounter with grace.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word shathah means to imbibe, swallow liquids, or drink. It occurs frequently in the Old Testament to denote the regular consumption of water, wine, or other beverages. While its primary sense is literal, it also serves as a metaphor for enjoying abundance, experiencing judgment (drinking the cup of wrath), or participating in covenant fellowship. In covenantal contexts, eating and drinking together seal a treaty or relationship, signifying peace and mutual obligation.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, shathah is used to describe:

• Physical survival and refreshment in arid landscapes.

• Feasting, celebration, and banquet settings.

• The metaphorical consumption of wisdom or divine favor.

• The terrifying reality of drinking the cup of God’s wrath or staggering under judgment.

The word picture in Ruth 2:9 portrays the vulnerable receiving sustenance directly from the storehouse of the redeemer. It represents complete satisfaction, safety, and rest under the wings of the God of Israel.

### 4. Theological Implications

The act of drinking in this passage demonstrates the practical theology of chesed (covenant faithfulness/lovingkindness). Ruth, as a Moabitess, has no legal claim to the labor of Boaz's servants. Boaz's invitation to shathah models God’s grace toward the spiritual outsider. It foreshadows the New Testament invitation where those who are spiritually thirsty are invited to come and drink without money and without price. Physical drinking becomes a physical sacrament of divine care and reception into the community of faith.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 2:9, Boaz instructs Ruth, "when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink." The immediate context is one of protection and provision. Boaz has commanded his young men not to touch or harm her. In addition to safety, he grants her equal status with his household servants by letting her drink water they have already drawn. In an agrarian economy, water is life, and labor is costly. Ruth is spared the extra labor of drawing her own water, shielding her from potential harassment at the public well. This elevates her from a scavenging stranger to an honored guest.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The connection between water and spiritual satisfaction runs throughout Scripture. In the Samaritan woman's encounter with Jesus at the well (John 4), we see a powerful New Testament parallel to Ruth 2:9. Like Boaz, Jesus breaks social, gender, and ethnic barriers to offer water to an outcast woman. Boaz's well provides temporal relief and safety; Jesus' well provides living water springing up into everlasting life. Both accounts illustrate how God meets our deepest physical and spiritual needs through unexpected, self-initiating grace.
