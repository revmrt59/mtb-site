Chapter Study

Ruth 2

Verse-by-Verse
Ruth 2:1

NKJV

Ruth 2:1 There was a relative of Naomi's husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech. His name was Boaz.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:1 Now there was a wealthy and influential man in Bethlehem named Boaz, who was a relative of Naomi’s husband, Elimelech. (NLT)

Main Idea

As Ruth and Naomi face an uncertain future, the story quietly introduces Boaz-a wealthy and respected relative of Naomi’s husband. Neither woman yet knows it, but God has already placed within their family the man who will become central to their provision and restoration.

Verse Explanation

“There was a relative of Naomi’s husband,”

The scene shifts from Ruth and Naomi to a man they have not yet encountered in the story. Boaz is connected to Naomi through her deceased husband, Elimelech. This family relationship will become extremely important because Israel’s law provided ways for relatives to help preserve a family’s property and future when death and poverty threatened them.

At this point, however, the narrator does not explain what Boaz will do. We are simply given information Ruth does not yet have: there is a relative nearby who may be in a position to help.

“a man of great wealth,”

The expression describes more than simply having money. It can carry the sense of strength, standing, ability, or substance. In this context, Boaz is evidently a man of considerable means and social standing.

That matters because Naomi and Ruth have returned to Bethlehem with very little. Boaz has the resources and position to provide meaningful help, creating an immediate contrast between their vulnerability and his ability.

“of the family of Elimelech.”

Boaz does not merely live in the same community; he belongs to Elimelech’s wider family. The repetition of the family connection makes clear that his relationship to Naomi is important to what follows.

Later chapters will explain more precisely how family responsibility and the right of redemption operate. Here, the story simply places an important fact before us: Naomi has a capable relative from Elimelech’s family living in Bethlehem.

“His name was Boaz.”

Boaz now enters the story by name. Ruth has not met him, and nothing has yet happened between them. Yet from this point forward he will become one of the central figures in the book.

The simplicity of the introduction is part of its effectiveness. Chapter 1 ended with Naomi and Ruth arriving in Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest. Chapter 2 begins by telling us that Boaz is already there.

Study Notes

“A man of great wealth” translates an expression that can describe wealth, strength, ability, valor, or social standing depending upon the context. Some translations therefore use expressions such as “a man of standing” or “a worthy man.” The NKJV’s “great wealth” fits Boaz’s evident resources, but the underlying expression is broader than financial wealth alone.

The family relationship introduced here prepares for the later role of the goel, usually translated “redeemer” or “close relative.” Verse 1 does not yet call Boaz the redeemer, and chapter 3 will reveal that another relative actually has a nearer claim than Boaz (Ruth 3:12). It is therefore better not to assume from this verse that Boaz is Naomi’s closest relative.

Spirit & Life

There is a beautiful piece of providence hidden in this simple introduction. Ruth and Naomi have arrived in Bethlehem carrying the losses of chapter 1, with no knowledge of how their situation will be resolved. Yet before Ruth ever walks into a field, Boaz is already there.

God’s providence often looks like that in Ruth. There are few dramatic miracles; instead, God has people, relationships, timing, and provision already in place before Ruth can see how any of them fit together. Verse 1 lets us see what Ruth cannot yet see: her need is real, but it is not the only thing already waiting for her in Bethlehem.

Ruth 2:2

NKJV

Ruth 2:2 So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, "Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor." And she said to her, "Go, my daughter."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:2 One day Ruth the Moabite said to Naomi, “Let me go out into the harvest fields to pick up the stalks of grain left behind by anyone who is kind enough to let me do it.” Naomi replied, “All right, my daughter, go ahead.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth takes the initiative to provide for herself and Naomi by going out to glean, trusting that someone will show her favor. Her plan rests on a provision God had already built into Israel’s law for the poor and the foreigner.

Verse Explanation

“So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi,”

Ruth is again identified as “the Moabitess.” She has committed herself completely to Naomi and to Naomi’s God, but she is still a foreign woman living in Bethlehem. That makes what follows especially significant, because Israel’s gleaning laws specifically included the stranger alongside the poor.

Ruth is also the one who takes the initiative. Naomi had returned to Bethlehem empty and deeply discouraged, but Ruth begins looking for a practical way to provide for them.

“Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain”

To “glean” meant gathering grain left behind by harvesters. God had commanded Israelite landowners not to harvest every corner of their fields or retrieve everything that fell during harvesting. What remained was to be available to the poor and the stranger (Leviticus 19:9-10; Deuteronomy 24:19-22).

Ruth therefore is not asking to steal or simply hoping to find an unattended field. She intends to make use of a provision God had established for people in exactly her vulnerable position.

Gleaning was also work. Ruth would have to follow behind the harvesters, gather what remained, and eventually process what she collected. Her willingness to glean shows initiative, humility, and diligence rather than passivity in the face of poverty.

“after him in whose sight I may find favor.”

Although the law permitted gleaning, Ruth recognizes that her experience will still depend greatly upon the attitude of the landowner and his workers. As a foreign woman without social standing, she hopes to find someone who will receive her kindly and allow her to glean safely.

The word “favor” becomes important throughout the chapter. Ruth goes looking for a field where someone will show her favor, without knowing that she is about to enter the field of Boaz.

“And she said to her, ‘Go, my daughter.’”

Naomi gives Ruth permission to go. Her words “my daughter” show the genuine bond that has developed between them. Ruth is no longer merely her deceased son’s Moabite widow; Naomi receives and speaks to her with the affection of family.

Study Notes

Israel’s gleaning laws are essential background for understanding this chapter. Leviticus 19:9-10 specifically commands that portions of the harvest be left “for the poor and the stranger.” Ruth qualifies on both counts: she is poor and a foreigner.

The system is noteworthy because it combined generosity with meaningful work. Landowners were required to leave part of their harvest accessible, while those in need gathered it themselves when able. Boaz will soon go considerably beyond these minimum requirements in the generosity he shows Ruth.

Ruth’s identification as “the Moabitess” continues a deliberate emphasis in the book. Her nationality makes her vulnerable in Bethlehem, but it will also make Boaz’s kindness toward her-and her eventual place within Israel’s story-all the more remarkable.

Spirit & Life

Ruth provides a healthy picture of the relationship between trust and action. She cannot control which field she will enter or whether someone will show her “favor,” but she can get up and glean. She acts faithfully with what is available to her while the larger outcome remains outside her control.

There is also something beautiful in the fact that Ruth is about to experience God’s provision through a command He had given generations earlier. Long before Ruth became poor in Bethlehem, God had told His people to leave grain for “the stranger.” Ruth does not yet know where provision will come from, but God had already made room for someone like her in His Word.

Ruth 2:3

NKJV

Ruth 2:3 Then she left, and went and gleaned in the field after the reapers. And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was of the family of Elimelech.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:3 So Ruth went out to gather grain behind the harvesters. And as it happened, she found herself working in a field that belonged to Boaz, the relative of her father-in-law, Elimelech. (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth goes out simply looking for a place to glean, but she unknowingly enters the field belonging to Boaz. What appears from Ruth’s perspective to be chance is the quiet providence of God moving her toward the person He will use to provide for and restore their family.

Verse Explanation

“Then she left, and went and gleaned in the field after the reapers.”

Ruth follows through on what she told Naomi she would do. She does not know where provision will come from, so she goes out and begins gleaning behind the workers who are harvesting the grain.

The “reapers” cut and gathered the standing grain, while gleaners followed behind collecting what was left. Ruth therefore takes the humble position available to the poor and begins working with what God’s law had provided for people in need.

“And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz,”

This is one of the most delightful lines in Ruth. From Ruth’s point of view, she simply “happened” upon this particular field. She did not know Boaz, had not arranged to meet him, and apparently did not know that the field belonged to a relative of Elimelech.

The wording emphasizes how unplanned the event was from Ruth’s perspective. Yet the story has already told us something Ruth does not know: Boaz was introduced in verse 1 as a wealthy and respected relative of Elimelech. What looks accidental to Ruth is moving the story directly toward God’s provision.

This is a major way God’s providence operates throughout Ruth. There is no spectacular miracle directing her to Boaz’s field. She simply chooses a field-and it turns out to be exactly the field where her story will change.

“who was of the family of Elimelech.”

The verse again emphasizes Boaz’s relationship to Elimelech. That detail has now appeared twice within three verses because it will become crucial to everything that follows.

Ruth does not yet understand its significance. She is only looking for enough grain to feed herself and Naomi. But God’s provision will ultimately reach much further than the immediate need that brought her into the field.

Study Notes

The expression translated “she happened to come” is unusually vivid. More literally, the Hebrew construction has the sense of “her chance chanced upon” the field of Boaz. The writer intentionally describes the event as an apparent coincidence from the human point of view. No Hebrew script is needed to appreciate the wordplay: Ruth has unknowingly arrived exactly where the story needs her to be.

The “field” should probably not be imagined like a modern farm separated from neighboring properties by obvious fences. Agricultural land could consist of adjoining strips or portions belonging to different owners. Ruth could begin gleaning without necessarily knowing whose particular section she had entered.

The repeated statement that Boaz “was of the family of Elimelech” keeps the family connection in view before Ruth herself discovers it. This gives us an advantage over Ruth: we can already see the significance of where she has arrived.

Spirit & Life

Ruth 2:3 gives us one of Scripture’s clearest pictures of ordinary providence. Nothing supernatural appears to happen. There is no vision, angel, audible voice, or miraculous sign. Ruth gets up, goes to work, chooses a field-and “she happened to come” to Boaz’s portion.

Yet God is quietly arranging far more than Ruth can see. She thinks she is looking for grain; God is leading her toward Boaz. She is trying to meet today’s need; God is moving a much larger story of redemption forward.

Sometimes God’s providence is most visible only when we look backward. What seemed like an ordinary decision, an unexpected meeting, or even a coincidence may turn out to have been one of the places where God was quietly directing our steps.

Ruth 2:4

NKJV

Ruth 2:4 Now behold, Boaz came from Bethlehem, and said to the reapers, "The LORD be with you!" And they answered him, "The LORD bless you!"(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:4 While she was there, Boaz arrived from Bethlehem and greeted the harvesters. “The LORD be with you!” he said. “The LORD bless you!” the harvesters replied. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz enters the field and immediately reveals something about the spiritual atmosphere surrounding him. His first words invoke the LORD’s presence and blessing, and his workers respond in kind, giving us an early glimpse of the kind of man Ruth has unknowingly encountered.

Verse Explanation

“Now behold, Boaz came from Bethlehem,”

The word “behold” draws attention to Boaz’s arrival. Ruth has just “happened” to enter his field, and now Boaz himself arrives. The timing continues the quiet providence of verse 3: Ruth and Boaz are being brought together through completely ordinary circumstances.

Boaz comes “from Bethlehem,” apparently from the town out to his fields where the harvesters are working. Nothing miraculous occurs. Ruth goes to work; Boaz goes to inspect his harvest; their paths are about to meet.

“and said to the reapers, ‘The LORD be with you!’”

Boaz’s first recorded words are a blessing upon his workers. “The LORD be with you!” is more than a casual “hello.” He invokes the covenant name of Israel’s God and desires His presence and blessing upon the people laboring for him.

This is our first direct glimpse of Boaz’s character. Before we see how he treats Ruth, we see how he speaks to his ordinary workers. His relationship with the LORD appears naturally in the everyday setting of the harvest field.

“And they answered him, ‘The LORD bless you!’”

The workers respond with a blessing of their own. Their answer suggests that Boaz’s greeting is neither awkward nor out of place among them. There appears to be genuine mutual respect between the landowner and those working for him.

This brief exchange also establishes the atmosphere into which Ruth has entered. She had gone looking for someone “in whose sight I may find favor” (v. 2). Without knowing it, she has entered a field where the LORD is openly acknowledged and where the owner’s first recorded interaction with his workers is one of blessing.

Study Notes

The timing created by verses 3-4 is easy to miss. Ruth “happened to come” to Boaz’s field, and then “behold, Boaz came.” The storytelling allows us to recognize God’s providence without ever needing to announce, “God caused this meeting.” The circumstances themselves make the point.

Greetings invoking the LORD were not unique to Boaz; similar expressions appear elsewhere in the Old Testament (Judges 6:12; Psalm 129:8). The significance here lies especially in what the greeting contributes to Boaz’s characterization when combined with everything that follows.

The exchange also takes place during the period of the judges, the troubled spiritual setting described in Ruth 1:1. Against that larger background, the simple, unforced acknowledgment of the LORD in Boaz’s field is especially noticeable.

Spirit & Life

There is something revealing about the fact that the first words we hear from Boaz are “The LORD be with you!” Ruth does not yet know what kind of man owns the field she has entered, but we begin learning before she does.

Boaz’s faith is not confined to a religious setting. The LORD is acknowledged in his workplace, in his relationships with employees, and in the ordinary routines of harvest. Before Boaz ever performs the extraordinary acts of kindness for which he is remembered, Ruth shows us a man whose everyday life already bears the marks of knowing God.

And once again, Ruth cannot yet see what we can see. She went out hoping merely to “find favor.” God has brought her into the field of a man whose character will soon make that favor wonderfully tangible.

Ruth 2:5

NKJV

Ruth 2:5 Then Boaz said to his servant who was in charge of the reapers, "Whose young woman is this?"(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:5 Then Boaz asked his foreman, “Who is that young woman over there? Who does she belong to?” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz immediately notices Ruth among the gleaners and asks about her identity. His question begins the personal encounter through which the quiet providence of the previous verses will become visible provision and kindness.

Verse Explanation

“Then Boaz said to his servant who was in charge of the reapers,”

Boaz speaks to the servant responsible for supervising the harvest workers. This fits the picture already given of Boaz as a man of substantial means: he owns fields, employs reapers, and has someone overseeing their work.

The supervisor would naturally know who was working and gleaning in the field. Boaz therefore directs his question to the person most likely to know about the unfamiliar woman he has noticed.

“Whose young woman is this?”

Boaz notices Ruth. His question is not primarily asking for her name but about her family or household connection-who she belongs with and where she comes from. In that culture, a person’s family relationships were a major part of establishing identity and social standing.

For Ruth, that question carries particular weight. She is a foreigner, a widow, and a newcomer to Bethlehem. She does not have the ordinary network of father, husband, or Israelite household through which a young woman might normally be identified.

The question also moves the story forward in a wonderfully simple way. Ruth had gone out hoping to “find favor” with someone (v. 2). She unknowingly entered Boaz’s field (v. 3). Boaz arrived (v. 4). Now he notices her and asks who she is. The meeting toward which the chapter has been quietly moving is about to begin.

Study Notes

“Whose young woman is this?” reflects the importance of household identity in the ancient world. Boaz is likely asking whose household or authority Ruth is connected with rather than treating her as someone’s property in the modern sense.

The servant “who was in charge of the reapers” appears again in verse 6, where he answers Boaz’s question and explains Ruth’s identity and conduct. His knowledge of Ruth suggests that gleaning in the field was supervised rather than entirely informal.

It is worth noticing the restraint of the text. Nothing in Boaz’s question says that he is immediately romantically attracted to Ruth. The story gives us no need to add that. He sees an unfamiliar young woman among the gleaners and asks who she is. The deeper relationship develops naturally from what follows.

Spirit & Life

There is a beautiful progression through these verses: Ruth goes, she “happened” upon Boaz’s field, Boaz arrives, and now Boaz notices her. God’s providence is unfolding through a series of ordinary moments, none of which looks remarkable by itself.

Ruth entered the field as an unknown foreign widow simply trying to gather enough grain for Naomi and herself. Yet she has now been noticed by the very person God will use to change the direction of their lives. God’s provision is often already moving before the person receiving it has any idea what He is arranging.

Ruth 2:6

NKJV

Ruth 2:6 So the servant who was in charge of the reapers answered and said, "It is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:6 And the foreman replied, “She is the young woman from Moab who came back with Naomi. (NLT)

Main Idea

The overseer identifies Ruth to Boaz as the Moabite woman who faithfully returned with Naomi. Even before Boaz speaks with her personally, Ruth’s loyalty to Naomi has become known in Bethlehem and provides the context for how he will receive her.

Verse Explanation

“So the servant who was in charge of the reapers answered and said,”

The servant is the overseer Boaz questioned in verse 5. Because he supervises the harvest, he knows who has entered the field and is able to explain Ruth’s identity and presence there.

His answer also shows that Ruth has not simply slipped unnoticed into the harvest. The overseer knows who she is and, as the next verse makes clear, has observed how she has conducted herself since arriving.

“It is the young Moabite woman”

Ruth is identified first by her nationality. She is “the young Moabite woman.” Although she has committed herself to Naomi, Israel, and the LORD, she remains visibly known in Bethlehem as an outsider from Moab.

That description carries added weight because relations between Israel and Moab had often been hostile, and Deuteronomy 23:3-6 placed serious restrictions upon Moabites within Israel’s assembly. Ruth therefore enters Bethlehem without the social advantages of being a native Israelite.

Yet the book repeatedly emphasizes her Moabite identity precisely while showing her remarkable faithfulness. The outsider is becoming one of the story’s clearest examples of loyal love.

“who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab.”

Ruth is also identified by what she has done: she “came back with Naomi.” Strictly speaking, Naomi returned to Bethlehem while Ruth came there for the first time. The expression joins Ruth so closely with Naomi that Ruth’s journey is treated as part of Naomi’s return.

This is the story people in Bethlehem have heard about her. Ruth is not merely “a Moabite woman”; she is the Moabite woman who left her homeland and accompanied Naomi back from Moab.

That matters because Boaz already knows something of Ruth before their first conversation. When he later tells her, “It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done for your mother-in-law” (v. 11), we see that her costly loyalty has become known within the community.

Study Notes

The repeated description of Ruth as a “Moabite” is deliberate throughout the book. Ruth 1:22 called her “Ruth the Moabitess,” and 2:2 repeated the identification. The writer does not allow us to forget her outsider status because it makes her welcome among God’s people-and eventually her place in Israel’s royal and messianic line-all the more remarkable.

The wording “came back with Naomi” may sound unusual because Ruth had never previously lived in Bethlehem. The idea is not that Ruth herself is geographically returning, but that she accompanied Naomi in Naomi’s return. The two women are treated together in the journey back from Moab.

Spirit & Life

Ruth does not enter Boaz’s field with status, wealth, or an impressive family position. What precedes her is the story of her faithfulness: she is the woman “who came back with Naomi.” Her costly love toward Naomi has quietly become her reputation.

There is also a beautiful movement in Ruth’s identity. Bethlehem still calls her “the young Moabite woman,” but Ruth has already said to Naomi, “Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God” (1:16). Others may still identify her primarily by where she came from, while God is already writing a much larger story about where she now belongs.

Ruth 2:7

NKJV

Ruth 2:7 And she said, "˜Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves.' So she came and has continued from morning until now, though she rested a little in the house."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:7 She asked me this morning if she could gather grain behind the harvesters. She has been hard at work ever since, except for a few minutes’ rest in the shelter.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The overseer describes Ruth as both respectful and remarkably diligent. She asked permission to glean and has worked steadily from the morning, giving Boaz his first report of the character that will soon draw his favor.

Verse Explanation

“And she said, ‘Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves.’”

The overseer reports Ruth’s request when she arrived. Although God’s law provided for the poor and the foreigner to glean, Ruth did not enter the field presumptuously. She respectfully asked permission.

“Among the sheaves” may indicate that Ruth requested access close behind the reapers where the cut grain was being gathered into bundles. That could provide better opportunity than merely searching the outer portions of the field, making her request for permission understandable.

Her approach reveals both humility and initiative. Ruth is willing to do the hard work available to her, but she does so respectfully within the harvest operation already underway.

“So she came and has continued from morning until now,”

Ruth began early and has remained at the work. The overseer has been watching long enough to give Boaz a meaningful report about her conduct.

This becomes another important part of Ruth’s character. Her loyalty to Naomi brought her to Bethlehem; now her diligence is visible in the field. She is not waiting passively for their circumstances to change. She is doing the difficult, ordinary work necessary to provide for them.

“though she rested a little in the house.”

Ruth has taken only a brief rest in the shelter or structure provided near the field. The point is not that resting is undesirable, but that Ruth has worked steadily enough for the overseer to notice.

This small detail makes the scene especially vivid. Boaz asks who Ruth is, and the overseer can tell him not only where she came from but what she has been doing all morning. Before Boaz has spoken a word to Ruth, her character has already been visible through her actions.

Study Notes

The final part of the verse is one of the more difficult lines in Ruth to translate. The underlying wording is unusually compressed, which explains why English translations differ. The NKJV understands it as Ruth working steadily with only “a little” rest in the shelter; other translations render the details somewhat differently. The safest point is the one clearly established by the context: Ruth has been present and diligently gleaning since morning.

“Among the sheaves” is also noteworthy because gleaners ordinarily followed the harvesters gathering what remained. Boaz will later give Ruth unusually generous permission, commanding his workers to let her “glean even among the sheaves” (v. 15). That later command makes clear that such access was a special favor rather than something Ruth could simply assume.

Spirit & Life

Ruth’s character is becoming visible before she ever has an opportunity to explain herself. The overseer has seen her humility in asking, her willingness to work, and her perseverance through the morning. Boaz is about to show her extraordinary favor, but the story does not portray Ruth as passive while waiting for provision.

There is a healthy picture here of faithfulness in ordinary things. Ruth cannot control whether Boaz notices her, what he will think of her, or what opportunities will come next. She can control whether she gets up, goes to the field, treats people respectfully, and works faithfully with what is before her. God’s providence and Ruth’s diligence are not competing explanations in this story; they are beautifully woven together.

Ruth 2:8

NKJV

Ruth 2:8 Then Boaz said to Ruth, "You will listen, my daughter, will you not? Do not go to glean in another field, nor go from here, but stay close by my young women.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:8 Boaz went over and said to Ruth, “Listen, my daughter. Stay right here with us when you gather grain; don’t go to any other fields. Stay right behind the young women working in my field. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz personally welcomes Ruth and gives her a secure place in his fields. The favor Ruth hoped to find now becomes tangible as Boaz invites this vulnerable foreign widow to remain with his household and under his protection.

Verse Explanation

“Then Boaz said to Ruth,”

For the first time, Boaz speaks directly to Ruth. Everything since verse 3 has quietly moved toward this moment: Ruth entered his field, Boaz arrived, noticed her, asked who she was, and heard the overseer’s report.

Ruth had come looking for grain. She is now about to receive something more-a personal welcome from the owner of the field.

“You will listen, my daughter, will you not?”

Boaz addresses Ruth warmly as “my daughter.” The expression suggests both kindness and a difference in age or social position. His tone is protective rather than distant.

For Ruth, this matters. She is a Moabite widow with no husband or father in Bethlehem to provide ordinary social protection. Boaz immediately speaks to her as someone he intends to care for rather than merely tolerate.

“Do not go to glean in another field, nor go from here,”

Boaz invites Ruth to remain exclusively in his fields. She no longer needs to spend each day searching for another landowner who might permit her to glean.

That removes a significant uncertainty from Ruth’s situation. In verse 2 she had gone out hoping to find someone “in whose sight I may find favor.” She has found that favor. Boaz is effectively telling her, “You have a place here.”

“but stay close by my young women.”

Boaz goes beyond allowing Ruth to gather leftover grain. He tells her to remain near the women who belong to his harvesting operation.

This gives Ruth both belonging and protection. Rather than leaving the foreign widow isolated among strangers, Boaz places her alongside the women of his own household and workforce. Verse 9 will make the protective purpose even clearer when he tells her that he has commanded the young men not to touch her.

Study Notes

The instruction “stay close by” is stronger than simply “work somewhere nearby.” The same underlying verb can describe close attachment or clinging. It recalls Ruth’s earlier decision when “Ruth clung to” Naomi (1:14). Ruth, who had attached herself faithfully to Naomi, is now invited to attach herself to the community working in Boaz’s field.

“My daughter” should not be read romantically. It is an affectionate and protective form of address and probably indicates that Boaz is older than Ruth. Ruth 3:10 later supports this when Boaz speaks of Ruth not pursuing younger men.

Boaz is already exceeding the bare minimum of the gleaning law. The law gave Ruth access to leftover grain; Boaz gives her continuing access, personal welcome, companionship, and-as the next verse shows-protection and water.

Spirit & Life

Ruth went out asking for the opportunity to gather leftovers, but Boaz begins giving her far more than she requested. She wanted permission to glean; he gives her a field where she can remain. She came as an outsider; he calls her “my daughter.” She was alone and vulnerable; he tells her to “stay close by my young women.” Grace is already exceeding her request.

Boaz also begins to provide a beautiful picture that will later make his role as redeemer so meaningful. He sees the vulnerable outsider, welcomes her, brings her near, protects her, and provides for her beyond what she could claim for herself. We should not make every detail an allegory of Christ, but it is difficult not to see here the kind of gracious welcome that will find its fullest expression in Jesus.

Ruth 2:9

NKJV

Ruth 2:9 Let your eyes be on the field which they reap, and go after them. Have I not commanded the young men not to touch you? And when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink from what the young men have drawn."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:9 See which part of the field they are harvesting, and then follow them. I have warned the young men not to treat you roughly. And when you are thirsty, help yourself to the water they have drawn from the well.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz extends his favor beyond permission to glean: he personally ensures Ruth’s safety, gives her continuing access to his harvest, and allows her to drink from the water provided for his own workers. Ruth is being treated not merely as a tolerated outsider but as someone welcomed under Boaz’s care.

Verse Explanation

“Let your eyes be on the field which they reap, and go after them.”

Boaz tells Ruth to watch where his workers are harvesting and follow along with them. She does not need to wander from field to field looking for somewhere she will be accepted. Wherever Boaz’s harvesting crew moves, she has permission to follow.

This gives Ruth continuing access to the harvest and places her near the workers Boaz has already instructed her to stay with.

“Have I not commanded the young men not to touch you?”

Boaz has taken deliberate steps to protect Ruth. “Touch” in this setting can include bothering, harassing, or physically mistreating her. As a foreign widow working among unfamiliar laborers, Ruth could have been especially vulnerable.

Boaz does not merely tell Ruth to be careful; he places responsibility upon his own men. They have been commanded to leave her alone. His field is to be a safe place for her.

“And when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink from what the young men have drawn.”

Boaz’s generosity now goes beyond the requirements of gleaning. Ruth is permitted to drink from the water drawn for his workers.

The detail is more significant than it may initially appear. Drawing and carrying water required labor, yet Ruth is not told to draw water for herself-or for the workers. She is invited to drink from what “the young men have drawn.” The poor foreign woman who came looking for leftover grain is being served from the provisions of Boaz’s household.

Study Notes

Boaz’s command that the men “not touch” Ruth helps us understand the practical dangers that could accompany gleaning. The law could give the poor a right to glean, but a vulnerable woman still depended upon the character of the landowner and his workers for her safety. Boaz actively makes sure Ruth can exercise that right without harassment.

The water provision represents a notable social reversal. Ruth is a Moabite woman and a gleaner, yet Boaz permits her to use water drawn by his male workers. She is receiving privileges associated with those working within his household rather than being kept at the margins.

Boaz’s generosity is developing in stages. In verse 8 he gives Ruth a permanent place to glean and companionship with his young women. Here he adds protection and water. Later he will invite her to his table and deliberately increase the amount of grain available to her.

Spirit & Life

Ruth asked only to “glean heads of grain” (v. 2), but Boaz keeps adding provision she never requested: a place to remain, people to stay near, protection from harm, and water when she is thirsty. His favor is not merely an attitude toward Ruth; it becomes practical care for her actual needs.

There is also a beautiful picture of grace here. Ruth arrives with nothing that places Boaz under obligation to her, yet she finds herself drinking water she did not draw and receiving protection she could not provide for herself. Without forcing Boaz into an allegory, his generosity gives us a glimpse of a pattern that reaches its fullness in Christ: grace does not merely permit the outsider to remain at a distance; it welcomes, protects, provides, and brings near.

Ruth 2:10 Ruth fell at his feet and thanked him warmly. “What have I done to deserve such kindness?” she asked. “I am only a foreigner.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth is overwhelmed by Boaz’s unexpected kindness. Knowing that she is a foreigner with no claim upon his generosity, she humbly asks why he has chosen to notice and favor her.

Verse Explanation

“So she fell on her face, bowed down to the ground,”

Ruth responds immediately and deeply to Boaz’s generosity. Falling facedown and bowing to the ground was an expression of profound respect and humility before someone of greater social position.

Her reaction shows how extraordinary Boaz’s treatment seems to her. She had hoped merely to find someone who would allow her to glean. Instead, Boaz has personally welcomed her, protected her, and provided for her.

“and said to him, ‘Why have I found favor in your eyes,”

Ruth uses the same idea she expressed before leaving Naomi. She had gone out hoping to glean after someone “in whose sight I may find favor” (v. 2). Now that she has found that favor, she is astonished by its extent.

Her question is not, “What have I done to deserve this?” but essentially, “Why are you treating me this way?” Ruth recognizes that Boaz’s generosity goes beyond anything she could demand from him.

“that you should take notice of me,”

Boaz has done more than allow Ruth to remain unnoticed at the edge of his field. He saw her, asked about her, spoke directly to her, arranged for her protection, and gave her access to his workers’ water.

For someone in Ruth’s vulnerable position, simply being “noticed” by a man of Boaz’s standing-and noticed with kindness rather than suspicion or exploitation-was significant.

“since I am a foreigner?”

Ruth understands the social distance between them. She is a Moabite woman living among Israelites, without land, wealth, or standing in Bethlehem.

This makes Boaz’s kindness especially surprising to her. Ruth knows that she has entered his field as an outsider. Yet Boaz has treated her as someone worthy of attention, protection, and generosity.

Her question also prepares for Boaz’s answer in verse 11. Ruth sees herself primarily as “a foreigner,” but Boaz has heard about her loyalty to Naomi and the costly choice she made to leave her own people and come to Israel.

Study Notes

There is a deliberate connection between Ruth’s request in verse 2 and her question here. She went looking for “favor” and now asks why she has “found favor.” The story is showing that what Ruth hoped for has already been granted in a measure far beyond simple permission to glean.

The word translated “foreigner” describes someone regarded as an outsider or stranger. Ruth’s Moabite identity has repeatedly been emphasized in the chapter (vv. 2, 6), so her statement reflects a real social distinction rather than false humility.

There is also a subtle wordplay in Ruth’s question. She asks why Boaz would “take notice” of her when she is a “foreigner”-someone who ordinarily would not be recognized as belonging within his community. The contrast between being an outsider and being personally noticed is central to the moment.

Spirit & Life

Ruth’s question captures something essential about grace: “Why have I found favor in your eyes?” Grace becomes astonishing when we recognize that it is favor we could never demand.

There is also something deeply personal in Ruth’s amazement that Boaz would “take notice of me.” Her circumstances could easily have made her feel invisible-widowed, poor, foreign, and gathering leftovers behind other people’s workers. Yet she has been seen.

That theme reaches far beyond Ruth. Scripture repeatedly reveals a God who sees people others overlook and welcomes those who have no claim upon Him. In Jesus, those who were “strangers” and “far off” are brought near (Ephesians 2:12-13). Ruth’s wonder before Boaz gives us a small but beautiful taste of the greater wonder of grace: Why would He take notice of me?

NKJV

Ruth 2:11 And Boaz answered and said to her, "It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband, and how you have left your father and your mother and the land of your birth, and have come to a people whom you did not know before.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:11 “Yes, I know,” Boaz replied. “But I also know about everything you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband. I have heard how you left your father and mother and your own land to live here among complete strangers. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz explains that his kindness is not uninformed generosity-he has heard about Ruth’s remarkable loyalty to Naomi and the costly faith behind her move to Israel. Ruth may still see herself mainly as a foreigner, but Boaz sees the faithfulness that has marked her new life among God’s people.

Verse Explanation

“And Boaz answered and said to her,”

Ruth has just asked why Boaz would show such favor to her “since I am a foreigner” (v. 10). Boaz now answers her directly.

His response is important because it explains what he knows about Ruth. She has entered his field as a stranger to him personally, but her story is not unknown in Bethlehem.

“It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband,”

Boaz has heard a thorough account of Ruth’s care for Naomi. Her loyalty was not limited to the emotional moment following her husband’s death. She remained with Naomi, accompanied her home, and is now working to provide for them both.

This also means Ruth’s faithfulness has become visible to others. She did not perform these acts to gain Boaz’s attention; in fact, she had not even met him. She simply remained faithful to Naomi, and the story of that faithfulness reached him.

“and how you have left your father and your mother and the land of your birth,”

Boaz understands the cost of Ruth’s decision. She did not merely relocate. She left her parents, her homeland, and the familiar world in which she had grown up.

The wording makes Ruth’s sacrifice personal: “your father and your mother and the land of your birth.” Following Naomi meant surrendering the security and relationships she might naturally have depended upon after becoming a widow.

There is a striking echo here of Abraham, whom God called to leave his country, family, and father’s house and go to a land God would show him (Genesis 12:1). Ruth likewise leaves homeland and family and steps into an unknown future among God’s people.

“and have come to a people whom you did not know before.”

Ruth entered a community where she had no established place. She had no guarantee of how she would be received, particularly as a Moabite woman.

Yet her decision in chapter 1 had gone deeper than attachment to Naomi. Ruth had declared, “Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God” (1:16). She had chosen Naomi’s people and Naomi’s God before knowing what that choice would cost or what provision awaited her in Bethlehem.

Boaz therefore sees more than a foreign gleaner standing before him. He sees a woman who has left the familiar behind and entrusted her future to a people-and ultimately to the God-she had only recently come to know.

Study Notes

The expression “It has been fully reported to me” is emphatic. Boaz is essentially saying that he has been thoroughly informed about Ruth. This helps explain why his generosity toward her begins so quickly: although this is apparently their first personal conversation, Ruth’s character is already known to him.

The similarity between Ruth’s departure and Abraham’s call is worth noticing without pressing it too far. Abraham left his country, family, and father’s household for the land God would show him (Genesis 12:1); Ruth has left “your father and your mother and the land of your birth” to live among a people previously unknown to her. Both involve leaving the familiar behind in a movement of faith toward the God of Israel.

Boaz does not say that Ruth has earned God’s grace through her good works. He is recognizing the genuine loyalty and faith that her actions have revealed. Verse 12 will make the deeper foundation explicit: Ruth has come to take refuge under the wings of the LORD God of Israel.

Spirit & Life

There is something beautiful in the fact that Ruth’s faithfulness was noticed even though she was not trying to be noticed. She cared for Naomi when there was no Boaz watching, no reward promised, and no reason to believe her sacrifice would ever be repaid. Her character was formed in the unseen choices before it was ever honored publicly.

Ruth also gives us a vivid picture of faith that actually moves. “Your God, my God” was not merely something she said in chapter 1. She left “the land of your birth” and “came to a people whom you did not know before.” Her actions gave substance to her confession. Genuine faith eventually becomes visible in the direction of a life.

Ruth 2:12

NKJV

Ruth 2:12 The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:12 May the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge, reward you fully for what you have done.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz prays that the LORD Himself will fully reward Ruth for the costly faithfulness she has shown. He recognizes that beneath Ruth’s loyalty to Naomi is something even greater: she has entrusted herself to the God of Israel and taken refuge under His protection.

Verse Explanation

“The LORD repay your work,”

Boaz asks the LORD to repay Ruth for what she has done. The “work” in view reaches back to verse 11-her faithful care for Naomi, leaving her family and homeland, and coming to live among a people she had not previously known.

Boaz recognizes that he can show Ruth kindness, but the reward appropriate to such faithfulness ultimately comes from God. His prayer directs Ruth beyond the generosity of the man standing before her to the LORD Himself.

“and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel,”

Boaz strengthens the thought. He does not merely pray that Ruth will receive something in return, but “a full reward.” She has left much behind without knowing what awaited her in Bethlehem; Boaz asks God to fill what she has emptied and honor the faith expressed in her costly choices.

Calling Him “the LORD God of Israel” is especially meaningful when spoken to Ruth the Moabitess. The God who had been Israel’s God is now the God Ruth herself has chosen: “Your God, my God” (1:16).

“under whose wings you have come for refuge.”

This reveals how Boaz understands Ruth’s story. Ruth had visibly come to Bethlehem with Naomi, but spiritually she had come to the LORD. Leaving Moab and joining God’s people was an act of taking refuge in Him.

The image of “wings” pictures God as a place of protection and security. Similar imagery appears elsewhere in Scripture for those who seek shelter beneath God’s care (Psalm 36:7; 57:1; 91:4).

Ruth therefore has exchanged one kind of security for another. She left father, mother, homeland, and everything familiar, but she did not step into emptiness. She came “for refuge” beneath the wings of the LORD.

Study Notes

The phrase “under whose wings you have come for refuge” is one of the theological centers of Ruth. The verb “take refuge” frequently describes trusting in the LORD for protection and security, particularly in the Psalms. Boaz interprets Ruth’s move to Israel not merely as devotion to Naomi but as an act of faith in Israel’s God.

There is also an important connection with Ruth 3:9. Ruth will ask Boaz, “Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative.” The same wing imagery links the two scenes. Here Boaz prays that the LORD will shelter Ruth under His wings; later Ruth asks Boaz to spread his wing over her. One of the beautiful movements of the book is that God answers Boaz’s prayer partly through Boaz himself.

“Repay” and “reward” should not be read as though Ruth has placed God in her debt. The language recognizes God’s gracious response to faithful trust. Ruth has taken refuge in Him, and Boaz prays that she will discover just how secure and generous that refuge is.

Spirit & Life

Boaz sees something in Ruth’s story that Ruth may not yet fully see herself. She thinks she has left almost everything behind. Boaz sees that she has actually “come for refuge.” Her life is no longer defined only by what she lost in Moab, but by the God beneath whose wings she now lives.

The connection with chapter 3 is especially beautiful. Boaz prays for God to care for Ruth, and before the story is finished, God will use Boaz to help answer that prayer. Sometimes when we ask God to provide, protect, encourage, or restore someone, He intends for us to become part of the answer.

And ultimately, Ruth’s refuge is not Boaz-it is the LORD. Boaz will become a wonderful instrument of God’s provision, but the wings belong first to God. People may be the means through which His care reaches us; He remains the refuge beneath it all.

NKJV

Ruth 2:13 “I hope I continue to please you, sir,” she replied. “You have comforted me by speaking so kindly to me, even though I am not one of your workers.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth responds to Boaz’s extraordinary kindness with gratitude and humility. Though she sees herself as having even less standing than one of his servants, his gracious words have given comfort and reassurance to a woman who entered his field as a vulnerable outsider.

Verse Explanation

“Then she said, ‘Let me find favor in your sight, my lord;’”

Ruth returns to the theme of “favor” that has run through the chapter. She went out hoping to “find favor” in someone’s field (v. 2), was amazed that she had “found favor” with Boaz (v. 10), and now expresses her desire to remain within that favor.

Calling Boaz “my lord” is a respectful form of address appropriate to the difference in their position. Ruth is not presuming upon the kindness she has received. She responds with gratitude and humility.

“for you have comforted me,”

Boaz has done more than provide practical help. His words have brought Ruth emotional encouragement.

That matters in light of everything Ruth has experienced. She has lost her husband, left her parents and homeland, entered an unfamiliar people as a foreigner, and gone into the fields because she and Naomi need food. Boaz’s welcome tells her that she is not merely being tolerated in Bethlehem.

“and have spoken kindly to your maidservant,”

Literally, the expression behind “spoken kindly” has the idea of speaking “to the heart.” Boaz’s words have reached Ruth personally. He has recognized her faithfulness, prayed God’s blessing over her, and described her as someone who has taken refuge beneath the LORD’s wings.

This helps explain why Ruth calls his words “comfort.” Boaz has not merely given her information; he has spoken in a way that strengthens and reassures her heart.

“though I am not like one of your maidservants.”

Ruth remains deeply conscious of her low social position. She does not even consider herself equal to the female servants who belong to Boaz’s household.

Yet this makes Boaz’s treatment of her all the more remarkable. Ruth entered the field as a foreign gleaner, but Boaz has spoken to her with a dignity and kindness she did not expect to receive.

There is a beautiful tension here: Ruth still sees how little claim she has upon Boaz, while Boaz continues treating her with increasing generosity.

Study Notes

The expression “spoken kindly” is a Hebrew idiom meaning literally “spoken to the heart.” It appears elsewhere for speech intended to comfort, reassure, persuade, or encourage (Genesis 50:21; Isaiah 40:2). No original-language script is necessary to catch the richness of the expression: Boaz has spoken words that reached Ruth’s heart.

Ruth’s “maidservant” and “your maidservants” are not exactly the same expression in the underlying text. Ruth uses a particularly humble term for herself and then says she does not even have the standing of Boaz’s regular female workers. The distinction reinforces rather than weakens the humility of her statement.

The repeated “favor” language gives this section cohesion. Ruth seeks favor (v. 2), discovers favor (v. 10), asks why she has received it (v. 10), and now responds gratefully to it. Boaz’s favor is progressively transforming her experience in Bethlehem.

Spirit & Life

There is something worth noticing in Ruth’s words: “you have comforted me, and have spoken kindly.” Boaz’s generosity has included grain, water, protection, and access to his field, but Ruth specifically mentions his words. Practical provision mattered, but so did being spoken to with dignity and kindness.

Sometimes we underestimate what gracious words can do for someone carrying loss, uncertainty, or the feeling that they do not belong. Boaz could not undo Ruth’s past, but he could speak courage into her present.

Ruth also continues to display the posture that makes grace so wonderful to receive. She does not approach Boaz with entitlement. She knows she has no claim upon his extraordinary generosity, and that makes his favor precious to her. The more clearly grace is recognized as grace, the more deeply it is treasured.

Ruth 2:14 At mealtime Boaz called to her, “Come over here, and help yourself to some food. You can dip your bread in the sour wine.” So she sat with his harvesters, and Boaz gave her some roasted grain to eat. She ate all she wanted and still had some left over. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz’s favor toward Ruth deepens from permission and protection to personal fellowship and abundant provision. The foreign gleaner is invited to the meal, personally served by Boaz, eats until satisfied, and even has food left over.

Verse Explanation

“Now Boaz said to her at mealtime, ‘Come here, and eat of the bread,”

Boaz now invites Ruth to join the harvest meal. This goes well beyond allowing her to glean. Ruth had described herself as beneath the standing of Boaz’s maidservants (v. 13), yet Boaz brings her into the company of his workers and gives her a place at the meal.

The movement through the chapter is striking. Ruth began at the margins of the field gathering what others left behind. Now the owner of the field is saying, “Come here.”

“and dip your piece of bread in the vinegar.”

The “vinegar” was likely a sour wine or wine-vinegar mixture used as a condiment with bread. It was a simple but refreshing part of the workers’ meal, especially appropriate during hot harvest work.

Ruth is not merely permitted to sit nearby with whatever food she has brought. Boaz gives her access to the food provided at his own harvest table.

“So she sat beside the reapers,”

Ruth now sits with the people whose leftovers she had originally come to gather. The foreign widow and poor gleaner has been brought into the workers’ fellowship.

The text does not suggest that Ruth has suddenly become one of Boaz’s employees. The significance is that Boaz treats someone socially beneath his workers as though she belongs at their table.

“and he passed parched grain to her;”

Boaz personally serves Ruth roasted grain, a common food during harvest. The detail is easy to pass over, but it is significant: the wealthy landowner introduced in verse 1 personally hands food to the Moabite gleaner.

His kindness is not merely delegated through his servants. Boaz himself participates in caring for Ruth.

“and she ate and was satisfied, and kept some back.”

Ruth receives more than enough. She eats until she is “satisfied” and still has food remaining.

The abundance matters because Ruth and Naomi had returned to Bethlehem in poverty. Ruth entered the field hoping to gather enough leftover grain to survive, but by midday she has eaten to fullness at Boaz’s table and has something left over.

Verse 18 will reveal why the remaining food matters: Ruth takes it home to Naomi. Boaz’s generosity toward Ruth therefore begins overflowing to the person Ruth came to the field to provide for.

Study Notes

“Vinegar” should not be imagined simply as modern distilled vinegar. It was likely a sour wine mixture commonly eaten with bread and valued for its refreshing quality.

“Parched grain” was freshly harvested grain roasted over heat and eaten as a normal food during harvest season (see also 1 Samuel 17:17; 25:18). Boaz is sharing the ordinary meal of his harvest crew with Ruth.

The wording “was satisfied, and kept some back” deliberately emphasizes abundance. Ruth does not merely receive a token meal. She eats fully and has a remainder, which she later gives to Naomi (v. 18).

Spirit & Life

The progression of Boaz’s favor is beautiful. Ruth asked for permission to gather leftovers. Boaz gave her a field in which to remain, protection, water, companionship, kind words-and now a place at his table. His generosity keeps exceeding the level of her request.

There is also a lovely movement from the edge of the field to “beside the reapers.” Ruth came as an outsider, yet Boaz says, “Come here.” Grace does more than allow someone to remain at a distance; it brings them near.

Without turning every detail into an allegory, it is difficult not to hear an echo of the greater invitation we receive in Christ. Those who had no natural claim upon the household of God are brought near and given a place at His table. And like Ruth, we discover that His provision is not barely sufficient: she “ate and was satisfied, and kept some back.”

After welcoming Ruth to his table, Boaz quietly increases his generosity toward her. He gives her access to better gleaning and protects her dignity by ordering his workers not to embarrass or rebuke her.

“And when she rose up to glean,”

After eating with Boaz and the reapers, Ruth returns to work. The generous meal does not end her day in the field. She gets up and resumes the task she came to do.

This continues the picture of Ruth’s diligence. Boaz is showing her extraordinary favor, but Ruth does not begin presuming upon his generosity. She continues gleaning.

“Boaz commanded his young men, saying,”

Boaz now speaks privately to his workers. Ruth does not ask for what follows, and the text gives no indication that she even hears the instructions.

“Let her glean even among the sheaves,”

Verse 7 records Ruth asking to glean “among the sheaves,” while here Boaz explicitly authorizes her to do so. Whatever exact access Ruth originally requested or had been given, verse 15 makes clear that Boaz is now granting unusually generous permission.

This is another example of Boaz going beyond the minimum requirements of Israel’s gleaning laws. The law required landowners to leave portions of the harvest for the poor and foreigner. Boaz deliberately increases Ruth’s opportunity to gather from his harvest.

One of the most beautiful details is that Ruth apparently does not hear this conversation. She will experience greater provision without knowing that Boaz has been working behind the scenes to make it possible.

That fits the larger picture of providence throughout Ruth. Ruth sees grain available to gather; we see Boaz giving instructions she never heard. In the same way, we often recognize God’s provision without seeing all the ways He prepared it before we arrived.

Boaz also shows that genuine generosity preserves dignity. He could simply have handed Ruth a large quantity of grain, but instead he enables her to continue working while quietly making her labor unusually fruitful. His kindness does not make Ruth feel small; it creates room for her to flourish.

“leave it that she may glean”

There is something beautiful about generosity that does not need to announce itself. Ruth may not even know at this point how much Boaz has arranged behind the scenes. She simply keeps working and discovers provision waiting in her path.

Boaz also continues to anticipate the greater Redeemer who will emerge through Ruth’s story. Ruth works faithfully until evening and ends the day with an unusually abundant amount of grain-evidence of both her diligence and Boaz’s generous provision.

“and beat out what she had gleaned”

Ruth experiences provision through an important combination: she works faithfully, while Boaz quietly supplies what she could never have produced by effort alone. She gathers the grain, but behind her gathering stands generosity she did not arrange for herself.

There is a beautiful picture here of how God's provision can sometimes be recognized only after the day's work is done. Ruth has simply been faithful in the field, yet when the grain is measured, the abundance reveals that more has been happening around her than her labor alone could explain.

Ruth does not receive kindness and immediately turn inward. Boaz has fed her generously, and Ruth instinctively carries some of that provision home to Naomi. Grace received becomes generosity extended.

There is also a quiet movement taking place in Naomi’s story. She returned to Bethlehem saying that she had come back “empty” (Ruth 1:21). Now Ruth walks through the door carrying an abundance of barley and food for her. Naomi’s circumstances have not yet been completely restored, but her emptiness is already beginning to be filled.

Ruth 2:19

NKJV

Ruth 2:19 And her mother-in-law said to her, "Where have you gleaned today? And where did you work? Blessed be the one who took notice of you." So she told her mother-in-law with whom she had worked, and said, "The man's name with whom I worked today is Boaz."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:19 “Where did you gather all this grain today?” Naomi asked. “Where did you work? May the LORD bless the one who helped you!” So Ruth told her mother-in-law about the man in whose field she had worked. She said, “The man I worked with today is named Boaz.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The extraordinary provision Ruth brings home immediately gets Naomi’s attention. When Naomi learns that Boaz is responsible for Ruth’s favor, she blesses the man whose kindness has begun to change their circumstances.

Verse Explanation

“And her mother-in-law said to her, ‘Where have you gleaned today? And where did you work?’”

The amount Ruth brings home is so unusual that Naomi immediately wants to know where she has been working. Her questions suggest that this could not have been an ordinary day of gleaning. Someone has evidently treated Ruth with exceptional generosity.

“Blessed be the one who took notice of you.”

Before Ruth even gives his name, Naomi recognizes that someone has shown her special favor. To “take notice” of Ruth means more than simply seeing her; this person has paid attention to her situation and acted kindly toward her. Naomi responds by pronouncing a blessing upon the unknown benefactor.

“So she told her mother-in-law with whom she had worked”

Ruth now explains that her remarkable day was connected to a particular man. Although she had been gleaning among the workers, Boaz’s personal intervention had transformed what she was able to gather.

“and said, ‘The man’s name with whom I worked today is Boaz.’”

For Ruth, Boaz is simply the man who has shown her extraordinary kindness that day. For Naomi, however, hearing his name will mean much more. Verse 20 will reveal that she knows exactly who Boaz is and understands a family connection that Ruth apparently does not yet recognize.

Study Notes

Ruth says she had “worked” with Boaz, although she had actually worked in his field alongside his servants. The wording naturally identifies the work with the man whose field and authority made Ruth’s extraordinary experience possible.

The narrative creates a significant moment around the name “Boaz.” Ruth had entered his field without knowing whose field it was (2:3). Now she returns home and names the man, while Naomi possesses information about him that Ruth does not. The significance of that name is about to open the story in a new direction.

Spirit & Life

Ruth began the day simply looking for a field where someone might show her favor. By evening, Naomi can see that unusual kindness has found her. Ruth still does not know how important Boaz will become, but God’s provision is already unfolding through circumstances whose full meaning she cannot yet see.

There is also a beautiful change beginning in Naomi. The woman who spoke so strongly of her emptiness and affliction in chapter 1 now sees evidence of kindness and responds with blessing. God has not yet resolved her story, but hope is beginning to awaken.

Ruth 2:20

NKJV

Ruth 2:20 Then Naomi said to her daughter-in-law, "Blessed be he of the LORD, who has not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead!" And Naomi said to her, "This man is a relation of ours, one of our close relatives."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:20 “May the LORD bless him!” Naomi told her daughter-in-law. “He is showing his kindness to us as well as to your dead husband. That man is one of our closest relatives, one of our family redeemers.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi recognizes that Ruth’s encounter with Boaz is far more significant than Ruth realized. She sees his kindness as evidence that the LORD has not abandoned His faithful care, and she reveals that Boaz is one of their close relatives with a special family responsibility toward them.

Verse Explanation

“Then Naomi said to her daughter-in-law, ‘Blessed be he of the LORD’”

Once Naomi hears Boaz’s name, her response becomes more specific. She blesses Boaz in the name of the LORD because she now understands who has shown Ruth such extraordinary favor.

“who has not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead!”

The wording most naturally points to the LORD as the One whose “kindness” has not ceased. Naomi, who had returned to Bethlehem feeling that the LORD had dealt bitterly with her, is beginning to recognize His faithful care again.

The “living” are Naomi and Ruth. The “dead” refers especially to their deceased husbands, Elimelech and Mahlon. God’s kindness toward the family has not ended with their deaths; He is providing through a relative who may help preserve the family and its future.

“And Naomi said to her, ‘This man is a relation of ours’”

Ruth knew Boaz only as the generous landowner she had met that day. Naomi now reveals something Ruth did not know: Boaz belongs to the extended family of Elimelech. What had appeared to Ruth as an extraordinarily fortunate day in a stranger’s field now carries much greater significance.

“one of our close relatives.”

The expression refers to a goel, a family redeemer or kinsman-redeemer. Under Israel’s law and customs, such a relative could have responsibilities connected with protecting family interests, including property that had passed out of the family’s possession.

Naomi does not say that Boaz is necessarily the closest relative or that he is already obligated to marry Ruth. In fact, chapter 3 will reveal that another relative has a prior claim. At this point, Naomi simply recognizes that Boaz belongs to the circle of relatives capable of acting as a redeemer.

Study Notes

The word goel comes from the idea of redeeming or reclaiming. Depending on the situation, a goel could redeem family property (Leviticus 25:25), redeem a relative who had fallen into servitude (Leviticus 25:47-49), or act in other ways to preserve and protect family interests.

The marriage situation in Ruth involves more than a simple application of the levirate marriage law of Deuteronomy 25:5-10, which specifically concerns a deceased man’s brother. Boaz is a more distant relative. Ruth brings together the themes of family redemption, inheritance, and preserving the deceased man’s family line in a distinctive way.

There is some grammatical ambiguity in “who has not forsaken His kindness.” The antecedent could theoretically be Boaz, but the LORD is most naturally understood as the source of the continuing kindness. Boaz’s generosity is the human means through which Naomi begins to recognize God’s faithful care.

Spirit & Life

This is an important turning point for Naomi. In Ruth 1:20-21, she interpreted her life almost entirely through loss: “the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me” and “the LORD has brought me home again empty.” Now she begins to interpret what is happening through the lens of God’s kindness. Her circumstances are not yet fully restored, but she can see His hand again.

Boaz also begins to bring the Bible’s theme of redemption into clearer view. He is not Christ, and every detail should not be turned into an allegory, but his role helps prepare us to understand the beauty of a redeemer: one who has the right relationship, willingness, and resources to act on behalf of those who cannot restore what has been lost by themselves. That theme will ultimately find its fullness in Jesus.

Ruth 2:21

NKJV

Ruth 2:21 Ruth the Moabitess said, "He also said to me, "˜You shall stay close by my young men until they have finished all my harvest.' "(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:21 Then Ruth said, “What’s more, Boaz even told me to come back and stay with his harvesters until the entire harvest is completed.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth tells Naomi that Boaz’s kindness extends beyond a single day. He has personally invited her to remain with his workers throughout the harvest, providing an ongoing place of safety and provision.

Verse Explanation

“Ruth the Moabitess said”

The description “the Moabitess” continues to remind us that Ruth is a foreigner in Bethlehem. What makes Boaz’s treatment of her so striking is that he has deliberately welcomed and protected someone who could easily have remained an outsider among the harvesters.

“‘He also said to me, “You shall stay close by my young men”’”

Ruth now tells Naomi something more about Boaz’s generosity. He has not merely helped her for one afternoon; he has invited her to remain with his harvesting company.

Earlier Boaz told Ruth to “stay close by my young women” (2:8). Ruth reports his words here using the broader reference to his “young men” or servants. The point is not that Ruth misunderstood Boaz’s instruction, but that she has permission to remain with his workforce rather than moving from field to field.

“‘until they have finished all my harvest.’”

Boaz’s provision has a future attached to it. Ruth does not need to begin each morning wondering whether she can find another safe field or another landowner willing to let her glean. She has been welcomed for the duration of Boaz’s harvest.

Since barley harvest was followed by wheat harvest, this invitation potentially provides Ruth and Naomi with weeks of continuing opportunity to gather food.

Study Notes

The difference between “young women” in verse 8 and “young men” here is worth noticing. The underlying terms can refer to female servants and male servants respectively. Naomi responds in verse 22 by specifically encouraging Ruth to remain with Boaz’s “young women,” reinforcing the practical arrangement Boaz originally gave her.

“All my harvest” extends beyond the barley harvest presently underway. Ruth 2:23 explicitly says Ruth continued gleaning “until the end of barley harvest and wheat harvest.” The narrative therefore confirms the breadth of Boaz’s invitation.

Spirit & Life

What Ruth first experienced as provision for one day is becoming provision for a season. She went out that morning simply hoping to “find favor” somewhere (2:2); she returns home having been given a secure place to remain throughout the harvest.

God’s provision does not always reveal its full dimensions at once. Ruth discovered one kindness after another as the day unfolded-permission to glean, protection, water, a meal, extra grain, and now an invitation to remain. What began as Ruth searching for enough food for today is quietly becoming part of God’s much larger work in her life.

Ruth 2:22

NKJV

Ruth 2:22 And Naomi said to Ruth her daughter-in-law, "It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women, and that people do not meet you in any other field."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:22 “Good!” Naomi exclaimed. “Do as he said, my daughter. Stay with his young women right through the whole harvest. You might be harassed in other fields, but you’ll be safe with him.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi recognizes the wisdom of Boaz’s arrangement and urges Ruth to remain with his female workers. Staying in his fields will not only provide food but also protect Ruth from the dangers she might encounter elsewhere.

Verse Explanation

“And Naomi said to Ruth her daughter-in-law”

Naomi responds to Ruth’s report about Boaz’s invitation. Because Naomi knows the customs and dangers Ruth could face as a foreign woman gleaning among strangers, she immediately sees the wisdom of remaining under Boaz’s protection.

“‘It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women’”

Naomi specifically directs Ruth toward Boaz’s female workers, matching Boaz’s original instruction in verse 8 to “stay close by my young women.” Ruth does not need to wander through unfamiliar fields looking for another place to glean. Boaz has already provided a favorable and protected place for her.

Naomi’s “my daughter” also reflects the genuine bond between them. Ruth is her daughter-in-law, but Naomi speaks to her with the affection and concern of a mother.

“‘and that people do not meet you in any other field.’”

The idea of “meet you” here is stronger than simply encountering someone. It carries the possibility of someone falling upon Ruth, troubling her, or harming her. Naomi understands that Ruth could be vulnerable while gleaning alone in another field.

This also sheds light on Boaz’s earlier command to his men not to touch Ruth (2:9). His protection addressed a real danger, not merely a matter of courtesy.

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “meet you” represents a verb that can describe encountering someone in a hostile or harmful way. In this context, Naomi is concerned about Ruth being mistreated or assaulted in another field. This becomes even clearer in Ruth 2:23, where Ruth follows Naomi’s advice and remains with Boaz’s female workers.

The repeated references to protection are significant. Boaz has already commanded his men not to touch Ruth (2:9), and Naomi now warns about what might happen elsewhere. The book does not portray gleaning as automatically safe simply because Israel’s law provided for it.

Spirit & Life

Naomi is beginning to care actively for Ruth’s future rather than remaining absorbed in her own grief. In chapter 1, Naomi was overwhelmed by what she had lost. Here she recognizes God’s provision and gives Ruth practical wisdom for remaining within it.

There is also wisdom in not walking away unnecessarily from a place where God has clearly provided safety and provision. Ruth does not need to keep searching simply because other fields exist. Sometimes wisdom is recognizing the good God has placed before us and faithfully remaining there.

Ruth 2:23

NKJV

Ruth 2:23 So she stayed close by the young women of Boaz, to glean until the end of barley harvest and wheat harvest; and she dwelt with her mother-in-law.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:23 So Ruth worked alongside the women in Boaz’s fields and gathered grain with them until the end of the barley harvest. Then she continued working with them through the wheat harvest in early summer. And all the while she lived with her mother-in-law. (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth follows the wise counsel she has received, remaining with Boaz’s workers through both harvest seasons. Her first day in Boaz’s field becomes a settled pattern of faithful work and continuing provision for her and Naomi.

Verse Explanation

“So she stayed close by the young women of Boaz, to glean”

Ruth does exactly what Boaz instructed and Naomi encouraged. Rather than moving among different fields, she remains with Boaz’s female workers, where she can glean under his protection and benefit from his generosity.

“until the end of barley harvest and wheat harvest”

Ruth’s connection with Boaz’s field continues well beyond the single day described earlier in the chapter. Barley was harvested first, followed by wheat. Together these harvests would ordinarily extend across several weeks, roughly from spring into early summer.

The statement also shows that Boaz’s promise in verse 21 was fulfilled. Ruth was permitted to remain throughout “all my harvest.” What began with her setting out to find somewhere to glean has become sustained provision.

“and she dwelt with her mother-in-law.”

Despite the remarkable change in Ruth’s circumstances, she continues living with Naomi. Her growing connection with Boaz has not displaced the commitment she made in chapter 1. She continues working and bringing provision home while sharing life with her mother-in-law.

The statement also closes this stage of the story without rushing ahead. Ruth has found safety, work, and food, but the larger question of the family’s future remains unresolved. That will become the focus of chapter 3.

Study Notes

Barley harvest began around Passover, while wheat harvest followed and extended toward the time of the Feast of Weeks. Ruth’s gleaning in Boaz’s fields therefore likely covered roughly six to seven weeks, though agricultural timing could vary.

The final clause, “she dwelt with her mother-in-law,” may sound like a routine detail, but it serves an important narrative purpose. Ruth has spent an extended period in proximity to Boaz, yet she remains in Naomi’s household. The relationship has not yet moved toward marriage; that development begins with Naomi’s initiative in chapter 3.

Spirit & Life

Ruth’s faithfulness is seen not merely in one dramatic decision but in what she continues doing day after day. She stays where she has been directed, works through the harvest, and remains committed to Naomi. Much of a faithful life is lived in precisely this kind of steady obedience.

Chapter 2 began with Ruth asking permission to go out and “glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor” (2:2). It ends with Ruth having found far more than permission to glean: she has experienced protection, abundant provision, belonging among Boaz’s workers, and the first unfolding of a redemption she does not yet fully understand.
