Chapter Study

Ruth 2

Verse-by-Verse
Ruth 2:1
Ruth 2:1
NKJV

Ruth 2:1 There was a relative of Naomi's husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech. His name was Boaz.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:1 Now there was a wealthy and influential man in Bethlehem named Boaz, who was a relative of Naomi’s husband, Elimelech. (NLT)

Main Idea

Before Ruth meets him, the narrator introduces Boaz to the reader as a relative of Naomi’s husband, a man of great wealth and standing from the family of Elimelech. The help the household needs is already near, though Ruth does not yet know him.

Verse Explanation

Before Ruth meets him, the narrator introduces Boaz to the reader as a relative of Naomi’s husband, a man of great wealth and standing from the family of Elimelech. The help the household needs is already near, though Ruth does not yet know him.

“There was a relative of Naomi’s husband,”

Chapter 2 opens by introducing a man the reader needs to know about before Ruth meets him. He is a “relative” of Elimelech, Naomi’s late husband. This kinship matters. In Israel a close relative carried responsibility for a family that had fallen into need, and that responsibility will drive the rest of the book.

“a man of great wealth,”

The phrase describes a man of substance and standing. It can include wealth, but it also carries the sense of strength, capability, and worth. Boaz is not simply rich; he is a man of honorable character and influence in Bethlehem. The same key word will describe Ruth as “a virtuous woman” (3:11), so the two are matched in character before they are matched in marriage.

“of the family of Elimelech. His name was Boaz.”

The narrator repeats that he is “of the family of Elimelech” and then gives his name, Boaz. The help this household needs is already near, though Ruth does not yet know him. The New Testament adds that Boaz’s mother was Rahab, the Canaanite woman who sheltered Israel’s spies at Jericho (Matthew 1:5; Joshua 2). The book of Ruth does not mention this, but it is meaningful background for a man who will soon welcome another woman from outside Israel.

Study Notes

The written Hebrew text (Kethiv) reads a word meaning acquaintance or one known, while the traditional reading (Qere), followed by the NKJV, gives a related word meaning kinsman or relative. The two forms come from the same root, and both describe a known family connection.

“A man of great wealth” (2:1) translates the Hebrew phrase gibbor chayil. Elsewhere the phrase describes mighty warriors (Judges 6:12; 11:1) and men of standing and ability (1 Samuel 9:1). The word chayil reappears in Ruth 3:11 and 4:11.

The text does not say whether Naomi had earlier sought help from Boaz, and nothing in Ruth suggests that he had refused any request.

Ruth 2:2
Ruth 2:2
NKJV

Ruth 2:2 So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, "Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor." And she said to her, "Go, my daughter."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:2 One day Ruth the Moabite said to Naomi, “Let me go out into the harvest fields to pick up the stalks of grain left behind by anyone who is kind enough to let me do it.” Naomi replied, “All right, my daughter, go ahead.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth the Moabitess asks Naomi’s permission to go to the fields and glean behind anyone in whose sight she may find favor, making use of the gleaning Israel’s law allowed the poor, the stranger, and the widow (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19). She takes responsibility for the household’s need, and Naomi sends her with a tender “Go, my daughter.”

Verse Explanation

Ruth the Moabitess asks Naomi’s permission to go to the fields and glean behind anyone in whose sight she may find favor, making use of the gleaning Israel’s law allowed the poor, the stranger, and the widow (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19). She takes responsibility for the household’s need, and Naomi sends her with a tender “Go, my daughter.”

“So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi,”

Ruth is again called “the Moabitess.” She is now living in Bethlehem, but the book keeps her origin before the reader. She is the one who speaks first and proposes a plan.

““Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.”

Ruth asks permission to “glean,” to gather the stalks of grain the harvesters left behind. Israel’s law commanded landowners not to reap to the very edges of their fields or gather what was dropped, but to leave it for the poor, the stranger, and the widow (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19). Ruth qualifies on all three counts. She also hopes to work behind someone “in whose sight I may find favor,” recognizing that not every landowner would welcome a foreign widow, and that kindness would make a great difference. She takes responsibility for providing for Naomi.

“And she said to her, “Go, my daughter.”

Naomi gives her consent with a tender “Go, my daughter.” Her short reply shows affection and trust, and it marks the first step in the providence that will unfold in the field.

Study Notes

The gleaning laws appear in Leviticus 19:9–10, Leviticus 23:22, and Deuteronomy 24:19–21. They provided for the vulnerable through their own labor in the field, rather than through a handout.

“Favor” (2:2) translates the Hebrew word chen, grace or favor shown by a superior to someone who has no claim on it. The word returns at 2:10 and 2:13, tying Ruth’s hope to its answer.

Ruth 2:3
Ruth 2:3
NKJV

Ruth 2:3 Then she left, and went and gleaned in the field after the reapers. And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was of the family of Elimelech.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:3 So Ruth went out to gather grain behind the harvesters. And as it happened, she found herself working in a field that belonged to Boaz, the relative of her father-in-law, Elimelech. (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth goes out and gleans behind the reapers, and she “happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz,” the relative from Elimelech’s family. What looks like chance is God’s providence working through her ordinary initiative.

Verse Explanation

Ruth goes out and gleans behind the reapers, and she “happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz,” the relative from Elimelech’s family. What looks like chance is God’s providence working through her ordinary initiative.

“Then she left, and went and gleaned in the field after the reapers.”

Ruth does not wait for help to come to her. She goes out, finds a field, and begins to glean behind the reapers. Her diligence and initiative are part of the way God provides.

“And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz,”

From Ruth’s point of view, it is simply where she ended up. The wording, literally ‘her chance chanced upon,’ stresses how unplanned it seemed. Yet the reader already knows who Boaz is (2:1), and the rest of the story shows that this was no accident. What looks like chance is God’s providence working through Ruth’s ordinary initiative. God directs events without overriding her choices; she chose to work, and He guided her steps (compare Proverbs 16:9).

“who was of the family of Elimelech.”

For the second time in three verses the narrator reminds the reader that Boaz belongs to Elimelech’s family. The repetition presses home the significance of the field Ruth has entered.

Study Notes

“Happened to come” (2:3) translates a Hebrew phrase built on the root qarah, to meet or happen, used twice in close succession. The emphatic wording highlights the apparent coincidence and invites the reader to see more in it.

The wording “the part of the field belonging to Boaz” (2:3) identifies a particular portion of the field as his. The text gives no further detail about how the land was divided among owners.

Ruth 2:4
NKJV

NLT

Ruth 2:4 While she was there, Boaz arrived from Bethlehem and greeted the harvesters. “The LORD be with you!” he said. “The LORD bless you!” the harvesters replied. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz arrives from Bethlehem and greets his reapers with “The LORD be with you!” and they answer, “The LORD bless you!” The exchange shows a man whose faith in the LORD shapes the way he treats the people who work for him.

Verse Explanation

Boaz arrives from Bethlehem and greets his reapers with “The LORD be with you!” and they answer, “The LORD bless you!” The exchange shows a man whose faith in the LORD shapes the way he treats the people who work for him.

“Now behold, Boaz came from Bethlehem,”

“Behold” draws attention to the timing. Just as Ruth is working in his part of the field, Boaz himself arrives from town. The story moves forward through another well-timed moment.

“and said to the reapers, “The LORD be with you!” And they answered him, “The LORD bless you!”

Boaz greets his workers in the name of the LORD, and they respond with a blessing of their own. This exchange is more than polite custom. It shows a man whose faith in the LORD shapes the way he treats the people who work for him, and a workplace where the LORD’s name is honored. Boaz’s later kindness to Ruth flows from the same character. It is the fruit of his faith, not the start of a romantic pursuit.

Study Notes

Similar greetings and blessings appear in Psalm 129:8, where passersby fail to bless the reapers of the wicked, and in Judges 6:12, where the Angel of the LORD greets Gideon with “The LORD is with you.”

Ruth 2:5

NLT

Ruth 2:5 Then Boaz asked his foreman, “Who is that young woman over there? Who does she belong to?” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz asks the servant in charge of the reapers whose young woman this is. He notices the newcomer among the gleaners and wants to know to whom she belongs.

Verse Explanation

Boaz asks the servant in charge of the reapers whose young woman this is. He notices the newcomer among the gleaners and wants to know to whom she belongs.

“Then Boaz said to his servant who was in charge of the reapers,”

Boaz speaks to the foreman, the servant responsible for the harvest crew. As owner, he notices who is working in his field.

““Whose young woman is this?”

Boaz asks to whom she belongs. In that culture a young woman was identified by her family or household, whether father, husband, or master. His question seeks to place her socially and suggests that she stands out among the gleaners.

Study Notes

“Young woman” (2:5) translates the Hebrew word na‘arah, a girl or young woman, often one in service. It does not by itself indicate marital status; Ruth is a widow.

Ruth 2:6
NKJV

Ruth 2:6 So the servant who was in charge of the reapers answered and said, "It is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:6 And the foreman replied, “She is the young woman from Moab who came back with Naomi. (NLT)

Main Idea

The servant identifies her as the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab. His answer stresses that she is a foreigner while connecting her to Naomi, Boaz’s own relative.

Verse Explanation

The servant identifies her as the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab. His answer stresses that she is a foreigner while connecting her to Naomi, Boaz’s own relative.

“So the servant who was in charge of the reapers answered and said,”

The foreman responds, and his description gives Boaz the essential facts about her.

““It is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab.”

The foreman identifies her in two ways. She is “Moabite,” and he even repeats “the country of Moab,” stressing that she is a foreigner. She is also the one who “came back with Naomi,” linking her to a woman Boaz knows and to his own relative. Ruth’s story has evidently been talked about in Bethlehem.

Study Notes

The servant’s phrase “came back” (2:6) uses the same verb of returning that dominates chapter 1, even though Ruth had never lived in Bethlehem before. The usage matches 1:22.

Ruth 2:7
NKJV

Ruth 2:7 And she said, "˜Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves.' So she came and has continued from morning until now, though she rested a little in the house."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:7 She asked me this morning if she could gather grain behind the harvesters. She has been hard at work ever since, except for a few minutes’ rest in the shelter.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The servant reports that Ruth asked permission to glean and gather among the sheaves and has worked steadily since morning, resting only a little in the shelter. His account shows her humility in asking and her diligence in working.

Verse Explanation

The servant reports that Ruth asked permission to glean and gather among the sheaves and has worked steadily since morning, resting only a little in the shelter. His account shows her humility in asking and her diligence in working.

“And she said, ‘Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves.’”

The foreman reports that Ruth asked permission before gleaning. Her request was respectful. She did not presume, and she asked to gather “after the reapers among the sheaves,” working behind the harvesters where the grain was being bundled.

“So she came and has continued from morning until now,”

The foreman adds that she has worked steadily since early morning. Her diligence has been noticed.

“though she rested a little in the house.”

Ruth has taken only a short break in “the house,” probably a field shelter for the workers. The report shows both her humility in asking and her diligence in working.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the last part of this verse is notoriously difficult, and translations vary considerably. The NKJV’s “though she rested a little in the house” is one reasonable reading; others take it to mean that her sitting in the shelter was brief, or that she had scarcely rested at all.

Some interpreters understand “among the sheaves” (2:7) as a request beyond normal gleaning rights, which Boaz grants later in 2:15. Others take it as describing where she wished to gather behind the reapers. The text allows either reading.

Ruth 2:8–13 — Boaz’s Protection and Blessing for the Foreigner

Ruth 2:8
Ruth 2:8
NKJV

Ruth 2:8 Then Boaz said to Ruth, "You will listen, my daughter, will you not? Do not go to glean in another field, nor go from here, but stay close by my young women.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:8 Boaz went over and said to Ruth, “Listen, my daughter. Stay right here with us when you gather grain; don’t go to any other fields. Stay right behind the young women working in my field. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz speaks to Ruth directly, calling her “my daughter,” and tells her not to glean in another field but to stay close by his young women. His instruction gives her a secure place to work in his field.

Verse Explanation

Boaz speaks to Ruth directly, calling her “my daughter,” and tells her not to glean in another field but to stay close by his young women. His instruction gives her a secure place to work in his field.

“Then Boaz said to Ruth, “You will listen, my daughter, will you not?”

Boaz now speaks to Ruth directly. He calls her “my daughter,” a warm address that also suggests he is older than she is. His question is a gentle way of introducing his instructions.

“Do not go to glean in another field, nor go from here,”

Boaz urges her to stay in his field for the harvest. He wants her to have a secure place to work rather than moving from field to field among strangers.

“but stay close by my young women.”

He tells her to keep near the women who work for him. The word for “stay close” is the same verb used when Ruth “clung” to Naomi (1:14). Among his women she will have companionship and safety.

Study Notes

“Stay close” (2:8) translates the Hebrew verb davaq, the verb used in 1:14 for Ruth’s clinging to Naomi. It reappears in 2:21 and 2:23.

Ruth 2:9
Ruth 2:9
NKJV

Ruth 2:9 Let your eyes be on the field which they reap, and go after them. Have I not commanded the young men not to touch you? And when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink from what the young men have drawn."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:9 See which part of the field they are harvesting, and then follow them. I have warned the young men not to treat you roughly. And when you are thirsty, help yourself to the water they have drawn from the well.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz tells Ruth to follow his women in the field, assures her that he has commanded the young men not to touch her, and invites her to drink from the water they have drawn. He provides both protection from harm and practical care for her needs.

Verse Explanation

Boaz tells Ruth to follow his women in the field, assures her that he has commanded the young men not to touch her, and invites her to drink from the water they have drawn. He provides both protection from harm and practical care for her needs.

“Let your eyes be on the field which they reap, and go after them.”

Boaz tells Ruth to watch where his crew is reaping and follow along behind them. He is making sure she has continuing access to the best gleaning in his fields.

“Have I not commanded the young men not to touch you?”

Boaz has already ordered his young men “not to touch” her, that is, not to harass or harm her. A foreign widow working alone in the fields was vulnerable, and Boaz uses his authority to protect her.

“And when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink from what the young men have drawn.”

He also gives her access to the water his workers have drawn. In the heat of harvest this was a real kindness. Boaz provides both protection from harm and practical care for her needs.

Study Notes

“Touch” (2:9) translates the Hebrew verb naga‘, which can mean harm, molest, or treat roughly. Naomi’s warning in 2:22 about being met in another field reflects the same concern for Ruth’s safety.

Ruth 2:10
Ruth 2:10
NKJV

Ruth 2:10 So she fell on her face, bowed down to the ground, and said to him, "Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?"(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:10 Ruth fell at his feet and thanked him warmly. “What have I done to deserve such kindness?” she asked. “I am only a foreigner.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth falls on her face and asks why she has found favor in his eyes, that he should take notice of her, since she is a foreigner. She comes from Moab, a people who had opposed Israel and were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6), so Boaz’s protection and welcome, far beyond ordinary gleaning, are unearned kindness and an expression of God’s redeeming grace.

Verse Explanation

Ruth falls on her face and asks why she has found favor in his eyes, that he should take notice of her, since she is a foreigner. She comes from Moab, a people who had opposed Israel and were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6), so Boaz’s protection and welcome, far beyond ordinary gleaning, are unearned kindness and an expression of God’s redeeming grace.

“So she fell on her face, bowed down to the ground,”

Ruth responds with deep humility and gratitude. Falling on her face and bowing to the ground was a gesture of profound respect toward someone of higher standing.

“and said to him, “Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?”

Ruth asks why he would show her such favor. Her hope in 2:2 to find someone “in whose sight I may find favor” has been answered beyond what she expected. She calls herself “a foreigner,” and the word carries real weight. She comes from Moab, a people who had opposed Israel on the way to Canaan and were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6). Gleaning itself was open to the stranger and the widow under Israel’s law, so Ruth was not asking for something forbidden. But Boaz’s protection, his water, and his personal welcome go far beyond ordinary gleaning. They are unearned kindness, and they express God’s redeeming grace toward a woman from a people who had been Israel’s enemies. Ruth’s ethnicity is not her personal guilt; it is the background that makes this grace so striking.

Study Notes

In Hebrew, “take notice of me” (2:10) and “foreigner” sound alike: the verb is from nakar, to recognize or regard, and the noun is nokriyyah, a foreign woman. The wordplay sharpens Ruth’s question.

The Hebrew word translated “foreigner” (nokriyyah) generally describes someone from another people without settled standing in the community, whereas a ‘stranger’ (ger) describes a resident alien. Ruth uses the stronger term of herself.

Main Idea

Boaz answers that he has heard everything Ruth has done for her mother-in-law since her husband’s death, and how she left her father, her mother, and her homeland to come to a people she did not know. Her costly loyalty to Naomi has become known in Bethlehem.

Verse Explanation

Boaz answers that he has heard everything Ruth has done for her mother-in-law since her husband’s death, and how she left her father, her mother, and her homeland to come to a people she did not know. Her costly loyalty to Naomi has become known in Bethlehem.

“And Boaz answered and said to her, “It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband,”

Boaz explains that Ruth’s reputation has gone before her. He has heard “all” she has done for Naomi since Mahlon’s death: her loyalty, her refusal to leave, and her care. Ruth’s costly love for Naomi has become known in Bethlehem.

“and how you have left your father and your mother and the land of your birth,”

Boaz recognizes the cost of her choice. She left her parents and her homeland. The words echo the LORD’s call to Abram to leave his country, his family, and his father’s house (Genesis 12:1), and like Abram, Ruth goes out without knowing what lies ahead.

“and have come to a people whom you did not know before.”

She has come to live among strangers. Boaz honors her courage in joining a people she did not previously know. His answer shows that he has noticed not only her work in the field but the faithfulness of her life.

Study Notes

The phrasing “your father and your mother and the land of your birth” (2:11) resembles Genesis 12:1 and Genesis 24:7, passages concerning Abraham’s departure from his homeland.

Ruth 2:12
Ruth 2:12
NKJV

Ruth 2:12 The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 2:12 May the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge, reward you fully for what you have done.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz prays that the LORD will repay Ruth’s work and give her a full reward, naming Him the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings she has come for refuge. He recognizes that she has come to trust Israel’s God, and his blessing prepares for her own request in 3:9.

Verse Explanation

Boaz prays that the LORD will repay Ruth’s work and give her a full reward, naming Him the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings she has come for refuge. He recognizes that she has come to trust Israel’s God, and his blessing prepares for her own request in 3:9.

“The LORD repay your work,”

Boaz prays that the LORD will reward Ruth for what she has done. He sees her faithful kindness to Naomi as work that the LORD notices and honors. His words are a prayer, not a claim that Ruth has earned God’s favor.

“and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel,”

He asks for a “full reward” from “the LORD God of Israel.” By naming Israel’s God, Boaz recognizes that Ruth has come to Him, not merely to Naomi.

“under whose wings you have come for refuge.”

This is one of the most beautiful phrases in the book. Boaz pictures the LORD as a bird sheltering its young under its wings, an image found in Israel’s worship (Psalm 36:7; 57:1; 91:4). Ruth has come to the LORD for protection and belonging. The same word for “wings” will return when Ruth asks Boaz to spread his “wing” over her (3:9); the man who prays for her refuge will become the means by which God answers that prayer. Boaz does not replace the LORD as her refuge, but his care becomes the way that refuge is given. In this way Boaz begins to show the character of a redeemer, and he becomes a picture of Jesus Christ, who longed to gather His people “as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings” (Matthew 23:37).

Study Notes

“Wings” (2:12) translates the Hebrew word kanaph, which can mean the wing of a bird or the corner or edge of a garment. Ruth uses the same word at 3:9.

“Full reward” translates the Hebrew word maskoret, a word for wages. Genesis 29:15 and Genesis 31:7 use it for Jacob’s wages from Laban.

“Maidservant” here translates the Hebrew word shiphchah, a servant girl of low standing. At 3:9 Ruth will call herself Boaz’s ’amah, a word often used for a servant who could become a wife.

Main Idea

At mealtime Boaz invites Ruth to eat bread and dip it in the vinegar, and she sits beside the reapers while he passes her parched grain. She eats until she is satisfied and keeps some back, receiving a place at his table and food to spare.

Verse Explanation

At mealtime Boaz invites Ruth to eat bread and dip it in the vinegar, and she sits beside the reapers while he passes her parched grain. She eats until she is satisfied and keeps some back, receiving a place at his table and food to spare.

“Now Boaz said to her at mealtime, “Come here, and eat of the bread, and dip your piece of bread in the vinegar.”

At the midday meal Boaz invites Ruth to join the workers. He offers her bread and the “vinegar,” a sour wine used for dipping bread. This invitation brings the foreign gleaner into the circle of his household at his table.

“So she sat beside the reapers, and he passed parched grain to her;”

Ruth sits beside the reapers, and Boaz himself serves her “parched grain,” roasted kernels of grain. The owner of the field personally hands food to the Moabite widow.

“and she ate and was satisfied, and kept some back.”

Ruth eats until she is “satisfied” and still has food left over. She keeps some back, which she will later give to Naomi (2:18). The text does not say she refused part of the meal or went hungry. She received enough for herself and more to share.

Study Notes

“Vinegar” (2:14) translates the Hebrew word chomets, a sour wine or wine vinegar. Mixed with water or used as a dip, it was refreshing in the heat of harvest.

“Parched grain” translates the Hebrew word qali, grain roasted over a fire, a common simple food (compare 1 Samuel 17:17; 25:18).

Verse Explanation

Naomi blesses Boaz by the LORD, “who has not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead,” and tells Ruth that he is a relation, one of their close relatives. Hope begins to stir, for a close relative had the right and duty to help redeem a family in need.

“Then Naomi said to her daughter-in-law, “Blessed be he of the LORD, who has not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead!”

Naomi bursts into blessing. She asks the LORD to bless Boaz and praises the one “who has not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead.” The NKJV reads this as the LORD’s kindness, and the word is the loyal love Naomi spoke of in 1:8. Naomi, who said the LORD had brought her home empty, now sees His faithful kindness reaching both the living, herself and Ruth, and the dead, Elimelech and Mahlon, whose family line might yet continue. Whether the kindness is the LORD’s or Boaz’s, the LORD’s kindness is reaching Naomi and Ruth through Boaz.

“And Naomi said to her, “This man is a relation of ours, one of our close relatives.”

Naomi now explains why Boaz matters: he is “one of our close relatives.” The Hebrew word is go’el, a kinsman-redeemer. In Israel a close relative had the right and responsibility to buy back family land that had been sold and to help a relative who had fallen into poverty (Leviticus 25:25, 47–49). For the first time in the book, hope begins to stir. A man with the right to help this family has shown kindness to Ruth. Boaz becomes a picture of the greater Redeemer, Jesus Christ, who shares our humanity and has the right and the will to redeem.

Study Notes

The Hebrew relative clause in Naomi’s blessing can refer either to the LORD or to Boaz as the one who has not forsaken kindness. The NKJV capitalizes “His,” taking it as the LORD. A similar blessing in 2 Samuel 2:5 uses the same construction for human kindness, which is why some interpreters refer it to Boaz.

“Close relatives” (2:20) translates the Hebrew word go’el, meaning one who redeems. This is its first appearance in Ruth; it occurs many times in chapters 3 and 4.
