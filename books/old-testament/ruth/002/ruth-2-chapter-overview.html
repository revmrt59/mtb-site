Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 2
Main Idea:

Ruth goes out to glean and comes to the field of Boaz, a relative of Elimelech, whose protection and generosity toward the Moabite widow give Naomi her first hope that a close relative may help the family.

Setting:

Naomi and Ruth have arrived in Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest (1:22). Israel’s law allowed the poor, the stranger, and the widow to glean the grain that harvesters left behind (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19).

Chapter Outline:

  • Ruth 2:1–3 — Ruth Comes to Glean in the Field of Boaz

  • Ruth 2:4–7 — Boaz Greets His Reapers and Asks About Ruth

  • Ruth 2:8–13 — Boaz’s Protection and Blessing for the Foreigner

  • Ruth 2:14–17 — A Place at the Meal and Grain Left on Purpose

  • Ruth 2:18–23 — Naomi Recognizes Boaz as a Close Relative

Key Verse:

“The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge.” (Ruth 2:12)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Ruth 2 shows God’s providence working through ordinary days and ordinary faithfulness. Before Ruth meets him, the reader learns that Boaz is a wealthy man of Elimelech’s family. Ruth takes responsibility for Naomi and goes out to glean, and she “happened to come” to his part of the field (2:3). What looks like chance is the LORD guiding the steps of a woman who is simply doing the next faithful thing.

Boaz greets his workers in the LORD’s name, and his kindness to Ruth grows out of that same faith. He protects her, gives her water, seats her at his meal, and has grain dropped for her on purpose. Ruth, a Moabite, asks why he would take notice of a foreigner, and Boaz answers with a blessing: may she be rewarded by the LORD God of Israel, “under whose wings you have come for refuge” (2:12). Her welcome is unearned grace toward a woman from a people who had been Israel’s enemies.

At home, Naomi hears the name Boaz and blesses him in the LORD’s name, praising the kindness shown “to the living and the dead.” Naomi tells Ruth that Boaz is “one of our close relatives” (2:20), and for the first time hope begins to stir. In Boaz’s care for a foreign widow, the book begins to picture the greater Redeemer, Jesus Christ.

Chapter Sections

Ruth 2:1–3 — Ruth Comes to Glean in the Field of Boaz

The narrator introduces Boaz, a wealthy relative from Elimelech’s family, before Ruth meets him. Ruth sets out to glean where she may find favor and, without planning it, comes to the part of the field that belongs to Boaz.

Ruth 2:4–7 — Boaz Greets His Reapers and Asks About Ruth

Boaz arrives and greets his reapers in the LORD’s name, and they bless him in return. His foreman tells him that the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi asked permission to glean and has worked hard since morning.

Ruth 2:8–13 — Boaz’s Protection and Blessing for the Foreigner

Boaz tells Ruth to stay in his field, protects her from harm, and offers her water. When she asks why he would notice a foreigner, he praises her loyalty to Naomi and blesses her as one who has taken refuge under the wings of the LORD God of Israel.

Ruth 2:14–17 — A Place at the Meal and Grain Left on Purpose

Boaz invites Ruth to eat with his reapers and serves her himself, and she eats until she is satisfied with food left over. He then has his men let her glean among the sheaves and drop grain on purpose for her, and she gathers about an ephah of barley by evening.

Ruth 2:18–23 — Naomi Recognizes Boaz as a Close Relative

Ruth brings the grain and the saved food home, and Naomi blesses the man who noticed her. On hearing the name Boaz, Naomi recognizes him as one of their close relatives, and Ruth gleans in his fields until the barley and wheat harvests end.

Why This Chapter Matters

Ruth 2 shows that God’s providence and human faithfulness work together. Ruth does not sit and wait; she works, asks, and cares for Naomi. Boaz does not merely keep the minimum of the law; he uses his resources to protect and provide. Through both, God’s provision goes beyond the grain Ruth set out to gather and opens hope through a family redeemer.

The chapter also shows the character of a redeemer before the act of redemption. Boaz’s kinship, means, and willing kindness prepare for chapters 3 and 4, and they give a first picture of how the greater Redeemer, Jesus Christ, welcomes and shelters those who come to Him.

Key Themes to Watch For

Providence through ordinary events. Ruth simply goes out to work, yet she comes to the field of Boaz (2:3), and Naomi later sees the LORD’s kindness in it (2:20).

Faithful work and generous grace. Ruth labors from morning until evening (2:7, 17), while Boaz gives far more than the gleaning law required (2:15–16).

Refuge under God’s wings. Boaz blesses Ruth as one who has come for refuge under the LORD’s wings (2:12), an image Ruth will take up in her request in 3:9.

Favor toward a foreigner. Ruth asks why she has found favor “since I am a foreigner” (2:10), and Boaz’s welcome shows God’s grace to a Moabite woman (2:10–13).

The close relative. Naomi recognizes Boaz as one of their close relatives (2:20), opening the possibility of redemption.

Key Words & Concepts

Gleaning (2:2). Gathering the stalks of grain left behind by the harvesters. Israel’s law required landowners to leave this grain for the poor, the stranger, and the widow (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19).

A man of great wealth (2:1). A man of standing, ability, and worth, not only riches. The Hebrew word chayil in this phrase appears again when Boaz calls Ruth “a virtuous woman” (3:11), so the book describes both of them as people of noble worth, matched in character before they are joined in marriage. Matthew 1:5 names Rahab, the Canaanite woman of Jericho, as Boaz’s mother, background that enriches his welcome of another woman from outside Israel. Boaz himself explains his kindness by what he has heard of Ruth’s loyalty to Naomi and her refuge in the LORD (2:11–12).

Foreigner (2:10). Ruth calls herself an outsider with no claim on the extraordinary personal favor Boaz shows her, beyond the ordinary gleaning the law allowed the foreigner and the widow. As a Moabite, she belongs to a people who had been barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6), which makes his kindness unearned grace.

Wings (2:12). The Hebrew word kanaph can mean the wing of a bird or the corner of a garment. Boaz uses it for the LORD’s protection, and Ruth uses the same word when she asks Boaz to cover her (3:9).

Ephah (2:17). A dry measure for grain. Its exact modern equivalent is uncertain, but about an ephah of barley was a remarkable amount for one gleaner in a single day.

Close relative (2:20). The Hebrew word go’el means one who redeems. A close relative had the right and duty to buy back family land and help a relative in need (Leviticus 25:25), a role Boaz will fulfill. Naomi blesses Boaz in the LORD’s name, and the kindness she praises (2:20) may be the LORD’s, as the NKJV’s “His kindness” indicates, or Boaz’s; either way, God’s kindness reaches the two widows through Boaz.
