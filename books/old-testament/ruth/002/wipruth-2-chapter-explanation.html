Ruth Chapter 2

Chapter Explanation – Ruth

Outline

  2. Boaz Notices Ruth (Ruth 2:4–7)

  3. Boaz Extends Grace and Protection (Ruth 2:8–16)

1. Ruth Goes to the Fields (Ruth 2:1–3)

Ruth 2:1 There was a relative (H4129) of Naomi's husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech. His name was Boaz. (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

Here Boaz is briefly introduced as he is about to become a significant part of the story.

The timing of this introduction is significant. Naomi and Ruth have returned to Bethlehem empty, grieving, and uncertain about the future. Before Ruth ever encounters him or Naomi speaks of him, the writer wants us to know there is a man of great wealth named Boaz connected to Elimelech's family. The author is signaling that God's provision is already present, though neither Naomi or Ruth is aware.

There are three things to note about Boaz at this point.

  1. Boaz is described as a "relative" of Elimelech. The Hebrew term indicates a family connection and prepares the way for the later discussion of the kinsman-redeemer (goel). In Israel, a close relative could assume certain responsibilities to preserve a family's inheritance and care for vulnerable family members. At this point, however, the author simply plants the seed without explaining its full significance.

  2. He is described as “a man of great wealth," showing that Boaz did not just have the family relationship required to help, but also had financial ability to do so as well. Additionally this phrase can carry a broader meaning indicating that Boaz was not just materially wealthy, but he was a person of standing, influence, capability, character, and significance in the community.

  3. Lastly he is "of the family of Elimelech". This reminds us that though the family line seemed destined for extinction at the end of Chapter 1 and that Naomi appears to have no future; God has already positioned someone qualified and capable whom restoration may come.

Dwell

How often do we assume there is no answer because we cannot yet see one? Ruth and Naomi had no idea that Boaz existed as part of God's plan for their future. Yet before they took a single step toward restoration, God had already placed a redeemer in Bethlehem.

Faith often means trusting that God is working behind the scenes even when we cannot see what He is doing. What appears to be an impossible situation may already contain the beginnings of God's answer. Christ our redeemer, and His work in our life, are closer than we think.

Selah

Ruth 2:2 So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor. And she said to her, Go, my daughter. (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

Having arrived in Bethlehem with no apparent means of support, Ruth takes the initiative to provide for herself and Naomi. Rather than waiting passively for circumstances to change, she seeks an opportunity to work.

The repeated description, "Ruth the Moabitess," is intentional. Even though Ruth has committed herself to Naomi, Israel, and Israel's God, the author continues to remind us she is a foreigner from Israel’s idolatrous enemy. She is an outsider who does not belong (at least from a human perspective).

Ruth recognizes the need for food and to provide for Naomi and so she requests going into the fields to glean. In ancient Israel, God protected the vulnerable through a system called gleaning. Landowners were commanded to leave the corners of their fields unharvested and to drop grain for the poor, the widow, and the foreigner to gather (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19–22). This both provided for the poor through their own labor, without stripping away their dignity or creating system in entitlement and dependence like we see today.

We should not miss the courage it took for Ruth to walk into those fields. When she says she will glean after the one “in whose sight I may find favor,” she understands her position. She is not only poor and widowed; she is a Moabite woman in Bethlehem.

God’s law made provision for the poor, the widow, and the foreigner, but Ruth still needed someone to receive her with kindness. This was the time of the Judges, when many people were doing what was right in their own eyes. Ruth could not assume she would be welcomed.

She was stepping into the fields as an outsider with no husband, no family covering, and no standing in the community. She needed grace. She needed favor. She needed to find someone whose heart was moved by mercy rather than suspicion or rejection.

Ruth was not simply looking for grain. She was stepping out in faith, trusting that somewhere in Bethlehem there might be a field where kindness would meet her need

Naomi’s response is brief: “Go, my daughter.” She gives Ruth permission, but she does not offer a plan or suggest a way forward. After the grief and bitterness of chapter 1, Naomi still seems weighed down by sorrow. At this point in the chapter, Ruth becomes the one who steps forward in faith. Naomi cannot yet see what God is doing, but Ruth’s simple act of going to the field will become the very place where God begins to reveal His hidden provision.

Dwell

1. Faith Takes Initiative

Ruth could have stayed home and focused on everything she had lost. She could have waited for someone else to solve the problem. Instead, she took initiative and sought a way to provide for Naomi and herself.

Faith is not passive. While we trust God for provision, guidance, and breakthrough, there are times when faith requires us to get up and take the next step that is in front of us. Ruth did not know how God would provide, but she was willing to move forward in obedience.

Are you waiting for the Christian Lotto or manna from heaven when God is waiting for you to take the first step?

2. We Don't Always Get to Choose the Means of God's Provision

Ruth's circumstances left her with few options. She was a widow, a foreigner, and without resources. The work before her was difficult, humbling, and uncertain, yet she was willing to embrace it because it was the provision God had placed before her.

Sometimes we miss God's provision because it does not arrive in the package we expected. We pray for help, but the answer comes through hard work or a job we do not particular want. It might be something we consider to be beneath us. We ask God to provide, but His provision requires humility, sacrifice, or perseverance on our part.

Ruth did not complain about having to glean. She did not wait for a better opportunity. She embraced the provision God had made available and trusted Him with the outcome.

Many people want God's provision but reject the path through which He chooses to provide it either because it is too hard, or because of their pride. Faith receives God's provision even when it comes in unexpected ways.

Selah

3. The Principle of Wise Compassion

God's law for gleaning reveals His heart for those in need. He made provision for the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the foreigner, ensuring that no one was completely abandoned. At the same time, He designed that provision in a way that preserved dignity, responsibility, and work.

The poor were not left without help, but neither were they encouraged to become dependent. They were given an opportunity to labor, gather, and participate in their own provision.

This balance reflects God's wisdom. Compassion without requiring responsibility can create dependence. Responsibility without compassion can be harsh. God's way joins both together. He calls His people to care for those in need while helping them walk in dignity, purpose, and responsibility.

Whenever God blesses us, we should ask two questions: How can I show compassion to those in need? And how can I do so in a way that strengthens rather than weakens them? God's provision often meets immediate needs while also preserving human dignity.

Selah

4. Grace Begins with Recognizing Our Need

Ruth says she will glean after the one "in whose sight I may find favor." She understands that she needs grace. As a Moabite woman in Bethlehem, she has no reason to presume upon anyone's kindness.

One of the first steps toward receiving grace is recognizing our need for it. Pride believes it deserves favor. Humility understands that everything we receive is ultimately a gift from God's hand.

Selah

5. Ruth Refused to Be Defined by Her Circumstances

Ruth had every reason to give up. She was a widow. She was poor. She was a foreigner living in an unfamiliar land. Yet she refused to allow grief, fear, or uncertainty to keep her from moving forward.

Many people become stuck because they focus on what has happened to them. Ruth focused on what she could do next. She could not change the past, but she could take the next faithful step.

Selah

6. God Blesses Faithful Effort

There is a simple principle illustrated throughout Scripture: God often meets us while we are moving in obedience.

Ruth did not know she was walking toward Boaz's field. She simply went looking for grain. Yet God directed her steps while she was faithfully doing what was in front of her.

As the saying goes, "The harder I work, the luckier I get." For the believer, we might say it this way: the more faithfully we walk in obedience, the more opportunities we have to see God's providence at work.

7. God's Providence Is Often Waiting in the Next Step

Ruth had no idea what that day would bring. She did not know she would meet Boaz. She did not know she was stepping into a story that would ultimately lead to redemption, King David, and eventually the Messiah.

The field looked ordinary, but God's provision and providence was waiting there. Often God does not reveal the entire journey. He simply asks us to take the next step of obedience. Many of God's greatest works begin when His people simply do the next faithful thing in front of them.

Selah.

Ruth 2:3 Then she left, and went and gleaned in the field after the reapers. And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was of the family of Elimelech. (NKJV)

Ruth immediately acts on the plan she proposed to Naomi. She leaves the safety of home and enters the fields to glean behind the reapers, the workers harvesting the grain. We should not overlook how hard and exhausting this work was. As the reapers cut the barley and gathered it into bundles, Ruth followed behind them, stooping again and again to pick up what had been missed or dropped. Gleaning was slow, exhausting work and often produced very little in return. Every handful mattered because it represented daily survival for Naomi and herself.

We must also remember that Ruth entered those fields completely alone. The other women gleaning out there belonged to local families and to the community. Ruth was a foreigner, a widow, and a Moabite living among strangers. She had no husband to protect her, no family network to support her, and stood out instantly as an outsider.

Yet, the most significant phrase in this entire verse is, "she happened to come" to the field belonging to Boaz.

On the surface, it sounds like a total coincidence. From Ruth's perspective, it likely appeared to be just another field among many. She had no way of knowing who owned it or what role Boaz would play in her future. Ruth "just happened" to arrive at the exact field owned by the very man through whom God intended to bring provision, protection, and ultimately redemption.

Dwell

1. Faith often requires hard work

Ruth did not only pray for provision; she went into the field and worked. Gleaning was slow, tiring, humbling labor. It meant walking behind the reapers, bending again and again, gathering what others had missed or dropped. It was not easy work, and it did not usually produce much. But Ruth was willing to do it because Naomi needed care and because this was the door of provision God had placed in front of her.

Faith is not afraid of hard work. Sometimes God's provision comes through effort, endurance, and humility. Ruth shows us that trusting God does not mean sitting still while others carry the burden. She took responsibility for the household in front of her and did the next faithful thing.

There are times when the most spiritual thing we can do is get up, go to the field, and faithfully gather what God has placed before us.

Selah.

2. Learn to Recognize the “It Just Happened” Moments

Ruth “happened to come” to the field of Boaz. From her perspective, it may have looked like chance. She simply chose a field and started working. But from God’s perspective, her steps were being directed.

How many “it just happened” moments have we had that were actually the hidden hand of God? A conversation, a meeting, a delay, an opportunity, an unexpected kindness, or even a closed door may look ordinary at first. But later, we realize God was guiding us all along.

Part of spiritual maturity is learning to discern and redeem these kairos moments-the appointed moments where God is quietly opening a door, ordering our steps, revealing His provision, or opening up new opportunities.

3. Faith Takes Responsibility for their Household

Ruth did not ignore Naomi’s need. She did not leave her mother-in-law to fend for herself. She took responsibility for the family God had placed in front of her and went to work.

Scripture teaches that caring for our own household matters deeply to God:

1 Timothy 5:8 But if anyone does not provide for his own, and especially for those of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever. (NKJV)

Faith is not only expressed in worship, our service, or by what we profess with mouths. It is also seen in practical responsibility, sacrifice, provision, and care for those entrusted to us, especially our family.

4. Providence Often Meets Us While We Are Moving

Ruth did not discover Boaz while sitting at home wondering what God might do. She found God's provision while faithfully doing what was in front of her.

Many believers want God to reveal the entire plan before taking the first step. Yet throughout Scripture, God often guides people while they are moving in obedience. Abraham left before he knew where he was going. Israel stepped into the Jordan before the waters parted. Ruth entered the field before she knew it belonged to Boaz.

God's guidance is often discovered on the path of obedience rather than before it.

5. God Is Working on More Than We Can See

Ruth entered the field because she needed food for the day. God was working on the future of a family, the restoration of an inheritance, the lineage of David, and ultimately the coming of the Messiah.

We tend to pray about the immediate problem in front of us. God certainly cares about those needs, but He is often accomplishing far more than we realize. While we are focused on today's provision, He is often preparing answers that reach far beyond the present moment and extend into eternity.

Ruth thought she was choosing a field. In reality, God was directing her steps. What looked like an ordinary day would become one of the most important days of her life. Never underestimate what God may be doing through an ordinary act of obedience. The next step of faith may place you in the very place where His providence is waiting.

6. We Are Looking for a Need to Be Met; God Is Looking to Reveal Christ

Ruth woke up that morning looking for grain. She was concerned about a very real and immediate need. She needed food for herself and Naomi. From her perspective, the goal was simple: find a field, work hard, and bring home enough grain to survive.

But God was doing something far greater. While Ruth was looking for grain, God was leading her toward a redeemer.

This is often how God works in our lives. We come to Him focused on a need, a problem, a burden, a crisis, or a prayer request. We are looking for provision, direction, healing, wisdom, restoration, or breakthrough. God certainly cares about those needs, but His ultimate purpose is always greater than the need itself. He is working to reveal Christ.

Throughout Scripture, God uses circumstances, trials, blessings, opportunities, and even disappointments to draw people to Himself. The provision is important, but the Provider is greater. The blessing matters, but the Redeemer matters more.

Ruth thought she needed grain. What she truly needed was a redeemer. Before the story is over, she will discover that God's answer to her immediate need was ultimately found in a person.

How often do we come to God asking for something when He is inviting us to discover Someone? We may be focused on the need in front of us, while God is working to reveal Christ more fully to our hearts. Bread (grain) matters, but the Bread of Life, Our Redeemer changes everything.

Selah

2. Boaz Notices Ruth (Ruth 2:4–7)

These verses introduce the first meeting between Boaz and Ruth. More importantly, they reveal the character of Boaz and begin to show why he is uniquely suited to redeem Elimelech's family line and inheritance (Naomi's family).

Ruth 2:4-7 Now behold, Boaz came from Bethlehem, and said to the reapers, The LORD be with you! And they answered him, The LORD bless you! 5 Then Boaz said to his servant who was in charge of the reapers, Whose young woman is this? 6 So the servant who was in charge of the reapers answered and said, It is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab. 7 And she said, 'Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves.' So she came and has continued from morning until now, though she rested a little in the house. (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

As Ruth faithfully gleans in the field, Boaz arrives from Bethlehem and is immediately introduced as a man of character. It is incredibly telling that his very first recorded words in Scripture are a blessing to his workers: "The LORD be with you!" and their immediate response is, "The LORD bless you!" In a dark period marked by severe spiritual decline and moral chaos during the days of the Judges, Boaz stands out. He is a man whose faith actively influences his daily life, his leadership, and his treatment of others, including those well-beneath his social status.

As Boaz surveys the harvest, he notices Ruth and asks the overseer, "Whose young woman is this?" The foreman identifies her as "the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab." It is worth noting that even after Ruth’s her beautiful commitment to Naomi and to the God of Israel, Ruth is still labeled by her past. In the eyes of the community, she remains a foreigner, and an outsider.

The overseer then explains that Ruth had respectfully asked permission to glean and had worked diligently from the early morning until that very moment, taking only a brief rest in the shelter. Her character is already speaking for itself. Ruth is not demanding, idle, or entitled. She is humble, hardworking, and entirely committed to caring for her mother-in-law.

What appears to be nothing more than an ordinary, daily conversation between a landowner and his foreman is actually the unfolding of God's providence. Boaz notices Ruth, completely unaware that this foreign woman will become central to his future and his family line. Ruth works faithfully, entirely oblivious to the fact that the owner of this field will become God's instrument of redemption. Behind this seemingly ordinary encounter, God is quietly and sovereignly directing every single detail according to His perfect purposes.

Dwell

1. Godly Character Is Revealed in Ordinary Life

The first thing we learn about Boaz is not that he is wealthy or influential, but that he is godly. His faith is evident in the way he speaks, leads, and treats those under his care. The Lord is not merely part of his worship; He is part of his work.

Genuine spirituality is revealed in everyday life. It is seen in our conversations, our relationships, our business practices, and the way we treat people when no one is watching.

2. Faithfulness Builds a Reputation

Ruth had not introduced herself to Boaz. She had simply gone to work. Yet the overseer already knew who she was and had noticed her diligence, humility, and work ethic.

A godly reputation is usually not built through self-promotion. It is built through consistent faithfulness over time. Long before Boaz ever spoke to Ruth, her actions were already speaking for her.

3. The Enemy of Grace Is Entitlement

Ruth does not come into the field demanding anything. She does not carry herself like someone who is owed something. She knows she needs mercy, so she asks, works, and receives with gratitude. That is humility.

Entitlement says, “I deserve this.” Humility says, “I am thankful for the grace I have been given.”

Ruth had real needs, but she did not use her need as an excuse for a demanding spirit. She worked hard, stayed low, and trusted God to open the right door.

Many people miss grace because they are too busy demanding rights and walking around like the world or the church owes them something. Ruth shows us a better way.

God gives grace to the humble, not to the entitled.

Selah.

4. God Sees Beyond the Labels

The foreman identifies her as "the Moabite woman." To many in Bethlehem, that would have been the most important thing about her.

But God saw something more. He saw a woman of faith who had left everything to follow Him.

People often see our failures, our past, our weaknesses, or the labels attached to us. God sees who we are becoming through His grace. The labels of men do not determine the purposes of God.

Jesus is the “name above every name”. His name vanquishes all the negative and derogatory labels we have ever had to carry.

4. God Often Begins His Greatest Works Quietly

Nothing dramatic happens in these verses. A landowner arrives at work. He notices a worker. He asks a question. A foreman gives an answer. Yet this ordinary conversation begins one of the most important relationships in biblical history.

We often expect God to move through dramatic moments, but many times His providence unfolds through ordinary conversations, ordinary days, and ordinary acts of obedience.

5. Faithfulness Positions Us for What God Is Doing

Ruth did not know Boaz would arrive that day. She did not know God was arranging circumstances on her behalf. Her responsibility was simply to be faithful in the field before her. Often we want to know God's entire plan, but He calls us to be faithful in today's assignment. As we walk in obedience, He directs our steps and unfolds His purposes in His time.

6. The Field Is Often the Place of Encounter

Ruth met Boaz while faithfully doing the work in front of her. She did not meet him sitting at home. She met him while gathering grain.

Throughout Scripture, God often meets His people in the place of faithful obedience. Moses was tending sheep. Gideon was threshing wheat. David was caring for his father's flock. Ruth was gleaning in a field.

Many believers spend their lives waiting for God to reveal the next chapter, while neglecting to steward the assignment already in front of them. The field of faithful stewardship and obedience is often the place where God reveals His purposes.

7. Rest, God Is Already at Work

Neither Ruth nor Boaz understood what God was doing that day. Ruth thought she was looking for grain. Boaz thought he was inspecting a harvest. Neither could see the larger story unfolding around them. Yet God was already at work.

Long before Ruth entered the field, God had prepared Boaz. Long before Naomi saw any sign of hope, God was arranging circumstances that would lead to provision, redemption, and ultimately the lineage of Christ.

One of the great comforts of God's providence and sovereignty is knowing that He is at work in our lives, even before we recognize it or understand it.

As we see with Ruth, the provision and future are already being prepared, even while we are still wondering how God will provide.

Ruth could not see what God was doing, but God could. And the same is true in our lives. While we are worrying, striving, and trying to figure everything out, God is often already working behind the scenes. The God of Ruth is still writing stories that His people cannot yet see.

This is why we can rest. We are not responsible for controlling every outcome. We are not required to see the entire plan. Our responsibility is faithfulness. God's responsibility is providence. Rest, truly everything is in His hands.

Selah.

3. Boaz Extends Grace and Protection (Ruth 2:8-16)

Ruth came to the field hoping to glean enough grain to survive. Instead she finds grace as Boaz gives her protection, dignity, provision, and favor.

Ruth 2:8-9 Then Boaz said to Ruth, You will listen, my daughter, will you not? Do not go to glean in another field, nor go from here, but stay close by my young women. 9 Let your eyes be on the field which they reap, and go after them. Have I not commanded the young men not to touch you? And when you are thirsty, go to the vessels and drink from what the young men have drawn. (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

Boaz could have treated Ruth as an outsider. She was a Moabite widow gleaning in his field, a stranger with no standing in the community. Instead, he speaks directly to her and calls her, "my daughter." He does not treat her as an enemy, a burden, or an inconvenience. He treats her with kindness, dignity, and respect.

Boaz then gives Ruth several instructions and promises.

  • First, he tells her not to glean in another field but to remain close to his young women. This is more than practical advice. Gleaning could be dangerous for a poor foreign widow. Ruth was vulnerable to harassment, rejection, and exploitation. By inviting her to stay in his field, Boaz is no longer treating Ruth as an outsider passing through. He is bringing her under his care and protection alongside the rest of his workers.

  • Second, he tells her to keep her eyes on the field where his workers are harvesting and to follow after his young women. There is an implied lesson here. Ruth is new to the work and unfamiliar with the field. By staying close to those who know the work, she can learn and grow. Boaz is teaching her to remain near those who can help her.

  • Third, he tells her that he has already commanded the young men not to touch her. Boaz uses his authority to ensure Ruth is protected. He does not simply feel compassion for Ruth; he acts on her behalf. Ruth is now under his protection.

  • Finally, Boaz gives her access to the water drawn by his servants. A poor foreign gleaner would not normally expect such treatment. Yet Boaz tells her that whenever she is thirsty, she may freely drink from what has been provided.

The Mosaic Law allowed Ruth to glean. Boaz goes far beyond the minimum requirements of the Law. The Law provided opportunity; Boaz adds grace, protection, acceptance, and care. Ruth came looking for grain, but Boaz begins giving her something much greater, a place of refuge (Ruth 2:12).

Dwell

1. No judgment zone

Boaz could have easily dismissed Ruth. She was a Moabite, a widow, poor, and unknown in Bethlehem. He could have looked at her through the lens of her background, her nationality, her poverty, or her need. But Boaz does not judge Ruth or look at here through “her label”.

He does not treat her as a problem in his field. He does not shame her for needing help. He does not assume the worst about her. Instead, he speaks to her with dignity, gives her protection, and invites her to remain.

This is a needed lesson for us. It is easy to walk past a homeless person, a smelly person, a poor person, an awkward person, or someone whose life looks broken, and quietly dismiss them in our hearts. We may not say it out loud, but we can judge them in our heart, avoid them, or treat them as an interruption.

Boaz shows us a better way. Godly people look deeper. They see people through the lens of mercy. They see people through God’s eyes.

Be careful not to dismiss or judge people simply because of their looks or labels. This may be someone God has placed in your field for a reason and ultimately even your benefit.

Selah.

2. Looking out for the interests of others

Boaz notices Ruth and saw what she truly needed. That may not sound remarkable until we remember how easy it is to overlook people. Most people saw a Moabite widow working hard to try and scrape some food together. Boaz saw a person who had some needs.

Philippians 2:4 Let each of you look out not only for his own interests, but also for the interests of others. (NKJV)

3. Stay Teachable

Boaz tells Ruth to keep her eyes on the young women and follow them. Ruth could have insisted on keeping to herself and doing things her own way. Instead, she remained teachable.

One of the great dangers in the Christian life is becoming unteachable. Pride believes it already knows. Humility remains willing to learn.

God often places people in our lives who can help us grow, but we must be willing to listen, observe, and learn.

4. He Will Not Cast You Out

Boaz welcomed Ruth when she had every reason to expect rejection. This points us to our heavenly Boaz, Jessu Christ.

John 6:37 All that the Father gives Me will come to Me, and the one who comes to Me I will by no means cast out. (NKJV)

Many people approach God expecting condemnation, rejection, or exclusion. Instead, they discover a Savior who welcomes sinners, restores the broken, and receives those who come to Him in faith.

Boaz's field becomes a picture of the heart of Christ.

Selah

5. More than we could ask of think.

Ruth came looking for bread but God gave her so much more. Ruth entered the field looking for grain but by the end of the day, she finds refuge, protection, acceptance, provision, hope, and the beginning of a relationship that will ultimately change her life.

Isn’t this the way it often is with the Lord? We come with a need. We come seeking help with a problem. We come asking for provision, healing, direction, or wisdom. God cares about those needs, but He frequently gives us far more than we asked for.

Ephesians 3:20 Now to Him who is able to do exceedingly abundantly above all that we ask or think, according to the power that works in us, (NKJV)

6. Every person is worthy of honor

Boaz treats Ruth with dignity despite her past, and despite her heritage. We live in a culture that honors only those we deem worthy.

Every person we meet bears the image of God. Their social standing, ethnicity, background, education, wealth, or failures do not diminish their value before Him.

This is why the Biblical value of honor is a value at East Gate.

HONOR | Through His Eyes. Relationships are protected when we ascribe heaven’s value to the people around us. Like Jesus, we choose to interact with others, not as they deserve, but according to their God given value.

One mark of spiritual maturity is learning to treat every person with dignity, honor, and respect.

7. Stay Where God Is Providing

One of the repeated instructions in this chapter is for Ruth to remain in Boaz's field.

The safest place for Ruth is not the field she chooses, but the field where God's provision has placed her.

Many believers are constantly searching for something different, something newer, or something better. Yet often God's blessing is found in being content where He has already placed us.

7. Our Generous Redeemer

The Mosaic Law gave Ruth permission to glean. Boaz gave her protection. The Law gave her access to leftover grain. Boaz gave her access to water, safety, acceptance, and belonging.

Boaz is like our Heavenly Father – generous. He is generous. He delights in giving His children more than they deserve and more than they could have imagined.

Selah

Ruth 2:10 So she fell on her face, bowed down to the ground, and said to him, Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner? (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

Ruth falls on her face and bows to the ground. Her response is one of humility, reverence, and astonishment. She knows she does not deserve Boaz’s kindness.

Her question “Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?”, reveals that Ruth knows her position. She knows who she is. She is quite aware she is an idolatrous worshipping, Moabite, foreigner. She knows she is a widow, poor, and without status in Bethlehem. She came hoping for favor, but when favor is given, she is overwhelmed by it.

Ruth understands that what she is receiving is not owed to her. Ruth experiences grace. Ruth feels grace.

Dwell

The Wonder of Grace

Ruth falls on her face because she understands that Boaz’s kindness is not something she earned, deserved, or could demand. She came as a foreigner looking for favor, and when favor found her, it overwhelmed her. That is what grace does.

When we forget what we have been rescued from, we begin to treat mercy like something God owes us. We lose the wonder. We lose the gratitude. We lose the humility.

Ruth’s question should live in every believer’s heart: “Why have I found favor?” Why would the Lord notice me? Why would He receive me? Why would He forgive me, cover me, provide for me, and bring me near? The answer is not our worthiness. The answer is His grace.

Grace is most deeply felt by those who know they have no claim to it. Never lose the wonder that God took notice of you.

Selah.

Ruth 2:11-12 And Boaz answered and said to her, It has been fully reported to me, all that you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband, and how you have left your father and your mother and the land of your birth, and have come to a people whom you did not know before. 12 The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge (H2620). (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

Boaz explains that he has heard about Ruth's loyalty to Naomi. Her kindness, sacrifice, and faith have become known throughout Bethlehem. He has heard how she cared for Naomi after the death of her husband and how she left her father, mother, homeland, and familiar life to accompany Naomi back to Israel.

This is important because Ruth's actions reveal that her commitment was genuine. When Ruth left Moab, she was not pursuing comfort, security, or opportunity. She was leaving all of those things behind. She chose to follow Naomi and the God of Israel while she was still a poor, widowed, and empty-handed foreigner. Her faith was not motivated by what she hoped to gain. It was demonstrated by what she was willing to leave behind.

Boaz then pronounces a blessing over her:

"The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge."

Boaz recognizes that Ruth has done more than travel from Moab to Bethlehem. She has come to take refuge under the wings of the God of Israel. The Hebrew word refuge means “to flee for safety, seek shelter, or trust securely in a protective hiding place. In Ruth 2:12, it describes the deliberate act of seeking spiritual and physical shelter beneath the protective care of the God of Israel, illustrating a radical transfer of allegiance and personal security

The image of the word refuge is one of protection, shelter, security, and care. Just as a mother bird gathers her young under her wings, Ruth has placed herself under the protection of the Lord. Her physical arrival in Bethlehem was the outward evidence of an inward decision that had already taken place in her heart to leave the gods of Moab behind for YHWH of Israel..

1. Ruth Chose God and Trusted Him with Her Future

Ruth was not raised in Israel. She grew up in Moab surrounded by the worship of false gods. Yet she turned her back on her homeland, her former way of life, and the gods of Moab to follow Naomi and the God of Israel.

What makes her decision remarkable is that she did it without any promise of provision, marriage, restoration, or security. She had only heard Naomi speak of the God of Israel. She knew where refuge could be found, but she did not know how her story would end.

That is often how faith works. God rarely reveals the entire journey before asking us to take the first step. Faith moves forward because it trusts the character of God even when it cannot see the outcome. Ruth committed herself to God before she knew what God would do for her.

Additionally, conversion always involves leaving something behind in order to follow God. Ruth left the familiar to embrace the unknown. She left false gods to seek refuge in the true God. Many of God's greatest blessings lie on the other side of obedience.

Notice that Ruth sought refuge in God before she found provision. She sought God before she found Boaz. She sought shelter under His wings before she knew how her practical needs would be met.

The same principle remains true today. Real security is not found in money, relationships, careers, or circumstances. It is found my making God our eternal refuge.

How often do we pray for God to help someone while overlooking the possibility that God wants to use us to be the answer to our own prayer?

God frequently answers prayers through willing people. He comforts through people. He provides through people. He encourages through people. He protects through people.

Never underestimate what God can do through a person who is willing to become the answer to the prayer they are praying.

This points us to an even greater truth. God knows far more about us that we know about Him. He sees the sacrifices nobody else sees. He sees the tears, the acts of obedience, the quiet faithfulness, and the costly decisions made in secret.

At times we may feel forgotten, overlooked, or unnoticed. Ruth could have felt that way. Yet before she ever met Boaz, her faithfulness had already been seen.

Comforted by Grace

Look who is invited to dinner

Ruth 2:14 Now Boaz said to her at mealtime, Come here, and eat of the bread, and dip your piece of bread in the vinegar. So she sat beside the reapers, and he passed parched grain to her; and she ate and was satisfied, and kept some back. (NKJV)

UNDERSTAND

Boaz now invites Ruth to mealtime. This meal likely took place around noon, as harvesters commonly began work early in the morning and paused in the middle of the day for food and rest. Ruth had been gleaning in the field "from morning till now" (Ruth 2:7), making this a welcome break after a number of hours of labor.

Boaz invites Ruth to come and share bread dipped in wine vinegar, and she sits among the harvesters to eat parched grain. In that day, dipping bread in vinegar or sour wine was a common way to add flavor and moisture to simple bread which was often dry or coarse, especially during a long workday. Parched grain was roasted grain commonly eaten during harvest because it was simple, filling, and easy to prepare.

This invitation is significant. Ruth moves from gleaning at the edges of the field to sitting among the reapers. She is no longer gathering leftovers at a distance. She is invited near.

Boaz personally serves her roasted grain, and she eats until she is satisfied and has some left over. The abundance is important. Ruth came to the field hoping to gather enough grain to survive, but now she is being fed directly from the hand of the landowner.

The text also notes that Ruth kept some back. This appears to show that even while enjoying Boaz's generosity, she is still thinking about Naomi and saving a portion to take home to her.

This meal is not yet redemption, but it is a foretaste of the kindness and provision that will continue to unfold throughout the story. The outsider is welcomed. The hungry are satisfied. The outcast is given a place at the table.

Explain

"Now behold, Boaz came from Bethlehem, and said to the reapers, 'The LORD be with you!' And they answered him, 'The LORD bless you!'"

Explanation

The chapter has emphasized God's providence in guiding Ruth to Boaz's field. Now Boaz himself enters the scene.

The first thing we learn about him is not his wealth, influence, or position, but his relationship with God. As he arrives from Bethlehem, he greets his workers with a blessing: "The LORD be with you." His workers respond in kind: "The LORD bless you."

This brief exchange reveals a great deal about Boaz's character. In a period marked by the spiritual chaos of the Judges, Boaz stands out as a godly man whose faith affects his daily life. He does not separate worship from work. The Lord is present in his business dealings, his leadership, and his relationships.

The response of the workers is equally significant. Their respect and willingness to return his blessing suggest that Boaz is not merely a wealthy landowner but a respected and honorable leader.

Key Themes

  • Godliness in ordinary life.

  • Leadership marked by blessing rather than exploitation.

  • Faith that permeates daily work and relationships.

Dwell

Boaz's first recorded words are words of blessing. The people closest to us often reveal the true condition of our character. Boaz's workers appear to respect him, suggesting that his public faith matches his private conduct.

Ruth 2:5

"Then Boaz said to his servant who was in charge of the reapers, 'Whose young woman is this?'"

Explanation

As Boaz surveys the harvest, one worker immediately captures his attention.

The question is not merely about identity but belonging. In that culture, a vulnerable woman would normally be connected to a family, husband, or household. Ruth appears different. Her presence stands out.

Some have suggested Boaz notices her because of her appearance, but the text gives a stronger indication. By verse 7 the overseer will report Ruth's extraordinary diligence and respectful conduct. Something about her behavior has distinguished her among the gleaners.

The reader knows more than Boaz at this point. We know Ruth has been brought to this field by God's providence. What appears to be a simple question is the beginning of a divinely orchestrated relationship.

Key Themes

  • God often works through ordinary encounters.

  • Character frequently attracts attention before words do.

  • Providence unfolds through seemingly small moments.

Dwell

Ruth was not trying to be noticed. She was simply being faithful. God often opens doors not through self-promotion but through consistent faithfulness.

Ruth 2:6

"So the servant who was in charge of the reapers answered and said, 'It is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab.'"

Explanation

The foreman immediately knows who Ruth is.

Notice that she is identified first as "the young Moabite woman." The label continues to follow her throughout the book. Ruth has left Moab, embraced Israel's God, and committed herself to Naomi, yet she is still viewed as an outsider.

At the same time, she is also identified by her connection to Naomi. The people of Bethlehem are beginning to associate Ruth with Naomi's return.

The statement highlights Ruth's vulnerability. She is young, foreign, widowed, poor, and without local family support. Humanly speaking, she possesses little social standing.

Yet these circumstances only magnify the grace that will soon be shown to her.

Key Themes

  • Ruth remains an outsider in the eyes of society.

  • God often works through those the world overlooks.

  • Our past may describe us, but it does not determine God's future for us.

Dwell

Many believers can identify with Ruth. We may carry labels from our past, our failures, our losses, or our circumstances. Yet God is not limited by the labels others place upon us.

Ruth 2:7

"And she said, 'Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves.' So she came and has continued from morning until now, though she rested a little in the house."

Explanation

The overseer explains why Ruth has attracted attention.

First, she asked permission. Although the Law allowed gleaning, Ruth approaches with humility and respect. She does not presume upon her rights or demand special treatment.

Second, she has worked diligently. The foreman reports that she has been laboring from morning until the present time with only a brief rest. Her reputation is already being formed by her work ethic.

Third, her conduct demonstrates character. She is neither lazy nor entitled. She is willing to perform difficult labor in order to care for Naomi and herself.

The verse also reveals Ruth's commitment to sacrificial love. She is not gleaning merely for her own benefit. Every stalk she gathers represents her devotion to Naomi and her determination to provide for their household.

Key Themes

  • Humility and diligence.

  • Faith expressed through faithful labor.

  • Godly character revealed in ordinary responsibilities.

Dwell

Ruth's faith is not merely found in her confession of loyalty in Chapter 1. It is now visible in her actions.

Many people desire God's provision, but Ruth was willing to enter the field and do the hard work required. Her diligence did not earn God's favor, but it positioned her to experience the opportunities God was providing.

Throughout Scripture, faithfulness in ordinary responsibilities often becomes the stage upon which God's greater purposes unfold.

Summary of 2:4–7

These verses present a striking contrast. Ruth is a poor foreign widow gleaning leftovers in the field. Boaz is a wealthy and respected landowner. Yet both are marked by godly character.

Ruth displays humility, diligence, and faithfulness.

Boaz displays godliness, kindness, and spiritual leadership.

Before redemption ever occurs, the author wants us to see the character of both the redeemed and the redeemer. God is already weaving their lives together through His unseen providence.

Ruth woke up looking for grain. God was leading her toward a redeemer. What looks like an ordinary step of obedience today may be the very place where God's hidden providence is waiting tomorrow.

Ruth could have allowed grief, uncertainty, or fear to immobilize her. Instead, she took the next faithful step available to her. She did not know where the path would lead. She did not know she would meet Boaz. She did not know she was walking into God's larger plan of redemption.

Often, God does not reveal the entire journey. He simply asks us to take the next step of obedience. The field Ruth entered that morning looked like an ordinary field, but it was the place where God's providence was waiting.

Many of God's greatest works begin when His people simply do the next faithful thing in front of them.
