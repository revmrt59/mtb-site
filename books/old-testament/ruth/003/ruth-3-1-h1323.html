DAUGHTER - ( bat , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bat primarily refers to a biological female child, but it extends far beyond genealogy to express deep relational warmth, covenantal belonging, and protective care. In the book of Ruth, particularly in Naomi’s address to Ruth in Ruth 3:1, bat is used not of a biological descendant, but as an affectionate term of endearment. It signifies the complete adoption of a foreign daughter-in-law into a mother’s heart and a covenant family. This simple word transforms Ruth's status from an outsider and a widow into a cherished child worthy of protection, provision, and a future.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun bat is the feminine counterpart to ben (son). While primarily used to designate a biological female child, its linguistic roots connect to the idea of building. A daughter is one who builds up a household and continues the family line. In the ancient Near East, a daughter's identity was closely tied to her father's household until marriage, when she became part of her husband's household. For a widow like Ruth, who was also a foreigner from Moab, losing both husband and father-in-law meant losing her legal cover, social standing, and identity. When Naomi addresses Ruth as bat, she is not merely using a polite idiom; she is actively rebuilding a broken relational structure, wrapping this young Moabite widow in the safety, warmth, and legal rights of a Hebrew home.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term bat has a broad semantic range in the Old Testament:

• Biological Daughter: A female immediate offspring (Genesis 5:4).

• Female Descendant: A granddaughter or female member of a specific lineage or nation, such as "daughters of Canaan" or "daughters of Israel" (Genesis 28:1).

• Affectionate Address: A term used by an elder to address a younger woman, expressing parental care, protection, and mentoring (Ruth 2:8; Ruth 3:1).

• Personification of a City or Nation: A poetic collective term, such as "daughter of Zion" or "daughter of Babylon," representing the inhabitants of a region as a single young woman (Zechariah 9:9).

• Subordinate Villages: The smaller towns surrounding a main city, often referred to literally as the "daughters" of that mother city (Numbers 21:25).

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

In Hebrew culture, the word bat carries a picture of cherished value and vulnerability requiring protection.

• The Apple of the Eye: In Psalm 17:8, the phrase "apple of my eye" translates literally as "the daughter of the eye" (bat ayin), representing the pupil—the most sensitive, vital, and protected part of the eye.

• The Builder of the House: Because the root relates to building, daughters were seen as those who build up the nation. Rachel and Leah are described as the two who "built the house of Israel" (Ruth 4:11).

• Covenant Relationship: God frequently uses the term "daughter of Zion" to describe His people. This portrays Israel not as an abstract political entity, but as a young woman whom He loves, disciplines, protects, and betrothes to Himself.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of bat in the Old Testament, particularly in the narrative of Ruth, highlights the expansive nature of God’s covenant mercy (chesed).

• Redemption of the Outsider: By law, Moabites were excluded from the assembly of Israel (Deuteronomy 23:3). However, when Naomi calls Ruth "my daughter," and later when Boaz uses the same term, the barrier of ethnicity is dismantled by covenant love. Ruth is brought from the margins into the center of Israel's redemptive history.

• The Heart of the Father: The pastoral warmth conveyed in this term mirrors God's own paternal heart. He is the Father of the fatherless and the protector of widows (Psalm 68:5). When godly leaders and mentors like Naomi and Boaz use bat, they act as the hands and feet of Yahweh, providing the "wings" of refuge under which Ruth has come to trust.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:1, Naomi says, "My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?"

• The use of bat (specifically the pronominal form bitti, "my daughter") here marks a critical transition. Naomi's focus shifts from her own grief and emptiness (Ruth 1:20-21) to an active, loving concern for Ruth’s future.

• By calling Ruth "my daughter," Naomi claims her as her own flesh and blood. She assumes the maternal responsibility of arranging a marriage that will secure Ruth’s rest and protection (manoach, meaning "resting place" or "security").

• This term of endearment establishes that the plan Naomi is about to propose (sending Ruth to Boaz at the threshing floor) is not an act of exploitation, but an act of parental love designed to secure the young woman’s long-term welfare.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 24:24 – "So she said to him, 'I am the daughter of Bethuel, Milcah’s son, whom she bore to Nahor.'" (Biological lineage and identity).

• Ruth 2:2 – "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'" (First use of the affectionate term by Naomi, validating Ruth's work).

• Ruth 3:10 – "Then he said, 'Blessed are you of the LORD, my daughter! For you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning, in that you did not go after young men, whether poor or rich.'" (Boaz uses the term to express honor, respect, and protective reassurance).

• Psalm 45:10 – "Listen, O daughter, Consider and incline your ear; Forget your own people also, and your father’s house." (A royal bridesmaid or bride addressed with affectionate instruction).

• Zechariah 9:9 – "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! Shout, O daughter of Jerusalem! Behold, your King is coming to you; He is just and having salvation, Lowly and riding on a donkey, A colt, the foal of a donkey." (Prophetic personification of God's covenant people).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the cultural framework of the Old Testament, calling a daughter-in-law "daughter" was not a universal requirement. Naomi could have legally and socially referred to Ruth as "my daughter-in-law" (kallah), which would maintain a formal, legal boundary. By choosing bat, Naomi strips away the legal distance. This choice of words serves as a powerful homiletical illustration for the Church: we are called to move beyond formal, transactional relationships with those who are hurting or new to the faith, adopting them into the deep, familial intimacy of the household of God.
