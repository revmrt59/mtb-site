WELL - ( yatab , Strong's H3190 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yatab carries the foundational meaning of being good, pleasing, glad, or prosperous. In Naomi's tender address to Ruth in Ruth 3:1, the word is translated "well" and expresses a deep pastoral and maternal desire for Ruth's ultimate welfare, security, and restoration. It transcends mere physical comfort, pointing to a state of wholeness, peace, and divine favor within the covenant community of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root yatab is a highly expressive Semitic term that captures the essence of goodness in action. In the ancient Hebrew mindset, goodness was not merely an abstract concept or an internal feeling; it was a visible, tangible reality that produced life, harmony, and joy. The word stands in stark contrast to ra'a' (to be evil, broken, or dysfunctional). The word picture behind yatab is one of a path made straight, a heart made glad, or a situation brought into its proper, beautiful alignment. When something is yatab, it functions exactly as God designed it to, resulting in flourishing and peace.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yatab occurs over 370 times in the Old Testament and has a rich semantic range:

• In the Qal stem, it means to be good, to go well, to be pleasing, or to be glad.

• In the Hiphil (causative) stem, it means to do good, to treat well, to deal bountifully, or to do something thoroughly and right.

The primary sense in passages concerning a person's life state is "to experience well-being, prosperity, and peace." It refers to a condition of life that is secure, stable, and marked by the favor of God and community.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs yatab in several distinct ways to paint a picture of wholeness:

• A Glad Heart: It describes a heart that is merry or cheerful. When a person is content and enjoying life's blessings, their heart is said to be "well" (yatab) within them.

• Divine Favor: It is used to describe God's active goodness poured out on His people. When God promises to "do good" to His covenant partners, yatab is the word of promise.

• Pleasing to the Eyes: It represents actions, attitudes, or proposals that are right, fitting, and beautiful to those who observe them.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yatab is deeply tied to the covenant relationship between Yahweh and Israel. Throughout the Torah, especially in Deuteronomy, the phrase "that it may go well with you" is a recurring refrain. This covenant wellness is the natural byproduct of living in obedience to and harmony with God. It represents the reversal of the curse. Where sin brings brokenness and grief, God's redeeming grace restores yatab—a state where life is sweet, secure, and aligned with divine purposes. Naomi’s search for Ruth’s wellness is a beautiful microcosm of God's larger redemptive design to bring His people out of the "famine" of exile and into the "wellness" of His presence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:1, Naomi says, "My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?"

The historical context is critical. Ruth and Naomi had returned from Moab entirely destitute, grieving, and empty. Ruth had spent months gleaning in the fields of Boaz as a vulnerable outsider. Naomi realizes that temporary provision is not enough; Ruth needs permanent rest and protection.

The word "security" (Hebrew: manoach) refers to a resting place or a home. Naomi links this permanent resting place directly with it being "well" (yatab) with Ruth. Naomi is seeking a complete reversal of Ruth's widowhood and poverty. By finding a kinsman-redeemer, Ruth would find legal protection, a family name, inheritance, and a secure future. Thus, yatab here denotes the spiritual, emotional, social, and physical restoration that comes when a destitute believer finds refuge under the wings of the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:16: "He treated Abram well for her sake..."

• Genesis 32:12: "For You said, 'I will surely treat you well, and make your descendants as the sand of the sea...'"

• Deuteronomy 4:40: "You shall therefore keep His statutes and His commandments... that it may go well with you and with your children after you..."

• Deuteronomy 5:16: "Honor your father and your mother... that your days may be long, and that it may be well with you..."

• Ruth 3:7: "And after Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was cheerful [well/merry]..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound literary connection in Ruth chapter 3 involving this root word. In verse 1, Naomi plans for things to go "well" (yatab) for Ruth. In verse 7, Boaz feasts and his heart is "cheerful" (yatab). When Ruth approaches Boaz at the threshing floor, she acts as the agent of God's providence to bring Naomi's plans to pass. Ultimately, Boaz's cheerful heart and Naomi's desire for Ruth's wellness intersect in a marriage covenant. What began as a desperate hope for things to be "well" ends with the birth of Obed, securing the lineage of King David and the Messiah.
