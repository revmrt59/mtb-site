SECURITY - ( manoach , Strong's H4494 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word manoach refers to a state or place of quiet rest, safety, and security. Derived from the root nuach (to rest), it transcends a mere temporary pause from labor, pointing instead to a permanent home, peace, and relational wholeness. In the context of ancient Israelite culture and specifically the book of Ruth, "security" (manoach) describes the protective, stable environment of a covenant marriage, where a vulnerable woman found shelter, economic provision, and social standing. It represents the transition from the vulnerability of wandering and widowhood to the peace of a settled home under the providence of God.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word manoach is deeply rooted in the pastoral and agricultural lifestyle of ancient Israel. It is built on the verbal root nuach, which means to settle down, rest, or dwell securely. A manoach is not just a brief rest stop along a journey, but a final destination of permanent rest. It evokes the image of a flock finding a safe pasture free from predators, or a weary traveler finally reaching a permanent, welcoming home.

In the ancient Near East, a widow or an unmarried foreign woman was highly vulnerable to exploitation, poverty, and hunger. She had no legal protector or economic anchor. Therefore, the search for manoach was not an emotional quest for self-actualization, but a critical search for a covenant boundary—a home—where she could be protected, nourished, and established in the community.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Manoach functions as a masculine noun with a clear semantic range:

• A resting place or a state of quietness and security.

• A permanent home or habitation characterized by peace.

• The settled, protective condition of marriage (specifically used in the context of a woman finding security under her husband’s care).

• Freedom from anxiety, displacement, and hostility.

It is closely related to the feminine noun menuchah (Strong's H4496), which similarly denotes a resting place, quietness, or the inherited land of peace promised by God.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses manoach to draw vivid word pictures of transition from instability to settled peace:

• The Searching Dove: In the Genesis flood narrative, the dove sent from the ark found no manoach (resting place) for the sole of her foot because the waters still covered the earth. She returned to the ark, which was her only source of security.

• The Homeless Exile: In Deuteronomy, Israel is warned that if they violate God's covenant, they will be scattered among the nations and find no manoach (rest or resting place) for their feet.

• The Quieted Soul: The psalmist exhorts his own soul to return to its manoach (rest) because Yahweh has dealt bountifully with him, portraying God Himself as the ultimate home and security for the human heart.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, manoach highlights God’s desire to bring His people out of chaos, wandering, and vulnerability into a state of secure rest.

• Provision through Covenant: While Naomi seeks a human agent (Boaz) to provide manoach for Ruth, this security is ultimately a gift from God. Human institutions like marriage and the family are designed by God to be earthly expressions of His divine protection and care.

• Redemption and Rest: The security of Ruth is achieved through the actions of a goel (kinsman-redeemer). This illustrates the grand biblical theme of redemption: true spiritual rest and security are found only under the protective wings of Yahweh, secured for believers through our ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ. Just as Ruth found security by placing herself at the feet of Boaz, the believer finds eternal manoach by surrendering to Christ.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:1, Naomi says to Ruth, "My daughter, shall I not seek security (manoach) for you, that it may be well with you?"

In the preceding chapters, Ruth has been working tirelessly as a gleaner in the fields of Bethlehem. While God has protected her, this labor is exhausting and temporary. Naomi recognizes that Ruth needs permanent stability.

The nuance of manoach in this passage is intensely practical, relational, and legal. Naomi is proposing a strategy for Ruth to appeal to Boaz as their kinsman-redeemer. Thus, "security" in this context refers to the legal and social protection of marriage. Naomi wants Ruth to transition from a foreign widow gleaning on the margins of society to a beloved wife established at the center of a household. It is a search for a place where Ruth's soul and body can finally rest.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 8:9 — "But the dove found no resting place for the sole of her foot, and she returned to him into the ark, for the waters were on the face of the whole earth."

• Deuteronomy 28:65 — "And among those nations you shall find no rest, nor shall the sole of your foot have a resting place; but there the LORD will give you a trembling heart, failing eyes, and anguish of soul."

• Ruth 3:1 — "Then Naomi her mother-in-law said to her, 'My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?'"

• Psalm 116:7 — "Return to your rest, O my soul, For the LORD has dealt bountifully with you."

• Lamentations 1:3 — "Judah has gone into captivity, Under affliction and hard servitude; She dwells among the nations, She finds no rest; All her persecutors overtake her in dire straits."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• From Prayer to Action: In Ruth 1:9, Naomi prayed that her daughters-in-law would find "rest" (menuchah) in the house of a husband. By Ruth 3:1, Naomi actively moves to seek "security" (manoach) for Ruth through Boaz. There is a beautiful progression here from a prayerful wish to strategic, faithful action.

• The Name Manoah: The father of Samson is named Manoah (Strong's H4495), which is the exact same spelling and phonetic word as manoach, meaning "rest" or "resting place."

• The Design of Marriage: This word reinforces the biblical definition of marriage. In God’s design, the home is intended to be a sanctuary of safety, emotional healing, and mutual protection, mirroring the ultimate security believers have in their relationship with God.
