NAOMI - ( No'omiy , Strong's H5281 )

Summary:

The Hebrew name Naomi (No'omiy) means "my pleasantness" or "delightful." Derived from the root na'em, which speaks of beauty, sweetness, and favor, the name carries deep theological weight in the narrative of Ruth. Having fled Bethlehem due to famine and subsequently losing her husband and two sons in Moab, she famously demands to be called Mara ("bitter") because she feels the Lord has dealt bitterly with her. However, God progressively restores her to the reality of her original name. In Ruth 3:1, "pleasantness" is actively at work as Naomi shifts her focus from her own grief to securing a future of rest and safety for her faithful daughter-in-law, Ruth.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The geographical setting of Bethlehem, the "House of Bread," contrasts sharply with the famine that drives Naomi's family out of Israel. This journey represents a departure from the "pleasantness" of the Promised Land.

• Names in Hebrew thought were not merely labels but descriptions of character, destiny, or spiritual condition. Naomi's name reflects the ideal state of covenant blessing under God's favor.

• Her subsequent self-renaming to Mara in Ruth 1:20 demonstrates how grief can obscure a believer's true identity and cause them to define themselves by their immediate pain rather than God's enduring covenant promises.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The name Naomi (No'omiy) is derived from the Hebrew verb na'em, meaning "to be pleasant, lovely, delightful, or sweet."

• The noun form no'am refers to beauty, favor, kindness, or pleasantness.

• The final letter yod functions as a possessive suffix, yielding the translation "my pleasantness," or as an adjectival suffix meaning "delightful."

• In the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint), the name is transliterated as Noemin.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The name occurs 21 times in the Hebrew Bible, restricted entirely to the narrative of Ruth.

• The literary arc of the book is structured around the loss and restoration of Naomi's name. She leaves Bethlehem "full" (experiencing pleasantness), returns "empty" and calls herself "bitter" (Mara), and is ultimately filled again when she holds her grandson Obed.

• The contrast between Naomi ("pleasant") and Mara ("bitter") serves as a powerful word picture of the spiritual journey of Israel. It illustrates how God's people may experience seasons of spiritual desertion and bitterness due to disobedience or trial, but God's ultimate purpose is always restoration to covenant sweetness.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Faithfulness (Hesed): The restoration of Naomi's pleasantness shows that God is committed to the long-term restoration of His people. Even when Naomi believes God has turned against her, His hidden providence is working to bless her.

• The Nature of Affliction: Naomi's temporary name change to Mara shows that believers may experience genuine, crushing grief. However, God does not allow bitterness to be the final word in the believer's story.

• Redemption and Rest: The story of Naomi highlights how redemption restores what was lost. The "pleasantness" of her life is not recovered by her own strength, but through the mediation of a redeemer (goel), pointing forward to the ultimate redemption found in Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:1, Naomi begins to take initiative on behalf of Ruth: "Then Naomi her mother-in-law said to her, 'My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?'"

• This passage represents a turning point where Naomi steps out of her passive mourning. The "pleasantness" of her character resurfaces as she seeks the welfare of another rather than dwelling on her own losses.

• The Hebrew word translated "security" (manoach) refers to a place of rest, security, and home. Naomi, who had lost her own home and rest, now acts as an instrument of divine grace to provide rest for Ruth, foreshadowing the ultimate rest found in the lineage of David.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:2: "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there."

• Ruth 1:20: "But she said to them, 'Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.'"

• Ruth 2:2: "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

• Ruth 4:17: "Also the neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'There is a son born to Naomi.' And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The book begins with Naomi’s family line seemingly cut off, but ends with a genealogy. This highlights how God uses ordinary, faithful actions—like Naomi's counsel to Ruth—to accomplish His massive, redemptive plans for history, culminating in the birth of the Messiah.

• The neighbor women who declare "There is a son born to Naomi" in Ruth 4:17 validate that her emptiness has been completely reversed. The community that once witnessed her return in bitter grief now celebrates the complete restoration of her "pleasantness."
