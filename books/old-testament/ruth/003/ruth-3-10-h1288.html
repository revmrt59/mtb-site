BLESSED - ( barak , Strong's H1288 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb barak fundamentally communicates the transmission of divine favor, life-giving power, and goodness from one person to another, or from God to humanity. In Ruth 3:10, Boaz uses this word to invoke Yahweh’s favor upon Ruth, recognizing her extraordinary loyalty (chesed) and self-giving character. This blessing is not a mere polite greeting but an active, authoritative declaration of God’s favor and reward, marking a pivotal turning point where Ruth's faithfulness is met with the promise of redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• barak is a foundational Old Testament verb, occurring over 300 times. The physical root of the word is closely related to the noun berek, which means "knee." From this association, the term carries the spatial image of bending the knee, kneeling in respect, or kneeling to receive a blessing. In the ancient Near East, to bless someone was to bestow power for prosperity, fertility, and success. When God blesses humanity, He bends down in grace to bestow His goodness. Conversely, when humanity blesses God (often translated as "praise" or "extol"), it represents kneeling before Him in adoration and acknowledging His supreme authority.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb barak refers to the invocation or conferral of beneficial power, prosperity, and divine favor. Lexically, it operates in three primary directions:

• God blessing humanity: God imparting life, productivity, protection, and covenantal success.

• Humanity blessing God: Praising, kneeling before, and thanking God for His character and deeds.

• Humanity blessing humanity: Invoking God’s name to bestow favor, protection, and success upon another person.

In the passive form (as in Ruth 3:10, baruk), it indicates the state of being the recipient of this divine favor, marked by God's preservation and grace.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The biblical concept of blessing is concrete and active, never abstract. It is closely linked with the concepts of life, peace (shalom), and fertility.

• The First Blessing: The first occurrence of barak is in Genesis 1:22, where God blesses the sea creatures and birds, commanding them to be fruitful and multiply. This links blessing directly with life-giving capacity and abundance.

• Patriarchal Blessing: The blessing of a patriarch (such as Isaac blessing Jacob in Genesis 27) was a legally binding, prophetic spoken word that shaped the destiny of the recipient, carrying the physical and spiritual weight of God's sovereign plan.

• The Aaronic Blessing: In Numbers 6:24-26, blessing is defined as God keeping His people, making His face shine upon them, being gracious to them, and giving them peace.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, barak shows that blessing is not an accidental stroke of luck, but a deliberate act of divine grace. True blessing originates only from Yahweh.

• Covenantal Alignment: Blessing is deeply tied to the covenant. To be blessed is to be aligned with God’s covenantal purposes and to experience His presence.

• Relational Response: When people bless one another in the name of the Lord, they act as mediators of God's covenantal love. Boaz’s blessing of Ruth is a manifestation of God's covenantal faithfulness (chesed) active through human agents.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:10, Ruth has just risked her reputation and safety by coming to the threshing floor at night, asking Boaz to spread his cloak over her as the family redeemer (goel). Instead of viewing her actions as manipulative or inappropriate, Boaz recognizes her deep moral integrity.

• Affirmation of Chesed: Boaz responds, "Blessed are you of the LORD, my daughter!" He recognizes that her request is motivated by loyalty to her deceased husband's family rather than youthful passion.

• Pronouncement of Favor: By pronouncing her baruk (blessed), Boaz immediately relieves her anxiety, validates her virtue, and assures her that her actions are approved by God.

• Agent of the Blessing: Boaz becomes the answer to his own prayer. Earlier in Ruth 2:12, he prayed that Yahweh would reward Ruth under whose wings she had come to trust. Now, in chapter 3, Boaz steps forward to be the "wing" (cloak) of protection and blessing that God uses to redeem her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:2: "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great; and you shall be a blessing."

• Numbers 6:24: "The LORD bless you and keep you;"

• Psalm 103:1: "Bless the LORD, O my soul; and all that is within me, bless His holy name!"

• Ruth 2:19: "And her mother-in-law said to her, 'Where have you gleaned today? And where did you work? Blessed be the one who took notice of you.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew grammar, the form used in Ruth 3:10 is a passive participle (baruk), meaning "having been blessed" or "recipient of blessing." This structure emphasizes that the subject is already under the stream of God’s active favor. Boaz does not merely wish for her to be blessed; he declares her state as one who is manifestly favored by Yahweh due to her faithful character.
