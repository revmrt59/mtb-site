DAUGHTER - ( bat , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bat refers primarily to a female offspring, but it carries a broad range of relational, communal, and idiomatic meanings in the Old Testament. In pastoral or relational contexts, it functions as a term of tender endearment spoken by an elder to a younger woman, signaling protection, familial care, and respect. In Ruth 3:10, Boaz uses "my daughter" not to indicate biological relation, but to convey his honorable intentions, recognition of Ruth's vulnerability, and deep pastoral affection for her virtuous character.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the patriarchal culture of ancient Israel, the family was the central unit of identity, security, and inheritance. The Hebrew noun bat is the feminine counterpart to ben (son). While sons carried the family name and inheritance, daughters were highly valued members of the household, representing the warmth, continuity, and future alliances of the family. The word itself is derived from a root associated with building (banah), suggesting that a daughter is one who builds up a home and family. In agrarian Israel, daughters were vulnerable to economic hardship, foreign exploitation, and social isolation if they lacked the protection of a father, brother, or husband. Thus, the term carries an inherent connotation of one who is precious, deserving of safety, and placed under the protective care of a household.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of bat is a biological daughter or female descendant. However, its semantic range extends significantly beyond direct lineage:

• A direct female offspring (daughter).

• A female descendant (granddaughter, or more distant female relative).

• An inhabitant of a specific city or region, personified collectively (e.g., "daughter of Zion").

• An idiomatic expression for a woman characterized by a certain trait (e.g., "daughter of worthlessness" or a wicked woman).

• A term of respect, warmth, and pastoral affection used by an older person addressing a younger woman who is not a biological relative.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, bat is woven into rich metaphorical imagery:

• The Cherished Lamb: In Nathan's parable to David, the poor man's ewe lamb is described as growing up with his children, eating his food, drinking from his cup, and lying in his bosom, becoming "like a daughter to him" (2 Samuel 12:3). This highlights the deep emotional warmth and intimacy associated with the term.

• The Personified City: God frequently addresses Jerusalem as the "daughter of Zion" or the "daughter of My people." This corporate personification highlights God's protective, fatherly stance toward His people, viewing them with tender affection and holding them accountable as a father would a daughter.

• The Kind Address: When an elder addresses a younger woman as bat, it transforms a potentially transactional or distant social interaction into an honorable, family-oriented relationship, establishing a boundary of safety and moral responsibility.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of bat reflects the heart of God for the vulnerable and the orphan. In the ancient Near East, a widowed foreign woman like Ruth had no legal standing or protection. By framing the relationship with familial language, the Old Testament law and covenantal practice show how God's people are to extend the boundaries of the family to include the outsider. The term foreshadows the New Testament reality of spiritual adoption, where those who were once far off are brought near and addressed as daughters and sons of the Living God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:10, Ruth has just approached Boaz at night on the threshing floor, asking him to spread his wings over her as her kinsman-redeemer. This was a bold, highly vulnerable, and potentially scandalous initiative. Boaz’s immediate response—blessing her and calling her "my daughter"—is of immense pastoral significance:

• Diffusing Tension: By using "my daughter" (bitti), Boaz instantly removes any sexual or exploitative undertones from the midnight encounter. He establishes himself not as a predator, but as a protective father-figure and honorable redeemer.

• Acknowledging the Age Gap: The term honors the difference in their ages. Boaz notes that she did not run after "younger men, whether poor or rich," indicating that he was significantly older.

• Affirming Virtue: He uses this familial title to validate her character. She is not an opportunistic foreigner; she is treated with the same dignity and respect as a biological daughter of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 2:8 - "Then Boaz said to Ruth, 'Listen, my daughter, will you not go to glean in another field, nor go from here, but stay close by my young women?'" (Boaz establishes his protective care during their very first meeting).

• 2 Samuel 12:3 - "But the poor man had nothing but one little ewe lamb... and it was like a daughter to him." (Illustrates the deep affection and warmth of the word).

• Psalm 45:10 - "Listen, O daughter, consider and incline your ear; forget your own people also, and your father’s house." (A royal wedding song using the term to counsel a bride entering a new covenant).

• Zechariah 9:9 - "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! Shout, O daughter of Jerusalem! Behold, your King is coming to you..." (Prophetic personification of the redeemed community of faith).

• Ruth 3:11 - "And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you request, for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman." (Boaz repeats the term to reassure Ruth of his honorable intentions).

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew grammar, idioms constructed with bat often describe the character or state of a person. For example, a "daughter of song" (Ecclesiastes 12:4) refers to singing maidens or musical notes, and a "daughter of a foreign god" (Malachi 2:11) refers to a woman devoted to pagan worship. When Boaz addresses Ruth as "my daughter," he effectively counters her self-description in Ruth 2:10 as a "foreigner." He elevates her status from an alien to an insider, preparing the way for her to be fully integrated into the lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.
