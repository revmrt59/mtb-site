POOR - ( dal , Strong's H1800 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word dal describes someone who is low, weak, lean, or socially and economically powerless. Rather than pointing simply to a lack of money, this word highlights a state of vulnerability, helplessness, and dependency. In the book of Ruth, Boaz uses this word to commend Ruth for her extraordinary loyalty. By not pursuing younger men, whether "poor" (dal) or rich, Ruth proved that her desire for marriage was driven by covenant faithfulness (chesed) and family redemption rather than physical attraction, youthful romance, or social advancement.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word dal is derived from the root verb dalal, which means to languish, hang down, be empty, or be brought low.

• It evokes the visual of a branch hanging down because it is weak and withered, or a bucket lowered into a well, emptied of its contents. It represents a state of being depleted of strength, resources, or social influence.

• In ancient agrarian societies, those classified as dal were at the bottom of the social ladder. They lacked land, family protection, or the physical capacity to secure their own well-being. Consequently, they were highly vulnerable to exploitation by the ruling class.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word denotes one who is physically weak, socially low, or economically poor.

• The semantic range includes:

• Physical thinness or haggardness, such as the lean cows in Pharaoh's dream or a person wasting away from illness.

• Social insignificance, referring to those of low rank or small influence.

• Material poverty, indicating a lack of basic resources and wealth.

• Helplessness, marking those who cannot defend themselves in legal or societal disputes.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Dal is often used metaphorically to describe a family or clan that has lost its strength, vitality, and prominence. For example, Gideon describes his clan as the "weakest" in Manasseh, signaling their low social standing and lack of military power.

• In poetic literature, the dal are depicted as those who are easily crushed, exploited, or ignored by the powerful. They are the ones whose boundaries are easily violated and whose legal rights at the city gate are frequently trampled.

• The word is frequently contrasted with 'ashir (the rich) or shôa' (the noble), illustrating the sharp division between those who hold power and those who are completely dependent on the mercy of others.

4. Theological Implications

• The Law of Moses contains specific protections for the dal. God commands that judges must not show partiality to the poor (dal) in judgment, nor should they favor the mighty. Justice must be blind to social status.

• God identifies Himself as the defender of the dal. Oppressing the poor is described as an insult to their Creator, whereas showing mercy to them is an act of worship.

• The sacrificial system accommodated the dal. If a worshiper could not afford a costly animal for an offering, the law permitted them to bring inexpensive turtledoves or pigeons, ensuring that poverty never barred someone from entering the presence of God or seeking atonement.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:10, Boaz is deeply moved by Ruth's midnight proposal at the threshing floor. He pronounces a blessing on her, noting that she has shown more kindness (chesed) at the end than at the beginning.

• Ruth was a young, attractive widow. By societal standards, she had the freedom to seek a husband based on romantic interest or physical attraction. Boaz contrasts "young men, whether poor (dal) or rich." In this context, dal refers to young bachelors who might be physically appealing but lack financial security or inheritance.

• Instead of following youthful passion, Ruth chose Boaz—an older man—because he was a close relative capable of acting as a kinsman-redeemer. Her choice was not about self-gratification, but about securing the family line and inheritance of her deceased husband and her mother-in-law, Naomi.

• Boaz recognizes that Ruth’s character is entirely free from vanity or greed. She did not seek a wealthy young man for luxury, nor a poor young man for mere romance. She sought a redeemer to fulfill a covenant duty.

• Leviticus 14:21: "But if he is poor and cannot afford so much, then he shall take one male lamb as a trespass offering to be waved, to make atonement for him..."

• Judges 6:15: "So he said to Him, 'O my Lord, how can I save Israel? Indeed my clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father’s house.'"

• Proverbs 14:31: "He who oppresses the poor reproaches his Maker, But he who honors Him has mercy on the needy."

• Proverbs 22:22: "Do not rob the poor because he is poor, Nor oppress the afflicted at the gate;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The structural contrast between "poor" (dal) and "rich" ('ashir) in Ruth 3:10 functions as a merism—a literary device using two opposites to encompass everything in between. Boaz is stating that Ruth did not pursue any young men, regardless of where they fell on the social or financial spectrum.

• Ruth’s actions elevate her as the ideal "virtuous woman" (eshet chayil), a title Boaz explicitly gives her in the very next verse (Ruth 3:11). Her willingness to bypass the natural desire for a younger mate of any financial status highlights her profound spiritual maturity.
