LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh (translated as "LORD" in the NKJV) is the personal, covenant-keeping name of the God of Israel. Revealed most profoundly to Moses at the burning bush, it denotes God's self-existence, eternal presence, and unwavering faithfulness to His promises. In the Book of Ruth, and specifically in Boaz's blessing of Ruth in Ruth 3:10, this name highlights that the unfolding redemption of Naomi and Ruth is not a product of mere luck or blind fate, but the deliberate, merciful intervention of Israel's covenant God. He is the God who actively rewards loyalty (chesed) and provides refuge for those who place their trust under His wings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The name Yahweh is the sacred tetragrammaton (YHWH), the four-letter name of God. It is derived from the Hebrew verb hayah, which means "to be" or "to exist."

• Unlike generic titles such as Elohim (God) or Adonai (Lord/Master), Yahweh is God's deeply personal, relational, and proper name.

• Historically, the Jewish people held this name in such intense reverence that they avoided pronouncing it aloud to prevent taking it in vain, substituting the title Adonai when reading Scripture. This custom is preserved in modern English translations, including the NKJV, by rendering the name in capital letters as "LORD."

• The background of the name is permanently tethered to the Exodus narrative, where God revealed Himself as "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14). This established Him as the eternally present, self-sufficient, and active Savior of His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The name signifies the One who is self-existent, eternal, and sovereignly independent of creation. It carries the primary meaning of "the One who is" or "the Active, Present One."

• Lexically, it denotes the specific, personal name of the covenant God of Israel. It represents Him as the initiator and keeper of covenants, the Redeemer of His people, and the faithful God who acts in human history to fulfill His oaths.

• It is characterized by absolute immutability, holiness, mercy, and relational intimacy toward those who are in covenant with Him.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The name occurs over 6,800 times in the Old Testament, making it the most frequent designation for God in the Hebrew Scriptures.

• It is frequently coupled with metaphors of protection and security, such as a rock, a shield, a fortress, and a shepherd. These word pictures emphasize that Yahweh is a personal Protector and Provider for His people.

• In the Book of Ruth, the name is used in blessings, prayers, oaths, and praise. This frequent usage connects the seemingly ordinary, everyday events of rural Israelite life—such as harvest cycles, family tragedies, and community customs—to the grand, sovereign design of the covenant God.

• The relational word picture is that of a faithful husband who binds Himself to His spouse by a solemn covenant, demonstrating unyielding devotion.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Faithfulness: Yahweh is the God of chesed (loyal, covenant-keeping love). His actions are always consistent with His perfect character and His revealed promises. He cannot deny Himself.

• Sovereign Grace in the Ordinary: The name teaches that God is not a distant, detached deity. He is intimately involved in the mundane, painful, and quiet details of human life, orchestrating them for the good of His people and the glory of His name.

• Redemption: Yahweh is the ultimate Redeemer. The human kinsman-redeemer (Boaz) serves as a physical, historical shadow pointing to the grander, spiritual redemption provided by Yahweh Himself to helpless outsiders.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage (Ruth 3:10)

• In Ruth 3:10, Boaz exclaims, "Blessed are you of the LORD, my daughter! For you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning, in that you did not go after young men, whether poor or rich."

• This statement occurs immediately after Ruth courageously asks Boaz to spread his cloak over her, inviting him to act as her kinsman-redeemer.

• By invoking Yahweh, Boaz recognizes that Ruth's extraordinary loyalty (chesed) and bold faith are a reflection of the character of Israel's covenant God.

• Boaz does not view this midnight encounter as a scandalous or opportunistic event. Instead, he recognizes it as an occasion of divine blessing. He invokes Yahweh as the source of blessing because Ruth has abandoned her own romantic and economic prospects to seek family continuity and refuge under the wings of the God of Israel.

• The use of Yahweh here highlights that their human relationship, love, and the legal work of redemption are ultimately acts of worship and obedience to the Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:6: "And he believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness."

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Ruth 1:17: "Where you die, I will die, And there will I be buried. The LORD do so to me, and more also, If anything but death parts you and me."

• Ruth 2:12: "The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge."

• Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Book of Ruth contains no overt, spectacular miracles like the parting of the Red Sea or fire falling from heaven. Instead, the name Yahweh is woven naturally into the dialogue of ordinary people, showing that God's presence is experienced through the providential steering of daily life and the faithful choices of His servants.

• When Boaz pronounces a blessing in the name of Yahweh, he unknowingly becomes the primary human instrument Yahweh uses to answer that very blessing.
