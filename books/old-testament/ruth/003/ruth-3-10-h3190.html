MORE - ( yatab , Strong's H3190 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yatab in Ruth 3:10, translated as "more" (in the phrase "shown more kindness"), fundamentally means to do something well, to make good, or to deal bountifully. In its causative form (Hiphil), it describes an action that is performed in an excellent, surpassing, or superior manner. When Boaz commends Ruth for showing "more" kindness, he is highlighting that her decision to seek a kinsman-redeemer to preserve her deceased husband's family line—rather than pursuing younger men based on personal preference or financial status—is an even greater, more beautiful expression of covenant loyalty (chesed) than her initial devotion in leaving Moab to care for Naomi. It represents a sacrificial love that prioritizes family redemption over personal interest.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root yatab carries the primary concrete idea of something being sweet, pleasing, or good to the senses, which historically expanded to ethical, moral, and functional goodness. In the ancient Near East, "goodness" was not merely abstract or internal; it was highly practical, visible, and relational. It described a land that was fertile and pleasant, a heart that was glad, or a person acting in a way that brought benefit, harmony, and joy to others. In the causative Hiphil stem used in Ruth 3:10 (hetavt), the word shifts from being in a good state to actively performing an action with exceptional goodness, beauty, or skill. The word picture is one of "making a good thing even better" or "surpassing a previous standard of goodness." Ruth does not merely perform a duty; she elevates her kindness to an extraordinary level.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its basic Qal stem, yatab means to be good, to go well, to be pleasing, or to be glad. In the Hiphil (causative) stem, the verb means to make good, to do good, to deal well, or to do something thoroughly and excellently. When paired with an action or another verb, it serves an adverbial function, meaning "to do an action well" or "to do an action more abundantly and better." In the specific construction of Ruth 3:10, the verb governs the noun chesed (lovingkindness, loyalty), meaning "you have made your kindness better" or "you have shown greater or more kindness." It denotes a deliberate escalation of goodness, where the second act of loyalty outshines the first.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb yatab appears over a hundred times in the Old Testament, frequently describing God's promise to do good to His people, or the call for Israel to amend their ways.

• The Goodness of Joy and Well-being: It is used to describe a glad heart, as when a person's heart is "merry" or "good" (Judges 19:6; 1 Kings 21:7).

• The Excellence of Action: In artistic or functional contexts, it denotes doing something skillfully or thoroughly, such as playing an instrument well (1 Samuel 16:17) or trimming lamps carefully (Exodus 30:7).

• The Relational Blessing: In covenantal contexts, it represents the active, tangible favor of God overflowing to His people, bringing them prosperity and peace (Genesis 32:12; Deuteronomy 8:16).

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of yatab in connection with chesed (covenant faithfulness) highlights the progressive and active nature of biblical love. Ruth’s love is not static; it grows and adapts to meet deeper needs. True biblical goodness is not a passive state of being but an active, creative force that seeks the ultimate well-being of others, even at great personal cost. Furthermore, this passage reflects the heart of God, who Himself constantly "does good" (yatab) and shows abundant kindness to His people. Ruth acts as a human agent of divine chesed, mirroring God’s own willingness to go above and beyond the letter of the law to bring redemption and restoration to the broken.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:10, Boaz is astonished and deeply moved by Ruth's proposal on the threshing floor. He recognizes that Ruth’s first chesed (leaving her homeland, her gods, and her family to care for her destitute mother-in-law, Naomi) was already monumental. However, her second act of loyalty is "more" or "better" (yatab). Instead of seeking a husband among the "young men, whether poor or rich"—which would be a natural choice for a young widow seeking personal romance, security, or social standing—she submits her future to the covenantal family laws of Israel. By choosing Boaz, an older man who is a close relative, she prioritizes the continuation of her deceased husband's name and the redemption of Naomi’s family land. Boaz recognizes that this act of self-sacrificing love is a superior, more mature expression of covenant loyalty than her initial devotion.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

These cross-references demonstrate how yatab is used in the Hiphil stem to denote doing good, amending behavior, or acting thoroughly:

• Genesis 4:7 — "If you do well, will you not be accepted? And if you do not do well, sin lies at the door."

• Genesis 32:12 — "For You said, 'I will surely treat you well, and make your descendants as the sand of the sea...'"

• Jeremiah 18:11 — "Return now every one from his evil way, and make your ways and your doings good."

• Psalm 33:3 — "Sing to Him a new song; play skillfully with a shout of joy."

• Micah 7:3 — "That they may successfully do evil with both hands..." (literally, "to make good at doing evil," using the word to mean doing something thoroughly or with great effort).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammar of the phrase hetavt chasdek ("you have made your kindness better") underscores the volitional nature of Ruth's character. The verb is in the Hiphil perfect second-person feminine singular, meaning Ruth is the active agent who caused this goodness to happen. She did not stumble into this kindness; she intentionally designed her actions to bring about the redemption of Naomi's household. Additionally, this phrase serves as a literary turning point in the Book of Ruth. It marks the transition from Ruth being the passive recipient of Boaz's charity in the fields to Ruth being recognized as a powerful, virtuous woman who actively drives the redemption of the family line, ultimately leading to the birth of Obed, the grandfather of King David, and the ancestor of Jesus Christ.
