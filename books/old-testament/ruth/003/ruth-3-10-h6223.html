RICH - ( asir , Strong's H6223 )

Summary:

The Hebrew adjective asir designates a person of substantial wealth, material abundance, or elevated social and financial standing. In the pastoral narrative of Ruth 3:10, Boaz uses this term to commend Ruth's moral integrity and loyalty. Rather than seeking a husband based on mere material security, youth, or financial status (whether "poor or rich"), Ruth prioritized covenant faithfulness by seeking a kinsman-redeemer to preserve her deceased husband's family line.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite society, wealth was largely agricultural, measured in land, livestock, crops, servants, and precious metals. A person described as asir possessed more than enough to meet daily needs; they held a position of influence, independence, and social responsibility. The term stands in frequent contrast to terms for the poor, needy, or marginalized. Rather than carrying an inherently negative or positive moral connotation, asir describes a physical and socioeconomic reality. In the context of the Ancient Near East, marriage to a rich man offered a widow immediate security, social standing, and freedom from the grueling labor of gleaning.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word asir means wealthy, rich, or influential. It refers to someone possessing abundant possessions, property, or money. It is derived from the verbal root asar, which means to become rich or accumulate wealth. The lexical range consistently refers to material wealth and the societal status that accompanies it, frequently paired with its opposite, the poor, to represent the entire spectrum of human society.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In Wisdom literature, asir is used to describe the practical realities of life, illustrating how wealth brings many friends, social influence, and a false sense of security, while also noting that God makes both the rich and the poor.

• In prophetic literature, the term is used to address systemic injustice, warning the rich who exploit the poor or trust in their fleeting material goods.

• In historical narratives, the word highlights the contrast between those of high estate and those of low estate, emphasizing God's sovereignty over human status and His valuation of the heart over material abundance.

4. Theological Implications

Scripture treats wealth as a neutral resource that carries immense spiritual responsibility. God is the ultimate owner of all wealth, and He elevates some to the status of asir while others remain poor. Ruth's refusal to pursue a rich man outside her husband's family shows that covenant loyalty (hesed) supersedes material ambition. The redemptive trajectory of the story showcases Boaz—himself a man of great wealth—acting not out of self-interest but out of a desire to redeem, reflecting God's own grace toward the spiritually bankrupt.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:10, Boaz is deeply moved by Ruth's proposal on the threshing floor. Ruth, as a young, attractive widow, had the social freedom to pursue younger men, whether poor or rich, to secure her own future. By choosing Boaz, an older kinsman, she sought the preservation of Elimelech's family line rather than her own romantic or financial advancement. Boaz recognizes this selflessness as a supreme act of covenant kindness. Her choice proved that her character was driven by devotion to Naomi and the God of Israel, not by the superficial allure of a husband's wealth or youth.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 2 Samuel 12:1 - "There were two men in one city, one rich and the other poor."

• Proverbs 22:2 - "The rich and the poor have this in common, The Lord is the Maker of them all."

• Proverbs 22:7 - "The rich rules over the poor, And the borrower is servant to the lender."

• Ecclesiastes 10:20 - "Do not curse the king, even in your thought; Do not curse the rich, even in your bedroom; For a bird of the air may carry your voice, And a bird in flight may tell the matter."

• Isaiah 53:9 - "And they made His grave with the wicked— But with the rich at His death, Because He had done no violence, Nor was any deceit in His mouth."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between "poor or rich" in Ruth 3:10 functions as a merism—a literary device using polar opposites to encompass everything in between. Ruth did not pursue any young man of any status. Boaz himself was a "man of great wealth" (Ruth 2:1), yet Ruth did not seek him out merely because of his wealth, but because of his role as a redeemer. The story beautifully contrasts the superficial pursuit of wealth with the holy pursuit of redemption.
