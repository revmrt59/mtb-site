BEGINNING - ( ri'shon , Strong's H7223 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ri'shon refers to that which is first in time, order, rank, or position. In the context of Ruth 3:10, it designates the "former" or "earlier" demonstration of Ruth's covenant-keeping loyalty (chesed). Boaz uses this word to contrast her initial acts of devotion toward her mother-in-law and deceased husband with her even greater, climaxing act of kindness in seeking a kinsman-redeemer. The term emphasizes a trajectory of expanding faithfulness that characterizes Ruth's entire journey.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word ri'shon is derived from the noun ro'sh, which means "head," "chief," or "summit." Just as the head is the foremost part of the body, ri'shon represents the foremost point of time, space, or rank. It points to the headwaters or the starting line of a sequence. In the ancient Near Eastern mindset, the "first" or "former" established the standard and trajectory for everything that followed. To speak of the ri'shon is to point back to the foundation upon which subsequent actions are built.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word functions as an adjective or ordinal number meaning "first," "former," "earlier," or "chief." It designates the initial element in a temporal sequence, such as the first month of the year or the first day of a festival. It also identifies ancestors or "former" generations. When contrasted with the "last" ('acharon), it establishes a historical or chronological boundary, encompassing the entirety of a period or a series of actions.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In historical narratives, the word is frequently used to designate the "first" month of the religious calendar, marking the beginning of God's redemptive cycle at the Passover.

• It depicts "former" states of being or previous conditions, such as a "former" husband or the "former" glory of the temple.

• In prophetic literature, it contrasts "former things" (ri'shonot)—prophecies already fulfilled—with the "new things" God is about to bring forth, demonstrating His absolute sovereignty over history.

4. Theological Implications

The word underscores the progressive nature of divine revelation and human response. God is identified as the First (ri'shon) and the Last, indicating His eternal, unchanging nature as the initiator and consummator of all things. In the realm of human faithfulness, ri'shon highlights that initial obedience, though vital, is meant to grow. It warns against relying solely on past or "former" righteousness, pointing instead to a life where subsequent faithfulness builds upon and even surpasses the beauty of the beginning.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:10, Boaz praises Ruth, saying, "you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning." Here, the "beginning" (ri'shon) refers to Ruth's initial acts of chesed (covenant loyalty) when she left her homeland of Moab, abandoned her family and gods, and bound herself to Naomi and the God of Israel. This "beginning" loyalty was already legendary in Bethlehem. Yet, Boaz recognizes that her "end" ('acharon) action—offering herself in marriage to an older kinsman-redeemer to preserve her deceased husband’s family line rather than pursuing younger, wealthier men—surpassed even her initial sacrifice. The word ri'shon establishes the baseline of Ruth's incredible character, showing that her devotion was not a fleeting, emotional decision but the start of an ever-deepening life of sacrificial love.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 25:25: "And the first came out red. He was like a hairy garment all over; so they called his name Esau."

• Deuteronomy 24:4: "then her former husband who divorced her must not take her back to be his wife after she has been defiled..."

• Isaiah 44:6: "Thus says the Lord, the King of Israel, And his Redeemer, the Lord of hosts: 'I am the First and I am the Last; Besides Me there is no God.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The pairing of "beginning" (ri'shon) and "end" ('acharon) in Ruth 3:10 forms a stylistic merism. This literary device uses two contrasting extremes to encompass everything in between. By comparing her "beginning" and "end" kindness, Boaz declares that Ruth’s entire life in Israel has been enveloped in unbroken, active covenant love.
