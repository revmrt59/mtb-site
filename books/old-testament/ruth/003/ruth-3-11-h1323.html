DAUGHTER - ( bath , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bath primarily denotes a female offspring, but it frequently carries broader relational, social, and emotional connotations throughout the Old Testament. In Ruth 3:11, Boaz uses "my daughter" not to signal biological descent, but as a tender, respectful, and protective term of address for a younger woman. This designation establishes a relationship of safety, moral propriety, and covenantal care, bridging the gap between Ruth's vulnerability as a foreign widow and Boaz's role as a potential kinsman-redeemer (goel). It reflects familial warmth and honor rather than mere social hierarchy, affirming Ruth's worth within the covenant community of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun bath is the feminine counterpart to ben (son), both derived from roots associated with building or establishing a family. A daughter in the ancient Near East was seen as an essential building block of the household, securing the continuation and expansion of family lines through marriage and childbirth.

• Beyond strict biological lineage, the term was also used idiomatically to describe women of a specific locality, female citizens, or as a term of affectionate address by an older individual to a younger female, establishing a bond of protection, guidance, and familial responsibility.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word bath denotes a daughter, female descendant, or young woman.

• It signifies a biological daughter in immediate family contexts.

• It expresses a broader relationship of female descendants, granddaughters, or women belonging to a nation, tribe, or city.

• It serves as a polite, affectionate address used by an elder to a younger woman, signaling kindness, parental concern, and protective interest.

• It designates a personified city or nation, such as "daughter of Zion" or "daughter of Babylon."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Relational security: In patriarchal Israel, a daughter was under the authority and protection of her father until marriage. Boaz’s address of Ruth as "my daughter" (bitti) extends this umbrella of familial protection to a vulnerable foreigner who has no living father or husband in Israel.

• Architectural metaphor: Rooted in the concept of building, the daughter is a builder of the home. This connects deeply with the wider narrative of Ruth, who, along with Rachel and Leah, is later blessed as one who "built the house of Israel" (Ruth 4:11).

• Idiomatic expressions: The term is used in Hebrew idioms like "daughter of the eye" (apple of the eye) to depict something precious requiring utmost protection, mirroring the care Boaz extends to Ruth.

4. Theological Implications

• Redemptive Grace and Adoption: Ruth is a Moabitess, an outsider excluded by the Law from the assembly of Yahweh. By addressing her as "my daughter," Boaz—acting as a type of Christ, the ultimate Redeemer—spiritually and socially adopts her into the sphere of covenant blessing.

• Covenant Kindness (Hesed): The term reflects the character of Yahweh's covenant love. It transforms a legalistic or transactional encounter on the threshing floor into a sacred, familial relationship.

• Dignity of the Vulnerable: Scripture consistently emphasizes God's care for the widow, the orphan, and the stranger. Boaz’s respectful address elevates Ruth’s status from a desperate gleaner to an honored member of the community.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:11, the context is the middle of the night at the threshing floor. Ruth has made a bold, culturally specific request for Boaz to spread his cloak over her, asking him to fulfill the role of kinsman-redeemer.

• Boaz’s response, beginning with "my daughter," immediately defuses any potential sexual tension, impropriety, or fear. It establishes a boundary of absolute moral purity and protective care.

• By calling her "my daughter," Boaz acknowledges the age difference between them while validating her moral character. He assures her that her request is honorable and that he will act righteously on her behalf.

• Ruth 2:2 - "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

• Ruth 2:8 - "Then Boaz said to Ruth, 'You will listen, my daughter, will you not? Do not go to glean in another field, nor go from here, but stay close by my young women.'"

• Psalm 45:10 - "Listen, O daughter, Consider and incline your ear; Forget your own people also, and your father’s house;"

• Zechariah 9:9 - "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! Shout, O daughter of Jerusalem! Behold, your King is coming to you;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The phrase "my daughter" appears multiple times in the Book of Ruth (used by Naomi in 2:2, 2:22, 3:1, 3:16, 3:18, and by Boaz in 2:8, 3:10, 3:11). This repetition underscores the transition of Ruth from a foreign outsider ("Ruth the Moabitess") to an integrated, loved, and protected daughter within Israel.

• The transition from "stranger" (2:10) to "handmaid" (2:13) to "maidservant" (3:9) culminates in this high recognition of familial honor ("my daughter") and civic honor ("a virtuous woman") in 3:11.
