KNOW - ( yada , Strong's H3045 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yada goes far beyond intellectual assent or the mere acquisition of information; it denotes experiential knowledge, relational intimacy, and deep personal recognition. In Ruth 3:11, Boaz uses this word to declare that the entire community of Bethlehem has personally observed, experienced, and recognized Ruth's noble character. It represents a public, undeniable verification of her virtue, establishing her reputation not on rumor, but on proven, lived-out righteousness.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew concept of knowledge is deeply concrete, sensory, and relational, differing significantly from the abstract, theoretical knowledge of ancient Greek philosophy. To "know" in the Hebrew mind is to experience, to see, to encounter, and to respond to a reality. It involves the heart, the will, and the senses. The word picture behind yada is one of close observation and personal connection. In the ancient Near East, a person's standing was determined by their public actions, which were observed by the community at the city gate. When a community "knew" someone, it meant they had watched their life, verified their integrity, and reached a collective consensus about their character.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yada carries a broad semantic range that includes:

• To perceive, learn, or understand.

• To know by experience, trial, or encounter.

• To possess skill, ability, or expertise in something.

• To be intimately acquainted with a person, including the deepest level of marital intimacy.

• To recognize, admit, acknowledge, or confess a public truth.

In Ruth 3:11, the word is used in its active sense of experiential recognition and public acknowledgment. The townspeople have not merely heard reports about Ruth; they have observed her life, assessed her conduct, and collectively acknowledged her noble character as an undeniable fact.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yada is used to describe the deepest realities of human and divine relationships:

• It describes the unique, covenantal choice of God, such as when God "knows" Abraham or Israel, choosing them for a special relationship.

• It is used of physical, marital intimacy, where a husband "knows" his wife, representing a total giving of oneself to another.

• It describes the discernment of reality, such as knowing the difference between good and evil, or knowing that the Lord is the true God through His mighty acts of deliverance.

The word picture is that of a path that has been walked upon. To know someone's character is to have traveled the road of life with them and seen how they handle hardship, labor, and relationships.

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of yada in the Old Testament teaches that true righteousness is visible and experiential. Godly character is not a hidden, gnostic secret; it bears fruit that can be tasted and seen by others. Furthermore, this word underscores the relational nature of God's covenant. God does not merely know about His people; He knows them intimately, and He calls them to know Him through obedience and love.

In the story of Ruth, the use of yada demonstrates God's providential care. Ruth was a foreign outsider from Moab, a nation often despised by Israel. Yet, through her faithful, covenant-keeping love (chesed), she lived in such a way that her character became known and celebrated. God took a nameless stranger and made her name highly respected among His covenant people.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:11, Boaz says, "And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you request, for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman."

This statement occurs during a highly vulnerable moment. Ruth has approached Boaz at night on the threshing floor, requesting that he act as her kinsman-redeemer. By reassuring her that the town "knows" she is a virtuous woman (eshet chayil), Boaz protects her honor. The phrase "all the people of my town" is literally "all the gate of my people," referring to the elders and leaders who sat at the city gate to judge legal and social matters.

The contextual nuance of yada here is legal and communal vindication. Boaz is telling Ruth that her reputation is secure. Her late-night appeal is not viewed as scandalous or opportunistic because her established track record of virtue is universally recognized. She is publicly acknowledged as the living embodiment of the virtuous woman described in Proverbs 31:10.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:1 - "Now Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived and bore Cain..."

• Job 19:25 - "For I know that my Redeemer lives, / And He shall stand at last on the earth."

• Psalm 1:6 - "For the LORD knows the way of the righteous, / But the way of the ungodly shall perish."

• Jeremiah 1:5 - "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you; / Before you were born I sanctified you..."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew phrase translated "virtuous woman" in Ruth 3:11 (eshet chayil) is the exact same phrase used to describe the ideal wife in Proverbs 31:10. By stating that the community knows Ruth is such a woman, the author of Ruth connects her directly to the wisdom literature of Israel. Ruth is not just a passive character in a love story; she is the concrete, historical realization of the wise, strong, and faithful woman of Proverbs. Her virtue is not a private opinion held by Boaz, but a public truth known by all.
