FEAR - ( yare , Strong's H3372 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yare fundamentally refers to the emotional and psychological state of fear, dread, or terror, as well as the positive attitude of moral awe, reverence, and respect, especially toward God. In Ruth 3:11, Boaz uses this word in its negative hortatory form ("do not fear") to comfort Ruth. In this context, it addresses her vulnerability and anxiety as a destitute foreign widow seeking protection and redemption. Boaz's reassurance transforms her fear of rejection and exposure into security and peace, grounded in his honorable character and readiness to fulfill his role as her kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word yare is a primary root in the Hebrew Scriptures. In its concrete ancient Near Eastern context, fear is not merely an internal emotion but a physical reaction. It is associated with trembling, a racing heart, and a desire to flee or hide. It reflects the natural human response to danger, power, or the unknown.

In the agrarian and patriarchal setting of ancient Israel, a foreign widow like Ruth had every objective reason to experience this kind of fear. Lying at the feet of an influential landowner in the middle of the night on a dark threshing floor was a position of immense vulnerability. If rejected, she faced public shame, deeper poverty, or physical danger. Boaz’s command to "not fear" directly counters this physical and social vulnerability.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yare functions primarily to describe:

• The emotion of fear or terror when facing danger, enemies, or death.

• The attitude of deep reverence, standing in awe, or honoring a person of high authority.

• The worshipful, obedient reverence directed toward God ("the fear of the Lord"), which forms the foundation of biblical wisdom.

In the grammatical structure of Ruth 3:11, the word is conjugated as a Qal imperfect with the negative particle al (al-tiyrei), which functions as an immediate prohibition. It translates to "stop fearing" or "do not be afraid." It is a pastoral reassurance designed to alleviate her immediate emotional distress and situational anxiety.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses yare over 300 times to portray a wide semantic range:

• Dread of Danger: Jacob experiencing terror before meeting his brother Esau (Genesis 32:7), or the Israelites crying out in dread at the Red Sea (Exodus 14:10).

• Awe of Majesty: The people standing in awe of Moses after his face shone with God's glory (Exodus 34:30).

• Covenant Reverence: Fearing Yahweh as the basis of keeping His commandments and experiencing His blessing (Deuteronomy 6:13; Psalm 111:10).

In Ruth 3:11, the word picture shifts from terror to peace. Boaz’s spoken word acts as a protective shield, absorbing Ruth's vulnerability and replacing her dread of the future with the covenant certainty of redemption.

### 4. Theological Implications

The phrase "Do not fear" is one of the most frequent divine imperatives in Scripture, often spoken by God or His messengers when initiating a covenant or revealing His redemptive plan. By using these words, Boaz acts as a human agent of divine care. He reflects the character of God, who repeatedly tells the vulnerable, the orphan, and the widow that they are safe in His hands. Redemption (goel) and the removal of fear are helper concepts; true redemption casts out fear. Ruth's security is established not by her own power, but by the faithful commitment of her redeemer.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:11, the context is highly delicate. Ruth has taken a bold, culturally specific step by asking Boaz to spread his cloak over her—a symbolic request for marriage and kinsman-redemption. Ruth's fear would have centered on several fronts: Would Boaz misinterpret her motives? Would he refuse because she was a Moabite? Would he expose her to public scandal?

Boaz’s immediate response, "And now, my daughter, do not fear," instantly defuses the tension. He validates her worth, calls her "daughter" (a term of protective warmth), and promises to do everything she asks. He highlights her reputation as a "virtuous woman," showing that her bold act has not tarnished her honor in his eyes.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:1 - "After these things the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward.'"

• Genesis 26:24 - "And the LORD appeared to him the same night and said, 'I am the God of your father Abraham; do not fear, for I am with you.'"

• Exodus 20:20 - "And Moses said to the people, 'Do not fear; for God has come to test you, and that His fear may be before you, so that you may not sin.'"

• Psalm 23:4 - "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; For You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me."

• Proverbs 31:21 - "She is not afraid of snow for her household, For all her household is clothed with scarlet."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful literary connection between Ruth 3:11 and Proverbs 31. Boaz calls Ruth a "virtuous woman" (eshet chayil), the exact Hebrew phrase used to describe the ideal woman in Proverbs 31:10. Just as the Proverbs 31 woman "does not fear" (yare) the winter snow because her household is prepared (Proverbs 31:21), Ruth is told "do not fear" because her kinsman-redeemer has her future fully covered. By commanding Ruth not to fear, Boaz is stepping into the role of her protector, assuring her that the legal, physical, and financial hurdles of her situation will be resolved by his hand.
