REQUEST - ( amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb amar is the primary Old Testament word for "to say," "to speak," or "to declare." While it typically refers to simple verbal communication, in specific relational contexts—especially when a petitioner addresses someone of authority or capacity—it functions as a formal "request" or "petition." In Ruth 3:11, Boaz responds to Ruth's vulnerable, midnight appeal at the threshing floor. By translating amar as "request," the NKJV captures how Ruth's humble spoken need moves the heart of her kinsman-redeemer, who commits to doing everything she has spoken because of her virtuous character.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb amar occurs over 5,000 times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most common words in the Hebrew Scriptures. At its most basic level, it represents the externalization of thought. It is the vocalization of what is conceived in the heart. In the ancient near eastern mind, speech was not merely empty breath; it carried substance, intent, and creative power. This is seen fundamentally in the creation narrative, where God speaks (amar) and reality comes into being. When applied to human relationships, particularly in covenant or legal settings, what a person "says" establishes their desires, boundaries, or pleas. In the context of Ruth and Boaz, Ruth’s midnight speech was not casual conversation; it was a formal, vulnerable petition for redemption, legal protection, and marriage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of amar is to say, speak, utter, command, or think (to "say in one's heart"). In the Qal stem, it denotes the direct act of verbalizing a thought or message. When the word is used in a context where a lower-status individual addresses a superior with a specific need, the semantic range narrows to mean "to petition," "to ask," or "to request." In these instances, the word carries the weight of an appeal that awaits a favorable response from the hearer. In Ruth 3:11, the verbal form is the second-person feminine singular imperfect (tomri), meaning "you say" or "you speak." Because Ruth's speech in verse 9 was a plea for Boaz to spread his wings over her as a redeemer, her "saying" is correctly understood and translated as a formal "request."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The usage of amar often paints a picture of relational vulnerability or authoritative decree:

• The Voice of the Needy: When used by the marginalized, amar is the vocalization of helplessness. It is the cry of the heart made audible to the one who has the power to deliver.

• Internal Monologue: Frequently, the Bible uses amar to describe a person "saying to themselves" or "saying in their heart" (e.g., Genesis 27:41). This links the spoken word directly to the deepest thoughts and intents of the soul.

• The Promise of the Redeemer: When a superior hears the amar of a petitioner and responds with their own amar, it establishes a verbal covenant. The word spoken becomes a binding promise of action.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of amar in the context of redemption carries rich theological truth. It illustrates how God relates to His people. Throughout Scripture, salvation and provision are initiated when God's people "say" or cry out their needs to Him, and He responds.

• The Power of Petition: Prayer is essentially the act of bringing our amar—our spoken requests—before the Lord. God invites this verbalization not because He is ignorant of our needs, but because the act of speaking expresses trust, dependence, and relationship.

• The Kind-Hearted Redeemer: Boaz acts as a type of Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer. Just as Boaz did not ignore or exploit Ruth's vulnerable request, Christ does not reject those who come to Him in spiritual poverty, speaking their need for His saving grace. He commits to doing "all that you request."

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:11, the context is filled with tension, high stakes, and cultural propriety. Ruth has risked her safety and reputation by going to the threshing floor at night, laying at Boaz's feet, and asking him to act as her redeemer. Her words in verse 9 were direct: "Spread your cloak over your maidservant, for you are a close relative."

When Boaz responds in verse 11, "I will do for you all that you request," he validates her bold initiative. He does not view her speech as manipulative or inappropriate. By recognizing her as a "virtuous woman," Boaz elevates her "saying" from a potentially scandalous proposition to a noble, righteous request. He binds himself to fulfill her words completely, demonstrating that a righteous redeemer responds to a humble petitioner with total commitment and immediate action.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 32:26 - "And He said, 'Let Me go, for the day breaks.' But he said [amar], 'I will not let You go unless You bless me!'"

• Genesis 44:18 - "Then Judah came near to him and said [amar]: 'O my lord, please let your servant speak a word in my lord's hearing, and do not let your anger burn against your servant; for you are even like Pharaoh.'"

• 1 Samuel 1:17 - "Then Eli answered and said [amar], 'Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition which you have asked of Him.'"

• Nehemiah 2:4 - "Then the king said [amar] to me, 'What do you request?' So I prayed to the God of heaven."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful contrast can be made between amar (to say) and dabar (to speak). While dabar often refers to the formal act of speaking, a speech, or the physical word itself, amar focuses on the content, meaning, and personal intent of the message. In Ruth 3:11, Boaz is not merely promising to react to Ruth's speech (dabar); he is promising to perform the exact substance of what she has expressed (amar). He aligns his future actions precisely with her stated needs, showcasing the perfect alignment between the petitioner's faith and the redeemer's grace.
