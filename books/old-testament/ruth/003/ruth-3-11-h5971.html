PEOPLE - ( 'am , Strong's H5971 )

Summary:

In Ruth 3:11, the word "people" ('am) refers to the collective community, citizens, or townsfolk of Bethlehem. Used within the idiomatic phrase "the gate of my people," it signifies the public, civic body that witnesses, validates, and affirms Ruth's noble character. Rather than designating a sterile political entity, the term emphasizes a relational, covenantal, and social unit that recognizes Ruth's transformation from a foreign outsider to an esteemed member of the community.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun 'am carries a deep sense of relational connection, kinship, and family association. Its ancient Semitic roots are tied to concepts of unclehood, paternal relatives, and being "together" or "reunited." Unlike other Hebrew terms for nations or groupings that emphasize geopolitical boundaries or political structures, 'am emphasizes a common bond, a shared identity, and a family-like connection. It is the language of a tribe, a clan, or a covenant household expanded into a larger society. To belong to the 'am is to be part of an intimate, mutually responsible community bound by shared customs, history, and spiritual commitment.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'am is a people, a nation, or a specific group of compatriots. It denotes:

• A group of people sharing a common ancestry, language, or culture.

• The common people or citizenry of a specific locality, town, or city, as opposed to its rulers or leaders.

• A close-knit kinship group or family circle (often used in the plural, 'ammin, to refer to one's paternal relatives or ancestors).

• The covenant people of Yahweh ('am Yahweh), distinguishing Israel from the surrounding pagan nations.

In the construct state in Ruth 3:11, Boaz refers to "all the gate of my people" (kol-sha'ar 'ammi), which the NKJV contextually translates as "all the people of my town." Here, the word defines the local covenant community of Bethlehem gathered in its official, civic capacity.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word 'am is used extensively throughout the Old Testament to construct powerful theological and cultural word pictures:

• The Family of God: It contrasts sharply with the word goy (nation). While goy often describes a political body or pagan nations in their external, political power, 'am describes Israel in her internal, familial relationship with God. God did not choose Israel because of her geopolitical power (goy), but to be His personal, treasured family ('am).

• The Gate of the People: In ancient Israel, the "gate" (sha'ar) was the place where the elders sat, legal transactions occurred, and public announcements were made. Linking the "gate" with the "people" ('am) paints a picture of a collective public conscience. The gate of the people is the place where a person's reputation is weighed and either validated or condemned by the community.

### 4. Theological Implications

The book of Ruth is a narrative masterpiece detailing the redemption of a Moabite widow and her inclusion into the covenant family of Israel. The theological trajectory of the word 'am in this book is profound:

• Redemptive Inclusion: Ruth begins as an outsider, a member of Moab (a pagan goy). When she pledges her loyalty to Naomi, she declares, "your people ('am) shall be my people ('am)" (Ruth 1:16). This was a covenantal step of faith, abandoning her ancestral identity to join Yahweh's family.

• Social Validation: By Ruth 3:11, Ruth’s faith and loyalty (hesed) have borne fruit. The community that she chose to join now fully adopts and claims her. Boaz declares that the entire "gate of my people" knows she is a virtuous woman. The Moabitess is no longer a tolerated stranger; she is celebrated by the covenant people as one of their own. This foreshadows the gospel truth that membership in the people of God is determined by faith, grace, and covenant loyalty, not biological descent.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:11, Boaz seeks to comfort Ruth during their late-night encounter at the threshing floor. He tells her, "do not fear... for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman."

• The literal Hebrew reads, "all the gate of my people ('ammi) know."

• In Bethlehem’s close-knit agrarian community, a poor foreign widow was highly vulnerable to gossip, slander, and exploitation. Boaz’s use of 'am assures Ruth that her reputation is pristine.

• Instead of being viewed with suspicion or prejudice as a Moabite foreigner, she has earned the universal respect of the local assembly. The "people of the town" serve as a collective, unanimous witness to her character, clearing the way for Boaz to legally pursue the role of her kinsman-redeemer.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A clear distinction exists in Biblical Hebrew between 'am (people) and goy (nation). In the plural, goyim almost always refers to the Gentile nations who do not know Yahweh, whereas 'am is reserved for God's chosen inheritance. When Moses pleads with God in the wilderness, he prays, "Consider that this nation (goy) is Your people ('am)" (Exodus 33:13). He asks God to view Israel not merely as a geopolitical group of refugees, but as His personal family. Ruth’s story beautifully charts this transition: she abandons her identity as a daughter of the goy of Moab to become a mother in Israel, the 'am of God, eventually appearing in the physical lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.
