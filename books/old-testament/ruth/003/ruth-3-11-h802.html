WOMAN - ( 'ishshah , Strong's H802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ishshah denotes a female human being or a wife. In Ruth 3:11, Boaz uses this word in the idiomatic construction 'eshet chayil ("virtuous woman" or "woman of valor") to describe Ruth. This designation elevates her from a vulnerable, foreign widow to a person of exceptional moral character, strength, and noble standing, placing her on an equal ethical level with Boaz himself, who was previously described as a "man of great wealth" or valor.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun 'ishshah is the feminine counterpart to the masculine 'ish (man).

• The word first appears in Genesis 2:23, where Adam declares, "She shall be called Woman ('ishshah), because she was taken out of Man ('ish)."

• This wordplay establishes a deep ontological relationship, reflecting correspondence, partnership, and shared human nature.

• Throughout the Ancient Near East, a woman's social standing was typically defined by her relationship to a male authority figure (father or husband). Without this connection, an 'ishshah was highly vulnerable to poverty and exploitation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Female human being: Denotes any woman, regardless of age or marital status.

• Wife: Frequently refers to a married woman. The context and the presence of possessive pronouns or genitive constructions determine when "wife" is the intended meaning.

• Female animal: Used occasionally in the context of breeding pairs (e.g., the animals entering Noah's ark).

• Idiomatic modifier: Combined with other nouns to describe character. For example, 'eshet chayil translates to a woman of strength, capability, virtue, or valor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Old Testament portrays 'ishshah in various roles, reflecting the multifaceted reality of female life in biblical times, including mother, sister, widow, prophetess, and queen.

• In wisdom literature, the 'ishshah is used metaphorically. Proverbs contrasts the "foolish woman" ('eshet kesilut) and the "adulterous woman" ('ishshah zarah) with the "virtuous woman" ('eshet chayil).

• The term 'ishshah is also used corporately to describe nations, cities, or Israel itself as the "wife" of Yahweh, emphasizing the covenantal bond and the pain of spiritual unfaithfulness.

4. Theological Implications

• Creation Dignity: The derivation of 'ishshah from 'ish in Genesis 2 affirms that women bear the image of God with equal dignity and value.

• Covenant Inclusion: In the redemptive-historical narrative, God frequently works through women to advance His covenant promises (e.g., Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Leah, Tamar, and Rahab).

• The Character of the Kingdom: The elevation of a foreign, destitute woman to the status of a virtuous matriarch demonstrates that God's covenant blessings are extended on the basis of faith and loyalty rather than ethnicity or social status.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:11, Boaz says, "And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you request, for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman ('eshet chayil)."

• The phrase 'eshet chayil is the exact feminine equivalent of gibbor chayil ("man of great wealth" or "man of valor"), which was used to introduce Boaz in Ruth 2:1.

• By calling Ruth an 'eshet chayil, Boaz publicly declares her to be his moral equal. Her poverty and Moabite heritage do not diminish her standing as a woman of noble character.

• This description serves as a powerful validation. Ruth had risked her reputation by approaching Boaz at the threshing floor. Instead of treating her as a loose woman, Boaz recognizes her loyalty (chesed) and honors her as a woman of ultimate virtue.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:23 - "And Adam said: 'This is now bone of my bones And flesh of my flesh; She shall be called Woman, Because she was taken out of Man.'"

• Proverbs 12:4 - "An excellent wife is the crown of her husband, But she who causes shame is like rottenness in his bones."

• Proverbs 31:10 - "Who can find a virtuous wife? For her worth is far above rubies."

• Proverbs 31:30 - "Charm is deceitful and beauty is passing, But a woman who fears the Lord, she shall be praised."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Literary Structure: In the Hebrew canon (the Tanakh), the Book of Ruth immediately follows the Book of Proverbs. This placement creates a deliberate link: the idealized "virtuous woman" ('eshet chayil) described in Proverbs 31:10-31 is historically personified in Ruth the Moabite.

• Divine Providence: The recognition of Ruth as a virtuous woman sets the stage for her marriage to Boaz, bringing her into the genealogy of King David and ultimately Jesus Christ.
