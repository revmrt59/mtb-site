HOWEVER - ( gam , Strong's H1571 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word gam is a highly versatile particle primarily meaning "also," "indeed," "moreover," or "even." In Ruth 3:12, the NKJV translates it as "however" to capture a crucial pivot in the narrative. While Boaz acknowledges his legal right and emotional willingness to act as Ruth's kinsman-redeemer, this small word introduces a formidable obstacle: another relative possesses a prior legal claim. It represents the tension between human desire and divine order, reminding us that God's redemption operates through perfect righteousness and legal integrity.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The particle gam is an ancient Semitic word used to accumulate, intensify, or contrast clauses. Its origin suggests a gathering or bringing together of facts. It functions like a cognitive hinge. In Hebrew narrative, when a storyteller introduces gam, the listener is forced to hold two distinct realities in tension. In the context of the ancient Israelite family and legal systems, rights and obligations had to be exercised in strict, hierarchical order. The word gam in Ruth 3:12 acts as a sudden roadblock, bringing a beautiful, highly charged midnight proposal into the sober light of covenantal law.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary semantic range of gam involves addition or emphasis:

• Additive: It connects words or clauses to indicate "also," "too," "as well as," or "moreover."

• Emphatic: It intensifies a statement, translating to "indeed," "truly," or "even."

• Adversative/Concessive: When paired with contrasting facts, it functions to qualify a statement, meaning "and yet," "although," or "however."

In Ruth 3:12, the word is prefaced by the conjunction we (wegam), creating a strong adversative transition. Boaz states his status as a redeemer, but immediately adds the complicating fact: "and also/however" there is a closer redeemer.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, gam is used to build rhetorical momentum. It can group people or concepts under a single banner, showing that none are exempt from a specific reality. When used in sequences, it emphasizes the thoroughness of an action—such as when both the righteous and the wicked, or both young and old, experience the same event. It serves as a narrative anchor, ensuring that the listener does not miss the secondary, often more critical, piece of information being added to the scene.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of gam in Boaz’s speech highlights the absolute integrity of true redemption.

• Redemption Requires Righteousness: God’s law cannot be bypassed for emotional convenience or romantic sentimentality. Boaz’s willingness to redeem Ruth must submit to the established order of the kinsman-redeemer law.

• Faith and Order: Godly character does not take shortcuts. Boaz demonstrates that faith trusts God to work through legal, moral, and orderly processes, rather than engineering outcomes through deceit or haste.

• Divine Providence: This obstacle tests and ultimately proves the purity of Boaz’s love and the hand of God in aligning the lineage of David (and ultimately Jesus Christ) with perfect legal and moral uprightness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3, Ruth has taken a massive step of faith by placing herself at the feet of Boaz, essentially asking him to marry her under the levirate and kinsman-redeemer customs. Boaz is deeply moved and pronounces a blessing over her. He promises to do all she asks. Then, at the height of this climactic, hopeful moment, Boaz utters wegam: "however." The word functions as a cold splash of legal reality. It tests the faith of both Ruth and Boaz. By introducing this closer relative, Boaz shows that his love for Ruth is not reckless; it is grounded in a deep reverence for God’s covenantal order.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:6: "She also gave to her husband with her, and he ate."

• Genesis 4:4: "Abel also brought of the firstborn of his flock and of their fat."

• Ruth 1:5: "Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband."

• Ruth 2:21: "Ruth the Moabitess said, 'He also said to me, "You shall keep close to my young men until they have finished all my harvest."'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the narrative structure of the Book of Ruth, the word gam appears frequently to tie the fates of the characters together. In Ruth 1:5, it links the deaths of Mahlon and Chilion. In Ruth 4:5 and 4:10, Boaz uses the word again to declare that in buying the field, he is also acquiring Ruth the Moabitess to perpetuate the name of the dead. The word acts as a thread of inclusion, showing how a foreign, destitute widow is step-by-step integrated ("also" included) into the covenant community and the royal line of Israel.
