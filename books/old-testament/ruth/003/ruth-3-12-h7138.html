CLOSER - ( qarob , Strong's H7138 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word qarob describes relational, spatial, or temporal nearness. In the Book of Ruth, it carries profound covenantal and legal weight, designating a "close relative" who possesses the right and responsibility of redemption. When Boaz acknowledges a relative "closer" than himself, he highlights the strict order of covenantal responsibility, illustrating that redemption requires a personal, legally qualified, and willing redeemer—a truth that ultimately points to Jesus Christ, who drew near to redeem us.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verbal root of qarob is qarab, which means to approach, draw near, or present an offering. In the ancient Near Eastern context, and specifically within the Mosaic covenant, nearness was not merely a matter of geographical proximity but of covenantal obligation. Life was lived in close-knit clan structures where survival depended on the mutual commitment of family members. A "near" relative was designated as a potential protector, avenger, or redeemer (goel). The word picture behind qarob is one of relational warmth and physical accessibility—someone who is close enough to hear a cry for help and possesses the familial right to step into a crisis and provide rescue.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The adjective qarob means near, close, neighboring, or allied. It functions across three primary dimensions:

• Spatial Nearness: Geographically close or neighboring.

• Temporal Nearness: An event or day that is fast approaching or imminent.

• Relational Nearness: Intimate friends or blood relatives who are next of kin.

In Ruth 3:12, the word is used in a comparative sense (mimmenni, "nearer than I") to denote a relative who has a prior, more immediate legal claim and responsibility to redeem Elimelech’s land and marry Ruth.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, qarob paints a vivid picture of accessibility. When applied to God, it signifies His immediate presence and availability to His people, contrasting with the distant, detached deities of the surrounding nations. When applied to family, it represents the protective boundaries of the home and clan. It delineates who has the right to redeem ancestral land, who must care for a destitute family member, and who is permitted to perform levirate marriage duties. To be qarob is to be within the circle of responsibility; it means one cannot ignore the plight of the suffering relative without violating the covenant.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of the kinsman-redeemer (goel) demands relational nearness (qarob). A stranger could not redeem the land or carry on the family name; only a close relative had that right. This reality establishes a major biblical-theological pattern:

• The Order of Redemption: True redemption is orderly and righteous. Boaz does not bypass the law or the rights of the closer relative; he respects the covenantal order, showing that God's redemption operates in perfect harmony with His justice.

• The Incarnation: This Old Testament pattern prefigures the work of Jesus Christ. For God to redeem humanity, He had to become our qarob—our near kinsman. He did not redeem us from a distance, but took on human flesh, becoming like us in every way yet without sin, qualifying Himself to be our ultimate Redeemer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:12, Boaz acknowledges Ruth’s request for redemption but immediately introduces a crucial legal detail: "Now it is true that I am a close relative; however, there is a relative closer than I." The phrase "closer than I" (qarob mimmenni) introduces narrative tension. Boaz is willing to redeem, but he is legally second in line. This test of integrity reveals Boaz's character; he will not manipulate the system or act lawlessly to get what he wants. The existence of the "closer" relative tests whether the redemption of Ruth will occur through human manipulation or divine providence. Ultimately, the closer relative declines due to self-interest, clearing the way for Boaz to act out of sacrificial love.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between the unnamed "closer relative" (Peloni Almoni) and Boaz is highly instructive. Relational proximity (qarob) grants the legal right to redeem, but it does not guarantee the heart to redeem. The closer relative had the physical and legal proximity, but he lacked the covenant love (hesed) required to make a personal sacrifice for Ruth and Naomi. Boaz, though slightly further in physical relation, possessed the willing heart of a redeemer. In Christ, we find both: the ultimate proximity through the Incarnation and the ultimate willing heart through the Cross.
