MORNING - ( boqer , Strong's H1242 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun boqer refers to the dawn, the break of day, or the early morning hours. In the context of Ruth 3:13, it serves as a critical temporal boundary, marking the transition from a night of vulnerability and suspense to a day of legal resolution and redemption. Boaz's command for Ruth to "lie down until morning" is a protective measure safeguarding her reputation and safety, while his promise to act "in the morning" conveys urgency, decisive action, and the dawn of a new hope for her future.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word boqer derives from a root verb meaning to penetrate, break through, or search out. Thus, the morning is pictured as the moment when the first rays of light break through, penetrate, and scatter the darkness of night.

• In ancient Israelite culture, the day ended at sunset, making the night a time of rest but also of vulnerability and potential danger. The morning brought light, safety, and the resumption of public life, especially legal and social activities conducted at the city gates.

• For Ruth, spending the night at the threshing floor of Boaz was an act of extreme vulnerability and faith. The morning represents the transition from the darkness of widowhood, poverty, and isolation to the light of redemption, protection, and belonging.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary definition of boqer is dawn, morning, or the break of day. It indicates the point at which daylight begins, immediately following the end of the night.

• It designates the beginning of the daylight hours, stretching from sunrise until noon.

• In legal, civil, and ritual contexts, the morning represents the designated time for executing justice, performing daily religious sacrifices, and initiating major tasks.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Light conquering darkness: The term is frequently paired with evening (ereb) to frame the completion of a daily cycle, representing order and renewal, as seen in the creation account of Genesis.

• A metaphor for relief and joy: Morning is poetically contrasted with the night of weeping. The arrival of the morning signifies the end of suffering and the sudden arrival of divine favor and joy.

• A time of divine help: The scriptures depict God as delivering His people "at the break of dawn" or "early in the morning," highlighting His timely intervention when human resources are exhausted.

• A time for active obedience: The morning is when righteous individuals rise early to carry out God's commands, showing promptness and zeal rather than delay.

4. Theological Implications

• The guaranteed return of boqer each day reflects the covenant faithfulness of God. His mercies are described as new every morning, establishing a physical, daily reminder that God’s grace is inexhaustible.

• Redemptively, the morning illustrates the transition from the curse to the blessing. The night of exile, grief, and emptiness is replaced by the morning of restoration and redemption.

• The concept of morning underscores the urgency of redemption. Boaz does not delay his legal duties; his immediate action when the sun rises reflects a righteous heart eager to perform the office of kinsman-redeemer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:13, boqer is used twice by Boaz: "in the morning it shall be..." and "Lie down until morning."

• The command to "lie down until morning" is a pastoral and protective act. To send Ruth back to Bethlehem in the middle of the night would expose her to physical harm and compromise her reputation. Boaz establishes the morning as the safe, honorable time for her departure.

• The "morning" functions as the point of decision. Ruth’s future hangs in the balance during the night, but the dawn brings clarity. The suspense of her situation will be resolved legally and publicly at the city gate as soon as the day begins.

• By promising to resolve the matter "in the morning," Boaz relieves Ruth of her anxiety. The assurance of morning-light action allows her to rest in peace, knowing her redeemer will not sleep on his promise.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:5: "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. So the evening and the morning were the first day."

• Psalm 30:5: "For His anger is but for a moment, His favor is life; Weeping may endure for a night, But joy comes in the morning."

• Lamentations 3:22-23: "Through the LORD's mercies we are not consumed, Because His compassions fail not. They are new every morning; Great is Your faithfulness."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The structural movement of the entire Book of Ruth mirrors the transition from night to morning. The narrative begins in the "night" of famine, death, and foreign exile in Moab, but steadily progresses toward the "morning" of harvest, marriage, and the birth of a child who would continue the line of Judah.

• Boaz’s promptness at dawn (Ruth 4:1) demonstrates that God uses active, obedient human agents to bring about the "morning" of His providential plans.
