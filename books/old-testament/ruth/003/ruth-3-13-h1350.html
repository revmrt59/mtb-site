PERFORM THE DUTY - ( ga'al , Strong's H1350 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ga'al means to redeem, rescue, or act as a kinsman-redeemer. In the ancient biblical world, it represents the sacred legal and moral obligation of a close family member to step in and rescue a relative who has fallen into poverty, slavery, or landlessness, as well as to preserve the family line through marriage. In Ruth 3:13, Boaz uses this word repeatedly to describe the familial responsibility of redeeming Ruth and Naomi. This action beautifully illustrates covenant loyalty (chesed), sacrificial love, and the restorative heart of God, ultimately pointing forward to the ultimate redemptive work of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The cultural background of ga'al is rooted in the social safety net established by God in the Law of Moses, particularly in Leviticus 25. In ancient Israel, land was not merely real estate; it was an eternal inheritance granted by God to each family. If a man became poor and had to sell his land or himself into slavery, he lost his heritage and freedom.

The goel (the kinsman-redeemer, a noun derived from ga'al) was the closest living male relative who had the right and the responsibility to step in and pay the ransom price. The redeemer’s duty was threefold:

• To buy back (redeem) the family land that had been sold.

• To buy back a relative who had sold himself into slavery.

• To marry the childless widow of a deceased relative (levirate marriage) to carry on the family name and ensure the widow was cared for.

This background paints a vivid word picture of restoration. The redeemer does not act for personal profit, but out of family loyalty to restore what was lost and bring the helpless back into a state of freedom, security, and honor.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ga'al refers to the act of freeing, reclaiming, or redeeming something or someone by paying a price or fulfilling a familial obligation. The semantic range of the word includes:

• To redeem land, houses, or inherited property.

• To ransom or buy back individuals from servitude.

• To act as a kinsman by marrying a deceased relative's widow.

• To deliver or rescue from distress, exile, or death.

The primary lexical focus of ga'al is the restoration of a original, rightful state. Crucially, this redemption is not performed by a stranger, but exclusively by a close relative (goel) who possesses both the legal right to redeem and the financial resources to do so.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, ga'al is used in both legal and deeply spiritual contexts. The term transitions from a strictly human, legal obligation to a profound metaphor for God's relationship with His people.

• The Shield of the Vulnerable: In legal texts, ga'al acts as a shield protecting the poor from permanent displacement. It ensures that no family in Israel is permanently wiped out by economic misfortune.

• The Deliverer of Israel: God Himself is frequently portrayed as Israel’s goel. When God rescued Israel from Egypt, He did not merely liberate them; He redeemed them as His own family, establishing a covenant relationship.

• A Picture of Resurrection and Life: In the midst of suffering, the term is used to describe a living advocate who will ultimately stand on the earth to vindicate the helpless, turning despair into certain hope.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theology of ga'al provides the foundational blueprint for the New Testament doctrine of redemption. It serves as a direct type and shadow of Jesus Christ as our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer:

• He Became Our Relative: Just as a redeemer had to be a close relative, Jesus took on human flesh and blood (Hebrews 2:14-17) to become our brother so that He might legally redeem us.

• He Paid the Full Price: We were enslaved to sin and had lost our spiritual inheritance. Jesus paid the ultimate redemption price—not with silver or gold, but with His own precious blood (1 Peter 1:18-19).

• He Acted Willingly: Redemption could not be forced upon a kinsman; he had to be willing. Out of sheer grace and love, Jesus willingly laid down His life to redeem us.

• He Restored Our Inheritance: Through Christ, we are not only set free from slavery, but we are also adopted into God's family, receiving a permanent, incorruptible inheritance.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:13, the context is charged with tension, hope, and integrity. Ruth has made a bold, late-night appeal to Boaz at the threshing floor, asking him to spread his cloak over her because he is a close relative (goel). Boaz, deeply moved by her virtue and loyalty, responds by using the verb ga'al multiple times in a single sentence:

• "Stay this night, and in the morning it shall be that if he will perform the duty of a close relative for you—good; let him do it. But if he does not want to perform the duty for you, then I will perform the duty for you, as the LORD lives!"

The repetition of ga'al stresses the absolute seriousness of the legal process. Boaz is a man of covenant integrity. He desires to redeem Ruth, but he acknowledges that there is a closer relative who has the first legal right. Boaz refuses to take shortcuts or bypass God’s law. The intense focus on "performing the duty" reveals that true redemption is not based on mere sentimentality, but on righteousness, covenant order, and sacrificial commitment.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 25:25 - "If one of your brethren becomes poor, and has sold some of his possession, and if his redeeming relative comes to redeem [ga'al] what his brother sold..."

• Job 19:25 - "For I know that my Redeemer [goel / ga'al] lives, and He shall stand at last on the earth;"

• Ruth 4:6 - "And the close relative said, 'I cannot redeem [ga'al] it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance. You redeem [ga'al] my right of redemption for yourself, for I cannot redeem [ga'al] it.'"

• Psalm 103:4 - "Who redeems [ga'al] your life from destruction, who crowns you with lovingkindness and tender mercies;"

• Isaiah 43:1 - "But now, thus says the LORD, who created you, O Jacob, and He who formed you, O Israel: 'Fear not, for I have redeemed [ga'al] you; I have called you by your name; you are Mine.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A critical lexical distinction exists between ga'al and padah (another common Hebrew word for "redeem"). While padah focuses primarily on the payment of a ransom price to transfer ownership (often used of redeeming the firstborn or redeeming from a master, with no requirement of a family relationship), ga'al is strictly relational. It is family-specific.

Furthermore, the legal right of redemption was a choice, not an absolute legal compulsion. As seen in Ruth 4, the closer relative refused to redeem Ruth because marrying her would jeopardize his own family's financial inheritance. This highlights the extraordinary nature of Boaz's love. Boaz was willing to risk his own estate, expend his resources, and bring a foreign Moabite widow into his home out of pure covenant loyalty (chesed). His willingness to perform the duty elevates the story of Ruth from a simple romance to a magnificent display of grace.
