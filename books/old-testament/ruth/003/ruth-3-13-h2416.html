LIVES - ( chay , Strong's H2416 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chay fundamentally denotes being alive, active, and vibrant, standing in stark contrast to death, stagnation, or non-existence. In Ruth 3:13, Boaz employs this word in the solemn oath formula, "as the LORD lives," to guarantee his pledge to act as Ruth's kinsman-redeemer. By binding his promise to the very existence and enduring life of Yahweh, Boaz elevates his commitment from a mere human pledge to an absolute covenantal certainty. This word highlights the active, dynamic, and life-giving nature of God, who is the ultimate guarantor of justice, redemption, and covenant faithfulness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb chayah means to live, have life, or remain alive. The adjective chay evokes images of fresh, flowing water ("living water"), vigorous health, and the active presence of living beings as opposed to lifeless idols or corpses. In the Ancient Near East, where pagan gods were often perceived as dying and rising or bound by fate, Yahweh was uniquely known as the living God. The oath formula "as the LORD lives" reflects a culture where the ultimate guarantee of truth and performance is the eternal, active, and self-sustaining life of the Creator.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word chay carries several layers of meaning depending on its grammatical context:

• Adjectival: Alive, living, active, or vigorous.

• Substantive: Life, lifetime, or a living thing (including animals and beasts).

• Idiomatic: Fresh or flowing (used of water, as in "living water").

• Oath-bound: Used as an indeclinable formula in oaths (literally, "by the life of..."). In the phrase "as the LORD lives" (chay-Yhwh), it asserts that the speaker's promise is as certain as the reality of God's existence.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Freshness and Vitality: Chay describes running or springing water, which is constantly moving and life-giving, unlike stagnant cisterns.

• Physical and Spiritual Recovery: It describes reviving from sickness, returning to life from the dead, or being preserved from danger.

• The Solemn Oath: In the Old Testament, making an oath by saying "as the LORD lives" is the highest possible form of self-imprecation and promise-making. It occurs over 40 times in the Hebrew Bible, signaling absolute resolve and reliability.

4. Theological Implications

• God is the source and definition of life. Unlike the idols of the nations, which have eyes but cannot see and mouths but cannot speak, Yahweh lives, acts, speaks, and intervenes in human history.

• The oath "as the LORD lives" ties human morality and truthfulness directly to the character of God. To break an oath made in the name of the living God is to dishonor His holy name and invite His divine judgment.

• Redemption is an act of life. Boaz's role as a kinsman-redeemer is designed to bring life, continuity, and hope out of the "death" of Elimelech's household.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:13, Boaz finds himself in a highly sensitive and vulnerable situation on the threshing floor. Ruth has made a bold appeal for redemption and marriage. Boaz, recognizing her virtue and the legal steps required, promises to act. However, there is a closer kinsman who has the first right of refusal. To reassure Ruth in this moment of suspense and vulnerability, Boaz swears a solemn oath: "as the LORD lives!" This oath is not casual; it is a binding legal and spiritual pledge. By using chay, Boaz reassures Ruth that her future is secure. His promise to redeem her is as certain as the fact that Yahweh is alive. It transforms a dark, uncertain midnight encounter into a moment of absolute covenant assurance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Judges 8:19: "Then he said, 'They were my brothers, the sons of my mother. As the LORD lives, if you had kept them alive, I would not kill you.'"

• 1 Samuel 14:39: "For as the LORD lives, who delivers Israel, though it be in Jonathan my son, he shall surely die."

• 2 Samuel 15:21: "But Ittai answered the king and said, 'As the LORD lives, and as my lord the king lives, surely in whatever place my lord the king shall be, whether in death or life, even there also your servant will be.'"

• 1 Kings 17:1: "And Elijah the Tishbite, of the inhabitants of Gilead, said to Ahab, 'As the LORD God of Israel lives, before whom I stand, there shall not be dew nor rain these years, except at my word.'"

• Jeremiah 4:2: "And you shall swear, 'As the LORD lives,' in truth, in judgment, and in righteousness; the nations shall bless themselves in Him, and in Him they shall glory."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Hebrew construct of chay in oaths (chay-Yhwh) stands in contrast to the way humans swear by their own lives or the lives of rulers (e.g., Joseph swearing "by the life of Pharaoh" in Genesis 42:15). Swearing by Yahweh's life acknowledges Him as the ultimate, eternal authority who outlives all human empires and kings.

• Boaz’s oath mimics the language of covenantal assurance found throughout Israel’s history, showing his deep piety and alignment with Yahweh's character. His words bring immediate peace to Ruth, allowing her to rest until morning.
