WANT ( chaphets , Strong's 2654 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chaphets carries the deep emotional weight of delighting in, desiring, taking pleasure in, or being pleased with something. In Ruth 3:13, where the NKJV translates it as "want," it denotes a personal inclination, choice, or desire to act. This word moves beyond a mere mechanical decision; it reveals the disposition of the heart. In the context of the kinsman-redeemer relationship, it contrasts the closer relative's lack of heart-felt desire to redeem Ruth with Boaz's eager, loving willingness to fulfill the covenant duty.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root of chaphets is deeply tied to emotional desire and delight. It is not a cold, legalistic determination of the will, but an inclination of the heart. In ancient Semitic contexts, the word represents bending or leaning toward something that pleases you. When a person chaphets, their heart "bends" in the direction of the object of their desire. It is used to describe a man's delight in a woman, God's delight in His people, or a soul's pleasure in wisdom. In the narrative of Ruth, this word picture highlights that redemption is not merely about legal obligation; it requires a heart-inclination—a willing delight to take on the burden of another.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb chaphets means to delight in, desire, or take pleasure in. It denotes a favorable disposition of the mind and heart toward an object or course of action. In a volitional sense, it means to wish, want, or choose based on personal pleasure or inclination. The semantic range spans from "to experience pleasure" (as in having a favorable disposition toward someone) to "to desire/will" (expressing a purposeful intent or choice). In legal and covenantal contexts, it expresses a willing consent or choice. When translated as "want," it indicates a voluntary, heart-driven decision rather than one coerced by outward pressure.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses chaphets in several key ways:

• Delight in people: It expresses a deep romantic or personal attraction, such as a prince's delight in a young woman.

• Divine pleasure: It describes God's pleasure in obedience, justice, and mercy rather than empty ritual sacrifices.

• Sovereign desire: It defines God's sovereign capability to do whatever He pleases in heaven and on earth.

• Willingness in duty: It is used in legal contexts to describe whether an individual has the willing desire to perform a familial covenant duty.

### 4. Theological Implications

Redemption in the Old Testament is a matter of the heart, not just the law. The law provided a framework for the kinsman-redeemer (goel), but it could not force a man's heart to delight in the sacrifice required. Boaz represents the true, willing redeemer. While the closer relative has the legal priority, he lacks the chaphets (the willing delight) to redeem because it might jeopardize his own inheritance. This highlights a major theological truth: God's redemption of His people is not done grudgingly or out of cold duty, but out of deep, loving delight. Ultimately, Boaz points to Jesus Christ, our ultimate Goel, who willingly laid down His life, delighting to do the Father's will to redeem us.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:13, Boaz uses the term on the threshing floor. He tells Ruth that if the closer relative "does not want to perform the duty" for her, then Boaz will do it. The use of chaphets here is crucial. The law of levirate-like redemption explicitly uses this term: "if the man does not want to take his brother's wife..." By using this specific word, Boaz is referencing the exact legal language of Deuteronomy, preparing for the formal legal proceedings at the city gate in chapter 4. It shows that redemption must be a voluntary act of grace and love. The closer relative's refusal is a failure of chaphets (heart-desire), which paves the way for Boaz to step in with his overwhelming chaphets for Ruth.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 25:7 - "But if the man does not want to take his brother's wife, then let his brother's wife go up to the gate..."

• 1 Samuel 15:22 - "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, As in obeying the voice of the LORD?"

• Psalm 40:8 - "I delight to do Your will, O my God, And Your law is within my heart."

• Psalm 115:3 - "But our God is in heaven; He does whatever He pleases."

• Isaiah 53:10 - "Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise Him; He has put Him to grief."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between the two kinsmen is a contrast of desires. The unnamed relative desires to protect his own inheritance; his chaphets is self-directed. Boaz’s chaphets is outward-facing, directed toward God's covenant and love for Ruth and Naomi. In the Septuagint (LXX), chaphets is often translated using thelo (to wish, will, desire) or eudokeo (to be well-pleased, delight in). In Ruth 3:13, the Greek translation uses thelo ("and if he does not wish/will..."). This underlines that redemption is a matter of the human and divine will aligned in love.
