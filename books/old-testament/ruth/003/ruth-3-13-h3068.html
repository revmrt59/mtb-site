LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh (LORD) is the personal, covenant-keeping name of the God of Israel, revealing His eternal self-existence, absolute faithfulness, and relational presence. In Ruth 3:13, Boaz invokes this sacred name in a solemn oath ("as the LORD lives") to guarantee his commitment to redeem Ruth if the nearer kinsman declines. This oath transforms a human promise of marriage and redemption into a covenantal obligation bound to the character and life of God Himself, assuring Ruth of ultimate security under the wings of Israel's faithful God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The name Yahweh is the Tetragrammaton (YHWH), the sacred four-letter name of God considered too holy by later Jewish tradition to be spoken aloud.

• It is fundamentally connected to the Hebrew verb hayah ("to be"), famously revealed to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus 3:14 as "I AM WHO I AM."

• Unlike generic titles for deity like Elohim (which emphasizes power and majesty), Yahweh is God's unique, personal name. It paints a picture of a God who is self-existent, unchanging, and intimately present with His people. It is the signature of the God who enters into history, binds Himself to covenants, and reveals Himself to humanity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The name signifies the eternal, self-existent One who has life in Himself and is the source of all life.

• It denotes the covenant God of Israel, representing His absolute faithfulness, mercy, grace, and unchanging nature.

• The name implies active presence: "He who is" or "He who brings into being." It is not a passive philosophical concept of existence, but an active, dynamic presence—the God who is near, hears the cries of His people, and acts on their behalf.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Tetragrammaton occurs over 6,800 times in the Old Testament, making it the most frequent name for God.

• It is used in covenantal contexts, frequently paired with descriptions of God's lovingkindness (chesed) and truth (emet).

• A prominent word picture associated with Yahweh is that of a Husband or Redeemer to Israel, emphasizing deep, relational intimacy and legal protection.

• In oaths, the phrase "as the LORD lives" (chay-Yahweh) serves as the ultimate legal and moral guarantee. Since God is the source of all life and cannot die or lie, binding an oath to His life makes the fulfillment of the promise as certain as God's own existence.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Faithfulness: Yahweh is the God who keeps His promises even when His people are unfaithful. The entire narrative of Ruth unfolds under the silent but pervasive sovereignty of Yahweh, who visits His people to give them bread (Ruth 1:6) and ultimately provides a redeemer.

• Self-Existence and Sovereignty: Because Yahweh is the self-existent One, He is dependent on nothing, yet all of creation depends on Him. He rules over the seemingly "chance" events of life, such as Ruth happening to glean in Boaz's field.

• Personal and Relational Nature: Yahweh is not a distant, abstract force. He is a God who can be known, who interacts with individuals like Ruth (a foreign Moabite) and Boaz, and who cares about the preservation of family lines and the plight of widows.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:13, Boaz says, "But if he does not want to perform that duty for you, then I will perform that duty for you, as the LORD lives!"

• The setting is the threshing floor at night. Ruth has taken a vulnerable step, asking Boaz to spread his cloak over her—a symbolic request for marriage and redemption under the kinsman-redeemer (goel) law.

• Boaz's response is an immediate, binding commitment. By invoking the name Yahweh in the oath "as the LORD lives," Boaz elevates his promise above a mere human agreement. He places his honor, his property, and his life on the line before the covenant God of Israel.

• This oath provides absolute assurance to Ruth. As a Moabite stranger, she has no legal standing or leverage. However, Boaz's invocation of Yahweh assures her that her redemption is secure, because Boaz fears the LORD and will not violate an oath made in His name.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Judges 8:19: "Then he said, 'They were my brothers, the sons of my mother. As the LORD lives, if you had kept them alive, I would not kill you.'"

• 1 Samuel 14:39: "For, as the LORD lives, who saves Israel, though it be in Jonathan my son, he shall surely die."

• Jeremiah 4:2: "And you shall swear, 'The LORD lives,' In truth, in judgment, and in righteousness; The nations shall bless themselves in Him, And in Him they shall glory."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The book of Ruth transitions the concept of God's covenant loyalty (chesed) from an abstract theological idea into flesh-and-blood human actions. Boaz acts as the hands and feet of Yahweh. When Boaz prays in Ruth 2:12 that Ruth would find refuge "under whose wings you have come to trust," he becomes the answer to his own prayer in Ruth 3:9 when he spreads his wings (cloak) over her. His oath in Ruth 3:13 by the name of Yahweh seals this divine-human partnership.
