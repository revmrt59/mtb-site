STAY - ( lûn , Strong's H3885 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb lûn primarily means to lodge, spend the night, or abide in a specific place. In the context of ancient Near Eastern hospitality and covenantal protection, it carries a deep sense of seeking temporary refuge and safe rest under another's guardianship. In Ruth 3:13, Boaz uses this command to offer Ruth immediate safety during the vulnerable hours of the night, signaling his honorable intentions and his commitment to act as her kinsman-redeemer once legal matters are resolved in the morning.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient agrarian culture of Israel, travel or exposure after dark was fraught with danger from wild beasts, bandits, and extreme temperatures. Finding a place to "lodge" (lûn) was not merely a matter of convenience but of survival. The word evokes the image of a weary traveler tarrying in a safe shelter or home for the duration of the darkness until the dawn breaks. It represents a temporary halt in a journey where one completely entrusts their safety to the host. In the book of Ruth, threshing floors were active, public, and potentially dangerous places at night for an unprotected young foreign woman. Boaz's instruction for Ruth to stay is an act of protective hospitality and moral righteousness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root verb lûn (also written as lîn) literally means to lodge, pass the night, or remain overnight. The primary definition denotes spending the night in a specific location, contrasting with daytime travel or activity. In figurative contexts, it can mean to abide, dwell permanently, or settle. The term is distinct from other Hebrew verbs for dwelling permanently (such as yashab or shakan); lûn specifically emphasizes the overnight duration, the transition of time from evening to morning, and the vulnerability associated with the night.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• lûn is frequently used to describe weary travelers seeking a place to sleep under a protective roof.

• It paints a picture of transition—surviving the dark hours of the night under a protective covering to face the morning with renewed strength.

• In poetic literature, it describes animals making their nests or resting in wild places, trusting their natural environments for overnight shelter.

• The term is also used in a secondary sense to describe the physical resting of inanimate things, such as a sacrifice that must not remain overnight until the morning.

4. Theological Implications

The word highlights the theme of divine and human stewardship of the vulnerable. Just as God offers ultimate shelter to His people, godly individuals like Boaz reflect Yahweh's character by providing safe lodging (lûn) to the stranger and the widow. In the unfolding narrative of redemption, "staying the night" represents a critical, suspenseful pause before the morning brings redemption and resolution. It teaches the spiritual posture of waiting in safety and trust when the final outcome is not yet fully visible. It also underscores the sacred duty of hospitality in Israel's covenant law, where providing safe shelter to a traveler is an act of righteousness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:13, Boaz tells Ruth, "Stay this night, and in the morning it shall be..." Ruth had come to the threshing floor under the cover of darkness, risking her reputation and physical safety to propose marriage and redemption to Boaz. By commanding her to lûn ("stay"), Boaz protects her from the physical dangers of traveling back to the city in the pitch black of night. This command demonstrates Boaz's high moral character. Instead of taking advantage of her presence or dismissively sending her away into danger, he provides her with a secure place to rest until dawn, ensuring that she leaves before she can be recognized, thereby preserving her honor and his own. The command serves as a reassuring promise: she is safe under his care for the night while he prepares to settle the legal requirements of redemption first thing in the morning.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A structural and thematic connection exists between Ruth 1:16 and Ruth 3:13. In the first chapter, Ruth committed to "lodging" (lûn) with Naomi in poverty and exile. Now, in the third chapter, Boaz invites Ruth to "lodge" (lûn) under his protection as she prepares to enter the household of her redeemer. Furthermore, the transition from night (lûn) to morning is a recurring biblical motif representing the transition from sorrow to joy, from danger to deliverance. The overnight stay is the final period of waiting before the fulfillment of God's promise.
