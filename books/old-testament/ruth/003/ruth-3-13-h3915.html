NIGHT - ( laylah , Strong's H3915 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word laylah refers to the period of darkness between sunset and sunrise. In the context of Ruth 3:13, it designates a specific, critical night of waiting and protection under Boaz's care at the threshing floor, serving as a literal setting that transitions into a symbolic dawn of redemption and hope.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, the night was a time of vulnerability, danger, and uncertainty. Without modern lighting, the darkness of night suspended normal labor and travel, forcing individuals to seek shelter.

• Culturally, spending the night at a threshing floor—a public, outdoor location associated with harvest celebration—carried significant risk to a woman's reputation and physical safety.

• In this setting, the night represents a boundary period, a time of hiddenness and transition where crucial decisions of life, protection, and covenantal commitment are made in secret before being executed in the light of day.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Laylah means night, nighttime, or the dark hours of the day. It is used in contrast to yom (day) and designates the temporal period from sunset to sunrise.

• It can also refer metaphorically to adversity, distress, or a period of spiritual or physical darkness.

• In the context of Ruth 3:13, it is used in its literal, temporal sense, indicating the specific overnight period that Ruth must remain at the threshing floor for her safety before Boaz can legally resolve the matter of redemption in the morning.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word occurs over 200 times in the Hebrew Bible.

• Literal night: Marking time, cycles of evening and morning, as seen from the creation account forward.

• A place of dreams and divine revelation: God often speaks to His people during the night.

• A symbol of adversity and sorrow: Night is a time of weeping, waiting, and seeking God's presence amidst trouble.

• A cover for danger or stealth: Night provides anonymity, sometimes for protection and sometimes for deceitful deeds.

4. Theological Implications

• Theologically, the night is not outside of God's sovereignty; He created both the day and the night.

• In the economy of redemption, the night is often the crucible of faith. It is the dark interval where God's promises are believed in the absence of visible sight. Abraham counted the stars at night; Jacob wrestled with the angel until dawn; the Israelites were redeemed out of Egypt during the night of Passover.

• In Ruth, the night at the threshing floor is a microcosm of Israel's dark period during the time of the Judges. It represents a period of waiting under the shadow of the Almighty's wings, trusting that the dawn will bring the redeemer's active deliverance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:13, Boaz commands Ruth, "Stay this night." This instruction has two profound nuances: safety and legal rectitude.

• First, sending Ruth home in the middle of the dark night would expose her to physical assault or public scandal. Retaining her until morning protects her honor and life.

• Second, it signifies a holy pause. The night is a time of resting in hope. Ruth has made her petition for redemption; Boaz accepts the responsibility but must respect the legal rights of the closer kinsman. "Stay this night" is an invitation to rest in the assurance that the redeemer is at work, preparing to resolve the crisis as soon as the day breaks.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 12:42 - "It is a night of solemn observance to the LORD for bringing them out of the land of Egypt. This is that night of the LORD, a solemn observance for all the children of Israel throughout their generations."

• Psalm 19:2 - "Day unto day utters speech, and night unto night reveals knowledge."

• Isaiah 21:11 - "The burden against Dumah. He calls to me out of Seir, 'Watchman, what of the night? Watchman, what of the night?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Ancient Israelites divided the night into three watches: the first watch (sunset to 10:00 PM), the middle watch (10:00 PM to 2:00 AM), and the morning watch (2:00 AM to sunrise).

• Ruth's departure "before one could recognize another" (Ruth 3:14) suggests she left during the transition from the morning watch to dawn, maximizing her safety and preserving her reputation.
