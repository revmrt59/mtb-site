DOWN - ( shakab , Strong's H7901 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shakab fundamentally means to lie down, rest, or sleep. In the context of the Ancient Near East, this word carries physical, relational, and covenantal significance depending on its setting. In Ruth 3:13, Boaz’s command to Ruth to "lie down until morning" is a highly charged but ultimately protective and honorable directive. It represents a vulnerable transition from suspenseful petition to restful trust under the wing of a potential kinsman-redeemer, maintaining strict moral boundaries while securing her safety through the night.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root shakab denotes the physical posture of reclining or prostrating oneself, whether for sleep, rest, illness, or death. In ancient agricultural and pastoral societies, lying down to sleep was the ultimate expression of safety, vulnerability, and trust. To lie down in peace required protection from predators, thieves, and enemies. Conversely, shakab is also the common euphemism for sexual intimacy ("to lie with"). In Ruth 3, the threshing floor setting activates both potential meanings. It is a place of vulnerability and potential scandal, yet under Boaz’s righteous stewardship, it becomes a sanctuary of covenantal protection and rest.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shakab occurs over 200 times in the Old Testament. Its primary semantic range includes:

• To lie down for sleep or physical rest.

• To lie sick or bedridden.

• To lie down in death, frequently expressed in the historical books as "sleeping with one's fathers."

• To engage in sexual relations, typically marked by the addition of the prepositions "with" ('eth or 'im).

In Ruth 3:13, the verb is used in the Qal imperative form (shikbi, "lie down!"). Boaz uses it here not in a sexual sense, but as a protective command. It is an instruction to remain resting in a safe position under his care until the break of day, avoiding the dangers of traveling in the dark.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Rest and Security: Lying down symbolizes peaceful security under divine protection. When God's people are obedient, they are promised the ability to lie down without fear.

• Vulnerability and Petition: Lying down at someone’s feet is an act of total submission and petition. Ruth's physical posture of lying down at Boaz's feet was a silent, symbolic plea for him to spread his cloak over her—a request for marriage and redemption.

• Covenantal Loyalty: By commanding her to lie down until morning, Boaz accepts the responsibility of her protection for the night, signaling his commitment to resolve her legal and marital status.

4. Theological Implications

The use of shakab in Ruth 3 highlights the theme of redemption and God’s providence through righteous human agents. Boaz’s response to Ruth’s vulnerable posture demonstrates the character of a true redeemer. Instead of exploiting her vulnerability, Boaz protects her reputation and physical safety, commanding her to rest until morning. This prefigures the spiritual rest and security believers find in Jesus Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, who invites the weary and heavy-laden to find rest in Him. It shows how covenant righteousness transforms a potentially compromised situation into a holy transaction of grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:13, the setting is highly sensitive. Ruth has approached Boaz at night on the threshing floor—a location often associated with harvesting and wild celebrations. When Boaz discovers her at his feet, he acknowledges her right of redemption but notes there is a closer relative. He commands her to "Lie down until morning." Here, the nuance is strictly protective. Boaz prevents her from traveling in the dark, which would be dangerous and raise suspicion, while ensuring nothing improper occurs. The text emphasizes this purity in verse 14, noting she lay (shakab) at his feet until morning but rose before anyone could recognize her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 28:11: "So he came to a certain place and stayed there all night, because the sun had set. And he took one of the stones of that place and put it at his head, and he lay down in that place to sleep."

• Deuteronomy 6:7: "You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, and when you rise up."

• Leviticus 26:6: "I will give peace in the land, and you shall lie down, and no one will make you afraid; I will rid the land of evil beasts, and the sword will not go through your land."

• Psalm 4:8: "I will both lie down in peace, and sleep; For You alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural symmetry of Ruth 3 is striking. Ruth's act of lying down (shakab) begins in verse 7 when she quietly uncovers Boaz's feet and lies down. Boaz's command in verse 13 to "lie down until morning" mirrors her initial action but elevates it to an act of obedience under his protection. The contrast between shakab used for holy rest versus sinful indulgence is stark throughout Scripture. While others in Israel's history used their strength to exploit the vulnerable, Boaz uses his position to guard the resting place of a destitute Moabite widow, demonstrating the law of kindness (chesed).
