KNOWN - ( yada , Strong's H3045 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yada means more than intellectual assent or the possession of information; it denotes experiential knowledge, relational intimacy, and deep personal awareness. In the context of Ruth 3:14, the passive form of the verb is used by Boaz to safeguard Ruth's reputation, expressing his desire that her presence at the threshing floor remain undisclosed and unexposed to public scrutiny. This highlights the protective care, discretion, and moral integrity that characterize Boaz as a worthy redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, "knowing" is not an abstract, purely cognitive exercise as it often is in Western, Greek-influenced philosophy. Instead, to know is to experience, to observe, to care for, or to have a personal, active relationship with someone or something. It is a word of involvement. The background of Ruth 3:14 involves the vulnerability of a young widow and the honor of a noble landowner. A threshing floor at night was a place of grain protection, but also a place prone to moral compromise and crime. If Ruth's nighttime visit to Boaz were to become common knowledge, her character would be instantly assassinated by local gossip, and Boaz's ability to act as a righteous kinsman-redeemer would be severely compromised.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of yada is to know, to learn to know, to perceive, or to become acquainted with. It encompasses cognitive knowing, experiential knowing, and relational knowing. In the passive stem (Niphal) used in Ruth 3:14, the verb means to be made known, to be revealed, to be discovered, or to become public information. It indicates a transition from secrecy and privacy to exposure and public awareness.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb yada is used in several distinct ways throughout the Old Testament scriptures:

• Intimate Union: It describes the physical and spiritual union between a husband and wife, denoting a deep, exclusive, and relational knowledge of one another (Genesis 4:1).

• Experiential Acquaintance: It refers to experiencing circumstances directly, such as the Messiah being "acquainted with grief" (Isaiah 53:3).

• Covenantal Selection: It expresses God’s sovereign choice and intimate care for His people, as seen when God tells Israel that He has "known" only them out of all the families of the earth (Amos 3:2).

• Passive Revelation: In the Niphal stem, it points to secrets being uncovered or identities being made manifest, such as Joseph making himself known to his brothers (Genesis 45:1).

4. Theological Implications

The biblical concept of yada reveals that faith, covenant, and righteousness are deeply relational. God does not merely know facts about His people; He knows them personally and intimately. In the context of human relationships, yada carries moral weight. In Ruth 3:14, the desire to prevent Ruth's presence from being "known" illustrates the theological value of discretion, wisdom, and the preservation of a good name (Proverbs 22:1). Boaz’s actions reflect the protective nature of God Himself, who covers the vulnerable, shields the innocent from slander, and acts with absolute moral integrity behind closed doors before executing His public plans of redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:14, the text records that Ruth lay at Boaz's feet until morning and arose before one could recognize (yakar) another. Boaz then says, "Let it not be known (yada) that the woman came to the threshing floor." There is a distinct contrast here between immediate visual recognition (yakar) and widespread public knowledge (yada). Boaz ensures that Ruth leaves in the dim light of dawn so no individual can identify her visually, and he commands silence so that the event does not become a matter of public gossip. This demonstrates Boaz’s wisdom; he wants to secure her redemption lawfully and honorably at the city gate without the interference of public scandal or premature rumors.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:1 - "Now Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived and bore Cain..."

• Psalm 1:6 - "For the LORD knows the way of the righteous, But the way of the ungodly shall perish."

• Hosea 13:5 - "I knew you in the wilderness, In the land of great drought."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The passive use of yada in Ruth 3:14 serves as a literary turning point. Up to this moment in the narrative, the plans of Naomi and Ruth have been carried out in secret. By keeping the encounter "unknown" to the public for a few more hours, Boaz is able to control the narrative and present the legal case for redemption to the closer relative in an orderly, honorable, and legally binding manner, demonstrating that true godly love protects the reputation of the beloved.
