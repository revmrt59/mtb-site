FEET - ( marge-lah , Strong's H4772 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun marge-lah refers specifically to the "place of the feet" or the "foot-end" of a bed or resting place. In the beautiful narrative of Ruth, it designates the precise location where Ruth positioned herself at Boaz's threshing floor. This term carries rich cultural, social, and spiritual significance, highlighting a posture of ultimate submission, vulnerability, and faith. Rather than carrying illicit or suggestive undertones, Ruth's presence at the marge-lah of Boaz was a formal, culturally understood appeal for protection, redemption, and marriage under the covenant of the kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word marge-lah is a denominative noun derived from the common Hebrew root regel, which means "foot." While regel refers to the physical foot itself, marge-lah refers to the space, direction, or location where the feet are situated. In the ancient Near East, the threshing floor was a place of hard labor, and during the harvest, owners would sleep at the foot-end of their grain heaps to guard their hard-earned crop from thieves. To lie down at someone's marge-lah was to occupy the lowest, most vulnerable position near them. It was the place reserved for servants waiting on their masters. By coming quietly in the night and lying at the place of Boaz's feet, Ruth adopted the posture of a humble handmaid, placing her life, future, and reputation entirely in the hands of her potential redeemer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Marge-lah means the "foot-place," "the space at the feet," or "the lower extremities." It is used almost exclusively in the plural form (margeloth) in the narrative of Ruth to denote the foot-end of Boaz’s makeshift bed on the threshing floor. It differs from the simple word for "feet" by emphasizing the location or the physical boundary of the sleeping area. Lexically, it describes the physical point of contact where one would approach another person who is reclining, representing a position of petition and subordination.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term occurs five times in the Hebrew Bible, four of which are found in the third chapter of Ruth. This high concentration highlights its central role in the narrative climax of the book.

• In the book of Ruth, the word picture is one of covenant seeking. Uncovering the feet of the sleeping redeemer was a symbolic gesture. It was a silent, dramatic plea for Boaz to "spread his wings" (or the corner of his garment) over her, a direct wordplay on her seeking refuge under the wings of the Lord God of Israel.

• In its single occurrence outside of Ruth (Daniel 10:6), the word is used to describe the lower limbs or feet of a majestic, heavenly being. Here, the word picture shifts from a place of humble submission to one of strength and stability, as the being's marge-lah glows like burnished bronze, representing pure, unshakeable foundation and authority.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, marge-lah illustrates the beauty of redemptive faith. Ruth, a destitute Gentile widow, had no legal claim on Boaz except through the grace of the levirate and kinsman-redeemer laws. Her posture at his feet is a physical manifestation of saving faith. She does not demand her rights; she submits to his authority and awaits his decision. This serves as a powerful Old Testament picture of how a sinner approaches Jesus Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer. We come to the "place of His feet" in absolute humility, trusting in His righteous character to cover our nakedness, redeem our lost inheritance, and bring us into a covenant relationship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:14, the text notes that "she lay at his feet until morning." The specific use of marge-lah here emphasizes both protection and purity. The threshing floor was often associated with pagan harvest festivals and immorality, yet the narrator uses marge-lah to show that Ruth remained strictly at the foot-end of the bed, maintaining physical and moral boundaries. Boaz’s response in the morning confirms this purity, as he pronounces a blessing upon her and immediately takes steps to fulfill his legal duties. Lying at his feet "until morning" highlights a night of quiet waiting and trust in the character of the redeemer, culminating in the assurance of redemption at dawn.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 3:4: "Then it shall be, when he lies down, that you shall notice the place where he lies, and you shall go in, uncover his feet, and lie down; and he will tell you what you should do."

• Ruth 3:7: "And after Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was cheerful, he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain; and she came softly, uncovered his feet, and lay down."

• Ruth 3:8: "Now it happened at midnight that the man was startled, and turned himself; and there, a woman was lying at his feet."

• Daniel 10:6: "His body was like beryl, his face like the appearance of lightning, his eyes like torches of fire, his arms and feet like burnished bronze in color, and the sound of his words like the voice of a multitude."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern legal and cultural customs, the foot was associated with ownership and authority. To place one's foot on a piece of land meant to claim it; conversely, to lie at someone's feet was to acknowledge their ownership and protective lordship. When Ruth lay at Boaz's marge-lah, she was actively surrendering her autonomy to him, asking him to claim her as his own. The absolute silence and darkness of the setting, contrasted with the integrity of both characters, turns what could have been a scene of scandal into a holy sanctuary of covenant love.
