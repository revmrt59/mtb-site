RECOGNIZE - ( nakar , Strong's H5234 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nakar primarily means to recognize, acknowledge, or pay special attention to someone or something. In the context of Ruth 3:14, the word refers to the physical act of identifying another person in the dim light of early dawn. However, this literal sensory recognition is deeply intertwined with the theme of relational recognition and redemption throughout the narrative. By rising before anyone could "recognize" another, Ruth and Boaz preserve her reputation and secure the integrity of the upcoming legal redemption, illustrating how godly wisdom and discretion protect the vulnerable.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root nakar has a unique semantic range in biblical Hebrew. It contains a fascinating tension: in some verb stems, it means to make oneself unrecognizable or act as a stranger, while in the Hiphil stem (used in Ruth 3:14), it means to recognize, distinguish, or take notice of. The concrete word picture behind nakar involves looking closely at a person or object to identify its distinctive markings or features. It is the visual and mental process of distinguishing one thing from another, transforming someone from an unknown stranger into an acknowledged person of significance.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb nakar means to recognize, discern, distinguish, or acknowledge. It refers to the cognitive process of identifying someone based on prior knowledge or close inspection. In the Hiphil stem, the verb denotes the active identification of a person or object by observing their distinctive traits. It also carries the broader, non-physical meaning of paying regard to, showing favor to, or declaring legal recognition of someone.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, nakar is used in several key contexts:

• Physical Identification: It describes recognizing someone's physical appearance or clothing, such as Jacob recognizing Joseph's blood-stained tunic, or Joseph's brothers failing to recognize him in his Egyptian attire.

• Legal and Forensic Proof: It is used in situations requiring identification of evidence, such as Tamar presenting Judah's signet, cord, and staff for him to identify and claim as his own.

• Relational Regard: It describes a person of status choosing to acknowledge or take notice of someone of lower status, elevating them from the status of an outsider to an insider.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theological significance lies in the connection between recognition and covenant responsibility. To "recognize" someone is to acknowledge a relationship with them that demands action. When God or a godly leader "recognizes" the needy, it results in deliverance and favor. Conversely, the scriptures warn judges against "recognizing faces" (showing partiality or favoritism) in judgment, as justice must remain blind to social status. In the context of redemption, nakar represents the crucial first step where the redeemer acknowledges the plight and identity of the one needing redemption.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:14, the word nakar is used as a physical and moral boundary. Ruth has spent the night at Boaz’s feet on the threshing floor, asking him to act as her kinsman-redeemer. She rises early in the morning, "before one could recognize another."

• Physical Protection: The physical darkness of twilight prevents the townspeople from identifying Ruth, protecting her from false accusations and preserving her spotless reputation as a virtuous woman.

• Structural Wordplay: This physical non-recognition stands in sharp contrast to the relational recognition she receives from Boaz. In Ruth 2:10, Ruth asked why Boaz had "taken notice" (nakar) of her, a "foreigner" (nokriyah—a word sharing the same root). Boaz’s willingness to recognize her worth paves the way for their redemption. Thus, the temporary lack of physical recognition in the dark of the threshing floor ensures that their eventual public, legal recognition at the city gate can proceed without scandal.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The relationship between nakar (to recognize) and nekar (stranger/foreign) is a key literary theme in the Book of Ruth. It highlights a profound spiritual truth: God's grace transforms the "unrecognized stranger" (nokriyah) into one who is highly "regarded" (nakar). Boaz, acting as a type of Christ, does not look past the foreign widow; instead, he chooses to recognize her loyalty, faith, and worth, bringing her into the family of Israel and the lineage of the Messiah.
