LAY - ( shakab , Strong's H7901 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shakab fundamentally means to lie down, recline, or rest. In the narrative of Ruth 3:14, it describes Ruth lying at the feet of Boaz on the threshing floor. Far from carrying any immoral connotation in this specific scene, the word underscores Ruth's vulnerable, submissive, and honorable petition for Boaz to act as her kinsman-redeemer. It represents a bold act of faith and covenant loyalty, demonstrating total reliance on God's provision while maintaining complete moral integrity.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of shakab refers to the physical action of reclining or resting the body horizontally on a surface. In the ancient agrarian world, lying down was associated with the end of labor, the search for safety during the night, or seeking shelter from the elements. To lie down in a specific location often communicated one's state of vulnerability, dependence, or trust in the owner or protector of that space. In a cultural context where nocturnal safety was paramount, choosing where and next to whom one lay down was a decision of immense social and personal significance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shakab is a primary Hebrew root occurring over 200 times in the Old Testament. Its primary semantic ranges include:

• To lie down for sleep, rest, or relaxation.

• To recline physically due to sickness, weakness, or exhaustion.

• To lie down in death, frequently captured in the historical books by the idiomatic phrase "slept with his fathers."

• To lie down with someone in a sexual sense, used euphemistically for intimate relations.

• To rest or remain in a state of inactivity or prostration.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs shakab to paint several vivid word pictures:

• Covenant Peace: Lying down without fear is a physical picture of divine protection and security, where God guards His people so thoroughly that their sleep is undisturbed by predators or enemies.

• Humility and Mourning: Lying in the dust or on the ground represents a state of deep grief, repentance, or utter helplessness before God.

• The Sleep of Death: The grave is depicted as a place where the weary lie down to rest from their earthly labors, awaiting the ultimate purposes of God.

4. Theological Implications

In the broader biblical narrative, shakab often acts as a pivot point for moral testing. For instance, the same verb describes the illicit encounters of Lot's daughters in Genesis and Joseph's temptation by Potiphar's wife. However, in the book of Ruth, the Holy Spirit deliberately redeems this terminology. By placing herself at Boaz's feet under the cover of night, Ruth risks her reputation and safety. The theological weight of shakab here shifts from potential temptation to a profound expression of covenant faith. Ruth seeks shelter under the "wings" of Boaz, who serves as the human instrument of Yahweh's wings of protection.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:14, the use of shakab is carefully qualified by the spatial preposition "at his feet" (margetotaw). The text notes that "she lay at his feet until morning." This detail is crucial; she did not lie beside him as a wife or concubine, but at his feet as a servant appealing to her master. This posture maintains strict moral boundaries. The verse further emphasizes that "she arose before one could recognize another," highlighting Boaz’s protective care to guard her honor and prevent any false appearances of impropriety. The use of shakab in this context showcases a holy, strategic, and legally minded appeal for redemption, completely devoid of the moral compromise often associated with the term in other nocturnal narratives.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 28:11 - "So he came to a certain place and stayed there all night, because the sun had set. And he took one of the stones of that place and put it at his head, and he lay down in that place to sleep."

• Deuteronomy 6:7 - "You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, and when you rise up."

• 1 Kings 19:5 - "Then as he lay and slept under a broom tree, suddenly an angel touched him, and said to him, 'Arise and eat.'"

• Psalm 4:8 - "I will both lie down in peace, and sleep; For You alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful literary contrast between Ruth's holy use of shakab and the history of her people, the Moabites. The Moabite nation originated from a deceptive, incestuous act of "lying down" (shakab) initiated by Lot's eldest daughter (Genesis 19:32-35). By contrast, Ruth, a Moabite widow, operates in broad daylight and honorable darkness. She utilizes the same physical posture of lying down (shakab) not to exploit or deceive, but to initiate an honorable, lawful covenant relationship that ultimately leads to the lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.
