WOMAN - ( 'ishshah , Strong's H802 )

Summary:

In the narrative of Ruth 3:14, the Hebrew word 'ishshah refers to Ruth herself, positioned at a critical crossroad of vulnerability and redemption. Used by Boaz to protect her reputation, the term highlights both her gender and her social vulnerability as a widow seeking shelter under the wings of Israel's God. Boaz's protective instruction—that no one should know "the woman" came to the threshing floor—guards her hard-won reputation as a virtuous woman, ensuring that her bold act of faith is not misconstrued as moral compromise.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'ishshah is the fundamental term for a female human being, occurring over 700 times in the Old Testament. Its linguistic origin is deeply connected to 'ish (man). In Genesis 2:23, the first man declares that she shall be called "woman" ('ishshah) because she was taken out of "man" ('ish). This linguistic mirroring conveys a profound sense of correspondence, equality, partnership, and complementary relationship. In the ancient Near East, a woman’s identity was heavily defined by her relationship to a household—first to her father, then to her husband. To be an 'ishshah without a husband or father (a widow) was to occupy one of the most defenseless positions in society, reliant entirely on the covenant faithfulness of the community and the grace of God.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term 'ishshah has a straightforward and consistent semantic range:

• Female human being / woman: A general designation of gender, distinguishing a female from a male.

• Wife: When used with a possessive pronoun or in a construct state (e.g., "his woman"), it denotes a married woman or spouse.

• Each / Every (idiomatic): In certain grammatical constructions, it can mean "each one" or "every one" when paired with distributive phrases.

In the context of Ruth 3:14, the word is used with the definite article (ha'ishshah, "the woman") to denote a specific, individual female—namely, Ruth—in her singular identity as a person of worth and vulnerability.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Scriptures, 'ishshah is used in rich idiomatic and poetic ways to describe character and status:

• The Virtuous Woman ('eshet chayil): Described in Proverbs 31 and applied directly to Ruth in Ruth 3:11. This word picture portrays a woman of strength, capability, moral integrity, and spiritual devotion.

• The Foolish/Adulterous Woman: Portrayed in Proverbs as a contrast to wisdom, using her speech and beauty to lead others astray.

• The Barren Woman: Often a figure of deep sorrow who becomes a recipient of miraculous divine grace, showcasing God’s power to bring life out of emptiness (e.g., Sarah, Hannah, and figuratively, Israel).

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the use of 'ishshah in the Old Testament highlights the dignity God bestows upon women. In a historical context where women were often treated as property, the Hebrew Scriptures consistently depict God as the defender of the widow, the orphan, and the stranger.

In the Book of Ruth, the narrative arc transforms Ruth from a foreign, destitute 'ishshah into a cherished, covenantal 'ishshah (wife) of Boaz. This transition mirrors the grander redemptive theme of God taking a vulnerable, marginalized people and bringing them into a secure, covenantal relationship. The preservation of her honor in Ruth 3:14 ensures that the lineage of the Messiah remains pure and beyond reproach.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:14, Ruth lies at the feet of Boaz at the threshing floor—a bold, symbolic request for marriage and redemption. She rises before dawn to depart in secret. Boaz commands, "Do not let it be known that the woman came to the threshing floor."

The nuance of 'ishshah here is intensely protective. A threshing floor at harvest time was often associated with pagan revelry and prostitution (cf. Hosea 9:1). Had the townspeople discovered a young foreign widow leaving Boaz's threshing floor at dawn, her reputation as a "virtuous woman" (Ruth 3:11) would have been ruined, and Boaz's integrity as a righteous leader would have been compromised. By referring to her as "the woman," Boaz speaks with discretion, shielding her identity from potential gossipers while validating her dignity. He treats her not as an object of convenience, but as a sister in the covenant worthy of highest honor.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:23: "And Adam said: 'This is now bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.'"

• Ruth 3:11: "And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you request, for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman."

• Proverbs 31:30: "Charm is deceitful and beauty is passing, but a woman who fears the Lord, she shall be praised."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural parallel between Ruth 3:11 and Ruth 3:14 is striking. In verse 11, Boaz pronounces her a "virtuous woman" ('eshet chayil). In verse 14, he takes active, protective measures to ensure that this public estimation of her character is not defiled by scandal. Righteousness in the Old Testament is never merely passive; Boaz’s protective care of "the woman" demonstrate how covenant love (hesed) acts decisively to guard the vulnerable from harm and slander.
