CAME - ( bo' , Strong's H935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo' is a foundational word in the Old Testament, primarily meaning "to come," "to enter," or "to go." In Ruth 3:14, it describes Ruth's quiet, strategic, and vulnerable arrival at the threshing floor of Boaz. While the word itself denotes physical movement, its placement in this context carries immense covenantal weight. Ruth’s act of coming to Boaz was not an act of indiscretion, but a courageous, faith-driven appeal for redemption under the wings of the God of Israel. Boaz's protective response—desiring that no one know "the woman came to the threshing floor"—safeguards her reputation and the integrity of their impending redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of bo' represents the basic physical action of crossing a boundary, entering a specific space, or arriving at a destination. In the ancient Near East, spaces carried distinct cultural and social boundaries. To "come" into a space was to subject oneself to the dynamics of that environment.

In Ruth 3:14, the environment is the threshing floor at night. During the harvest season, the threshing floor was a place of hard work, celebration, and vulnerability, as men slept there to guard the grain from thieves. For a young, foreign widow to "come" to this male-dominated space in the dark was an incredibly high-risk action. It was a step across physical, social, and moral boundaries. The word picture here is one of deliberate, quiet entry into a space of destiny, where Ruth risks everything to seek the protection of her kinsman-redeemer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bo' occurs over 2,500 times in the Hebrew scriptures, making it one of the most common verbs in the biblical text. Its primary meaning in the simple active stem (Qal) is to come in, enter, or arrive. It is the antonym of yatsa' ("to go out").

Lexical analysis outlines its semantic range:

• Physical Entry: Arriving at a specific geographic location, entering a house, tent, or city.

• Temporal Movement: The setting of the sun (e.g., the sun "goes down").

• Relational/Covenantal Entry: Entering into an alliance, oath, or marriage.

• Eventual Fulfillment: The coming of an event, a prophecy, or a expected person.

In Ruth 3:14, the verb is used in the Qal perfect third-person feminine singular form, referring strictly to the physical arrival of "the woman" (Ruth) at the threshing floor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word bo' is frequently used in word pairs and metaphorical idioms throughout the Old Testament:

• The Goings Out and Comings In: This phrase is a common idiom representing a person’s daily conduct, administrative leadership, or the entirety of their life (e.g., Psalm 121:8).

• Entering into Covenant: To "come into" (bo' be) a covenant or oath means to fully bind oneself to the terms and relationships of that agreement (Ezekiel 16:8).

• The Coming of the Redeemer: Prophetic literature uses bo' to announce the arrival of the ultimate Redeemer, the Messiah, who will "come" to Zion to bring salvation (Zechariah 9:9).

4. Theological Implications

The book of Ruth is a narrative of progressive "comings." Ruth’s journey begins when she "comes" from the pagan fields of Moab to Bethlehem, seeking refuge under the wings of Yahweh (Ruth 1:22, 2:12). In chapter 3, her physical act of "coming" to the threshing floor is the human outworking of her faith. She goes to the one who can act as Yahweh's hands and feet.

Theologically, this highlights the intersection of human agency and divine providence. Ruth did not merely wait passively for redemption; she actively "came" to the redeemer. Furthermore, Boaz’s concern for her reputation shows the holiness required in the process of redemption. God's redemptive work is not characterized by scandal or disorder, but by righteousness, discretion, and the preservation of honor.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:14, Boaz states, "Do not let it be known that the woman came to the threshing floor." The nuance here is highly defensive. In the ancient world, the harvest threshing floor was sometimes associated with moral looseness and cultic prostitution (Hosea 9:1). If the public learned that a woman "came" to Boaz under the cover of darkness, it would immediately be interpreted as an illicit encounter, destroying Ruth's hard-earned reputation as a "virtuous woman" (Ruth 3:11) and compromising Boaz’s integrity.

Boaz uses the simple fact of her arrival (bo') to construct a shield of privacy around her. He acknowledges the righteousness of her plea while protecting her from the slanderous tongues of the community. Her "coming" was holy, but to the eyes of the world, it would look compromised. Righteousness therefore demands secrecy until the redemption can be legally and publicly executed at the city gate.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:22 - "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab. Now they came to Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest."

• Ruth 2:12 - "The LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given you by the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings you have come for refuge."

• Psalm 24:7 - "Lift up your heads, O you gates! And be lifted up, you everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound literary symmetry in how bo' is used in the narrative of Ruth. In Ruth 2:12, Boaz commends Ruth because she has "come" (bo') to trust under the wings of Yahweh. In Ruth 3:9, Ruth asks Boaz to spread his "wings" (or cloak) over her. Thus, when Ruth "came" (bo') to the threshing floor in Ruth 3:14, she was physically pursuing the very refuge she had spiritually sought when she first "came" to Bethlehem. Her physical footsteps matched her spiritual walk.
