BRING - ( yahab , Strong's H3051 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yahab is an action-oriented word meaning "to give," "to provide," "to bring," or "to permit." Frequently occurring in the imperative form, it acts as an urgent command, invitation, or exhortation, equivalent to "come," "give here," or "provide." In the context of Ruth 3:15, Boaz uses this word to instruct Ruth to present or hold out her outer cloak to receive a generous gift of barley. This command marks a critical narrative transition, symbolizing Boaz’s willingness to provide for, protect, and ultimately redeem Ruth and Naomi.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb yahab primarily conveys the direct, concrete action of handing something over to another person. Unlike passive verbs of receiving, yahab demands active, cooperative participation from both the giver and the receiver. In ancient Near Eastern culture, giving and bringing were actions deeply tied to covenantal hospitality, legal transactions, and familial responsibility. The word has a conversational, immediate force, often used in spoken dialogue to initiate an immediate action or demand a response. In the agricultural setting of ancient Israel, bringing an object like a shawl or vessel to be filled was a common physical picture of dependency, trust, and provision.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of yahab is to give, bring, provide, or place. It is used almost exclusively in the imperative form (such as habah, havi, or havu). The semantic range includes:

• To hand over or deliver a physical object to someone (e.g., "Give me my wife").

• To present or offer worship and praise to God (e.g., "Ascribe/Give to the Lord glory").

• To prompt immediate, cooperative action, acting as an interjection (e.g., "Come, let us build").

• To appoint or designate individuals for a specific task (e.g., "Choose/Provide wise men").

In Ruth 3:15, the feminine singular imperative form havi is used, which translates directly to "Bring" or "Give here," instructing Ruth to hold out her shawl.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word yahab paints vivid pictures across the Old Testament:

• The Call to Collaboration: In the Tower of Babel narrative (Genesis 11:3-4), the repetitive use of the imperative habah ("Come") depicts humanity's unified, rebellious resolve to build a name for themselves.

• Desperation and Demand: Rachel uses the word in Genesis 30:1 ("Give me children") to express the agonizing, urgent desire of her heart to her husband Jacob.

• Sacred Ascription: In the Psalms, yahab is the vehicle for high worship, where the assembly is commanded to "give" or "ascribe" (havu) strength, glory, and worship to Yahweh (Psalm 29:1-2). Here, the word pictures creation rendering to the Creator what is rightfully His.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yahab in Ruth 3:15 highlights the themes of grace, provision, and redemption.

• Grace Demands an Open Hand: Ruth cannot earn or demand the barley; she must simply obey the instruction to "bring" her shawl and hold it open. This illustrates the nature of divine grace—it is a free gift, but the recipient must actively receive it in faith and obedience.

• The Character of the Redeemer: Boaz represents the kinsman-redeemer (Goel), a type of Christ. His command to "bring" the shawl is not a demand for her labor, but an invitation to receive his abundance. This mirrors the heart of God, who invites the needy to come and receive His abundant spiritual provision without cost (Isaiah 55:1).

• The Security of the Covenant: The six measures of barley Boaz places in her shawl serve as a pledge of rest and future security, indicating that he will not rest until the matter of her redemption is settled.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:15, the scene takes place at the threshing floor in the dark of night. Ruth has courageously placed herself at Boaz’s feet, asking him to spread his wings over her as her redeemer. Boaz responds with deep kindness and moral integrity. Before she departs in the early morning to protect her reputation, Boaz commands her, "Bring the shawl that is on you and hold it."

The use of yahab here is gentle yet authoritative. By instructing her to "bring" the cloak, Boaz shifts the dynamic from Ruth asking for help to Boaz taking active charge of her welfare. The physical act of holding open her shawl represents her trust in his promise. The six measures of barley he then measures out are a massive physical load, representing a tangible down payment on the redemption he intends to secure later that day at the city gate.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The word yahab is closely related to the concept of casting one's burdens. In Psalm 55:22, the related noun yehab is translated as "burden" or "lot" ("Cast your burden on the Lord"). This connection reveals a beautiful spiritual symmetry: when we "bring" (yahab) our needs, emptiness, and weakness to God, He commands us to cast our heavy burdens (yehab) onto Him, replacing our lack with His abundant strength and provision.
