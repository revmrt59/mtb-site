MEASURED - ( madad, Strong's H4058 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb madad means to stretch, measure, or mete out, representing the physical act of determining dimensions or quantity. In the narrative of Ruth 3:15, Boaz's act of measuring six ephahs of barley serves as a tangible, generous sign of his commitment to redeem Ruth and Naomi. This deliberate, calculated measure of grain goes far beyond a casual handout, symbolizing the abundant provision, security, and covenant faithfulness of the kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The cultural and physical world of the Old Testament relied heavily on manual measurements. Whether constructing a tent, building a temple, dividing land, or trading grain at the market, measuring (madad) required active participation, precision, and standards of justice. To measure grain on a threshing floor was a highly visual, physical act. It involved scooping grain into a container of standard volume until it was full. When Boaz measured the barley for Ruth, he did so on the threshing floor under the cover of night—a setting of vulnerability and transition. This act of measuring was a physical guarantee of his intention to act on her behalf.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb madad fundamentally means to measure or take the dimensions of something. It describes the physical act of measuring length, distance, or volume. In its intensive or reflexive stems, the word can mean to stretch oneself out or to extend. When applied to dry goods and food supplies, it denotes portioning out a specific quantity of grain or flour. In Ruth 3:15, it refers to the precise action of pouring out and counting six measures of barley, transferring a substantial, calculated portion from Boaz's secure stores to Ruth's cloak.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Divine Architecture: madad is frequently used in Ezekiel's vision of the future temple, where an angel measures the gates, walls, and courts. This demonstrates that God's dwelling place is carefully planned, orderly, and holy down to the exact cubit.

• Sovereignty and Creation: The word paints a vivid picture of God's unmatched scale and power in Isaiah 40:12, where God measures the waters of the earth in the hollow of His hand. Here, the verb highlights God's ownership and intimate knowledge of His vast creation.

• Sustenance and Equal Sufficiency: In the wilderness, the Israelites measured (madad) the manna they gathered. Through this act, God ensured that everyone had exactly what they needed—neither too much nor too little.

4. Theological Implications

• Measured Grace: In biblical theology, God's grace is never haphazard or accidental. It is measured out perfectly to meet the specific needs of His people. Just as Boaz measured a portion of grain that was the maximum Ruth could carry, God measures His strength and provision to align with our capacity and calling.

• The Down Payment of Redemption: In the context of the kinsman-redeemer (goel), Boaz’s gift is a down payment. It is a physical pledge of the ultimate redemption he intends to secure. This mirrors the work of Christ, who gives the Holy Spirit as a guarantee of our future inheritance, ensuring that our ultimate redemption is secure.

• Filling the Empty: The book of Ruth begins with emptiness—Naomi laments that she went out full and returned empty. Boaz's act of measuring out abundance is a physical sign that God is actively filling the emptiness of His covenant people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:15, Ruth has just risked everything by laying at Boaz's feet, asking him to spread his wings over her as redeemer. Boaz responds with protective care, insisting she stay until morning and then sending her back with six measures of barley. The physical act of measuring this barley serves several immediate purposes:

• It protects Ruth’s reputation, ensuring she does not return to the city empty-handed, which might look as if she were cast out or went on a shameful errand.

• It is a direct signal to Naomi. When Naomi sees the six measured portions, she immediately understands that Boaz will not rest until he resolves the matter that very day.

• It represents a generous, heavy load—often understood as six ephahs—which was the maximum amount a single person could carry, signaling Boaz's overflowing generosity.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 16:18: "So when they measured it with an omer, he who gathered much had nothing left over, and he who gathered little had no lack."

• Isaiah 40:12: "Who has measured the waters in the hollow of His hand, Measured heaven with a span And calculated the dust of the earth in a measure? Weighed the mountains in scales And the hills in a balance?"

• Ezekiel 40:5: "Now there was a wall all around the outside of the temple. In the man’s hand was a measuring rod of six cubits, each of which was a cubit and a handbreadth. So he measured the width of the wall, one rod; and the height, one rod."

• Habakkuk 3:6: "He stood and measured the earth; He looked and startled the nations. And the everlasting mountains were scattered, The perpetual hills bowed. His ways are everlasting."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The "Six" Measures: The text simply states "six of barley," leaving the unit of measurement implied. Many scholars suggest six seahs or six ephahs. If it were six ephahs, it would be an enormous weight, suggesting a miraculous capacity or a smaller symbolic unit. However, the number six itself is highly symbolic. In the Hebrew mindset, six days of labor precede the seventh day of Sabbath rest. By giving Ruth six measures, Boaz is symbolically telling her that her days of heavy labor, gleaning in the fields, and carrying burdens are coming to an end. Her "Sabbath rest" in a secure home is about to begin.
