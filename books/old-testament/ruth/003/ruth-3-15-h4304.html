SHAWL - ( mitpahath , Strong's H4304 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mitpahath refers to a large, wide, cloak-like shawl or outer wrap worn by women in the ancient Near East. Woven from durable, heavy fabric, this garment was designed to protect against the elements, wrap around the body for modesty and warmth, or serve as a carrying sheet. In the narrative of Ruth 3:15, this practical everyday garment becomes a beautiful vessel of grace, abundance, and covenant security. As Boaz fills Ruth's shawl with six measures of barley, he provides a physical pledge of his commitment to redeem her, demonstrating how God transforms our empty vessels into instruments of His overflowing provision.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun mitpahath is derived from the verbal root taphach, which means to extend, spread out, or span. This linguistic origin provides a vivid word picture of a garment that can be spread wide to cover, protect, and hold. In the domestic life of ancient Israel, clothing was highly functional. The mitpahath was not a delicate, decorative lace veil, but a substantial, wide, enveloping outer wrap or cloak. Worn over the head and shoulders and draped down the body, its heavy fabric (usually linen or wool) made it robust enough to be taken off and used as an improvised sack for carrying heavy goods, grain, or household items.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term denotes a wide cloak, shawl, mantle, or large outer wrap worn by women. It represents a specific, heavy piece of outerwear designed for warmth, protection, modesty, and utility, rather than an ornamental veil or simple head covering. It is large enough to be laid out flat, held by its corners, and filled with a substantial volume of material.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Because mitpahath is a rare term occurring only twice in the Old Testament, its usage points to contrasting word pictures:

• A Vessel of Grace and Provision: In Ruth 3:15, the shawl becomes a practical tool for receiving and carrying an abundant blessing. The physical action of spreading out the shawl and holding it open to receive grain serves as a picture of faith-filled expectancy. It is a vessel that is filled to capacity, symbolizing the transition from emptiness to fullness.

• An Object of Haughty Luxury: In Isaiah 3:22, the plural form mitpachot is translated as "outer garments" or "cloaks." In this context, it is listed among the extravagant finery and luxury items of the proud daughters of Zion. What God intended as a practical, modest covering was corrupted into an instrument of vanity and self-indulgence, leading to judgment.

4. Theological Implications

• The Empty Made Full: The book of Ruth begins with the theme of emptiness. Naomi laments that she "went out full, and the Lord has brought me home again empty" (Ruth 1:21). The filling of Ruth's mitpahath with six measures of barley is a prophetic sign that God is reversing their emptiness. God is a restorer who fills the empty with good things.

• Covenant Security and Rest: When Ruth first arrived at the threshing floor, she asked Boaz to spread the corner of his garment (kanaph) over her, appealing to him as a kinsman-redeemer (Ruth 3:9). Boaz responds by filling her own garment (mitpahath) with grain. This acts as a tangible pledge of future security. Ruth's mother-in-law, Naomi, immediately recognizes the sign, noting that Boaz will not rest until he concludes the matter that very day (Ruth 3:18). The six measures of grain point toward a seventh—the sabbath rest of home, marriage, and redemption.

• Providential Grace: The grain placed in the shawl represents unmerited favor. Ruth did not glean for this grain; it was measured out and given to her freely by the redeemer. This mirrors the grace of God, who pours blessings into the lives of His people far beyond what their labor could ever secure.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:15, Boaz commands Ruth, "Bring the shawl that is on you and hold it." This instruction requires active participation; Ruth must hold the heavy fabric steady to receive the gift. The weight of the gift is immense. Boaz measures out "six measures of barley." While the exact unit of measurement is not specified in the Hebrew text, the volume was substantial—likely between 60 to 80 pounds of grain. A flimsy veil would have torn instantly under such a load. The mitpahath was strong enough to bear this weight, showing that Ruth, whom the town recognized as a "virtuous woman" (eshet chayil, meaning a woman of strength and noble character in Ruth 3:11), was equipped to carry the heavy blessings of the redeemer back to her home.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern customs, symbolic transactions involving garments were deeply legally and socially binding. By filling Ruth's shawl with grain, Boaz was not only feeding two hungry widows but also sending a silent, powerful legal message to Naomi. He was confirming his intention to act as the redeemer. The shawl, which kept Ruth warm during a vulnerable night on the cold threshing floor, became the very vehicle that carried the physical proof of her salvation and future hope.
