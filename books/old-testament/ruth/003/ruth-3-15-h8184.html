BARLEY - ( se'orah , Strong's H8184 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, barley represents the provision of God in times of transition and hope, specifically marking the end of famine and the beginning of redemption. In Ruth 3:15, Boaz's gift of six measures of barley to Ruth is not merely a generous handout, but a covenantal sign of security, rest, and future abundance. It serves as a physical pledge that Ruth and Naomi will no longer wander in emptiness, signaling that their redeemer is actively working to restore their heritage and provide a secure home.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Barley (se'orah) derives from the Hebrew root sa'ar, which means "to stand on end," "to be bristly," or "to dread." The plant gets its name from the long, hair-like bristles (awns) that project from the ears of the grain.

• In the agricultural cycle of ancient Israel, barley was the first crop harvested in the early spring, coinciding with the season of Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread.

• Because it was highly resilient, matured rapidly, and could grow in poorer soils compared to wheat, barley was the food of the common people, the impoverished, and domestic livestock. It represents humility, endurance, and the initial wave of God's physical deliverance to His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The noun se'orah refers to the barley plant, the standing crop, or the harvested grain.

• The semantic range covers the cereal grain in its various forms: as a crop in the field vulnerable to plague or hail, as threshed grain on the threshing floor, and as a measured commodity used for making basic loaves of bread or for trade.

• Rather than indicating luxury, se'orah denotes basic, life-sustaining nourishment. It is the food of survival and recovery, carrying the primary nuance of practical provision that preserves life.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Sign of Firstfruits: Since the barley harvest was the first of the year, it was offered as a wave offering to God (Leviticus 23:10). It represents the first evidence of God’s faithfulness in breaking a season of barrenness or winter.

• The Measure of Lowly Value: Barley was valued at half the price of wheat, marking it as the food of the poor. In Hosea 3:2, the prophet purchases his wayward wife for fifteen shekels of silver and one and one-half homers of barley—a price that reflects both her low estate and the humble grace of her redeemer.

• The Offering of Suspicion: In cases of suspected adultery, the "offering of jealousy" was mandated to be barley flour instead of fine wheat flour, and was to be offered without oil or frankincense (Numbers 5:15). This portrays a state of brokenness, trial, and the absence of joy.

4. Theological Implications

• Providential Reversal: The Book of Ruth begins with a severe famine in Bethlehem (which ironically means "House of Bread") and ends with the abundant barley harvest. The presence of barley signifies the return of Yahweh's favor, showing that God reverses the emptiness of His people and restores life to the desolate.

• Redemptive Grace: God’s law of the corners of the field (gleaning) allowed the poor and the stranger to gather barley. Through this provision, Ruth meets Boaz. The barley crop becomes the vehicle through which God's grace intersects with human helplessness, demonstrating that God provides for those who have no legal claim on the land.

• The Earnest of Final Rest: In the economy of God, the initial harvest of barley is the guarantee of the greater wheat harvest to follow. It stands as a physical pledge of complete redemption, a theme that points forward to the spiritual provision of Christ, who feeds the hungry and brings ultimate rest.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:15, Ruth lies at the feet of Boaz on the threshing floor, asking him to perform the duty of a kinsman-redeemer. Boaz responds by measuring out "six measures of barley" into her shawl (mitpahath) before she returns to Naomi.

• The quantity of "six measures" (likely six seahs, though the text does not specify the unit of measure) was a substantial weight—likely as much as Ruth could carry, weighing approximately 60 to 80 pounds. This physically demonstrated Boaz’s strength and his immediate capacity to provide.

• The number six carries deep symbolic weight. In Hebrew culture, six represents the days of labor and preparation that lead to the seventh day, the Sabbath of rest. By sending Ruth home with six measures of barley, Boaz communicates a non-verbal message to Naomi: the time of labor, wandering, and mourning is complete. The "seventh day" of marital rest and redemption is at hand.

• Naomi immediately understands this nuance, declaring, "the man will not rest until he has concluded the matter this day" (Ruth 3:18). The barley was not just food; it was a covenantal guarantee of Boaz's active, unwavering commitment to redeem them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 2:23 - "So she stayed close by the young women of Boaz, to glean to the end of barley harvest and wheat harvest; and she dwelt with her mother-in-law."

• 2 Kings 7:1 - "Then Elisha said, 'Hear the word of the Lord. Thus says the Lord: "Tomorrow about this time a seah of fine flour shall be sold for a shekel, and two seahs of barley for a shekel, at the gate of Samaria."'"

• Hosea 3:2 - "So I bought her for myself for fifteen shekels of silver, and one and one-half homers of barley."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Practical Weight of Redemption: The physical effort required for Ruth to carry six measures of barley back to the city shows her strength (eshet chayil, "virtuous woman" or "woman of strength," as Boaz calls her in verse 11). She was not a passive recipient but an active participant in carrying the evidence of redemption.

• Unleavened Bread Correlation: The barley harvest began during the Feast of Unleavened Bread, which celebrated Israel's redemption from Egypt. Just as God brought Israel out of bondage with haste, Boaz acts swiftly to bring Ruth out of her state of vulnerability.
