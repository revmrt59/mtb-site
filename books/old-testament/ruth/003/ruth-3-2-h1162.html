BOAZ - ( bo'az , Strong's H1162 )

Summary:

Boaz is a wealthy, righteous Bethlehemite of the tribe of Judah who serves as the kinsman-redeemer (goel) for Ruth and Naomi. His name is traditionally understood to mean "in him is strength" or "fleetness," embodying both the physical and moral capacity to rescue a destitute family line. In the narrative of Ruth, Boaz represents the ideal Israelite who operates not merely by the letter of the law but by the spirit of covenant loyalty (hesed), ultimately serving as a physical type of Jesus Christ, our ultimate Redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Boaz is introduced in the book of Ruth during the dark period of the Judges, a time characterized by moral decay, spiritual compromise, and social instability.

• He is a man of great wealth and standing (gibbor chayil) in Bethlehem, but his true riches lie in his character, faith, and adherence to the Torah.

• His lineage is highly significant; he is the son of Salmon and Rahab of Jericho, placing him in the direct messianic line of Judah.

• The agricultural setting of Ruth—specifically the harvest and the threshing floor—serves as the backdrop where Boaz’s integrity is tested and proven.

• Additionally, Boaz is the name given to one of the two massive bronze pillars erected by Solomon at the vestibule of the temple, symbolizing strength and stability.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The name Boaz is a compound Hebrew proper noun.

• It is derived from the prepositional prefix b- ("in") and the noun oz ("strength"), translating literally as "in him is strength" or "in strength."

• Alternatively, some Semitic etymologies suggest a root meaning "quickness" or "fleetness," though the connection to "strength" remains the primary theological and literary focus in biblical tradition.

• The name denotes physical vigor, social influence, moral fortitude, and the capacity to protect and provide.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The name occurs 24 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in the book of Ruth (chapters 2 through 4), with additional occurrences in the genealogies of 1 Chronicles and the temple descriptions in 1 Kings and 2 Chronicles.

• The primary word picture associated with Boaz in the book of Ruth is that of the goel—the kinsman-redeemer. Under Mosaic law, a kinsman-redeemer was responsible for buying back family land sold due to poverty, marrying the widow of a deceased relative to carry on the family name, and redeeming relatives sold into slavery.

• Boaz's actions paint a vivid picture of grace; he steps beyond the strict demands of legal obligation to offer shelter, safety, and redemption to a foreign widow who has no legal claim on him.

4. Theological Implications

• Boaz serves as a prominent Old Testament type of Jesus Christ. Just as Boaz was a near kinsman who possessed the legal right, the financial resources, and the willing heart to redeem Ruth, so Christ became our kinsman in the flesh, possessing the infinite worth and willingness to redeem humanity from spiritual destitution.

• The name highlights the doctrine of divine providence. Boaz does not enter the story by accident; his appearance at the harvest field is governed by God’s sovereign care for the line of David and, ultimately, the Messiah.

• Boaz’s character illustrates the concept of hesed—covenant love, kindness, and loyalty. His treatment of Ruth demonstrates how God’s love transcends ethnic and national boundaries to embrace the outcast and the stranger.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:2, Naomi speaks to Ruth: "Now is not Boaz our kinsman, with whose young women you were? Behold, he is winnowing barley tonight at the threshing floor."

• The mention of Boaz here acts as the catalyst for Naomi's plan of redemption. After a full harvest season of gleaning in his fields, Naomi recognizes Boaz not merely as a generous employer, but as their legitimate moda (an intimate relative or kinsman).

• The context of the "threshing floor" is highly symbolic. Winnowing barley in the evening breeze was a time of securing the harvest and celebrating God's provision. It represents a vulnerable yet decisive moment where Ruth must appeal to Boaz's strength and honor to take her under his "wing."

• Naomi's reference to Boaz underscores his reliability, his accessibility, and his established relationship with Ruth's household, paving the way for the ultimate redemption of Naomi's family line.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 2:1 - "There was a relative of Naomi’s husband, a man of great wealth, of the family of Elimelech; his name was Boaz."

• Ruth 4:9 - "And Boaz said to the elders and all the people, 'You are witnesses this day that I have bought all that was Elimelech’s, and all that was Chilion’s and Mahlon’s, from the hand of Naomi.'"

• 1 Kings 7:21 - "Then he set up the pillars by the vestibule of the temple; he set up the pillar on the right and called its name Jachin, and he set up the pillar on the left and called its name Boaz."

• 1 Chronicles 2:11 - "Salmon begot Boaz, and Boaz begot Obed;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Boaz’s lineage is notable for its inclusion of Rahab (Matthew 1:5). Having a Canaanite mother may have uniquely prepared Boaz to show extraordinary empathy and grace toward Ruth, a Moabitess, understanding firsthand that faith in Yahweh transcends biological descent.

• The contrast between Boaz and the unnamed closer relative (ploni almoni in Ruth 4:1) is sharp. The other relative refuses to redeem because it might jeopardize his own inheritance, whereas Boaz willingly risks his own estate out of love and duty, securing his name eternally in the genealogy of Jesus Christ.
