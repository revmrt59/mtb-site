WINNOWS - ( zarah , Strong's H2219 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb zarah refers to the agricultural process of winnowing, where tossed grain is separated from the worthless chaff by the wind. In Ruth 3:2, Boaz is found winnowing barley at the threshing floor in the evening. This word carries a rich physical and spiritual meaning, symbolizing separation, testing, judgment, and purification. In the context of Ruth, the threshing floor—a place of winnowing and hard labor—becomes a sacred site of redemption, covenantal loyalty, and transition from empty handiness to fruitful abundance.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

Winnowing in the ancient Near East was a labor-intensive, seasonal process that occurred at the end of the harvest. After stalks of barley or wheat were cut and gathered, they were brought to the threshing floor. This was usually a flat, hard, outdoor surface located on an elevated hill to optimize exposure to natural breezes. Oxen trod upon the stalks to loosen the hulls from the grain. The farmer then used a winnowing fork or shovel to toss the crushed mixture high into the air against the evening breeze. The heavy, valuable grain fell straight back to the floor, while the wind blew away the lighter, worthless chaff and straw. This process separated the valuable harvest from the useless waste, leaving a clean, purified pile of grain ready for the granary.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew verb zarah means to scatter, fan, winnow, or disperse. At its semantic core, the word describes the action of spreading something out widely. In agricultural contexts, it specifically denotes the act of winnowing grain to separate it from chaff. In metaphorical and poetic contexts, it refers to the scattering of a people or nation in judgment, as well as the close personal examination or "sifting" of an individual's life, path, and choices.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Literal Agriculture: The word describes the physical act of tossing grain to let the wind blow away the chaff, preparing the harvest for storage (Ruth 3:2, Isaiah 30:24).

• Divine Dispersion and Judgment: The prophets frequently use the term to describe God scattering His disobedient people among the pagan nations, treating them like worthless chaff blown away by the wind of His wrath (Leviticus 26:33, Ezekiel 5:10).

• Sifting and Close Examination: The term represents God's thorough examination or sifting of a person’s daily life, paths, and motives to evaluate their righteousness (Psalm 139:3).

• Overcoming Enemies: The word depicts the total defeat and scattering of hostile nations, who vanish like chaff in the wind before God’s mighty power (Isaiah 41:16).

4. Theological Implications

Theological concepts bound up in zarah center on divine discrimination, purification, and judgment. God is the ultimate Winnowed who separates the righteous from the wicked, and the genuine from the hypocritical. Just as winnowing does not destroy the precious grain but preserves and cleanses it, God’s winnowing of His people through trials, hardships, and exile is designed to purge sin and preserve a holy, faithful remnant. The process of winnowing demonstrates that God deeply values the grain (the righteous) and will secure it, while the chaff (the wicked and rebellious) is temporary, weightless, and destined for destruction.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:2, Naomi tells Ruth that Boaz "is winnowing barley tonight on the threshing floor." The timing is highly significant. Winnowing was done in the late afternoon and evening when the cool sea breezes swept across the hills of Judea. This required Boaz to sleep at the threshing floor to guard his freshly cleaned crop from thieves. This setting sets the stage for Ruth's bold, faith-filled act of redemption. The threshing floor, normally a public place of hard, dusty labor and rigorous separation, becomes the quiet, private sanctuary where Ruth asks Boaz to spread his cloak over her as her kinsman-redeemer. The winnowing of barley signals the completion of the harvest, marking the end of Naomi and Ruth’s season of famine and emptiness, and ushering in a season of covenant security and restoration.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 26:33: "I will scatter you among the nations and draw out a sword after you; your land shall be desolate and your cities waste."

• Psalm 139:3: "You comprehend my path and my lying down, and are acquainted with all my ways."

• Isaiah 41:16: "You shall winnow them, the wind shall carry them away, and the whirlwind shall scatter them; you shall rejoice in the Lord, and glory in the Holy One of Israel."

• Jeremiah 15:7: "And I will winnow them with a winnowing fan in the gates of the land; I will bereave them of children, I will destroy My people, since they do not return from their ways."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient Near East, threshing floors were sometimes associated with local deities and fertility rites, as pagan cultures believed gods controlled agricultural abundance. However, the narrative of Ruth stands in sharp contrast to these Canaanite practices. The interaction between Ruth and Boaz at the threshing floor is marked by absolute moral purity, dignity, and commitment to the Torah's laws of redemption. Boaz’s careful work of winnowing highlights his industrious character as a worthy man of standing (Ruth 2:1), who protects both his harvest and the vulnerable widow who seeks refuge under his wings.
