KNOWN - ( yada , Strong's H3045 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yada fundamentally means to know, experience, or perceive, extending far beyond mere intellectual assent to encompass deep relational intimacy, personal encounter, and recognition. In the context of Ruth 3:3, Naomi counsels Ruth not to make herself known—to remain unrecognized and hidden—until Boaz has finished eating and drinking. This deliberate concealment sets the stage for a strategic, honorable, and culturally significant encounter at the threshing floor, where Ruth will ultimately propose that Boaz act as their kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word yada is one of the most versatile and profound verbs in the Old Testament, occurring nearly a thousand times. Its background is rooted in experiential knowledge rather than abstract philosophy. To "know" in the ancient Hebrew mind is not merely to possess cognitive facts about someone or something, but to experience them directly. It describes the physical senses of seeing, hearing, and touching, as well as the emotional and relational dimensions of care, concern, and covenantal commitment. In its most intimate sense, it refers to the deep marital bond between husband and wife.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yada possesses a broad semantic range including: to perceive, find out, discern, distinguish, understand, know by experience, and make oneself known. In the reflexive stem (Niphal), as used in Ruth 3:3, it carries the meaning of making oneself recognized, revealing oneself, or becoming known. It signifies a transition from hiddenness, anonymity, or ignorance to clear recognition, exposure, and identification.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Relational Intimacy: The term is used to describe the deepest human bond, as when a husband intimately knows his wife.

• Divine Election and Covenant: It describes God's intimate care and choice of His people, meaning He has chosen them and entered into a personal covenant relationship with them.

• Skill and Ability: It describes those who "know" how to perform a task, such as skilled hunters, sailors, or craftsmen.

• Revealing and Manifesting: In the reflexive stem, it describes God making Himself known through His judgments or mighty acts, or individuals revealing their true identity to others.

4. Theological Implications

In the biblical narrative, "knowing" is linked to timing, wisdom, and disclosure. God's redemptive plans often involve seasons of hiddenness followed by strategic self-revelation. Naomi’s instruction to Ruth to remain unknown until the appropriate time illustrates the wisdom of timing in human affairs and divine providence. It highlights that true, covenantal "knowing" must not be rushed, manipulated, or subjected to premature public scrutiny. It respects the boundaries of propriety and honor, ensuring that when the revelation occurs, it is clear, intentional, and spiritually significant.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:3, Naomi commands Ruth, "do not make yourself known to the man." Ruth is to wash, anoint herself, and dress in her finest cloak, but she must remain in the shadows at the threshing floor. The threshing floor was a public, bustling place during the harvest, but at night it became vulnerable to moral compromise and theft. By remaining unknown, Ruth avoids public scandal and protects both her reputation and Boaz's. She waits until Boaz is in a contented state, having finished eating and drinking, and lies down to sleep. Only then, in a private, quiet space, does she reveal her presence. The delayed "knowing" ensures that their subsequent conversation about redemption and marriage is handled with the utmost dignity, free from the prying eyes of the townspeople, and framed as a sacred, covenantal proposal rather than an opportunistic seduction.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between Naomi’s instruction "do not make yourself known" (Ruth 3:3) and Boaz’s subsequent reaction when he discovers her is striking. When Boaz wakes up startled and asks, "Who are you?" (Ruth 3:9), Ruth responds by making herself fully known: "I am Ruth, your maidservant." This transition from the hidden, unknown stranger to the fully revealed, potential bride mirrors the broader biblical motif of redemption. The redeemer must first "know" the plight of the one needing redemption before he can act on their behalf.
