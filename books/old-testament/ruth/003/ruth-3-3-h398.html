EATING - ( akal , Strong's H0398 )

Summary:

In Ruth 3:3, the Hebrew verb akal (to eat, consume) refers to Boaz's evening meal at the threshing floor after a long day of harvest work. The act of eating and drinking in this context is not merely biological nourishment but represents satisfaction, rest, and the completion of labor. Naomi strategically counsels Ruth to wait until Boaz has finished this meal, ensuring he is in a relaxed, content, and receptive frame of mind before Ruth approaches him to request redemption. This word highlights the transition from labor to rest, setting the stage for a critical turning point in the narrative of redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The primary root akal is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over eight hundred times. Its basic word picture is the physical act of consuming food, chewing, and swallowing.

• Historically and culturally in the ancient Near East, eating was intimately linked with fellowship, covenants, and the rhythm of agricultural life. To eat together was to share life, establish peace, and experience community.

• In agricultural contexts, eating the fruits of the harvest represented the culmination of hard labor, divine provision, and a shift from anxious toil to peaceful satisfaction.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb akal primarily means to eat, devour, consume, or feed. In its simplest Qal stem, it refers to human consumption of food or animal grazing.

• In metaphorical extensions, it describes fire consuming wood, a sword devouring enemies, or jealousy eating away at a person.

• In the infinitive construct form used in Ruth 3:3 (le'ekol), it specifically denotes the action or process of eating a meal. The lexical range spans from literal nourishment to total consumption or destruction, depending on the context.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Literal Consumption: The most frequent use describes daily sustenance, such as eating bread, meat, or agricultural produce.

• Covenantal Fellowship: Eating is often paired with drinking to seal covenants, express hospitality, or celebrate sacred feasts before Yahweh.

• Metaphor for Destruction: The word frequently paints a picture of complete devastation, where fire, plague, or enemy nations devour resources, land, or people.

• Spiritual Nourishment: Eating the words of God represents internalizing, digesting, and delighting in divine truth.

4. Theological Implications

• Provision and Blessing: Eating in the Old Testament is deeply connected to God's providence and covenant faithfulness. Yahweh is the provider of food, and eating the harvest is a sign of His blessing.

• Covenant Curse: Conversely, famine—the inability to eat—is a sign of covenant curse and judgment.

• Communion with God: In the broader scope of redemption history, eating points to fellowship with God. It echoes the Garden of Eden, where eating was restricted but fellowship was perfect, and points forward to the messianic banquet where God's redeemed people will feast in His presence forever.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Contentment and Peace: In Ruth 3:3, Naomi instructs Ruth to wait until Boaz has "finished eating and drinking." The threshing floor was a place of hard work, but the evening meal marked the transition to celebration and security.

• Wisdom in Timing: By waiting until Boaz finished eating, Ruth approached him when his heart was cheerful—a state of contentment, peace, and gratitude for God’s harvest. Naomi's timing was wise and respectful; she did not want Ruth to interrupt Boaz's work or his period of rest.

• Redemptive Transition: It underscores the transition of Boaz from a provider of grain to a potential redeemer of lives.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:16: "And the Lord God commanded the man, saying, 'Of every tree of the garden you may freely eat;'"

• Exodus 12:11: "And thus you shall eat it: with a belt on your waist, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand. So you shall eat it in haste. It is the Lord’s Passover."

• Exodus 24:11: "But on the nobles of the children of Israel He did not lay His hand. So they saw God, and they ate and drank."

• Jeremiah 15:16: "Your words were found, and I ate them, And Your word was to me the joy and rejoicing of my heart;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Morality and Threshing Floors: In the Ancient Near Eastern culture, the threshing floor could sometimes be a place of moral compromise, associated with pagan fertility rites. However, the use of akal here, followed by Boaz's blessing of Ruth, highlights the purity and righteousness of their interaction.

• Noble Character: The simple acts of eating and sleeping on the threshing floor are kept wholesome, demonstrating that Boaz is a man of noble character who enjoys God's physical provisions with gratitude and maintains moral integrity under pressure.
