ANOINT - ( suk , Strong's H5480 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb suk refers to the practical or cosmetic act of applying oil or ointment to the skin. Unlike the sacred, ceremonial anointing (mashach) reserved for kings and priests, suk belongs to the realm of daily life, hygiene, and preparation for special occasions. In the context of Ruth 3:3, Naomi instructs Ruth to wash, apply oil, and change her clothes, symbolizing the end of her period of mourning and her readiness to enter a new season of life, courtship, and redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the arid, windy, and dusty climate of the ancient Near East, the daily elements took a harsh toll on the skin. Applying olive oil mixed with aromatic spices was not a luxury for the elite but a basic necessity for physical health, hygiene, and comfort. It prevented the skin from cracking and masked body odors. Consequently, oil was deeply associated with joy, vitality, and human flourishing. Conversely, to neglect the application of oil was a universal sign of grief, distress, or mourning. When a person was in mourning, they left their skin dry and unanointed, signaling their internal sorrow to the community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root verb suk signifies the act of smearing, pouring, or applying oil to oneself or another person. The semantic range covers:

• Applying oil for physical refreshment after washing.

• Beautifying the appearance with scented ointment for social gatherings or festive events.

• Refraining from cosmetic application as an expression of fasting or mourning.

• The physical act of preparing the body for a significant change of status.

Unlike mashach (to anoint ceremonially for holy office), suk carries no inherent cultic or priestly connotation. It is thoroughly domestic, relational, and physical.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament presents several key word pictures for suk:

• The End of Grief: The acts of washing and anointing represent the deliberate decision to rise above sorrow and rejoin the community.

• Honor and Hospitality: To pour soothing oil on a weary guest is a supreme gesture of welcome and care.

• Preparing for a Spouse: A bride prepares herself by bathing and perfume-anointing to present herself in beauty to her bridegroom.

4. Theological Implications

While suk is a domestic term, its usage conveys profound spiritual truths:

• God's Desire for Joy over Mourning: The transition from the dry skin of mourning to the glistening, perfumed skin of joy illustrates God's ultimate plan for His people. He replaces the ashes of grief with the oil of joy.

• Human Agency in Divine Redemption: Naomi's instruction to Ruth to anoint herself demonstrates the partnership between human preparation and divine providence. Ruth must actively prepare herself to receive the redemption that Boaz offers.

• Sanctification of the Ordinary: The physical body and daily grooming are not spiritually neutral; they can be used to honor others and prepare oneself for God-ordained opportunities.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:3, Naomi tells Ruth: "Therefore wash yourself and anoint yourself, put on your best garment and go down to the threshing floor."

Since her arrival in Bethlehem, Ruth has lived as a grieving widow, laboring daily in the hot fields under the blistering sun, likely wearing the drab, dust-laden clothes of a mourner and a pauper. To win the attention of Boaz, Naomi does not suggest seductive deception, but rather a formal declaration of Ruth's availability for marriage. By washing, anointing (suk) herself with fragrant oil, and changing her clothes, Ruth symbolically casts off her widowhood and her past in Moab. She steps into the present as a woman ready for a new covenant. The fragrant oil signals her readiness for joy, life, and the protection of a kinsman-redeemer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 2 Samuel 12:20: "So David arose from the ground, washed and anointed himself, and changed his clothes; and he went into the house of the LORD and worshiped."

• 2 Samuel 14:2: "And Joab sent to Tekoa and brought from there a wise woman, and said to her, 'Please pretend to be a mourner, and put on mourning apparel; do not anoint yourself with oil, but act like a woman who has been mourning a long time for the dead.'"

• Daniel 10:3: "I ate no pleasant food, no meat or wine came into my mouth, nor did I anoint myself at all, till three whole weeks were fulfilled."

• Deuteronomy 28:40: "You shall have olive trees throughout all your territory, but you shall not anoint yourself with the oil; for your olives shall drop off."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The distinction between suk (cosmetic/personal anointing) and mashach (sacred/consecrating anointing) is vital for proper biblical interpretation. When King David was anointed by Samuel to be king, the term used was mashach, denoting his divine appointment. When David anointed himself after his son's death, the term was suk, denoting his return to daily life. Understanding this distinction prevents readers from over-spiritualizing Ruth's action in Ruth 3:3 as a priestly or messianic ordination; rather, it is a beautifully human act of hope and marital preparation.
