WASH - ( rachats , Strong's H7364 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb rachats means to wash, bathe, or cleanse the body, feet, hands, or garments with water. In the Old Testament, it describes both physical cleansing and ceremonial purification. In the context of Ruth 3:3, Naomi instructs Ruth to wash herself, marking a significant transition from her period of mourning and widowhood to preparation for marriage and redemption. This act signifies personal preparation, readiness, and a transition from grief to hope.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root rachats fundamentally refers to washing with water. Unlike other Hebrew words for washing clothes by treading or kneading, rachats typically refers to washing the human body, its parts, or the sacrificial pieces of an offering. In the ancient Near East, washing was not only a matter of personal hygiene in a dry, dusty climate, but also carried profound social and spiritual significance. To wash after a period of mourning was a physical demonstration that the time of lamentation had ended and a new season of life had begun.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of rachats is to wash, bathe, or rinse. It occurs over 70 times in the Hebrew Bible.

• In the active Qal stem, it means to wash or bathe oneself, or to wash parts of the body (such as hands or feet).

• In the passive Pual stem, it refers to being washed or cleansed.

• In the reflexive Hithpael stem, it denotes washing or bathing oneself thoroughly.

The word is used literally of bathing in a river or washing feet as an act of hospitality. Figuratively, it denotes moral and spiritual cleansing, the washing away of sin, or the declaration of innocence.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term rachats represents several distinct word pictures in the Old Testament:

• Hospitality and Refreshment: Washing the feet of guests was a fundamental courtesy in ancient Israel. It represents comfort, welcome, and restoration after a long journey.

• Priestly and Sacrificial Consecration: Before entering the Tabernacle or performing sacrifices, Aaron and his sons were required to wash their hands and feet at the bronze laver to avoid death. The inward parts of burnt offerings were also washed with water. This depicts the absolute holiness required to approach God.

• Moral and Spiritual Purity: It serves as a metaphor for spiritual reformation and the forgiveness of sins, representing the purging of moral defilement from the heart and hands.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, rachats highlights the necessity of cleansing before entering the presence of the holy or embarking on a divinely appointed task. It reminds the reader that humanity, in its natural and dusty state, requires purification to interact with the sacred. This earthly washing points forward to the New Testament concept of the washing of regeneration and being cleansed from sin. Cleansing is never merely external; the external washing is meant to reflect an internal reality of consecration, turning away from defilement, and entering into a covenant relationship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:3, Naomi gives Ruth specific instructions: "Therefore wash yourself and anoint yourself..." Since arriving from Moab, Ruth had lived in the status of a destitute, grieving widow. She had been working hard in the dusty fields of Boaz, wearing the garments of her widowhood. Naomi's command to wash is the first step in seeking redemption from Boaz. It is a deliberate action to cast off the dust of the fields and the garments of grief. By washing, Ruth prepares herself to meet her potential kinsman-redeemer not as a needy, dusty gleaner, but as a bride prepared for her husband. It represents a transition from death and loss to new life and hope.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 18:4 - "Please let a little water be brought, and wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree."

• Exodus 30:19 - "for Aaron and his sons shall wash their hands and their feet with water from it."

• 2 Samuel 12:20 - "So David arose from the ground, washed and anointed himself, and changed his clothes; and he went into the house of the LORD and worshiped."

• 2 Kings 5:10 - "And Elisha sent a messenger to him, saying, 'Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and your flesh shall be restored to you, and you shall be clean.'"

• Psalm 26:6 - "I will wash my hands in innocence; so I will go about Your altar, O LORD,"

• Isaiah 1:16 - "Wash yourselves, make yourselves clean; put away the evil of your doings from before My eyes. Cease to do evil,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The order of Naomi’s instructions in Ruth 3:3 is highly significant: wash, anoint, and put on garments. This mirrors the exact sequence used in Ezekiel 16:9-10, where God describes how He rescued, cleansed, anointed, and clothed Israel to make her His bride. Thus, Ruth’s preparation carries profound symbolic echoes of Israel being prepared for her covenant relationship with Yahweh.
