PUT - ( sum , Strong's H7760 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sum (also transliterated as sim) primarily means to put, place, set, or appoint. In Ruth 3:3, within Naomi’s strategic instructions to Ruth, the word is used in the sense of putting on clothing—specifically, putting on her best garments. It represents a transition from mourning to anticipation, symbolizing readiness, dignity, and purposeful presentation as Ruth prepares to meet Boaz at the threshing floor.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root sum is a highly versatile and common Hebrew verb, occurring over 500 times in the Old Testament. Its concrete, physical meaning is to take an object and position it in a specific location. It suggests deliberate action and intentional placement rather than an accidental or casual dropping of something. Whether placing a hand on someone's head for blessing, setting a crown upon a king, or appointing someone to a position of authority, sum implies order, arrangement, and purposeful intent. In the ancient Near East, the act of putting on specific garments was not merely for physical utility or fashion; it was a profound declaration of status, emotional state, or spiritual posture.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary lexical definitions of sum include to put, place, set, station, establish, or appoint. Depending on the grammatical context and the object of the verb, it carries several distinct nuances:

• To place or put an object in a physical location (e.g., placing the ark in the tabernacle).

• To array or clothe oneself (putting on garments, ornaments, or armor).

• To ordain, establish, or appoint a person to an office or task (e.g., setting a king over the people).

• To direct one’s attention or heart toward something (setting one's heart on).

In Ruth 3:3, the verb is conjugated in the second-person feminine singular (perfect with vav consecutive, vesamt), functioning as an imperative command: "and you shall put [on] your garments."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word sum frequently creates rich theological and practical word pictures in the Old Testament:

• The Sovereign Placement of God: God "sets" the boundaries of the sea (Proverbs 8:29) and "puts" His words in the mouths of His prophets (Isaiah 51:16).

• The Armor of Battle or Office: Putting on garments or armor (1 Samuel 17:38) signifies preparation for a specific duty or conflict.

• A Change of Identity: Changing garments (putting on new clothes) symbolises moving from one season of life to another, such as shifting from captive status to freedom, or from mourning to celebration (as seen in Joseph's life or the restoration of Jerusalem in Isaiah 52:1).

4. Theological Implications

In the narrative of redemption, the act of "putting on" garments carries deep spiritual weight. Throughout Scripture, garments reflect an individual's inward state or relational status. Naomi instructs Ruth to wash, anoint herself, and "put on" her garments. This marks the formal end of Ruth's period of mourning for her deceased husband.

Theologically, this mirrors the transition from the old life of grief, emptiness, and spiritual destitution to a new life of hope, covenant relationship, and redemption. Just as Ruth had to put off her widow's weeds and put on her best apparel to approach the kinsman-redeemer, believers are called to put off the old self and put on the garments of salvation, righteousness, and praise (Isaiah 61:3; Ephesians 4:22-24). It highlights human cooperation with divine grace—Ruth must actively prepare herself to receive the redemption Naomi envisions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:3, Naomi gives three instructions before Ruth goes to the threshing floor: wash, anoint, and put on garments. The Hebrew text literally reads "and put your garments upon you." The NKJV translates this as "put on your best garments" (interpreting the Hebrew simlah or "garment/mantle" in this context as her outer cloak or finest attire, rather than her daily working clothes).

This action is highly intentional. Ruth has spent months working hard in the dusty fields of Boaz as a poor gleaner, wearing the clothes of a destitute foreigner and a grieving widow. By "putting on" these garments, Ruth is presenting herself not as a beggar seeking scraps, but as a worthy woman seeking covenant marriage and redemption. It is an act of faith, stepping out of the identity of a widow and into the identity of a prospective bride.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew syntax, the placing of garments (sum / simlah) is closely connected to the concept of spreading a wing or cloak (kanap) over someone, which appears later in this same chapter (Ruth 3:9). When Ruth asks Boaz to "spread your cloak over your maidservant," she uses the imagery of covering and clothing. Thus, Ruth "putting on" her garments to meet him serves as a beautiful prelude to her petition for Boaz to "put" his protective covering over her.
