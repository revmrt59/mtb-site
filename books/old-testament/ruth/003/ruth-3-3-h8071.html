GARMENT - ( simlah , Strong's H8071 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word simlah refers to an outer garment, mantle, or cloak that served as a person's primary covering by day and blanket by night. In Ruth 3:3, Naomi instructs Ruth to put on her simlah—translated in the NKJV as "best garment"—signifying a pivotal transition in her life. Ruth is directed to shed the clothing of her widowhood, grief, and manual labor as a foreign gleaner, and instead put on the garment of preparation, presenting herself as a clean, honorable woman ready for covenant redemption and marriage.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The simlah was the most common outer garment worn by men and women in the ancient Near East. It was a large, rectangular piece of coarse wool or linen wrapped around the body over an inner tunic. The garment served a vital dual purpose: it was a protective wrap against the elements during the day and a warm blanket at night. Because of its protective necessity, the simlah was a person's most essential and valuable physical possession. Under the Mosaic Law, if a creditor took a neighbor's simlah as a pledge, he was commanded to return it before sunset so the owner would have a covering in which to sleep. In everyday life, the condition, quality, and style of one's simlah instantly communicated social status, occupation, and whether a person was in a state of mourning or celebration.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun simlah means a wrapper, mantle, cloak, or garment. It denotes the outer cloth wrapping that completes a person's attire. The plural form with the second-person feminine suffix in Ruth 3:3 (simlotayich) literally means "your garments." In this context, it functions as a Hebrew idiom for putting on one's finest outer cloak or "best garment." The semantic range of the word encompasses:

• An outer cloak or mantle used for warmth and protection.

• A general term for clothes or apparel.

• A sheet or large cloth used for carrying objects or covering things.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses simlah in several vivid word pictures:

• A Symbol of Destitution and Pledge: Because the simlah was a basic human right, taking it as a pledge represented extreme vulnerability. Returning it at nightfall was an act of mercy and righteousness.

• An Expression of Grief: Tearing the simlah was a physical manifestation of deep grief, trauma, or repentance. When Jacob believed Joseph was dead, and when Joshua faced defeat at Ai, they tore their outer clothes to express their brokenness.

• Provision and Preservation: In the wilderness, the preservation of the Israelites' simlah was a tangible sign of Yahweh's miraculous provision; their clothes did not wear out during the forty years of wandering.

### 4. Theological Implications

Clothing throughout the Scriptures carries profound spiritual significance, representing a person's internal state, identity, and standing before God.

• From Mourning to Joy: Changing one's garments represents a spiritual shift from sorrow to joy, from judgment to grace, and from uncleanness to purity.

• The Bride's Preparation: Ruth's preparation mirrors the spiritual preparation of the bride of Christ. She must wash, anoint herself, and put on her best garments to meet the redeemer.

• The Garment of Righteousness: Just as Ruth laid aside her garments of foreign widowhood to receive the covering of Boaz, believers lay aside the filthy rags of their own righteousness to put on the garments of salvation and the robe of Christ’s righteousness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:3, Naomi’s instructions to Ruth are deeply strategic and symbolic. Up to this point, Ruth had been wearing the clothes of a grieving Moabite widow and a low-class harvester, covered in the dust of the fields. To approach Boaz at the threshing floor in those clothes would signal that she was still a destitute solicitor of charity. Naomi tells her to wash, anoint herself with oil, and put on her simlah (her best garment). This was not an act of seduction, but of formal presentation. By changing her clothes, Ruth publicly signals that her period of mourning is over. She presents herself as a woman ready for redemption, covenant marriage, and a new life.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A beautiful linguistic connection exists between Ruth 3:3 and Ruth 3:9. In verse 3, Ruth puts on her own simlah (garment) to present herself to Boaz. In verse 9, when Boaz asks who she is, she replies, "I am Ruth, your maidservant. Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative." The Hebrew word for "wing" or "corner of a garment" is kanap. There is a poignant play on words: Ruth, having put on her best simlah (garment), asks Boaz to spread the kanap (the corner of his own mantle/garment) over her. This act was a formal ancient Near Eastern custom of claiming a woman in marriage and promising her protection. The garment she put on in preparation was ultimately met and covered by the garment of her redeemer.
