DRINKING - ( shathah , Strong's H8354 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shathah primarily refers to the physical act of drinking, consuming liquids, or feasting. In the Old Testament, it frequently appears alongside eating to depict the basic necessities of life, times of fellowship, or moments of celebration. In the context of Ruth 3:3, it describes Boaz finishing his evening meal at the threshing floor, marking a transition from labor and sustenance to rest, contentment, and vulnerability, which sets the stage for Ruth's courageous appeal.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root shathah is a common Semitic verb found throughout ancient Near Eastern languages, consistently referring to the consumption of liquids. In agricultural societies like ancient Israel, drinking was not merely a mechanical act of hydration but was deeply tied to the rhythms of harvest, hospitality, and communal life. Water, milk, and wine were the primary beverages. Drinking after a hard day of labor, particularly during the harvest season, symbolized the enjoyment of God's provision, the completion of work, and a state of relaxation. The threshing floor was a place of intense labor, but the evening meal and drink that followed represented a transition into peace, security, and satisfaction.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shathah means to drink, imbibe, or swallow liquid. It is used transitively with an object (such as water or wine) or intransitively to describe the act of drinking or feasting. Its semantic range includes:

• The physical consumption of water to quench thirst.

• Participating in a banquet, feast, or social gathering where wine is consumed.

• Figuratively, absorbing or taking in something in large quantities (such as violence or injustice).

In its nominal and participial forms, it can refer to banquet halls or the state of drinking. In Ruth 3:3, the infinitive construct form describes Boaz completing his meal, specifically his drinking, signaling that his heart was merry and he was ready for sleep.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb shathah is frequently paired with akal (to eat) to describe the standard human experience of nourishment or celebration.

• Hospitality: Serving a guest a drink is a primary gesture of hospitality and safety in ancient Near Eastern culture, as when Rebekah offered water to Abraham's servant.

• Covenant and Celebration: To eat and drink in the presence of God or with others often sealed covenants, marked festivals, or celebrated victories.

• Judgment and Wrath: Figuratively, the prophets speak of drinking the cup of God’s wrath, portraying judgment as a bitter liquid that must be consumed to the dregs.

• Spiritual Satisfaction: The word is used metaphorically for finding delight and satisfaction in wisdom or in God's presence.

4. Theological Implications

In the Old Testament, eating and drinking are gifts from God, meant to be received with thanksgiving. Ecclesiastes repeatedly emphasizes that for a person to eat, drink, and enjoy the good of their labor is a gift of God. However, physical drinking also serves as a boundary marker: it can lead to drunken self-indulgence (as with Noah or Lot) or it can be a holy expression of joy and gratitude. In the redemptive-historical narrative of Ruth, Boaz's eating and drinking represent the quiet, ordinary blessings of a faithful Israelite living under the covenant. After years of famine in Israel, the fact that Boaz is eating and drinking in abundance at the harvest is a testament to Yahweh's restored favor and provision to His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:3, Naomi instructs Ruth to wait until Boaz has finished eating and drinking before she approaches him. This timing is highly strategic and culturally significant. Naomi knows that after Boaz has eaten and drunk, he will be in a state of contentment, peace, and physical relaxation ("his heart was cheerful," Ruth 3:7). It is a vulnerable and private moment, away from the bustling activity of the daytime harvest. By waiting for him to finish his shathah, Ruth ensures that her petition is presented in a quiet, undisturbed setting where Boaz can focus entirely on her request without the distractions of his workers or the stress of the day's labor. It highlights Naomi’s wisdom and Ruth's careful obedience.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The pairing of eating and drinking in the ancient world was a social equalizer. Whether rich or poor, all human beings shared this daily need. In the book of Ruth, which transitions from extreme lack and famine (chapter 1) to gleaning and survival (chapter 2), the abundance of eating and drinking in chapter 3 highlights the theme of redemption. The transition from starvation to satisfaction mirrors the spiritual reality of God's people being brought out of spiritual famine into the rich banquet of His covenant love.
