UNCOVER - ( galah , Strong's H1540 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb galah primarily means to uncover, remove, disclose, or reveal. In the context of Ruth 3:4, where Naomi instructs Ruth to "uncover his feet and lie down," the word carries a literal meaning of pulling back a coverlet, while serving as a highly significant cultural gesture of petition. It denotes a humble act of submission and a formal appeal for protection and marriage under the custom of the kinsman-redeemer. By uncovering Boaz’s feet, Ruth positions herself as a servant seeking refuge under the "wings" of her redeemer, demonstrating faith, vulnerability, and honor rather than inappropriate forwardness.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root concept of galah involves stripping away a barrier or removing a covering to expose what was previously hidden. In the ancient Near East, coverings—such as garments, tents, or veils—represented protection, identity, and personal boundaries. To uncover someone or something was to lay it bare, removing its concealment or defense. In an agrarian society, this verb was used for clearing land, exposing the foundations of a building, or exposing a person to the elements. In the narrative of Ruth, the setting is the threshing floor at night. Uncovering the feet of a sleeping man was a specific, culturally understood gesture. By exposing Boaz's feet to the cool night air, Ruth gently and non-verbally awakened him, signaling her presence and her humble petition without causing a public disturbance.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb galah is a primitive root occurring over 180 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its semantic range spans several distinct areas depending on the grammatical stem:

• To uncover or lay bare: Exposing what is covered, such as a physical object, a body part, or the ground.

• To reveal or disclose: Uncovering information, secrets, or divine truth (e.g., God "uncovering" the ear of a prophet to speak to him, or revealing His glory).

• To go into exile: A secondary development of the word meaning "to strip a land of its inhabitants" or "to depart bare," which describes captivity and deportation.

• In the active Piel stem used in Ruth 3:4 (u-gilliyt), the verb means "to uncover," specifically referring to the physical action of removing a covering from a specific area.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs galah in various descriptive ways:

• The Exposed Ear: A beautiful idiom for revelation is "to uncover the ear" (galah ozen), depicting the pulling back of long hair or a head covering to whisper a secret. It indicates intimate, direct communication from God to man.

• Exile as Nakedness: The word describes nations being stripped of their defenses and deported. To be exiled is to be "uncovered" before one's enemies, exposed to shame and vulnerability.

• Exposing Nakedness: In legal and priestly contexts, such as Leviticus, the phrase "uncover nakedness" is a technical term for illicit sexual relations. Crucially, the author of Ruth avoids this terminology, using "uncover his feet" (margetav—the place of his feet) rather than "uncover nakedness" (erwah), emphasizing the purity of Ruth's actions.

### 4. Theological Implications

The act of uncovering Boaz’s feet represents a physical enactment of prayer, faith, and covenant-seeking. Ruth had previously been commended by Boaz for seeking refuge under the "wings" of the Lord God of Israel (Ruth 2:12). By uncovering his feet and asking him to spread his cloak over her (Ruth 3:9), Ruth asks Boaz to be the physical answer to that prayer. This action underscores the biblical doctrine of redemption through the goel (kinsman-redeemer). Redemption is not an aggressive demand but a vulnerable appeal. Ruth’s submission at the feet of Boaz mirrors the posture of any believer coming to Christ—acknowledging helplessness, seeking shelter, and trusting in the redeemer's character to cover, protect, and restore.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:4, Naomi instructs Ruth: "...go, uncover his feet, and lie down; and he will tell you what you should do."

• A Posture of a Servant: In ancient times, lying at a person's feet was the place of a servant. Ruth does not sit at his side as an equal or slide under the covers next to him; she lies perpendicular at his feet, demonstrating her status as his handmaid.

• A Request for Marriage: Uncovering the feet was a silent proposal. It set the stage for Ruth to ask Boaz to spread the corner of his garment over her. In Ezekiel 16:8, God uses this exact imagery to describe His covenant marriage to Israel.

• Absolute Propriety: The narrative guards the moral integrity of both characters. By using galah in relation only to Boaz's feet, the text frames this as a righteous, legal appeal for marriage under the levirate and redemption customs, completely free from the taint of immorality.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Play on Words: There is a subtle thematic connection throughout the book of Ruth between "covering" and "uncovering." Ruth's life had been stripped bare (galah in the sense of loss and exposure) by the deaths of her husband, father-in-law, and brother-in-law. By "uncovering" Boaz's feet, she initiates the process that will lead to her being permanently "covered" and protected by a righteous husband.

• The Cloak and the Wing: The Hebrew word for the corner of a garment (kanaph) is the exact same word used for "wings." Thus, when Ruth asks Boaz to cover her with his garment, she is directly linking his human protection to the divine shadow of God's wings under which she has trusted.
