FEET - ( mergeloth , Strong's H4772 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mergeloth literally refers to the "place of the feet" or the feet themselves. Occurring only five times in the Old Testament—four times in the book of Ruth and once in Daniel—it carries a highly specific contextual nuance. In Ruth 3, it designates the foot-end of Boaz's sleeping place on the threshing floor. Ruth’s action of uncovering his feet and lying down there was a culturally understood gesture of submission, appeal for protection, and a symbolic request for marriage under the custom of the kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun mergeloth is derived from the root regel, which is the standard Hebrew word for "foot." While regel refers to the physical foot itself, mergeloth is a locational noun that specifically denotes the place where the feet lie, the foot-end of a bed, or the lower extremities of a reclining person. In the ancient Near Eastern agricultural context, landowners and workers frequently slept at the threshing floors during harvest season to guard their valuable grain from thieves. Sleeping at a person's mergeloth was the position of a servant or dependent, signaling complete submission, vulnerability, and trust.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term mergeloth means the place of the feet, the foot-end of a bed, or simply the feet of a reclining person. It defines the physical space occupied by or immediately surrounding a person's feet while they are lying down. Unlike common terms for body parts, mergeloth is primarily spatial and situational, describing a posture of proximity and humble positioning relative to another person.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Submission and Servanthood: In the book of Ruth, mergeloth is used exclusively in the context of Ruth placing herself at the feet of Boaz. By choosing the foot-end of his bed rather than sleeping beside him as an equal, Ruth assumes the posture of a humble maidservant.

• Radiance and Majesty: In prophetic literature, specifically in Daniel, the term describes the lower extremities of a celestial being. Here, the word picture shifts from agricultural simplicity to heavenly majesty, portraying feet that shine with the brilliant luster of polished bronze.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of mergeloth in the narrative of Ruth centers on redemption and covenant. When Ruth places herself at Boaz's feet, she is enacting a living petition for covenant protection. Earlier in the narrative, Boaz prayed that Ruth would find full reward under the "wings" of Yahweh, the God of Israel (Ruth 2:12). At the threshing floor, by laying at his mergeloth and asking him to spread his cloak over her, Ruth asks Boaz to be the physical instrument of Yahweh’s protective wings. This demonstrates that redemption requires coming to the lowest place, waiting in complete dependence on the redeemer to act.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:4, Naomi instructs Ruth to wait until Boaz has finished eating and drinking, note where he lies down, and then "uncover his feet" (mergeloth) and lie down. This action was culturally precise and modest. Uncovering Boaz's lower legs would eventually cause him to wake up from the midnight chill, allowing for a private, quiet conversation without causing a public scene. It was a formal, quiet appeal for Boaz to fulfill the role of the goel (kinsman-redeemer) by marrying her and preserving the family line of Elimelech.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 3:7 - "And when Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was cheerful, he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain; and she came softly, uncovered his feet, and lay down."

• Ruth 3:8 - "Now it happened at midnight that the man was startled, and turned himself; and there, a woman was lying at his feet."

• Ruth 3:14 - "So she lay at his feet until morning, and she arose before one could recognize another."

• Daniel 10:6 - "His body was like beryl, his face like the appearance of lightning, his eyes like torches of fire, his arms and feet like burnished bronze in color, and the sound of his words like the voice of a multitude."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Some modern commentators attempt to attribute a sexual euphemism to "uncovering the feet" in Ruth 3. However, the lexical use of mergeloth and the broader biblical narrative strongly refute this. The text repeatedly emphasizes the moral integrity and virtue (chayil) of both Ruth and Boaz. Boaz immediately commends Ruth for her kindness (chesed) and vows to resolve the legal matter of redemption at the city gate first thing in the morning. The use of mergeloth underscores a highly respectful, legally binding, and morally pure petition for redemption.
