TELL - ( nagad , Strong's H5046 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nagad means to declare, announce, make known, or report. It carries the primary idea of bringing something to light or placing it conspicuously before someone's eyes. In Ruth 3:4, Naomi assures Ruth that once she takes the courageous step of faith and submits to Boaz's authority, he will "tell" her what to do. Rather than leaving her in suspense, Boaz will provide clear, authoritative instruction and declare the path forward, stepping into his role as her potential redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The verbal root of nagad does not appear in the basic Qal stem in Biblical Hebrew, but occurs almost exclusively in the Hiphil (causative) and Hophal (passive) stems.

• The original concrete concept of the root relates to being high, prominent, or conspicuous, as seen in the related prepositional noun neged, which means "in front of" or "before."

• To "tell" (nagad) is to place a matter directly "in front of" someone, bringing what was hidden, uncertain, or private into the open. It is not merely casual speaking, but the deliberate disclosure of vital information, instructions, or a formal declaration.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• In the Hiphil stem, nagad means to declare, announce, report, make known, explain, or inform.

• It denotes making a clear, unmistakable statement that answers a question, solves a dilemma, or reveals a secret.

• The semantic range includes:

• To report or deliver a message (such as bringing news of a battle).

• To explain or interpret a riddle, dream, or difficult matter.

• To declare or confess openly, making one's identity or intentions known.

• To issue authoritative directions or commands.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• nagad is frequently used when a messenger brings report of major events, standing before a leader to lay out the facts clearly so there is no ambiguity.

• It is used of Samson "telling" his riddle (Judges 14) and Joseph "telling" his dreams (Genesis 37). In these contexts, it signifies turning obscurity into absolute clarity.

• The word picture is that of a messenger standing directly before a superior, presenting truth in a way that demands attention and response.

4. Theological Implications

• In the Old Testament, God is often the subject of nagad. He "declares" His word to Jacob and His statutes to Israel (Psalm 147:19). God does not keep His covenant community in the dark; He reveals His character, His law, and His future plans.

• In the context of redemption, the act of "telling" represents divine guidance and the revelation of grace. When God's people submit to Him, He makes their path straight, declaring the steps they must take.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:4, Naomi instructs Ruth on how to approach Boaz at the threshing floor. Ruth is to put herself in a vulnerable, petitionary posture at Boaz's feet.

• Naomi's promise—"he will tell you what you should do"—conveys absolute confidence in Boaz's character and legal responsibility. As a potential goel (kinsman-redeemer), Boaz will not take advantage of Ruth, nor will he leave her in social limbo.

• The use of nagad indicates that Boaz will take charge of the situation and lay out a clear, definitive plan of action. His "telling" will establish the legal and relational steps required for her redemption.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:11 - "And He said, 'Who told [nagad] you that you were naked? Have you eaten from the tree of which I commanded you that you should not eat?'"

• Joshua 2:14 - "So the men answered her, 'Our lives for yours, if none of you tell [nagad] this business of ours. And it shall be, when the LORD has given us the land, that we will deal kindly and truly with you.'"

• 1 Samuel 24:18 - "And you have shown [nagad] this day how you have dealt well with me; for when the LORD delivered me into your hand, you did not kill me."

• Psalm 19:1 - "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament shows [nagad] His handiwork."

• Micah 6:8 - "He has shown [nagad] you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In Ruth 3:16, the same verb nagad is used when Ruth returns to Naomi: "she told her all that the man had done for her." The story begins with Naomi assuring Ruth that Boaz will tell her what to do, and ends with Ruth telling Naomi how he fulfilled that promise.

• The word emphasizes transition from mystery to resolution. Just as Boaz's instructions resolved Ruth's uncertainty, his eventual public declaration at the city gate resolved the crisis of the family inheritance.
