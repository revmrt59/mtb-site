LIES - ( shakab , Strong's H7901 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shakab primarily means to lie down, recline, or sleep. In the context of the book of Ruth, it transitions from a simple physical description of resting after hard labor to a profound, symbolic act of submission and covenantal appeal. By observing where Boaz lies down, Ruth positions herself in quiet humility at his feet, signaling her desire for his protection, redemption, and marriage. This word highlights the delicate balance between human vulnerability and righteous, covenant-keeping faith.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, agricultural life dictated the daily rhythm of work and rest. The threshing floor was a place of intense, joyous labor during the harvest, followed by overnight stays to guard the grain from thieves. At the end of the day, workers would recline on the straw or ground, wrapping themselves in their cloaks for warmth and security. The word shakab paints a picture of setting aside the day's tools, relinquishing active labor, and entering a state of rest, vulnerability, and sleep. To lie down on the threshing floor was a normal, necessary practice of self-preservation and recovery for the master of the estate and his workers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shakab occurs over 200 times in the Hebrew Bible. It possesses a well-defined semantic range:

• Physical reclining for sleep, rest, or recovery (Genesis 28:11; 1 Kings 19:5).

• The posture of sickness or physical weakness (2 Samuel 13:6).

• The sleep of death, often expressed in the historical books as "slept with his fathers" (1 Kings 2:10).

• Sexual relations, either lawful or unlawful, typically indicated by the addition of the preposition "with" (Genesis 39:7; 2 Samuel 11:4).

In Ruth 3:4, the primary lexical meaning is the literal, physical act of reclining for sleep at the end of the winnowing process.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Rest and Safety: Throughout the Old Testament, the ability to shakab in peace is a sign of divine favor and national security. When God blesses Israel, they can lie down without fear of wild beasts or invading armies.

• Surrender of Control: Lying down is an act of trust. A person who is reclined and asleep is defenseless. When Boaz lies down at the edge of the grain heap, he is trusting God and his servants for his safety.

• Covenantal Submission: Ruth's action of lying down at Boaz's feet is a lived-out word picture. She does not lie down beside him (which would carry sexual or egalitarian connotations), but at his feet, the traditional posture of a servant awaiting orders or a petitioner seeking mercy.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of shakab in Ruth 3 is found in its connection to the concept of rest (menuchah). In Ruth 3:1, Naomi states her desire to find "rest" or security for Ruth. This rest is not merely physical sleep, but the permanent, covenantal security of a home and a husband.

Ironically, to secure this permanent "rest," Ruth must go to the threshing floor and lie down (shakab) at the feet of her potential redeemer. This demonstrates that true covenant rest is obtained through humble submission to the redeemer. It prefigures the New Testament invitation to find rest in Christ by coming to Him in complete surrender.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:4, the verb shakab is used three times in rapid succession: "when he lies down... where he lies... and lie down." This repetition serves several narrative purposes:

• It emphasizes Naomi's precise tactical instructions. Ruth must not disrupt Boaz's work or meal; she must wait until he has entered his state of rest.

• It highlights the tension of the scene. The potential for misunderstanding is immense. Because shakab can refer to sexual intimacy, a late-night encounter at a threshing floor could easily be misconstrued as scandalous.

• By focusing on the physical location ("at his feet"), the text carefully guards the moral purity of the encounter. Ruth's "lying down" is an act of bold, righteous faith, claiming the protection of the kinsman-redeemer under the law of Moses, rather than an act of seduction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 6:7: "You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, and when you rise up."

• Leviticus 26:6: "I will give peace in the land, and you shall lie down, and no one will make you afraid; I will rid the land of evil beasts, and the sword will not go through your land."

• Psalm 4:8: "I will both lie down in peace, and sleep; For You alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The threshing floor was located on an elevated, breezy area outside the city gates. While it was a public space by day, it became isolated and dark by night. Ruth's willingness to go to this place and lie down at the feet of Boaz demonstrates her absolute trust in his character. She knew Boaz to be a man of "worthy character" (chayil), just as he knew her to be a "virtuous woman" (Ruth 3:11). The vulnerability of the posture of shakab was met with the protective, honorable response of a true redeemer.
