HEART - ( leb , Strong's H3820 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word leb represents the inner man, serving as the seat of physical life, intellect, emotion, will, and moral choice. In the Old Testament, the heart is not merely the organ that pumps blood or the seat of fleeting emotions; it is the center of the entire human personality. In the context of Ruth 3:7, Boaz's "merry" heart reflects a state of deep contentment, peace, and gratitude following a successful harvest, demonstrating his satisfaction with God's physical and providential blessings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The ancient Near Eastern concept of the heart (leb) differs significantly from the modern Western view. While modern culture associates the heart almost exclusively with romantic or poetic emotion, the ancient Hebrews viewed the heart as the central workshop of human existence. It is the core from which all thoughts, decisions, desires, and actions flow. It is the command center of the person, integrating the mind, the will, and the emotions into a unified whole.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun leb refers to the inner man, the mind, the will, the heart, and the seat of emotions. It encompasses intellectual capabilities (understanding, reflection, and wisdom), emotional states (joy, sorrow, courage, and fear), and volitional choices (purposes, decisions, and moral determinations). In passages describing eating and drinking, a "good" or "merry" heart denotes a state of contentment, well-being, and cheerfulness resulting from physical nourishment, safety, and peaceful circumstances.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses leb in several vivid word pictures:

• The heart as a tablet: God's commands are to be written upon the tablet of the heart, indicating deep internalization and remembrance.

• The heart as a fountain: It is the spring from which all the issues of life flow, requiring diligent guarding.

• The heart as a vessel: It can be broken by grief, hardened by rebellion, or enlarged by joy and divine understanding.

• The heart as a guide: When applied to the mind, it represents the seat of wisdom; when applied to the spiritual life, it represents the arena of moral decision-making.

4. Theological Implications

The heart is the primary object of God's evaluation and transformation. Because the heart represents the true self, God looks past outward appearances to examine the heart. Human spiritual ruin begins in the heart, and redemption requires a circumcised, cleansed, and renewed heart. Spiritual devotion is measured by whether one loves and serves God with the "whole heart," indicating undivided loyalty and authentic worship rather than mere outward ritual.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:7, Boaz's heart is described as "merry" (yatab) after eating and drinking. This phrase indicates physical satisfaction, emotional peace, and quiet joy. It marks the end of a long, arduous harvest season fraught with famine and uncertainty. Boaz's merry heart is not a state of drunken revelry, but a state of tranquil gratitude and contentment under God’s abundant provision. This peaceful, relaxed state of mind sets the stage for Ruth's vulnerable, faith-filled petition at his feet, highlighting Boaz's gentle, righteous response as a kinsman-redeemer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:5: "Then the LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intent of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually."

• Judges 19:6: "So they sat down and ate and drank together, both of them. And the young woman’s father said to the man, 'Please be pleased to stay all night, and let your heart be merry.'"

• Psalm 51:10: "Create in me a clean heart, O God, And renew a steadfast spirit within me."

• Proverbs 3:5: "Trust in the LORD with all your heart, And lean not on your own understanding;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The close connection between the physical state (eating and drinking) and the emotional state (a merry heart) in the Old Testament underscores the holistic view of human nature in Hebrew thought. Body and soul are not sharply divided; physical refreshment naturally leads to emotional and spiritual thanksgiving. This holistic satisfaction prepares Boaz to act with clear-minded integrity, justice, and kindness when confronted with Ruth's midnight request.
