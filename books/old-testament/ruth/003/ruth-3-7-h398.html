HAD EATEN - ( 'akal , Strong's H398 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'akal primarily means to eat, consume, or devour. In Ruth 3:7, it describes Boaz participating in the post-harvest feast, representing nourishment, satisfaction, and the completion of a hard season of labor. Beyond physical sustenance, the term reflects communal joy, divine provision, and a transition from anxious labor to peaceful rest under God's covenant care.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root 'akal is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible. Its most basic concrete sense is the physical act of eating food to sustain life. In the ancient Near East, eating was rarely a solitary, utilitarian act; it was deeply social, covenantal, and religious. Sharing a meal symbolized fellowship, peace, and mutual obligation. In agrarian Israel, eating the fruits of the harvest was the ultimate celebration of Yahweh's covenant faithfulness, as the agricultural cycle depended entirely on His rain and blessing.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'akal possesses a broad semantic range centered on consumption:

• To eat, chew, and swallow food for physical nourishment.

• To consume or devour, used metaphorically of fire (consuming wood), war/the sword (devouring people), or pests (destroying crops).

• To enjoy, participate in, or experience the benefits of something (such as eating the good of the land).

In the Qal stem (as found in Ruth 3:7), it denotes the active, literal consumption of food, indicating physical replenishment and satisfaction.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Sustenance and Fellowship: Eating together sealed covenants (Genesis 26:30) and marked sacred feasts (Exodus 18:12).

• Metaphor for Destruction: Fire "eats" offerings or cities (Leviticus 9:24; Amos 1:10), and the sword "devours" (2 Samuel 2:26).

• Metaphor for Assimilation: Jeremiah "ate" God's words, finding them to be the joy and rejoicing of his heart (Jeremiah 15:16).

• Covenant Blessing vs. Curse: Obedience leads to eating "the good of the land" (Isaiah 1:19), while disobedience results in being "devoured by the sword" (Isaiah 1:20).

### 4. Theological Implications

Eating in the Old Testament is directly tied to the doctrine of Providence. Man does not live by bread alone, but by every word from the mouth of God (Deuteronomy 8:3); yet, the physical bread is a tangible expression of that divine word. When Boaz eats, he is tasting the very goodness of God who "visited His people in giving them bread" (Ruth 1:6). The transition from the famine of Ruth chapter 1 to the eating and drinking of chapter 3 showcases the progression of redemption. God moves His people from spiritual and physical starvation back to a state of blessed fullness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:7, the phrase "Boaz had eaten and drunk" occurs at the threshing floor after the winnowing of barley. This meal represents the culmination of the harvest. The labor is done, the grain is secured, and Boaz enjoys the fruit of his labor with a cheerful heart.

The contextual placement of this eating is critical: it establishes Boaz's vulnerability and his state of peace. He is not eating in gluttony or drunkenness, but in grateful contentment. This sets the stage for Ruth's humble approach. Because Boaz has eaten and is satisfied, the atmosphere is ripe for grace, covenant redemption, and the security (rest) that Naomi sought for Ruth.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:16: "And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, 'Of every tree of the garden you may freely eat;'"

• Exodus 24:11: "But on the nobles of the children of Israel He did not lay His hand. So they saw God, and they ate and drank."

• Deuteronomy 8:10: "When you have eaten and are full, then you shall bless the LORD your God for the good land which He has given you."

• Psalm 22:26: "The poor shall eat and be satisfied; Those who seek Him will praise the LORD."

• Isaiah 1:19: "If you are willing and obedient, You shall eat the good of the land;"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The pairing of "eating and drinking" ('akal and shatah) is a common biblical merism representing the complete enjoyment of life’s basic blessings (Ecclesiastes 2:24, 3:13, 5:18). It contrasts sharply with the fasts of mourning or distress.

In Ruth 2:14, Boaz had already invited Ruth to "eat of the bread" at his table, where she "ate and was satisfied." In Ruth 3:7, Boaz now eats and is satisfied. This symmetry highlights that the redeemer and the redeemed share the same covenant table and the same divine abundance.
