FEET - ( margelah , Strong's H4772 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word margelah refers specifically to the place of the feet, the foot area, or the feet themselves. Occurring only five times in the Old Testament—strictly within the books of Ruth and Daniel—this term carries a distinct spatial and relational nuance. In the narrative of Ruth 3, it denotes the place at Boaz’s feet where Ruth submissively lays down. This act is not one of impropriety but of deep cultural and covenantal significance, representing an appeal for protection, redemption, and marriage under the wings of the Almighty and the guardianship of a kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• margelah is a denominative noun derived from the common Hebrew root regel (foot). While regel refers to the physical foot itself, margelah shifts the focus to the location or space occupied by the feet—the "footplace" or "the place of the feet."

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, the feet represented subjection, vulnerability, and petition. To place oneself at someone's feet was an act of complete humility, recognizing their authority and seeking their favor.

• The background of Ruth 3 involves the threshing floor, a place of hard work, harvest celebration, and vulnerability at night. Sleeping on the threshing floor was common to guard the grain from thieves.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The noun margelah means "place of the feet" or simply "feet" when referring to the lower extremities of a reclining person.

• It denotes the foot-end of a bed, couch, or sleeping mat.

• The semantic range is highly localized, specifically pointing to the area surrounding or covering the feet of an individual, rather than the anatomical foot itself in motion.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word is used almost exclusively in two contexts in the Hebrew Bible: the narrative of Ruth in the threshing floor scene (Ruth 3:4, 7, 8, 14) and the prophetic vision of the metal-and-clay feet of the statue in Daniel 10:6.

• Word Picture: In Ruth, margelah creates a picture of quiet, humble positioning. It highlights the boundary of a person's personal space. By positioning herself at his "foot-place," Ruth takes the position of a servant, placing herself at his mercy without invading his personal upper-body space, which would imply inappropriate intimacy.

4. Theological Implications

• Redemption and Refuge: Ruth’s action at the margelah of Boaz is a physical reenactment of her spiritual reality. In Ruth 2:12, Boaz prayed that Ruth would find refuge "under the wings" of Yahweh. In Ruth 3:9, Ruth asks Boaz to "spread your wing [skirt/corner of garment] over your maidservant." By lying at his feet, she seeks the physical manifestation of God's protective wings through the agency of the kinsman-redeemer (goel).

• Humility and Faith: True redemption begins at the feet of the redeemer. Ruth does not demand her rights; she submits herself completely, waiting for Boaz's response. This represents the posture of faith—vulnerable, expectant, and humble.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 3:7, Boaz has eaten, drunk, and his heart is cheerful. He lies down at the end of the heap of grain. Ruth comes softly, uncovers his margelah (the place of his feet), and lies down.

• Uncovering the feet serves a practical and symbolic purpose. Practically, the cold night air hitting Boaz's exposed feet would gently wake him without startling him. Symbolically, it was a culturally understood petition for marriage and protection.

• The use of margelah instead of the standard regel underscores that Ruth stayed at the periphery of his sleeping area, maintaining a posture of modesty and respect. It refutes any implication of sexual immorality; she was at his "foot-place," waiting as a petitioner.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 3:4 - "But it shall be, when he lies down, that you shall notice the place where he lies; and you shall go in, uncover his feet, and lie down; and he will tell you what you should do."

• Ruth 3:8 - "Now it happened at midnight that the man was startled, and turned himself; and there, a woman was lying at his feet."

• Ruth 3:14 - "So she lay at his feet until morning, and she arose before one could recognize another. Then he said, 'Do not let it be known that the woman came to the threshing floor.'"

• Daniel 10:6 - "His body was like beryl, his face like the appearance of lightning, his eyes like torches of fire, his arms and feet like burnished bronze in color, and the sound of his words like the voice of a multitude."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between margelah (place of the feet) and regel (foot) highlights the narrative's delicacy. While regel can occasionally be used as a euphemism in certain Hebrew contexts, the deliberate, repeated use of margelah in Ruth 3 emphasizes the literal location at the end of the bed/mat, reinforcing the purity and honorable intentions of both Ruth and Boaz.

• The connection to Daniel 10:6 shows that margelah can also refer to the lower limbs/feet in a grand, visual description, emphasizing them as the stable base or foundation of a glorious figure.
