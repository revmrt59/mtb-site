GRAIN - ( aremah , Strong's H6194 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word aremah refers to a heap, pile, or mound, most frequently associated with harvested agricultural produce such as grain, sheaves, or fruit. In Ruth 3:7, it describes the large heap of winnowed barley at the threshing floor where Boaz slept. This term highlights the abundance of the harvest, Yahweh's gracious provision to Bethlehem after years of famine, and the setting of security and blessing wherein Ruth seeks Boaz's protection as her kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the agricultural life of ancient Israel, the threshing floor was a place of intense labor, community celebration, and security. After the grain was harvested, the stalks were brought to an elevated, hard-packed dirt floor. Oxen would tread over them to separate the grain from the chaff, and workers would throw the mixture into the air using winnowing forks. The wind blew the lighter chaff away, while the heavier grain fell back down to the earth.

Over days of winnowing, this grain accumulated into a massive pile or mound—the aremah. This heap represented the culmination of the year’s labor and, more importantly, God’s physical answer to the prayers of His people. Because these heaps of grain were highly valuable and subject to theft by bandits or desperate travelers, the owner or a trusted worker slept at the edge of the pile to guard it through the night.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun aremah means a heap, pile, or accumulation of material. It is derived from the verbal root aram, which means to heap up, pile up, or accumulate.

While aremah most frequently refers to heaps of agricultural bounty (such as wheat, barley, or sheaves), its usage is determined by the context:

• Agricultural Heaps: Piles of sheaves or grain gathered together during harvest (Ruth 3:7; Song of Solomon 7:2).

• Heaps of Rubbish or Ruins: Piles of debris, stone, and dirt left behind by war, destruction, or decay (Nehemiah 4:2).

• Heaps of Dedicated Gifts: Piles of tithes, offerings, and agricultural produce brought by the people of God to the temple chambers (2 Chronicles 31:6-9).

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, aremah serves as a vivid visual aid to describe both blessing and ruin.

In positive contexts, the aremah represents overflow, health, and vitality. For instance, in Song of Solomon 7:2, the groom describes his bride’s belly as "a heap of wheat set about with lilies." This metaphor uses the image of a grain heap to denote nourishment, fruitfulness, warmth, and physical well-being. Similarly, the piles of offerings in King Hezekiah's day (2 Chronicles 31:6) showed the physical evidence of Israel's spiritual renewal and generous response to God.

In negative contexts, the word captures the tragic reality of devastation. When Sanballat mocks the rebuilding of Jerusalem in Nehemiah 4:2, he asks if the Jews can "revive the stones from the heaps of rubbish." Here, aremah paints a picture of complete destruction, where once-glorious walls have been reduced to disorganized, dusty mounds.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of the aremah in the book of Ruth carries profound covenantal and theological weight.

First, the heap of grain is a tangible sign of God's covenant faithfulness. The story begins with a severe famine in Bethlehem (which literally means "House of Bread"), driving Elimelech's family to Moab. By Ruth 3:7, the famine has been broken, and the threshing floor is piled high with grain. This is a physical demonstration that Yahweh has visited His people and restored life, sustenance, and hope to Israel.

Second, the aremah serves as the backdrop for redemption. It is at this site of divine provision that Ruth seeks personal, relational redemption from Boaz. The abundance of the harvest points to the even greater spiritual abundance found in the kinsman-redeemer, who does not merely provide food for a day, but secures a future, a name, and a lineage for the empty widow.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:7, Boaz eats, drinks, and his heart is "cheerful" (or "good"). He then goes to sleep at the end of the "heap of grain" (aremah).

The term "cheerful" does not imply drunkenness; rather, it reflects the deep, quiet satisfaction of a godly man who has witnessed God's hand of blessing at the end of a long, grueling harvest season. By choosing to sleep at the edge of the aremah, Boaz acts as a responsible steward of the resources God has entrusted to him.

When Ruth approaches him secretly in the dark, she enters the space of his security. She places herself at the foot of the man who guards the heap of grain. Her request for him to "spread your wings over your maidservant" (Ruth 3:9) is a plea for him to extend the same protective care over her life that he is currently extending over his grain. The heap of grain is the literal and symbolic threshold where physical survival meets covenantal redemption.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern culture, the threshing floor was also a public space of judicial and social transactions because of its flat, open nature. However, at night, it became a quiet, private sanctuary for the workers. Ruth's choice to approach Boaz at the aremah during the night shows a deliberate desire for privacy and discretion, protecting both of their reputations as they negotiated a delicate matter of levirate redemption.

The contrast between the "empty" hands of Naomi and Ruth in chapter 1 and the massive "heap" of grain in chapter 3 highlights the narrative arc of the book: God moves His children from emptiness to fullness, from famine to an overflowing aremah.
