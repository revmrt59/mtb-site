DRUNK - ( shathah , Strong's H8354 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shathah means simply to drink, representing the basic human action of consuming liquids for sustenance, refreshment, or celebration. In Ruth 3:7, where it is translated in the past perfect tense as "drunk" (referring to Boaz having finished his meal), the word denotes completion and satisfaction at the end of the harvest season. Rather than suggesting intoxication, it portrays a scene of peaceful contentment, gratitude for God's physical provision, and rest after labor, setting the stage for Ruth's bold appeal to him as her kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, particularly within the agricultural rhythm of Israel, eating and drinking were deeply connected to community, survival, and worship. The completion of the harvest was the most joyous time of the year. After months of grueling labor under the sun, reaping, binding, and threshing the grain, the workers gathered at the threshing floor to winnow the barley. To sit down at the end of the day, eat bread, and drink water or wine was a physical expression of God's faithfulness in fulfilling His promise of rain and harvest. The act of drinking (shathah) in this setting represents the transition from weary labor to rest, security, and joy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shathah is a primary Hebrew root that refers directly to the act of drinking. It is used to describe:

• The physical ingestion of liquids, such as water (Genesis 24:14), milk (Judges 4:19), wine (Genesis 9:21), or vinegar (Numbers 6:3).

• Participation in a feast or banquet, where eating and drinking occur together as an expression of covenant, fellowship, or celebration (Genesis 26:30).

• Figurative expressions of taking in or absorbing something, such as drinking iniquity like water (Job 15:16) or drinking from the cup of God's wrath (Isaiah 51:17).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, shathah is frequently paired with the verb akal (to eat). This word pair ("eating and drinking") serves as a standard biblical idiom for the enjoyment of life's basic necessities and blessings.

• It is a picture of covenant fellowship. When God made a covenant with Israel at Mount Sinai, the elders of Israel went up the mountain, beheld God, and "ate and drank" (Exodus 24:11) in His presence, signaling peace and communion.

• It is a picture of divine hospitality. Wisdom builds her house, prepares her feast, and invites the simple to come, eat of her bread, and "drink" of the wine she has mixed (Proverbs 9:5), symbolizing the internalization of divine truth.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of food and drink in the narrative of Ruth carries profound theological weight. The book of Ruth begins with a severe famine in Bethlehem (the "House of Bread"), causing Elimelech's family to flee. The famine represented the spiritual dryness and judgment of the period of the Judges. When Boaz eats and drinks at the threshing floor, it signifies that Yahweh has visited His people by giving them bread (Ruth 1:6). The act of drinking is a sign of restoration, abundance, and the return of God's blessing. It demonstrates that God cares for both the spiritual redemption and the physical sustenance of His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:7, the NKJV states: "And after Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was cheerful, he went to lie down..."

• The English word "drunk" in modern usage almost exclusively implies alcohol-induced intoxication. However, the Hebrew shathah here is simply the past-perfect tense of "to drink."

• The text does not say Boaz was intoxicated (for which the Hebrew verb shakar would be used). Instead, he had completed his evening meal.

• The phrase "his heart was cheerful" (Hebrew yatab, meaning to be glad, well, or content) points to a state of healthy, godly satisfaction. Boaz was grateful for a successful harvest, peaceful because the labor was done, and physically satisfied. He was in a clear state of mind, which is proven by his protective watchfulness over the grain and his highly honorable, controlled, and legally precise response to Ruth when he awoke at midnight.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 26:30: "So he made them a feast, and they ate and drank."

• Exodus 24:11: "But on the nobles of the children of Israel He did not lay His hand. So they saw God, and they ate and drank."

• 1 Kings 19:8: "So he arose, and ate and drank; and he went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights as far as Horeb, the mountain of God."

• Nehemiah 8:10: "Then he said to them, 'Go your way, eat the fat, drink the sweet, and send portions to those for whom nothing is prepared; for this day is holy to our Lord.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast with Intoxication: Scripture carefully distinguishes between shathah (the normal, healthy act of drinking) and shakar (to drink to the point of drunkenness or stupor, as seen in Noah's account in Genesis 9:21). The narrator's choice of shathah protects Boaz's reputation as a "man of great wealth" or virtue (gibbor chayil), ensuring the reader understands that the subsequent encounter with Ruth is marked by absolute purity and dignity.

• Spiritual Satisfaction: The physical act of drinking in the Old Testament points forward to the invitation of Jesus in the New Testament, who stood and cried out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink" (John 7:37). The physical satisfaction Boaz felt after his harvest is a shadow of the ultimate spiritual satisfaction found in Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer.
