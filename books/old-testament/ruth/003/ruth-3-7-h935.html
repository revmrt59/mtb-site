WENT - ( bo' , Strong's H0935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo' is a foundational, highly versatile word of movement meaning "to go in," "to enter," or "to come." In the context of Ruth 3:7, it describes Boaz going to lie down at the edge of the grain heap after a joyful harvest celebration. This simple physical movement sets the stage for a critical, high-stakes encounter of redemption, highlighting the quiet transition from the public work of the harvest to the private, covenantal space where Ruth will make her bold appeal for Boaz to act as her kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root bo' carries the primary spatial concept of movement toward or into a specific destination. Unlike other verbs of motion that emphasize the process of walking, wandering, or traveling, bo' focuses on the arrival, the entry, or the crossing of a threshold. In ancient Israelite daily life, this verb was associated with entering a tent, coming home from labor in the fields, or arriving at a city gate. These physical transitions often marked a shift from exposure to safety, from public labor to private rest, or from common spaces to sacred ground. In the context of Ruth 3:7, Boaz's movement to lie down at the end of the heap of grain signifies a deliberate boundary crossing—moving from the bustling communal activity of winnowing to a solitary place of rest and vulnerability under the night sky.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bo' is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 2,500 times. In its basic Qal form, the semantic range includes:

• To enter, go in, or come in (spatially, as into a tent, house, or land).

• To come or arrive (of persons, events, or periods of time).

• To go to bed or lie down (when paired with words indicating sleep or nightfall).

• To be brought in or harvested (of crops or grain).

In Ruth 3:7, the verbal form is the Qal waw-consecutive imperfect wayyabo ("and he went"), which links his eating and drinking directly to his act of going to lie down. Here, it signifies the intentional entry into a state of sleep and security at the harvest floor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Sun Setting: The verb bo' is frequently used to describe the sun "going down" or entering its nocturnal chamber, signaling the transition from day to night and the calling of workers to rest.

• Entering Covenant: The term is used in legal and covenantal contexts to describe entering into an oath, a treaty, or a covenant relationship.

• Intimacy and Marriage: Spatially, "going in" to someone is a well-established Hebrew idiom for marital intimacy and physical union, creating a subtle, highly respectful literary tension when the same root is used of both Boaz and Ruth in the very same verse.

• Agricultural Ingathering: The harvest itself is described as the "income" or "ingathering" (from the same root), linking the physical grain Boaz is guarding to the physical arrival of Ruth, who represents Naomi's restored "harvest" of family line.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, bo' points to the reality of divine orchestration within human movement. Throughout the patriarchal narratives and the history of Israel, God's promise often centers on "entering" (bo') the Land of Promise, a place of ultimate rest and inheritance. In the book of Ruth, the movements of the characters are not random; they are guided by God's silent providence. Boaz going to sleep amidst his abundance represents the righteous man enjoying the fruit of his labor under God's blessing. His rest serves as a picture of the peace available to those who walk in obedience. Ruth’s entry into this space of rest prefigures the way the vulnerable and the outcast are invited to enter under the "wings" of the Lord God of Israel for refuge.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:7, bo' functions as a structural pivot. After eating, drinking, and experiencing a cheerful heart, Boaz went (wayyabo) to lie down at the edge of the grain heap. This detail highlights his humility and diligence; despite his wealth, he sleeps at the threshing floor to guard his crop. His movement to this specific location sets up a highly controlled, private environment. Immediately afterward, the text records that Ruth came (wat-tabo) softly. The juxtaposition of these two forms of bo'—Boaz going to sleep and Ruth coming to uncover his feet—creates a profound narrative symmetry. Boaz enters physical rest, which opens the way for Ruth to enter the space of redemption. The word choice emphasizes that this was not an accidental encounter, but a highly purposeful meeting designed to transition Ruth from a destitute foreigner to a redeemed wife.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:12: "Now when the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell upon Abram; and behold, horror and great darkness fell upon him."

• Joshua 21:45: "Not a word failed of any good thing which the Lord had spoken to the house of Israel. All came to pass."

• Ruth 1:22: "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab. Now they came to Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest."

• Ruth 4:13: "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the Lord gave her conception, and she bore a son."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The threshing floor in the ancient Near East was often associated with pagan fertility rites and loose moral behavior. By highlighting that Boaz simply "went to lie down" at the end of the grain heap, and that Ruth "came softly" to uncover his feet, the author of Ruth reclaims this space. The double use of bo' frames their actions not as a tawdry tryst, but as a holy, strategic, and honorable covenant request. The "end of the heap of grain" refers to the outermost boundary of the threshed barley, highlighting the modesty and isolation of Boaz's sleeping spot, which preserved the absolute privacy and integrity of the conversation that followed.
