STARTLED - ( charad , Strong's H2729 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb charad conveys a sudden, physical reaction of shaking, trembling, or being startled due to fear, awe, or an unexpected shock. In Ruth 3:8, it describes Boaz waking up in the dead of night to find a woman lying at his feet. His physical reaction of being "startled" reflects the sudden transition from deep sleep to alert, trembling awareness. This word underscores the purity and vulnerability of the encounter, demonstrating that the scene is not one of premeditated seduction but of surprising, reverent encounter. It highlights Boaz’s immediate physical shock, which quickly gives way to godly self-control and protective covenant concern.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root charad fundamentally refers to physical shaking, shuddering, or trembling. It represents the involuntary bodily response to a sudden crisis, terrifying sound, or unexpected event. It is the physical sensation of a racing heart, shivering limbs, and gasping breath. In the ancient Near Eastern world, physical trembling was the natural human reaction to encountering the divine, facing an overwhelming military force, or experiencing a shocking turn of events. Rather than a purely internal, cognitive emotion of worry, this word paints a vivid picture of a body physically jolted into high alert.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb charad means to tremble, shake, shudder, or be terrified. In its basic active form (the Qal stem, which is used in Ruth 3:8), the word describes the physical act of trembling or being startled. The semantic range spans from physical shaking due to cold, shock, or fear, to the emotional state of deep anxiety or awe. In other grammatical contexts, such as the causative Hiphil stem, it means to terrify, startle, or drive away in panic. In rare contexts, it carries the positive nuance of showing anxious, diligent, and trembling care to please someone.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Shaking at Sinai: The word is used to describe the violent shaking of the people when Yahweh descended upon Mount Sinai in fire, showcasing human frailty in the presence of holiness.

• Military Terror: It describes the absolute panic and shaking that overtakes an army or a city when faced with sudden defeat or divine judgment.

• Anxious Care and Devotion: It illustrates the diligent, trembling, and thorough care that a host provides for a prophet of God, demonstrating a highly sensitive attentiveness.

• Sudden Awakening: In the context of sleep, it portrays the abrupt, heart-pounding leap from unconsciousness to full awareness upon realizing something is dangerously or strangely out of place.

4. Theological Implications

Trembling before God or in moments of divine providence highlights human limitation and vulnerability in the presence of the extraordinary. Throughout the Old Testament, trembling is the proper, reverent response of creation to the presence of the Creator. When Boaz trembles, it signals that the moment is charged with moral and covenantal weight. It strips away any notion of a cheap, illicit encounter. His physical trembling reflects a healthy moral sensitivity and fear of God, setting the stage for a holy, covenant-affirming decision rather than a compromise of integrity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:8, the setting is midnight on the dark, vulnerable threshing floor. Boaz has eaten, drank, and slept soundly. Suddenly, he is jolted awake. The term charad indicates his heart raced and his body shook as he realized a human form was at his feet. The context is vital: Boaz does not react with anger, lust, or immediate recognition, but with a startled, trembling posture. This physiological reaction proves that Ruth's presence was entirely unexpected. The suddenness of his awakening and his physical trembling highlight the purity of the situation—there was no pre-arranged tryst. It emphasizes Boaz’s shock, which instantly transitions into a protective, self-controlled, and godly response when Ruth identifies herself and asks him to spread his wings over her.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The cognate noun charadah refers to fear, trembling, or anxiety, as seen in the warning that "the fear of man lays a snare." This reinforces the intensity of the verb. In Ruth 3:8, after Boaz is "startled" (charad), the text notes that he "turned himself" or "bent forward" (laphath), a rare verb meaning to twist, bend, or grasp. The combination of these two actions depicts a man suddenly waking up in panic, shivering from the cold night air and shock, bending forward in the dark to grope and see what or who is lying at his feet. This raw, unvarnished depiction adds to the historical realism and moral integrity of the narrative.
