TURNED - ( laphat , Strong's H3943 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb laphat conveys the physical action of twisting, bending forward, or grasping. In the context of Ruth 3:8, it describes Boaz waking up in the middle of the night, startled to find someone at his feet, causing him to instinctively bend forward or turn over to investigate. This term highlights the sudden, unexpected nature of the encounter, underscoring both Boaz’s shock and the delicate, vulnerable posture of Ruth as she sought his protection as her kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb laphat fundamentally refers to a physical bending, twisting, or winding. It portrays a sudden, reflexive bodily movement in response to an external stimulus. In ancient agricultural and domestic life, waking up in the pitch black of an outdoor threshing floor would require physical groping or bending over to perceive what was near. The word evokes a sense of disorientation, sudden awareness, and physical readjustment in the dark.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb laphat means to bend, twist, turn, grasp, or hold fast. In the Niphal stem (as seen in Ruth 3:8 and Job 6:18), it carries the sense of turning aside, winding, or twisting oneself. In the Piel stem (as in Judges 16:29), it means to grasp or take hold of with a twisting motion. The term is highly physical and kinetic, indicating a concrete bodily action rather than an abstract mental turning.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb occurs only three times in the Hebrew Bible, with each occurrence illustrating a distinct aspect of physical twisting or bending:

• A twisting or grasping action: Samson reaching out and taking hold of the two middle pillars of the temple of Dagon (Judges 16:29).

• A winding or turning aside: Caravan paths winding or turning aside into the waste and perishing (Job 6:18).

• A reflexive turning or bending forward: Boaz turning over or bending forward in shock in the dark (Ruth 3:8).

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of laphat in Ruth 3:8 underscores the providence of God operating through ordinary, human, and startling moments. The sudden twisting or turning of Boaz in the dark marks the pivotal transition of the narrative from desperation to redemption. God uses a startling midnight awakening to bring the redeemer and the seeker together. It illustrates how divine appointments often manifest in moments of human disorientation, where a sudden "turning" reveals a grace-filled opportunity.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:8, Boaz is sleeping on the threshing floor to guard his grain. At midnight, he shivers from the cold or is startled awake. The verb laphat depicts his sudden physical reaction: he twists or bends forward to see what is at his feet. The dark threshing floor was a place of potential danger, and his reflexive turn shows surprise, not expectation. This physical movement immediately precedes his recognition of Ruth, emphasizing the purity and unexpectedness of the encounter, far removed from any premeditated or improper scheme.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 3:8 – "Now it happened at midnight that the man was startled, and turned himself; and there, a woman was lying at his feet."

• Judges 16:29 – "Then Samson took hold of the two middle pillars which supported the temple, and he braced himself against them, one on his right and the other on his left."

• Job 6:18 – "The paths of their way turn aside, they go into nothing and perish."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The rarity of this word in the Hebrew Bible (only three times) highlights its highly specific, physical nature. It is not the standard Hebrew word for turning (shub or panah), which often carry ethical, spiritual, or directional connotations. Instead, laphat is a raw, physical verb of twisting, highlighting the somatic, heart-stopping reality of Boaz's sudden awakening in the dark.
