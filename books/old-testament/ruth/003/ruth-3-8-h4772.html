FEET - ( meragloth , Strong's H4772 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word meragloth refers specifically to the place of the feet, the foot of a bed, or the feet themselves. Occurring only five times in the Old Testament—four times in the book of Ruth and once in the book of Daniel—this term carries rich cultural and symbolic significance. In the narrative of Ruth and Boaz, it describes the precise location where Ruth lay down at the threshing floor, symbolizing her posture of submission, petition for protection, and appeal for Boaz to fulfill his role as her kinsman-redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun meragloth is a denominative word derived from the common Hebrew noun regel, which means "foot." While regel refers to the physical foot itself, the prefix and structural form of meragloth shift the focus to a specific location: the place or territory where the feet rest. In the agricultural and domestic settings of the ancient Near East, the foot of a sleeping mat or bed was the lowest and most vulnerable position. For a servant to lie at the meragloth of a master was a recognized physical demonstration of total dependence, loyalty, and humble petition.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term means the place of the feet, the foot of a bed, or the lower limbs. In prose, it functions as an adverbial accusative of place, indicating the specific spot where someone lies down or stands in relation to another person. It does not carry any inherent sexual or illicit connotation; rather, it designates a spatial boundary of respect and service. The term emphasizes the physical distance maintained by a petitioner, showing respect for the superior's personal space while remaining close enough to seek their attention and favor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The usage of meragloth creates a powerful picture of covenantal petition and submission:

• The Posture of a Servant: In Ruth 3, Naomi instructs Ruth to go to the threshing floor, wait until Boaz has finished eating and drinking, and then "uncover his feet (meragloth)" and lie down. This action was a culturally symbolic petition. By placing herself at the spot of his feet, Ruth took the position of the lowest handmaid, signaling her willingness to serve and her absolute reliance on his mercy.

• The Appeal for Redemption: In the ancient Near East, covering a woman with the corner of one’s garment (a request Ruth makes immediately after Boaz discovers her at his meragloth) was a symbolic act of marriage and protection. Lying at his feet was the preliminary step, a quiet, non-verbal plea for Boaz to act as her goel (kinsman-redeemer).

• The Glory of the Heavenly Being: In its single non-Ruth occurrence (Daniel 10:6), the word describes the feet or lower limbs of a magnificent heavenly being, glowing like burnished bronze. Here, the word underscores the majestic and powerful stance of God's messenger.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of meragloth in the book of Ruth centers on the doctrine of redemption and the nature of faith:

• Faith in the Redeemer: Ruth's presence at the meragloth of Boaz is a beautiful type of the sinner coming to the feet of Jesus Christ. Ruth had no legal claim on Boaz other than his willingness to act out of lovingkindness (chesed). Her posture represents a faith that casts itself entirely upon the grace and character of the redeemer.

• The Posture of Worship and Prayer: Throughout Scripture, falling at someone's feet is the ultimate expression of worship, submission, and desperate prayer. Though meragloth is a specific spatial term, it reinforces the biblical pattern that redemption begins at the feet of the one who has the power and right to redeem.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:8, the narrative reaches its dramatic climax: "And it came to pass at midnight, that the man was afraid, and turned himself: and, behold, a woman lay at his feet."

The context of the harvest festival and the threshing floor could easily suggest opportunity for moral compromise. However, the writer's deliberate use of meragloth preserves the moral purity and dignity of the scene. Ruth did not lie by Boaz's side as an equal or an intimate partner; she lay strictly at the "place of his feet." This spatial detail highlights her modesty and legal intent. When Boaz wakes up startled at midnight, he finds her not in a compromising embrace, but in the submissive posture of a petitioner waiting for her protector to wake.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 3:4: "...then you shall go in, uncover his feet, and lie down; and he will tell you what you should do."

• Ruth 3:7: "And after Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was cheerful, he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain; and she came softly, uncovered his feet, and lay down."

• Ruth 3:14: "So she lay at his feet until morning, and she arose before one could recognize another."

• Daniel 10:6: "His body was like beryl, his face like the appearance of lightning, his eyes like torches of fire, his arms and feet like burnished bronze in color, and the sound of his words like the voice of a multitude."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Pastors and teachers should note the absolute integrity demonstrated by both Ruth and Boaz in this account. In ancient agricultural festivals, pagan practices often involved fertility rites at the threshing floors. By contrast, the interaction at Boaz's meragloth is characterized by righteousness, covenant keeping, and high moral standards. Boaz acknowledges Ruth’s virtue immediately, calling her a "virtuous woman" (Ruth 3:11), and protects her reputation by sending her home before dawn. The "place of the feet" became the birthplace of a redeeming relationship that would eventually lead to the lineage of King David and, ultimately, Jesus Christ.
