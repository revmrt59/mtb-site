LYING - ( shakab , Strong's H7901 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shakab primarily means to lie down, rest, or sleep. In the context of Ruth 3:8, it describes Ruth's posture of humble vulnerability and submission at the feet of Boaz on the threshing floor. While shakab can carry sexual connotations in other Old Testament contexts, its use here highlights a physical position of protection-seeking and covenant appeal, strictly devoid of immorality, illustrating Ruth's righteousness and Boaz's honorable response.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb shakab is a highly common Hebrew word used to describe the physical act of reclining or lying down. It depicts a state of rest, vulnerability, or submission. In the ancient Near East, lying down at someone's feet was a cultural gesture of deep humility, servant-like submission, and petitioning for protection. The threshing floor setting in Ruth 3 adds cultural weight: it was a place of hard labor, harvest celebration, and vulnerability to thieves. By lying down at Boaz's feet, Ruth physically acted out her petition for Boaz to act as her family's kinsman-redeemer, casting her care upon his honor.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shakab means to lie down, recline, sleep, or rest. It describes:

• The physical action of laying oneself down for sleep or rest.

• The act of lying down in death, often phrased as sleeping with one's fathers.

• In specific contexts, intimate or sexual relations ("to lie with").

• The posture of physical prostration, helplessness, or disease.

In Ruth 3:8, the word functions as a feminine singular participle ("lying"), describing a continuous state of quiet rest and waiting. It denotes a stationary posture of vulnerability and absolute trust.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Rest and Security: The word often paints a picture of safety and peace, where one can lie down without fear of harm.

• Vulnerability and Submission: Lying down at another's feet is a picture of total dependence. A person lying down cannot easily defend themselves; they are entirely at the mercy of the one standing or sitting.

• Covenant Petition: Ruth's posture is a physical representation of her request for Boaz to spread his cloak over her, symbolizing the protective wings of God.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of shakab in Ruth 3:8 lies in its contrast with potential impropriety. Although shakab is frequently used elsewhere for illicit sexual relations, the narrative of Ruth meticulously guards the purity of this encounter. Ruth lies at Boaz's feet, not beside him, and her action is framed as a righteous appeal for redemption. This portrays redemption as an act initiated through humble, faith-filled submission. Ruth's posture of lying down represents the believer's posture before God—helpless to redeem oneself, waiting in quiet confidence for the Redeemer to act.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:8, the discovery of Ruth lying at his feet startles Boaz at midnight. The surrounding context emphasizes honor, discretion, and covenant law. Ruth is not seducing Boaz; she is claiming her right under the levirate and kinsman-redeemer customs. By remaining in this posture until morning, the physical action of shakab transitions from a shocking surprise to a solemn, quiet commitment of protection. Boaz acknowledges her worthiness, calling her a "virtuous woman," which confirms that her act of lying at his feet was viewed as honorable, not shameful.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 28:11: "So he came to a certain place and stayed there all night, because the sun had set. And he took one of the stones of that place and put it at his head, and he lay down in that place to sleep."

• Leviticus 26:6: "I will give peace in the land, and you shall lie down, and no one will make you afraid; I will rid the land of evil beasts, and the sword will not go through your land."

• Ruth 3:14: "So she lay at his feet until morning, and she arose before one could recognize another. Then he said, 'Do not let it be known that the woman came to the threshing floor.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrasts in the book of Ruth are striking. In Ruth 2, Ruth is working hard, gleaning in the fields from morning till night. In Ruth 3, she is instructed by Naomi to stop working and simply "lie down." This mirrors the biblical pattern of salvation: believers must cease their self-striving and rest in the finished work of their Redeemer. Boaz’s command to "Lie down until morning" (Ruth 3:13) is a word of assurance, promising that he will take care of the redemption process while she rests in peace.
