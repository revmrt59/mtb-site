WOMAN - ( 'ishshah , Strong's H0802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ishshah is the primary Old Testament term for a woman or wife, representing the feminine counterpart to man ('ish). In Ruth 3:8, it marks a pivotal, suspenseful moment in the narrative where Boaz awakens in the darkness of the threshing floor to discover an unidentified female lying at his feet. This term transitions from describing a generic, vulnerable female stranger to revealing her as a woman of noble character ('eshet chayil) and a rightful claimant to covenant redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'ishshah is phonetically and relationally linked to the word for man, 'ish. This linguistic connection dates back to the creation account in Genesis 2:23, where the woman is named 'ishshah because she was taken out of 'ish. The word represents the essential companion, partner, and complementary equal to man. In the agrarian and patriarchal society of the ancient Near East, an 'ishshah without a husband, father, or adult son was incredibly vulnerable, lacking social standing, economic security, and physical protection. Ruth embodies this perilous status as a foreign widow—a marginalized 'ishshah seeking refuge.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term 'ishshah refers to a female human being of any age or marital status. When the context involves a marriage relationship, the word is translated as "wife." It designates gender, marital state, or, in idiomatic Hebrew, can even mean "each" or "every one" when describing feminine objects or groups. In the context of Ruth 3:8, the word is used in its basic, gender-specific sense to denote an adult female whose identity is temporarily unknown to the startled Boaz in the middle of the night.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• 'ishshah occurs over 780 times in the Old Testament, showcasing several rich word pictures:

• The Companion of Creation: She is the designed counterpart to man, sharing the same nature but expressing it in distinct, complementary femininity.

• The Wife of Covenant: Often used to describe the marriage partner, symbolizing faithfulness, domestic stability, and the building up of a household.

• The Personification of Wisdom and Folly: In the Book of Proverbs, both wisdom ('Chokmah) and folly ('Iwwalet) are personified as women ('ishshah), calling out to young men to choose their paths.

• The Virtuous Woman: In Proverbs 31, the 'eshet chayil (woman of valor or virtue) is celebrated as a crown to her husband and a blessing to her community.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of the 'ishshah in Ruth 3:8 carries profound covenantal and redemptive significance. Throughout redemption history, God uses vulnerable women to accomplish His purposes and preserve the messianic line (such as Tamar, Rahab, and later Mary). Ruth, as a foreign 'ishshah, steps onto the threshing floor not out of desperation for an illicit encounter, but in an act of bold, covenant-keeping faith. She seeks the shelter of Boaz's "wings" (Ruth 3:9), demonstrating that God’s redemptive grace extends beyond ethnic and national boundaries to include any 'ishshah who trusts in Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:8, the word 'ishshah serves a dramatic narrative function. Boaz wakes up at midnight, startled and shivering, and bends forward to discover a woman ('ishshah) lying at his feet. At this precise moment, her identity is hidden by the darkness. The text uses the general term 'ishshah to capture Boaz's initial shock: there is an unidentified female in a place where only men slept during the harvest season. This creates a holy tension. The narrative quickly resolves this tension as this nameless 'ishshah identifies herself as "Ruth, your maidservant" (Ruth 3:9) and Boaz pronounces her to be a "virtuous woman" (Ruth 3:11). The transition from an anonymous, potentially scandalous female at his feet to a recognized woman of noble character highlights the purity of their interaction and the righteousness of their upcoming marriage.

• Ruth 4:11: "And all the people who were at the gate, and the elders, said, 'We are witnesses. The LORD make the woman who is coming to your house like Rachel and Leah, the two who built the house of Israel; and may you prosper in Ephrathah and be famous in Bethlehem.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Hebrew Bible (the Tanakh), the Book of Ruth is placed in the Ketuvim (Writings) immediately following the Book of Proverbs. This structure creates a beautiful literary connection. Proverbs ends with the description of the virtuous woman ('eshet chayil) in Proverbs 31:10–31. The very next pages in the Hebrew canon introduce Ruth, whom Boaz explicitly calls a virtuous woman ('eshet chayil) in Ruth 3:11. Ruth is thus presented to the reader as the historical, living embodiment of the ideal woman described in the wisdom literature.
