CLOSE RELATIVE - ( ga'al , Strong's H1350 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ga'al refers to the kinsman-redeemer, a close relative who steps in to rescue, deliver, or restore a family member who has fallen into hardship, poverty, or bondage. In Ruth 3:9, the term transitions from a cold legal category into a beautiful picture of covenant love, mercy, and redemption. It highlights Boaz’s role as the willing protector who has the authority and the heart to buy back Ruth’s lost inheritance, provide security, and preserve her family line, ultimately serving as a profound shadow of Jesus Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near Eastern culture of Israel, family solidarity was the bedrock of society. The goel (the active participle of ga'al) was the "kinsman-redeemer"—the designated relative responsible for defending the family's economic and physical survival. If an Israelite fell into poverty and had to sell his ancestral land or sell himself into slavery, the goel was legally responsible to buy back the land or the person. If a man died without an heir, the goel was called upon to marry the widow to carry on the deceased relative's name. This system ensured that no family was permanently wiped out, no inheritance was permanently lost, and no marginalized relative was left abandoned.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary root meaning of ga'al is to redeem, deliver, avenge, or act as a kinsman. It carries the dual sense of legal obligation and familial protection. Unlike other Hebrew words for redemption that imply paying a ransom in a general commercial transaction, ga'al is strictly relational. Redemption under this term can only be performed by a close blood relative who possesses the right to redeem, the financial capacity to redeem, and the willingness to redeem.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, the word is used in several distinct ways:

• Property Redemption: Buying back land that had been sold due to debt, keeping the tribal inheritance intact.

• Personal Redemption: Paying the price to free a relative who had sold himself into slavery to a foreigner.

• The Blood Avenger (goel haddam): Acting as the protector of the family's bloodline by seeking justice for a murdered relative.

• Divine Redemption: Yahweh Himself is frequently pictured as the Goel of Israel, particularly in the Exodus and in the prophecies of Isaiah, where He delivers His people from bondage not because of a commercial transaction, but because of His covenant relationship with them.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of ga'al provides one of the most vivid Old Testament foundations for the New Testament doctrine of redemption. For a redemption to take place, three criteria had to be met: the redeemer must be a close relative, must have the means to pay the redemption price, and must act voluntarily.

This points directly to Jesus Christ:

• He became our close relative: Through the Incarnation, Jesus became a human being, taking on flesh and blood to relate to us.

• He had the means to pay: He was sinless, possessing the infinite value required to pay the debt of sin that we could never pay.

• He acted voluntarily: He willingly laid down His life to redeem us from the bondage of sin and restore our lost eternal inheritance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 3:9, Ruth makes a bold, faith-filled proposal to Boaz at the threshing floor: "Spread your skirt over your maidservant, for you are a close relative (ga'al)."

The request to spread his "skirt" or "wing" (kanaph) is a direct appeal for Boaz to become her husband and protector. By calling Boaz her ga'al, Ruth is not merely asking for a financial handout or the redemption of Elimelech's land; she is asking him to redeem her life and destiny.

The context reveals that while Boaz was indeed a close relative with the ability and willingness to redeem, there was another relative even closer than he. This setup creates the dramatic tension of the narrative, highlighting that redemption must be carried out in strict accordance with covenant law and selfless integrity. Boaz’s immediate willingness to pursue the legal right to redeem Ruth elevates the concept of ga'al from mere duty to an act of deep covenant kindness (chesed).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 25:25: "If one of your brethren becomes poor, and has sold some of his possession, and if his redeeming relative comes to redeem it, then he may redeem what his brother sold."

• Job 19:25: "For I know that my Redeemer lives, And He shall stand at last on the earth;"

• Isaiah 43:1: "But now, thus says the Lord, who created you, O Jacob, And He who formed you, O Israel: 'Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; You are Mine.'"

• Ruth 4:4: "And I thought to inform you, saying, 'Buy it back in the presence of the inhabitants and the elders of my people. If you will redeem it, redeem it; but if you will not redeem it, then tell me, that I may know; for there is no one else to redeem it besides you, and I am after you.' And he said, 'I will redeem it.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A beautiful literary connection exists between Ruth 2:12 and Ruth 3:9. In Ruth 2:12, Boaz blesses Ruth, praying that she will find refuge under the "wings" (kanaph) of the Lord God of Israel. In Ruth 3:9, Ruth uses the exact same Hebrew word when she asks Boaz to spread his "wing" (kanaph) over her. Ruth's appeal teaches us that God often answers our prayers for protection and refuge through the hands and actions of faithful, obedient believers who are willing to act as redeemers in a broken world.
