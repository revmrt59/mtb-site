ANSWERED - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the foundational word for communication in the Old Testament, primarily meaning "to say," "to speak," or "to declare." In the context of Ruth 3:9, where it is translated "answered," it marks a pivotal moment of vulnerable dialogue between Ruth and Boaz in the darkness of the threshing floor. More than a mere verbal reply, Ruth's answer is a covenantal appeal, responding to Boaz's question of identity with a bold request for redemption, demonstrating how simple speech can carry profound theological and relational weight.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over 5,000 times. Unlike other Hebrew verbs for communication that emphasize the mechanical act of speaking or the loudness of a proclamation, 'amar focuses on the cognitive and relational substance of what is said. It bridges the gap between the internal thoughts of the heart and the external reality of the listener.

In ancient Near Eastern culture, spoken words were not considered empty breath; they were dynamic, active, and legally binding. To speak was to set something in motion. In the narrative of Ruth, where quiet actions and subtle loyalties drive the plot, the spoken dialogue carries immense weight. Ruth's "answering" in the dark of the threshing floor is the climactic moment where her silent, faithful actions are finally translated into a clear, spoken commitment.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'amar is "to say," "to speak," "to utter," or "to command." When utilized in a conversational pattern where a question has been asked, it is properly translated as "to answer," "to reply," or "to respond."

The semantic range of the word also includes:

• Internal monologue: "To say in one's heart," meaning to think, intend, or purpose (e.g., Genesis 20:11).

• Command or decree: Used of monarchs or divine authority commanding an action to take place (e.g., Genesis 1:3).

• Covenantal declaration: Promising or declaring a stance of loyalty or agreement.

In Ruth 3:9, 'amar occurs in the Qal imperfect conjugational form (watto'mer, "and she said/answered"), representing a direct, active response to a specific inquiry.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Hebrew Scriptures use 'amar to paint several vivid pictures of speech:

• The Creative Word: God speaks ('amar) and the universe leaps into existence. His spoken word carries inherent, creative power.

• The Heart's Disclosure: Because 'amar can mean "to think" (saying within oneself), the spoken 'amar represents the uncovering of the heart. When a person speaks, they reveal their hidden thoughts, exposing their true character.

• The Responsive Dialogue: In covenants, 'amar is the verbal seal of agreement. When God offers a covenant, and the people "answer" or "say" they will obey, the relationship is formally ratified.

### 4. Theological Implications

Ruth's answer in Ruth 3:9 is highly theological, serving as the turning point of the entire narrative:

• The Answer of Faith: Ruth’s speech is a human response that aligns with divine providence. Her answer is the catalyst for the redemption of Naomi's family line, which eventually leads to King David and Jesus the Messiah.

• The Invitation to Redeem: Ruth does not merely state her name; she defines her relationship to Boaz and requests his protection: "take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative." Her answer holds Boaz accountable to the law of the kinsman-redeemer (go'el), showing that human speech can appeal to and activate covenantal responsibilities.

• The Echo of Divine Protection: When Ruth asks Boaz to spread his "wing" over her, her spoken words mirror Boaz's own prayer in Ruth 2:12, where he prayed that she would find refuge under the "wings" of Yahweh. Ruth's answer reveals that God often answers prayers for protection through the willing, physical actions of His people.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The context of Ruth 3:9 is charged with tension. It is midnight. Boaz wakes up startled to find a woman lying at his feet. In the dark, he asks, "Who are you?"

Ruth’s answer (watto'mer) is brilliant in its humility, clarity, and courage:

• Humility: She identifies herself as "Ruth, your maidservant." She uses the term 'amah, a word denoting a lower-status servant, showing her complete submission and lack of entitlement.

• Boldness: Without pausing, she petitions Boaz to act: "take your maidservant under your wing." This is a direct proposal of marriage and redemption.

• Covenant Focus: She bases her request not on romantic attraction, but on covenant law: "for you are a close relative."

Had Ruth remained silent or given a timid reply, the moment would have passed. Her articulate, faithful answer transforms a potentially scandalous and dangerous encounter into a holy, covenantal engagement.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew narrative style, dialogue is never wasted. While Western stories often rely on heavy description of settings or internal feelings, Hebrew writers use direct dialogue ('amar) to reveal character, drive the plot, and deliver theological truths. The extended verbal exchange between Boaz and Ruth in the middle of the night (Ruth 3:9-13) slows down the pace of the book, forcing the reader to hang on every spoken word. The spoken answers between these two individuals demonstrate that righteousness is not merely an abstract concept, but a lived reality expressed through honest, faithful, and courageous conversation.
