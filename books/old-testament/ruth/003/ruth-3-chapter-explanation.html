Ruth 3:1
Ruth 3:1
NKJV

Ruth 3:1 Then Naomi her mother-in-law said to her, "My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:1 One day Naomi said to Ruth, “My daughter, it’s time that I found a permanent home for you, so that you will be provided for. (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi asks Ruth, “My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?” The rest she once prayed Ruth would find in a husband’s house now becomes her own aim, and her concern is for Ruth’s settled future.

Verse Explanation

Naomi asks Ruth, “My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?” The rest she once prayed Ruth would find in a husband’s house now becomes her own aim, and her concern is for Ruth’s settled future.

“Then Naomi her mother-in-law said to her,”

Chapter 3 begins with Naomi taking the initiative. Ruth has been gleaning in Boaz’s fields through the harvest (2:23), and Naomi, who has been receiving Ruth’s provision, now acts on Ruth’s behalf. She speaks as her “mother-in-law,” but her words show the heart of a mother.

““My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?”

Naomi’s question means that she must seek “security” for Ruth: a settled home, a husband’s protection, and a secure future. On the road from Moab she prayed that the LORD would grant her daughters-in-law rest in the house of a husband (1:9). Now she takes steps toward that answer for Ruth. Her aim is Ruth’s good, “that it may be well with you,” not her own advantage. Naomi’s plan is an expression of the same unselfish care she showed when she released her daughters-in-law, and it should not be read as manipulation.

Study Notes

“Security” (3:1) translates the Hebrew word manoach, a place of rest, closely related to menuchah, “rest,” in 1:9. The two words link Naomi’s earlier prayer with her present plan.

Naomi’s question is rhetorical in form, expecting the answer yes. Its sense is ‘I must seek security for you.’

Ruth 3:2
Ruth 3:2
NKJV

Ruth 3:2 Now Boaz, whose young women you were with, is he not our relative? In fact, he is winnowing barley tonight at the threshing floor.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:2 Boaz is a close relative of ours, and he’s been very kind by letting you gather grain with his young women. Tonight he will be winnowing barley at the threshing floor. (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi reminds Ruth that Boaz, whose young women she worked beside, is their relative, and that he is winnowing barley that night at the threshing floor. The kinship named in 2:20 now becomes the basis for a plan, and she knows where Boaz can be found.

Verse Explanation

Naomi reminds Ruth that Boaz, whose young women she worked beside, is their relative, and that he is winnowing barley that night at the threshing floor. The kinship named in 2:20 now becomes the basis for a plan, and she knows where Boaz can be found.

“Now Boaz, whose young women you were with, is he not our relative?”

Naomi points to Boaz, the man in whose field Ruth worked beside his young women. She reminds Ruth that he is “our relative.” The kinship Naomi announced in 2:20 now becomes the basis for a plan.

“In fact, he is winnowing barley tonight at the threshing floor.”

Naomi knows where Boaz will be. After threshing, harvested grain was winnowed by tossing it into the air so that the evening breeze would carry away the chaff while the heavier grain fell back to the floor. The “threshing floor” was a flat, hard surface, usually outside the town where the wind could reach it. During the harvest season, owners often stayed there overnight to guard their grain. Naomi’s plan depends on knowing that Boaz will be there tonight.

Study Notes

“Relative” (3:2) translates the Hebrew word moda‘at, a word related to the term used for Boaz in 2:1. It describes a known family connection rather than the legal role of go’el used in 2:20.

Winnowing was done in the late afternoon or evening, when a steady breeze often blew in the hill country; the breeze would carry off the lighter chaff.

Ruth 3:3
NKJV

Ruth 3:3 Therefore wash yourself and anoint yourself, put on your best garment and go down to the threshing floor; but do not make yourself known to the man until he has finished eating and drinking.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:3 Now do as I tell you — take a bath and put on perfume and dress in your nicest clothes. Then go to the threshing floor, but don’t let Boaz see you until he has finished eating and drinking. (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi tells Ruth to wash, anoint herself, put on her best garment, and go down to the threshing floor, but not to make herself known until Boaz has finished eating and drinking. The preparations present Ruth as a woman seeking marriage, and the timing allows a private approach.

Verse Explanation

Naomi tells Ruth to wash, anoint herself, put on her best garment, and go down to the threshing floor, but not to make herself known until Boaz has finished eating and drinking. The preparations present Ruth as a woman seeking marriage, and the timing allows a private approach.

“Therefore wash yourself and anoint yourself, put on your best garment”

Naomi tells Ruth to bathe, anoint herself with perfumed oil, and dress in her best clothes. These preparations present Ruth as a woman seeking marriage rather than a gleaner in work clothes. They may also signal the end of her time of mourning, since washing, anointing, and changing clothes are elsewhere associated with leaving mourning behind (compare 2 Samuel 12:20).

“and go down to the threshing floor;”

Ruth is to “go down” from the town to the threshing floor. The text does not identify where the threshing floor was; the wording simply describes the movement from the town down to the place of threshing.

“but do not make yourself known to the man until he has finished eating and drinking.”

Ruth is not to reveal herself until Boaz has finished his evening meal. The timing allows a private approach after the work and meal are done, when he will be settled and at rest. Naomi wants Ruth’s appeal to be made in private rather than in front of the workers.

Study Notes

“Garment” (3:3) translates the Hebrew word simlah, the outer cloak or mantle; the NKJV supplies “best” to convey that Ruth is to dress with care.

In this verse and the next, the written Hebrew text (Kethiv) of two verbs has first-person forms, ‘I will go down’ and ‘I will lie down,’ while the traditional reading (Qere), followed by the NKJV and most translations, has the second-person forms appropriate to Naomi’s instructions. The written forms may preserve an older spelling used when addressing a woman as “you”.

Verse Explanation

Naomi instructs Ruth to note where Boaz lies down, then go in, uncover his feet, and lie down, trusting that he will tell her what to do. The gesture is an appeal for marriage within a custom Boaz would understand, though the text does not explain its exact form.

“Then it shall be, when he lies down, that you shall notice the place where he lies;”

Ruth is to watch carefully where Boaz lies down for the night so that she can find him in the dark.

“and you shall go in, uncover his feet, and lie down;”

Naomi instructs Ruth to go to the place, uncover his feet, and lie down there. The gesture is an appeal for marriage within a custom that Boaz would understand. Boaz’s own response in 3:9–13 shows how he understood it: as an honorable request for marriage and protection. The text does not explain the custom’s exact form, and the scene should not be read as an invitation to sexual misconduct. The narrative commends both Ruth and Boaz, and Boaz takes care to protect her reputation (3:14).

“and he will tell you what you should do.”

Naomi trusts Boaz to respond honorably and to take it from there. In fact, Ruth will speak first and make the request herself (3:9).

Study Notes

“His feet” (3:4) translates the Hebrew word margelot, a word meaning the place of the feet. It occurs only in Ruth 3 and in Daniel 10:6. Its rarity has led to discussion, but in context it most naturally refers to the area at Boaz’s feet.

Interpreters differ on the precise meaning of the gesture of uncovering his feet. Many see it as a symbolic request for the protection of marriage, a meaning made explicit by Ruth’s words in 3:9.

Main Idea

After Boaz has eaten and drunk and is in good spirits, he lies down at the end of the heap of grain, and Ruth comes quietly, uncovers his feet, and lies down. She carries out the plan exactly as Naomi described it.

Verse Explanation

After Boaz has eaten and drunk and is in good spirits, he lies down at the end of the heap of grain, and Ruth comes quietly, uncovers his feet, and lies down. She carries out the plan exactly as Naomi described it.

“And after Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was cheerful,”

Boaz has finished his meal and is in good spirits after the harvest work. The phrase “his heart was cheerful” describes contentment and well-being after a good meal at the end of a successful harvest, not drunkenness.

“he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain;”

Boaz lies down beside his pile of grain, guarding the harvest through the night. The location places him at the edge of the threshing floor, apart from others.

“and she came softly, uncovered his feet, and lay down.”

Ruth approaches quietly, uncovers his feet, and lies down, exactly as Naomi described. She comes “softly,” without drawing attention, so that her appeal can be made in private.

Study Notes

“His heart was cheerful” (3:7) describes contentment, often after eating and drinking. The same expression is used in Judges 19:6 and 1 Samuel 25:36; the context, not the phrase itself, determines whether excess is involved.

“Softly” (3:7) translates a word meaning quietly or secretly, used also of David cutting the corner of Saul’s robe without being noticed (1 Samuel 24:4).

Ruth 3:9
NKJV

Ruth 3:9 And he said, "Who are you?" So she answered, "I am Ruth, your maidservant. Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:9 “Who are you?” he asked. “I am your servant Ruth,” she replied. “Spread the corner of your covering over me, for you are my family redeemer.” (NLT)

Main Idea

When Boaz asks who she is, Ruth answers, “I am Ruth, your maidservant. Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative.” Rather than waiting to be told what to do, she makes an honorable request for marriage and protection, echoing the refuge under the LORD’s wings that Boaz had spoken of in 2:12.

Verse Explanation

When Boaz asks who she is, Ruth answers, “I am Ruth, your maidservant. Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative.” Rather than waiting to be told what to do, she makes an honorable request for marriage and protection, echoing the refuge under the LORD’s wings that Boaz had spoken of in 2:12.

“And he said, “Who are you?”

In the darkness Boaz cannot see who she is, so he asks directly.

“So she answered, “I am Ruth, your maidservant.”

Ruth names herself and calls herself his “maidservant.” Earlier she had said she was not even one of his servant girls (2:13). Here she uses a term for a woman who could become a man’s wife.

“Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative.”

Instead of waiting to be told what to do, Ruth makes her request. To spread a garment’s “wing” over a woman was a way of speaking about marriage and the protection that came with it (compare Ezekiel 16:8). Her words echo Boaz’s own blessing, when he prayed that she would be rewarded by the LORD, “under whose wings” she had come for refuge (2:12). Ruth now asks Boaz to become the means of that refuge. She grounds her request in the fact that he is “a close relative,” a go’el, the Hebrew term for one who had the right and responsibility to act for the family. Her request joins marriage to the redeemer’s responsibility for Naomi’s household. It is an honorable proposal, made with integrity, and it brings the story to its turning point. In the larger picture of Scripture, Ruth’s request to come under the covering of her redeemer foreshadows the way Jesus Christ, our Redeemer, receives those who come to Him and brings them into belonging with Himself.

Study Notes

“Maidservant” in this verse translates the Hebrew word ’amah, whereas in 2:13 Ruth used shiphchah. ’Amah often refers to a female servant who could be taken as a wife or concubine.

“Wing” (3:9) is the Hebrew word kanaph, the same word Boaz used for the LORD’s “wings” in 2:12. It also means the corner or edge of a garment. Ezekiel 16:8 uses the same expression of the LORD spreading His “wing” over Israel in covenant love.

Boaz was not the brother of Ruth’s late husband, so the duty described in Deuteronomy 25:5–10 did not strictly apply to him. Ruth’s appeal rests on his role as a close relative within the wider family.

Ruth 3:10 “The LORD bless you, my daughter!” Boaz exclaimed. “You are showing even more family loyalty now than you did before, for you have not gone after a younger man, whether rich or poor. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz blesses Ruth by the LORD and says this latest kindness is greater than her first, because she has not gone after young men, poor or rich. He understands her request and honors it as loyal love that serves Naomi’s family.

Verse Explanation

Boaz blesses Ruth by the LORD and says this latest kindness is greater than her first, because she has not gone after young men, poor or rich. He understands her request and honors it as loyal love that serves Naomi’s family.

“Then he said, “Blessed are you of the LORD, my daughter!”

Boaz responds with blessing, not suspicion or rebuke. He calls on the LORD to bless Ruth and again calls her “my daughter.” His first words show that he understands her request and honors it.

“For you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning,”

Boaz says that this latest “kindness” is greater than the first. Her first kindness was her loyalty to Naomi, which Boaz praised in 2:11. This greater kindness is seeking a marriage that would serve Naomi’s family and continue its line.

“in that you did not go after young men, whether poor or rich.”

Ruth could have sought a younger husband, whether poor or rich, according to her own preference. Instead she has sought a husband within the family who could act as redeemer. Boaz sees her choice as loyal love that serves Naomi’s family rather than her own interests.

Study Notes

“Kindness” (3:10) translates the Hebrew word hesed, the loyal love that runs through the book (1:8; 2:20). Boaz uses the word for Ruth’s commitment to the family.

The contrast between “the end” and “the beginning” most likely compares this request with Ruth’s earlier devotion to Naomi. Some interpreters take “the beginning” to refer to her original marriage to Mahlon.

Ruth 3:11
NKJV

Ruth 3:11 And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you request, for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman.(NKJV)

NLT

Main Idea

Boaz tells Ruth not to fear and promises to do all she asks, for all the people of his town know that she is a virtuous woman. The Moabite widow is publicly recognized for her character, and Boaz commits himself to act.

Verse Explanation

Boaz tells Ruth not to fear and promises to do all she asks, for all the people of his town know that she is a virtuous woman. The Moabite widow is publicly recognized for her character, and Boaz commits himself to act.

“And now, my daughter, do not fear.”

Boaz reassures Ruth. She has taken a great risk in coming to him at night, and he tells her she has nothing to fear.

“I will do for you all that you request,”

Boaz commits himself to do everything she has asked. He promises to act on her behalf.

“for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman.”

Boaz explains that her character is known throughout Bethlehem. She is “a virtuous woman,” a woman of noble character and worth. The same word described Boaz as “a man of great wealth” in 2:1. The Moabite widow is publicly recognized as a woman of excellence, and the two are now matched in character.

Study Notes

“Virtuous woman” (3:11) translates the Hebrew phrase ’eshet chayil, the same phrase used in Proverbs 31:10 for the “virtuous wife” and in Proverbs 12:4. In the Hebrew Bible’s order of books, Ruth often follows Proverbs, so that the book’s portrait of Ruth comes right after that poem.

“All the people of my town” literally reads ‘all the gate of my people,’ referring to the town gate where public life and legal business took place. The expression anticipates the scene at the gate in 4:1–12.

Boaz tells Ruth to stay the night and promises that in the morning, if the nearer relative will perform the duty, let him do it; if not, Boaz will do it himself, as the LORD lives. He binds himself by oath to see the matter through.

“Stay this night, and in the morning it shall be that if he will perform the duty of a close relative for you—good; let him do it.”

Boaz tells Ruth to remain until morning rather than walk home alone in the dark. In the morning he will put the matter to the nearer relative. If that man is willing to act as redeemer, Boaz will respect his right.

“But if he does not want to perform the duty for you, then I will perform the duty for you, as the LORD lives!”

Before Ruth leaves, Boaz asks her to bring the shawl she is wearing and hold it out.

“And when she held it, he measured six ephahs of barley, and laid it on her.”

Boaz pours a large amount of barley into the shawl and helps her carry it. The Hebrew names six measures without saying which unit; the NKJV supplies “ephahs.” Whatever the measure, the generous gift is both provision for Naomi and Ruth and a pledge that Boaz intends to act.

The traditional Hebrew text known as the Masoretic Text reads ‘he went into the city,’ referring to Boaz, while many medieval Hebrew manuscripts and some ancient versions read ‘she went.’ The NKJV follows the reading “she went into the city.”

Verse Explanation

Ruth shows Naomi the six ephahs of barley and reports Boaz’s words, “Do not go empty-handed to your mother-in-law.” The woman who came home empty now receives a gift meant for her.

“And she said, “These six ephahs of barley he gave me;”

Ruth shows Naomi the barley Boaz gave her.

“for he said to me, ‘Do not go empty-handed to your mother-in-law.’ ”

Ruth adds words of Boaz not recorded earlier: he did not want her to go back to Naomi “empty-handed.” Naomi had said that the LORD brought her home “empty” (1:21). The barley is a sign that her emptiness is beginning to be filled, and that Boaz has Naomi in mind as well as Ruth.

Study Notes

“Empty-handed” (3:17) translates the Hebrew word reqam, the same word Naomi used when she said the LORD had brought her home “empty” (1:21).

As in 3:5, the words “to me” are not in the written Hebrew text but are supplied by the traditional reading (Qere), which the NKJV follows.

Verse Explanation

Naomi tells Ruth to sit still until she knows how the matter will turn out, for the man will not rest until he has concluded it that day. Ruth has acted faithfully; now she waits while the one who can redeem does his part.

“Then she said, “Sit still, my daughter, until you know how the matter will turn out;”

Naomi tells Ruth to wait patiently. Ruth has done her part; now she must wait to see how things will turn out.

“for the man will not rest until he has concluded the matter this day.”

Naomi is confident that Boaz will act quickly. “The man will not rest” until the matter is settled, so that Ruth may finally find rest. Ruth has acted faithfully; now she waits while the one who can redeem does his part. The chapter ends with expectation, looking ahead to the meeting at the city gate.

Study Notes

“Rest” (3:18) translates the Hebrew verb shaqat, to be quiet or at rest. Naomi’s word about Boaz not resting stands in contrast with her goal of “security,” or rest, for Ruth in 3:1.
