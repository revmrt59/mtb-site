Chapter Study

Ruth 3

Verse-by-Verse
Ruth 3:1

NKJV

Ruth 3:1 Then Naomi her mother-in-law said to her, "My daughter, shall I not seek security for you, that it may be well with you?(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:1 One day Naomi said to Ruth, “My daughter, it’s time that I found a permanent home for you, so that you will be provided for. (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi now begins seeking a lasting future for Ruth rather than simply food for each day. Her desire is for Ruth to have the security and well-being that could come through marriage and an established home.

Verse Explanation

“Then Naomi her mother-in-law said to her”

Chapter 2 ended with Ruth continuing to live with Naomi while gleaning throughout the barley and wheat harvests. Now Naomi takes the initiative. Having recognized Boaz as one of their close relatives, she begins thinking beyond their immediate provision toward Ruth’s long-term future.

“‘My daughter, shall I not seek security for you’”

Naomi calls Ruth “my daughter,” expressing the affection that has developed between them. The word translated “security” carries the idea of rest-a settled place of well-being rather than an unsettled and vulnerable existence.

Naomi had used the same idea in Ruth 1:9 when she prayed that Ruth and Orpah would “find rest, each in the house of her husband.” What Naomi once hoped Ruth would find back in Moab, she now begins seeking for her in Bethlehem.

“‘that it may be well with you?’”

Naomi’s concern is genuinely for Ruth’s good. Ruth has faithfully cared for Naomi, but Naomi does not want Ruth’s future to consist indefinitely of supporting her widowed mother-in-law through gleaning. She wants Ruth to have a home, security, and a future of her own.

Study Notes

The Hebrew term behind “security” is manoach, related to the idea of rest or a resting place. Here it does not mean simply relaxation. In the social setting of Ruth, marriage could provide the settled household, protection, provision, and family future that a young widow like Ruth lacked.

There is an important connection between Ruth 1:9 and 3:1. Naomi first prayed that the LORD would give Ruth “rest” in the home of a husband. Now Naomi begins taking practical steps toward the very thing for which she had prayed. Prayer and responsible action are not presented as opposites.

Spirit & Life

There is a significant change in Naomi. The woman who returned to Bethlehem overwhelmed by her own emptiness is now actively seeking Ruth’s good. As hope returns to Naomi, her vision begins extending beyond her own loss toward the future of someone she loves.

Naomi’s question also introduces a movement from temporary provision toward deeper restoration. Ruth has grain for the present, but she still needs “rest.” God’s work in their lives is not finished with getting them through the harvest. The story is moving toward redemption, belonging, and a restored future.

Ruth 3:2

NKJV

Ruth 3:2 Now Boaz, whose young women you were with, is he not our relative? In fact, he is winnowing barley tonight at the threshing floor.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:2 Boaz is a close relative of ours, and he’s been very kind by letting you gather grain with his young women. Tonight he will be winnowing barley at the threshing floor. (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi identifies Boaz as the relative through whom Ruth’s future may change and points to an immediate opportunity to approach him. Boaz will be winnowing barley that night, setting the stage for Naomi’s plan.

Verse Explanation

“Now Boaz, whose young women you were with, is he not our relative?”

Naomi returns to what she had revealed in Ruth 2:20: Boaz is one of their close relatives. Ruth already knows him as the landowner who welcomed, protected, and generously provided for her, but his family relationship makes him especially important to Naomi’s plans.

The reference to “whose young women you were with” also reminds us that Ruth has now spent the harvest season working in Boaz’s fields. Boaz is not a complete stranger. Ruth has already experienced his character and kindness firsthand.

“In fact, he is winnowing barley tonight at the threshing floor.”

Winnowing was the process of separating the grain from the remaining chaff after threshing. The grain was tossed into the air with a winnowing implement so that the breeze could carry away the lighter chaff while the heavier grain fell back to the ground.

Winnowing was often done in the late afternoon or evening when suitable breezes arose. Naomi knows that Boaz himself will be at the threshing floor that night, providing the opportunity around which she will build her instructions to Ruth.

Study Notes

The word “relative” here identifies Boaz as belonging to Elimelech’s extended family, but it is not the specific word goel used for “close relative” in Ruth 2:20. Naomi has already established that Boaz is among those who could potentially act as a family redeemer.

The threshing floor was typically an open, elevated, hard-packed area where moving air could assist the winnowing process. Boaz’s personal presence there is not unusual; the harvested grain was valuable, and the work could continue into the evening.

The reference to “tonight” gives the chapter a sudden immediacy. Naomi is not merely discussing Ruth’s future in general terms. She has a particular opportunity in mind and is about to tell Ruth what she should do.

Spirit & Life

Naomi had prayed in Ruth 1:9 that Ruth would find “rest” in the home of a husband. Now, after recognizing Boaz as a potential redeemer, she sees an opportunity and acts. The story continues to place God’s providence alongside ordinary human decisions rather than treating them as competitors.

There is also a quiet progression in Ruth’s experience with Boaz. She first happened into his field, then received his favor, remained under his protection through the harvest, and now discovers that this generous man may have a much deeper role in her future. God’s larger purposes have been unfolding through circumstances that initially looked like nothing more than a widow searching for somewhere to gather grain.

Ruth 3:3

NKJV

Ruth 3:3 Therefore wash yourself and anoint yourself, put on your best garment and go down to the threshing floor; but do not make yourself known to the man until he has finished eating and drinking.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:3 Now do as I tell you — take a bath and put on perfume and dress in your nicest clothes. Then go to the threshing floor, but don’t let Boaz see you until he has finished eating and drinking. (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi tells Ruth to prepare herself carefully before going to the threshing floor. Her instructions signal that this is not an ordinary evening of gleaning but a deliberate approach to Boaz concerning Ruth’s future.

Verse Explanation

“Therefore wash yourself and anoint yourself”

Naomi first tells Ruth to bathe and apply oil. In the ancient world, fragrant oil served both for personal grooming and refreshment. Ruth is to present herself carefully and intentionally for what Naomi is planning.

Some understand these actions as possibly marking the end of Ruth’s formal period of mourning for her husband. That is possible, but the text does not explicitly say so. What is clear is that Naomi wants Ruth appropriately prepared for a significant personal encounter with Boaz.

“put on your best garment”

The NKJV supplies “best” to express the sense of Naomi’s instruction; the underlying wording simply refers to Ruth’s garment or clothing. In context, however, Naomi is plainly telling Ruth to dress appropriately and present herself well.

The emphasis is not on extravagance. Ruth is preparing herself for an encounter in which she will effectively ask Boaz to consider taking responsibility for her future.

“and go down to the threshing floor”

Ruth is to go where Naomi knows Boaz will be winnowing barley. The threshing floor now becomes the setting for the pivotal encounter that follows.

“but do not make yourself known to the man until he has finished eating and drinking.”

Naomi tells Ruth not to approach Boaz immediately. She is to wait until the evening’s work and meal are finished. Verse 7 will show Boaz eating and drinking and then lying down beside the grain.

The instruction is deliberate: Ruth is not to interrupt Boaz while he is working or eating. Naomi wants her to wait for the appropriate moment before making her request.

Study Notes

Anointing with oil was a normal part of grooming in the ancient Near East, particularly for occasions when someone wished to present themselves well. Nothing in this verse by itself suggests seduction.

The NKJV’s “best garment” is interpretive. The Hebrew does not contain a separate word meaning “best.” The context supports the idea of dressing suitably for an important encounter, but we should not imagine details about Ruth’s clothing that the text does not provide.

Naomi’s plan will become clearer in verses 4-9. Reading verse 3 by itself can make the scene sound more suggestive than the narrative ultimately presents it. Ruth’s decisive action will be a request for Boaz to “take your maidservant under your wing” because he is a close relative-a direct appeal for him to act as redeemer.

Spirit & Life

Ruth’s preparation shows that seeking Boaz’s help is not casual. She is about to place her future before a man whose character she has already observed throughout the harvest.

There is also a helpful restraint in the narrative. Ruth prepares carefully, but the heart of what follows will not be her appearance. The decisive issue is redemption. Ruth will ultimately appeal not to attraction alone but to Boaz’s relationship to the family and his willingness to take her under his protection.

Ruth 3:4

NKJV

Ruth 3:4 Then it shall be, when he lies down, that you shall notice the place where he lies; and you shall go in, uncover his feet, and lie down; and he will tell you what you should do."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:4 Be sure to notice where he lies down; then go and uncover his feet and lie down there. He will tell you what to do.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi instructs Ruth to wait until Boaz is asleep, uncover his feet, and lie down there. This unusual symbolic action will place Ruth in a position of humility and allow Boaz himself to respond to her appeal.

Verse Explanation

“Then it shall be, when he lies down, that you shall notice the place where he lies”

Ruth is not to approach Boaz publicly or interrupt the activity at the threshing floor. She is to observe where he settles for the night so she can approach the correct place after he has fallen asleep.

The secrecy of Naomi’s plan makes the scene unusual and creates genuine tension in the story. Ruth will be placing herself in a vulnerable situation, which makes the established character of both Ruth and Boaz especially important for understanding what follows.

“and you shall go in, uncover his feet, and lie down”

After Boaz is asleep, Ruth is to uncover the area at his feet and lie there. The action is deliberately humble: Ruth places herself at Boaz’s feet rather than beside him.

The precise cultural significance of uncovering his feet is uncertain, and the text does not explain a standard custom behind it. The safest reading is to allow the following verses to interpret the action. Ruth is positioning herself so that Boaz will awaken, discover her there, and hear the request she will make in verse 9.

“and he will tell you what you should do.”

At this point Naomi expects Boaz to take the initiative. Ruth’s presence at his feet will communicate that she has come with a serious request, but Boaz must decide how to respond.

Interestingly, Ruth will do more than simply wait for instructions. When Boaz asks who she is, she directly asks him to “take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative” (3:9). Her request makes the issue explicit: she is asking Boaz to act as her redeemer.

Study Notes

The scene contains intentional ambiguity and should neither be sanitized nor sexualized beyond what the text says. A woman approaching a man secretly at night on a threshing floor could create vulnerability and potential misunderstanding. The narrative depends heavily upon the demonstrated integrity of Ruth and Boaz.

“Uncover his feet” should not automatically be treated as a euphemism for sexual activity. Although biblical Hebrew can sometimes use references to “feet” euphemistically, nothing that follows indicates sexual relations here. Boaz instead praises Ruth’s character, protects her reputation, and insists on handling the redemption properly (3:10-14).

There is no clear evidence that Ruth’s precise action represents a widely established Israelite marriage ritual. Its meaning becomes clear through Ruth’s explicit request in verse 9 rather than through reconstructing an uncertain custom.

Spirit & Life

Ruth is being asked to take a significant personal risk. She cannot control Boaz’s response; she can only approach the man whose kindness and integrity she has already experienced and place her future before him.

The scene also moves the theme of redemption from possibility toward a personal appeal. Boaz has been generous to Ruth, but Ruth now needs more than grain from his field. She needs the redeemer himself to act on her behalf. That distinction gives the story spiritual depth: God's greatest provision is ultimately not merely what the Redeemer gives, but the Redeemer to whom we entrust ourselves.

Ruth 3:5

NKJV

Ruth 3:5 And she said to her, "All that you say to me I will do."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:5 “I will do everything you say,” Ruth replied. (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth agrees without hesitation to follow Naomi’s instructions. Her brief response continues the pattern of humility, trust, and obedience that has characterized her throughout the story.

Verse Explanation

“And she said to her”

Ruth has heard Naomi’s entire plan, including the unusual instruction to approach Boaz secretly at the threshing floor. The verse gives no indication of argument or resistance.

“‘All that you say to me I will do.’”

Ruth willingly accepts Naomi’s direction. This is significant because Naomi’s plan requires courage. Ruth will enter a vulnerable situation in which Boaz’s response cannot be guaranteed.

Her response also continues what we have already seen in Ruth’s relationship with Naomi. Ruth did not merely promise loyalty on the road from Moab; she has consistently lived out that commitment through concrete actions.

Study Notes

Ruth’s statement is emphatic in its completeness: “All that you say to me I will do.” The narrative immediately confirms her words in verse 6: “she did according to all that her mother-in-law instructed her.” Her promise and her action match.

There is no need to reconstruct an unspoken conversation here. The brevity is part of the force of the verse. Naomi gives the instructions; Ruth accepts them and proceeds.

Spirit & Life

Ruth’s faithfulness repeatedly appears in ordinary obedience. She committed herself to Naomi, went out to glean, remained where Boaz and Naomi told her to remain, and now acts upon Naomi’s counsel. Her character is being revealed not mainly through what she says about herself, but through what she consistently does.

Her obedience is not passive weakness. What Naomi asks will require considerable courage. Ruth’s “I will do” is the response of a woman willing to step into an uncertain situation because she trusts Naomi and is prepared to pursue the possibility of redemption that has opened before her.

Ruth 3:6

NKJV

Ruth 3:6 So she went down to the threshing floor and did according to all that her mother-in-law instructed her.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:6 So she went down to the threshing floor that night and followed the instructions of her mother-in-law. (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth follows through on her promise to Naomi. She goes to the threshing floor and carefully carries out the instructions she has been given.

Verse Explanation

“So she went down to the threshing floor”

Ruth now moves from agreement to action. The threshing floor is where Boaz is spending the evening winnowing and guarding his harvested grain, and it is where Naomi believes Ruth can privately approach him about her future.

This step requires courage. Ruth is entering an unusual and potentially vulnerable situation, but she proceeds because she trusts Naomi’s counsel and, from her experience during the harvest, has reason to trust Boaz’s character.

“and did according to all that her mother-in-law instructed her.”

Verse 5 recorded Ruth’s promise, “All that you say to me I will do.” This verse emphasizes that she keeps that promise. Ruth does not selectively modify Naomi’s plan once she reaches the threshing floor; she follows the instructions she has received.

The statement also prepares us for the details beginning in verse 7. Rather than repeating each instruction immediately, the narrative first establishes that Ruth faithfully carried out Naomi’s plan and then describes what happened.

Study Notes

“Went down” is geographically natural. Threshing floors were often located on exposed higher ground where breezes aided winnowing, but Ruth is going out from Bethlehem to the threshing floor, and biblical descriptions of travel frequently reflect local geography. There is no special symbolic meaning that needs to be attached to “down.”

The close verbal connection between verses 5 and 6 is intentional. Ruth says she will do “all” Naomi tells her, and the narrator immediately confirms that she “did according to all” Naomi instructed. The repetition highlights the consistency between Ruth’s words and actions.

Spirit & Life

Ruth’s character continues to be revealed through follow-through. “I will do” in verse 5 becomes “she...did” in verse 6. Scripture often places considerable weight on this simple movement from intention to action.

Ruth also steps forward without knowing how the night will end. She cannot control Boaz’s response or secure the outcome herself. She can only faithfully take the next step toward the possibility of redemption that has been placed before her.

Ruth 3:7

NKJV

Ruth 3:7 And after Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was cheerful, he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain; and she came softly, uncovered his feet, and lay down.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:7 After Boaz had finished eating and drinking and was in good spirits, he lay down at the far end of the pile of grain and went to sleep. Then Ruth came quietly, uncovered his feet, and lay down. (NLT)

Main Idea

After Boaz finishes the evening satisfied with the harvest and falls asleep beside the grain, Ruth quietly carries out Naomi’s unusual instructions. The anticipated encounter is now set in motion.

Verse Explanation

“And after Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was cheerful”

Boaz finishes his meal after the work at the threshing floor. “His heart was cheerful” describes his contented, satisfied state. The harvest has been gathered, the work of the evening is complete, and Boaz settles down in good spirits.

The text does not say that Boaz was drunk. Drinking is mentioned, but the emphasis is on his cheerful state after eating and drinking rather than on intoxication.

“he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain”

Boaz sleeps beside the harvested grain. This was practical: the threshing floor held the valuable product of the harvest, and remaining nearby allowed him to guard it during the night.

The detail also gives Ruth the precise location Naomi told her to observe. Boaz is now settled for the night, and Ruth knows where he is lying.

“and she came softly”

Ruth waits until Boaz is asleep and then approaches quietly. The secrecy is intentional. This is a private encounter concerning a deeply personal matter, and Ruth does not want to awaken Boaz prematurely or attract the attention of others at the threshing floor.

“uncovered his feet, and lay down.”

Ruth carries out Naomi’s instruction from verse 4. She positions herself at Boaz’s feet and waits for him to awaken.

The action is unusual, but the narrative does not describe sexual activity. Ruth’s position at his feet is one of vulnerability and humility, and verse 9 will make her purpose explicit: she has come to ask Boaz, as a close relative, to spread his wing over her and act as her redeemer.

Study Notes

“His heart was cheerful” is a common biblical expression for being pleased or merry after eating and drinking. While wine was likely involved, nothing in the text requires the conclusion that Boaz was intoxicated. His conversation and actions after awakening are controlled and thoughtful.

Boaz lying “at the end of the heap of grain” fits the practical need to remain near the harvested crop. It also makes Ruth’s approach possible without requiring a more elaborate reconstruction of the threshing-floor setting.

The narrative continues to maintain deliberate tension. A nighttime encounter between a man and woman at a threshing floor could easily become morally compromising, yet the following verses emphasize the opposite: Ruth and Boaz act with restraint, honor, and concern for what is right.

Spirit & Life

Ruth has now done what she can and lies at Boaz’s feet waiting. She cannot force redemption or determine how Boaz will respond. She can only place herself before the one who has the ability to act for her.

That posture gives the scene a beautiful spiritual resonance without turning every detail into allegory. There comes a point when need must become an appeal to the redeemer. Ruth has already received Boaz’s grain, protection, and generosity; now the deeper question is whether Boaz himself will take responsibility for her future.

Ruth 3:8

NKJV

Ruth 3:8 Now it happened at midnight that the man was startled, and turned himself; and there, a woman was lying at his feet.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:8 Around midnight Boaz suddenly woke up and turned over. He was surprised to find a woman lying at his feet! (NLT)

Main Idea

In the middle of the night, Boaz is suddenly startled awake and discovers a woman lying at his feet. The moment Naomi had anticipated has arrived, but Boaz does not yet know who she is or why she has come.

Verse Explanation

“Now it happened at midnight”

Some time has passed since Boaz lay down beside the grain. Ruth has quietly remained at his feet until the middle of the night, when something awakens him.

“that the man was startled”

Boaz wakes suddenly and is alarmed or startled. The wording suggests an abrupt reaction rather than a gradual awakening. Something unexpected has disturbed his sleep.

“and turned himself”

Boaz shifts or bends over to determine what has awakened him. The darkness and unexpected circumstances help explain the surprise expressed in the next phrase.

“and there, a woman was lying at his feet.”

Boaz discovers that someone is lying at his feet-and that the person is a woman. At this point he does not yet know that it is Ruth. His question in the next verse, “Who are you?”, confirms that he cannot identify her in the darkness.

The narrative has now reached the moment toward which Naomi’s instructions were directed. Ruth has placed herself at Boaz’s feet; what happens next will depend upon how she explains her presence and how Boaz responds.

Study Notes

The word translated “was startled” can convey trembling, fear, or sudden alarm. Boaz’s reaction is therefore understandable: he awakens during the night and unexpectedly discovers someone at his feet.

The expression translated “turned himself” is somewhat difficult to render precisely and may carry the idea of bending or twisting around to investigate. The central point is clear even if the exact physical movement is not: Boaz awakens abruptly and discovers Ruth.

The Hebrew narrative uses an attention-drawing expression behind “and there,” marking Boaz’s unexpected discovery. The reader knows Ruth is present; Boaz does not. The wording lets us experience the surprise from his perspective.

Spirit & Life

This verse is mostly narrative tension, and it is best allowed to remain that. Ruth has taken the step Naomi instructed, but nothing has yet been resolved. She still must identify herself, make her request, and await Boaz’s answer.

There is something important in that waiting. Ruth has come to the right person, but coming to the redeemer does not mean controlling the outcome. She has placed herself at his feet; the next movement must come through his response.

Ruth 3:9

NKJV

Ruth 3:9 And he said, "Who are you?" So she answered, "I am Ruth, your maidservant. Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:9 “Who are you?” he asked. “I am your servant Ruth,” she replied. “Spread the corner of your covering over me, for you are my family redeemer.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth openly asks Boaz to take her under his protection and act as her family redeemer. What began as Naomi’s plan now becomes Ruth’s own direct appeal for Boaz to take responsibility for her future.

Verse Explanation

“And he said, ‘Who are you?’”

Awakened in the darkness and startled to find a woman at his feet, Boaz asks her identity. The question gives Ruth the opportunity to explain both who she is and why she has come.

“So she answered, ‘I am Ruth, your maidservant.’”

Ruth identifies herself immediately and speaks with humility. Earlier, she had called herself Boaz’s “maidservant” when amazed that he would show favor to a foreigner (2:13). Now she again places herself humbly before him, but this time she has come with a remarkable request.

“‘Take your maidservant under your wing’”

Literally, Ruth asks Boaz to spread his “wing” or the corner of his garment over her. In this setting, the request carries the idea of taking her under his protection and entering a marriage relationship with her. Ruth is not merely asking Boaz for continued food or assistance; she is asking him to receive her as his wife.

There is a beautiful connection with Boaz’s own words in 2:12. He had praised Ruth for coming to take refuge under the LORD’s “wings.” Now Ruth asks Boaz to spread his own “wing” over her. In effect, she is asking Boaz to become part of the way the LORD answers the blessing Boaz had spoken over her.

“‘for you are a close relative.’”

Ruth grounds her request in Boaz’s role as a goel, a family redeemer. His relationship to Elimelech’s family gives him the ability to act on behalf of Ruth and Naomi in a way an unrelated man could not.

Ruth therefore makes Naomi’s plan explicit. She is not at Boaz’s feet merely to attract his attention. She is asking him to take up the responsibility of redemption.

Study Notes

The word translated “wing” can also mean the edge or corner of a garment. This creates a deliberate connection between the physical image of Boaz spreading his garment over Ruth and the larger idea of placing her under his protection.

The connection with Ruth 2:12 is especially important. The same word stands behind the LORD’s “wings” there and Boaz’s “wing” here. Ruth has sought refuge under God’s wings; now Boaz is being asked to become a human instrument of that refuge.

Ruth’s appeal combines marriage and redemption, but Israel’s laws should not be flattened into one rule. The goel responsibilities concerning family property (Leviticus 25) and the duty to preserve a deceased brother’s family line (Deuteronomy 25:5-10) are related themes, yet Boaz is not Mahlon’s brother. Ruth’s situation brings these family responsibilities together in a distinctive way, and verse 12 will reveal that another relative has a prior claim.

Spirit & Life

Ruth has moved from gleaning in Boaz’s field to asking for Boaz himself. Grain could sustain her for a season; redemption could give her a new future. The story has moved from receiving the redeemer’s provision to seeking the redeemer’s covering.

The connection between the LORD’s “wings” in 2:12 and Boaz’s “wing” here is particularly rich. God’s care does not make human action unnecessary. Boaz had prayed that Ruth would be rewarded by the God under whose wings she had taken refuge; now Ruth effectively asks Boaz to participate in the answer to his own prayer.

Ruth 3:10

NKJV

Ruth 3:10 Then he said, "Blessed are you of the LORD, my daughter! For you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning, in that you did not go after young men, whether poor or rich.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:10 “The LORD bless you, my daughter!” Boaz exclaimed. “You are showing even more family loyalty now than you did before, for you have not gone after a younger man, whether rich or poor. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz responds to Ruth’s request with blessing rather than rejection. He sees her appeal as another expression of her remarkable faithfulness because she has sought redemption through the family rather than simply pursuing a younger husband.

Verse Explanation

“Then he said, ‘Blessed are you of the LORD, my daughter!’”

Boaz immediately reassures Ruth. His first response is not suspicion or embarrassment but blessing. By calling her “my daughter,” he speaks with tenderness and also suggests an age difference between them, something he makes more explicit later in the verse.

Boaz invokes the LORD because he recognizes something spiritually admirable in Ruth’s actions.

“‘For you have shown more kindness at the end than at the beginning’”

The “kindness” here is hesed, describing loyal, committed kindness expressed through action. Ruth’s earlier kindness was her extraordinary loyalty to Naomi: she left Moab, accompanied her widowed mother-in-law to Bethlehem, and devoted herself to caring for her.

Her “kindness at the end” is what she is doing now. Rather than treating marriage simply as a matter of her own preference, Ruth is seeking a marriage that can preserve the family line and contribute to the restoration of Naomi’s family.

Boaz therefore sees Ruth’s present action as an even greater expression of the same loyal love that brought her to Bethlehem.

“‘in that you did not go after young men, whether poor or rich.’”

Boaz recognizes that Ruth could have sought a younger husband based on attraction, wealth, or personal advantage. Instead, she has approached him because he is a close relative capable of acting as redeemer.

The contrast between “poor or rich” shows that money is not Boaz’s main point. Ruth has not simply chosen the wealthiest available man. Her decision reflects faithfulness to the family she joined when she committed herself to Naomi.

Study Notes

Hesed, translated “kindness,” is one of the important words in Ruth. It describes steadfast, loyal kindness demonstrated through concrete action. Ruth has shown it toward Naomi, Boaz has shown it toward Ruth, and Naomi has recognized the LORD’s continuing kindness toward their family (2:20).

Boaz’s reference to “young men” strongly suggests that he is older than Ruth, although the text never gives their ages. His words help explain why Ruth’s approach surprises him: he apparently did not assume that a younger woman would consider him as a husband.

“At the end” and “at the beginning” compare Ruth’s present act of loyalty with the loyalty she had already shown Naomi. Boaz is not saying her earlier kindness was small. He is emphasizing how remarkable he considers her present decision.

Spirit & Life

Boaz sees beneath Ruth’s action to the character behind it. Others might simply see a young widow seeking a husband; Boaz sees hesed-a woman continuing to live out the loyal love that has characterized her since she left Moab.

There is also something beautiful in Boaz’s response to Ruth’s vulnerability. She has placed her future before him, and his first words bring blessing and reassurance rather than taking advantage of her. As the story moves toward redemption, the character of the redeemer continues to matter as much as his ability to redeem.

Ruth 3:11

NKJV

Ruth 3:11 And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you request, for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:11 Now don’t worry about a thing, my daughter. I will do what is necessary, for everyone in town knows you are a virtuous woman. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz assures Ruth that he is willing to fulfill her request. Her character is already well known in Bethlehem, and he recognizes her as a woman of exceptional worth.

Verse Explanation

“‘And now, my daughter, do not fear.’”

Boaz immediately reassures Ruth. She has taken a vulnerable step by approaching him and asking him to act as her redeemer, but she does not need to fear rejection, humiliation, or mistreatment from him.

His repeated “my daughter” remains warm and respectful and again fits the apparent age difference suggested in verse 10.

“‘I will do for you all that you request’”

Boaz responds positively to Ruth’s appeal. She asked him to “take your maidservant under your wing” because he is a close relative (3:9), and Boaz now declares his willingness to act.

This is not yet an unconditional promise that he will marry her. Verse 12 introduces an obstacle: another relative has a prior right to redeem. Boaz is promising to pursue Ruth’s request faithfully while honoring that responsibility.

“‘for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman.’”

Ruth’s character is no longer known only to Naomi and Boaz. During her months in Bethlehem, she has developed a reputation throughout the community.

“Virtuous woman” translates an expression meaning a woman of strength, worth, ability, or noble character. Ruth came to Bethlehem as a widowed Moabite with virtually nothing, yet what the community has come to recognize is the quality of the woman herself.

Study Notes

The expression behind “virtuous woman” is eshet chayil. Chayil has a broad range that can include strength, ability, valor, wealth, or excellence depending on context. Here it describes Ruth as a woman of proven worth and strong character.

The same expression appears in Proverbs 31:10: “Who can find a virtuous wife?” Ruth is the only individual woman in the Old Testament explicitly described with this exact expression. The connection is especially striking because Proverbs 31 describes such a woman in general terms, while Ruth provides a narrative picture of a woman whose character has become publicly recognized.

The NKJV’s “all the people of my town” represents an expression literally referring to the “gate” of Boaz’s people. The town gate was the center of public and legal community life. This anticipates chapter 4, where Boaz will go to the gate to settle the redemption publicly.

Spirit & Life

Ruth arrived in Bethlehem identified repeatedly as “Ruth the Moabitess.” Now Boaz can say that the community knows her as a “virtuous woman.” Her background has not disappeared from the story, but her faithful life has established a new reputation.

There is also a beautiful correspondence between Ruth and Boaz. Boaz was introduced as “a man of great wealth” (2:1), using the related word chayil; Ruth is now called a woman of chayil. The story is bringing together two people distinguished not merely by circumstances but by strength of character.

Ruth 3:12

NKJV

Ruth 3:12 Now it is true that I am a close relative; however, there is a relative closer than I.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:12 But while it’s true that I am one of your family redeemers, there is another man who is more closely related to you than I am. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz confirms that Ruth is right to view him as a potential redeemer, but he immediately reveals an important complication: another relative has a stronger legal claim. His integrity requires him to honor that man’s right before acting himself.

Verse Explanation

“‘Now it is true that I am a close relative’”

Boaz confirms the basis of Ruth’s request in verse 9. She had appealed to him because he was a “close relative,” and he acknowledges that she is correct. He belongs to the family circle from which a redeemer could act for Naomi and Ruth.

This also confirms that Boaz’s positive response in verse 11 is serious. He is willing to act, and he has the family relationship necessary to do so.

“‘however, there is a relative closer than I.’”

Boaz now reveals something Ruth and perhaps Naomi did not know: another man is more closely related to Elimelech’s family than Boaz is. That man therefore has the first opportunity to act as redeemer.

This creates the central obstacle leading into chapter 4. Boaz wants to help Ruth, but he will not bypass the rights of the nearer relative to obtain the outcome he desires. Redemption must be handled rightly.

Study Notes

The word translated “close relative” is goel, the same important term used in Ruth 2:20 and 3:9. It refers to a family member able to perform responsibilities of redemption on behalf of relatives.

The text does not tell us exactly how either Boaz or the nearer relative was related to Elimelech. It only gives the information necessary for the story: both qualify as relatives, but the unnamed man has priority.

This verse is important for interpreting Boaz’s statement in verse 11, “I will do for you all that you request.” He was not promising to disregard the nearer relative. He was committing himself to pursue Ruth’s redemption faithfully, subject to the proper family order.

Spirit & Life

Boaz’s integrity becomes especially clear when doing what is right could cost him what he wants. Ruth has asked him personally to redeem her, and he is willing, yet he refuses to take a shortcut around another man’s legitimate right.

That makes Boaz’s role as redeemer more compelling. He possesses not only the ability and willingness to redeem but also the righteousness to accomplish redemption properly. The story is moving toward a beautiful biblical pattern that ultimately finds its fullness in Jesus: redemption is not accomplished by setting righteousness aside, but in complete harmony with what is right.

Ruth 3:13

NKJV

Ruth 3:13 Stay this night, and in the morning it shall be that if he will perform the duty of a close relative for you"”good; let him do it. But if he does not want to perform the duty for you, then I will perform the duty for you, as the LORD lives! Lie down until morning."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:13 Stay here tonight, and in the morning I will talk to him. If he is willing to redeem you, very well. Let him marry you. But if he is not willing, then as surely as the LORD lives, I will redeem you myself! Now lie down here until morning.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz asks Ruth to remain safely until morning and solemnly promises that her redemption will be settled. The nearer relative will receive his rightful opportunity, but if he refuses, Boaz personally commits himself to redeem Ruth.

Verse Explanation

“‘Stay this night’”

Boaz tells Ruth to remain until morning rather than leave the threshing floor alone in the middle of the night. In the immediate context, this is a practical matter of safety, not an invitation to sexual intimacy. The narrative continues to emphasize Boaz’s protection of Ruth.

“‘and in the morning it shall be that if he will perform the duty of a close relative for you-good; let him do it.’”

Boaz will give the nearer relative the first opportunity to act as redeemer. Although Boaz clearly desires to help Ruth, he refuses to ignore the other man’s prior right.

The expression “perform the duty of a close relative” comes from the same word family as goel. Boaz is speaking specifically about carrying out the responsibility of redemption.

“‘But if he does not want to perform the duty for you’”

The nearer relative has priority, but he may decline. Boaz therefore anticipates both possibilities. Ruth will not be left wondering indefinitely whether anyone will act for her.

Chapter 4 will show exactly how Boaz brings the matter before the nearer relative and allows him to make that decision publicly.

“‘then I will perform the duty for you, as the LORD lives!’”

Boaz now gives Ruth an exceptionally strong assurance. “As the LORD lives” is a solemn oath invoking the living God as witness to his promise.

If the nearer relative refuses, Boaz will redeem her himself. Ruth came to the threshing floor uncertain how Boaz would respond; she can now rest knowing that Boaz has personally committed himself to see the matter through.

“‘Lie down until morning.’”

Boaz again tells Ruth to remain until morning. There is nothing more she needs to accomplish that night. She has made her request, Boaz has received it, and he has promised to act.

Study Notes

The language of redemption is especially concentrated here. Forms related to goel occur repeatedly, giving the statement something like the force of: “If he will redeem you, let him redeem; but if he will not redeem you, I will redeem you.” The repetition keeps redemption at the center of the scene.

“As the LORD lives” is a formal oath formula found elsewhere in Scripture. Boaz is not merely expressing good intentions; he binds his promise before the LORD.

Boaz’s handling of the nearer relative is important. He has both willingness and restraint. He will not abandon Ruth, but neither will he secure her by violating the proper claims of another relative.

Spirit & Life

Ruth came to Boaz asking for redemption; she now receives a promise from the redeemer: if the nearer man will not act, “I will.” The uncertainty has shifted. Ruth still does not know exactly how the situation will resolve, but she now knows Boaz will faithfully take responsibility for what comes next.

There is a beautiful movement here from appeal to rest. Ruth has made her request and received Boaz’s word; now he tells her, “Lie down until morning.” She cannot settle the matter herself, and she no longer needs to try. The one willing to redeem her has promised to act.

Ruth 3:14

NKJV

Ruth 3:14 So she lay at his feet until morning, and she arose before one could recognize another. Then he said, "Do not let it be known that the woman came to the threshing floor."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:14 So Ruth lay at Boaz’s feet until the morning, but she got up before it was light enough for people to recognize each other. For Boaz had said, “No one must know that a woman was here at the threshing floor.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth remains at Boaz’s feet until just before daylight, then leaves before she can be recognized. Boaz carefully protects Ruth’s reputation, making sure their honorable encounter is not turned into damaging gossip.

Verse Explanation

“So she lay at his feet until morning”

Ruth remains exactly where she had positioned herself earlier-at Boaz’s feet. Nothing in the narrative suggests that the nature of their relationship changed during the night. The detail reinforces the restraint that has characterized their encounter.

“and she arose before one could recognize another.”

Ruth leaves while it is still dark enough that people cannot clearly identify one another. This allows her to return to Naomi without being recognized leaving the threshing floor after spending the night there.

The concern is not that Ruth and Boaz have done something immoral. Rather, their circumstances could easily be misunderstood. Their conduct has been honorable, but Boaz recognizes that appearances and reputation also matter.

“Then he said, ‘Do not let it be known that the woman came to the threshing floor.’”

Boaz does not want Ruth’s nighttime visit becoming public knowledge. A woman secretly visiting a man at a threshing floor and remaining until morning could create damaging assumptions regardless of what actually happened.

His instruction therefore protects Ruth, himself, and the redemption process that still must be handled publicly and properly with the nearer relative.

Study Notes

The final statement may be Boaz’s instruction to Ruth or to others who were present nearby but had not identified her. The Hebrew does not specify the audience. Either way, the point is clear: Boaz wants Ruth’s presence at the threshing floor kept private.

The wording “the woman” rather than Ruth’s name may help preserve the anonymity Boaz is seeking. The narrative itself has repeatedly emphasized Ruth’s good character, and Boaz has just declared that the whole community knows she is a “virtuous woman” (3:11). His concern here is therefore reputation, not concealing misconduct.

This verse also guards against interpreting the nighttime scene sexually. Ruth remains “at his feet,” and the morning concern is that an innocent encounter not be misconstrued.

Spirit & Life

Boaz understands that integrity involves more than knowing privately that you have done nothing wrong. He also cares about protecting Ruth from unnecessary suspicion. Her reputation matters to him, and he refuses to treat it casually.

There is also a striking contrast between secrecy here and what Boaz will do next. Ruth’s vulnerable nighttime appeal is protected from public exposure, but the redemption itself will be handled openly at the city gate. Boaz protects what should remain private while bringing into the light what must be settled publicly.

Ruth 3:15

NKJV

Ruth 3:15 Also he said, "Bring the shawl that is on you and hold it." And when she held it, he measured six ephahs of barley, and laid it on her. Then she went into the city.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:15 Then Boaz said to her, “Bring your cloak and spread it out.” He measured six scoops of barley into the cloak and placed it on her back. Then he returned to the town. (NLT)

Main Idea

Before Ruth leaves, Boaz gives her another generous supply of barley to take home. The gift provides for Ruth and Naomi immediately and gives Naomi tangible evidence that Boaz intends to follow through on his promise.

Verse Explanation

“Also he said, ‘Bring the shawl that is on you and hold it.’”

Before Ruth leaves the threshing floor, Boaz asks her to use her outer garment as a container for grain. The exact type of garment is uncertain, but it was apparently large and strong enough to hold a substantial quantity of barley.

“And when she held it, he measured six ephahs of barley”

The NKJV italicizes “ephahs” because the Hebrew text simply says “six of barley” without naming the unit of measurement. It is therefore better not to assume that Boaz gave Ruth six full ephahs.

Six ephahs would have been an enormous amount-far beyond what Ruth could reasonably carry in her garment. The intended unit was almost certainly smaller, though the text does not tell us exactly what it was. The point is that Boaz gives Ruth another generous quantity of grain.

“and laid it on her.”

Boaz helps place the bundled grain onto Ruth so she can carry it back to Bethlehem. Once again, his promise concerning the future is accompanied by practical provision for Ruth’s present need.

“Then she went into the city.”

Ruth leaves before daylight, as verse 14 explained, and returns to Bethlehem. The nighttime meeting has ended, but Boaz’s work as potential redeemer is only beginning. He will soon go to the city gate to settle the matter.

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “ephahs” is supplied rather than stated in the Hebrew and should not be treated as certain. This is an important Bible-reading clarification because Ruth gathered about one ephah after an entire day of gleaning in 2:17. Six ephahs could weigh well over two hundred pounds, making that interpretation highly unlikely.

Ancient translations and interpreters have proposed different measures, but the safest conclusion is simply that the precise unit has been omitted. The narrative emphasizes Boaz’s generosity, not the exact modern weight of the barley.

There is also a textual variation at the end of the verse. Some Hebrew manuscripts and ancient versions read essentially “she went into the city,” while the traditional Hebrew text can be read “he went into the city.” Either reading fits the unfolding narrative: Ruth returns to Naomi, while Boaz will go into Bethlehem to handle the redemption.

Spirit & Life

Boaz does not send Ruth back to Naomi with promises alone. He sends provision with her. The grain cannot accomplish Ruth’s redemption, but it is tangible evidence of the character of the man who has promised to act.

There may also be significance in the six measures, especially because Naomi will say in the next verse that Boaz “will not rest until he has concluded the matter this day.” Some see six as suggesting unfinished work before the “rest” of completion, but the text never explicitly makes that symbolism, so it is better held as a possibility rather than presented as the meaning.

The stronger point is already beautiful: Ruth leaves the threshing floor carrying both Boaz’s provision and Boaz’s promise. She does not yet have the completed redemption, but she has every reason to trust the redeemer who has given his word.

Ruth 3:16

NKJV

Ruth 3:16 When she came to her mother-in-law, she said, "Is that you, my daughter?" Then she told her all that the man had done for her.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:16 When Ruth went back to her mother-in-law, Naomi asked, “What happened, my daughter?” Ruth told Naomi everything Boaz had done for her, (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi immediately recognizes that something significant has happened between Ruth and Boaz. Ruth returns from the threshing floor and tells Naomi everything Boaz has done for her.

Verse Explanation

“When she came to her mother-in-law, she said, ‘Is that you, my daughter?’”

Ruth returns to Naomi after leaving the threshing floor early in the morning. Naomi’s question can sound strange in English because she obviously knows Ruth. The sense is likely closer to asking, “How did it go, my daughter?” or “What happened?” Naomi wants to know the outcome of Ruth’s encounter with Boaz.

“Then she told her all that the man had done for her.”

Ruth gives Naomi a full account rather than simply reporting Boaz’s answer. What Boaz “had done for her” includes his willingness to act as redeemer, his promise to settle the matter, his protection of Ruth’s reputation, and the generous provision of grain he sent home with her. Ruth has returned with far more than a verbal promise-Boaz’s actions have already demonstrated his concern and commitment.

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “Is that you, my daughter?” translates a very brief Hebrew expression that literally resembles “Who are you, my daughter?” In context, Naomi is almost certainly not asking Ruth to identify herself. The wording functions naturally as an inquiry into Ruth’s situation: “What happened?” or “How did things turn out?” This explains why translations differ at this point.

The phrase “all that the man had done for her” prepares for Ruth’s further report in verse 17 concerning the six measures of barley. Naomi will interpret both Boaz’s words and his actions together.

Spirit & Life

Ruth does something simple but important: she comes back and tells Naomi what happened. She does not have the whole matter resolved yet, but she can recount what Boaz has already said and done. Sometimes faith is strengthened in the waiting by carefully remembering what God has already done rather than focusing only on what remains unfinished.

Boaz is also increasingly revealed through actions, not merely intentions. His willingness to redeem Ruth is accompanied by protection, generosity, and concrete steps toward her future. That pattern beautifully anticipates the greater Redeemer, Jesus, whose love for us is not merely declared but demonstrated and accomplished on our behalf.

Ruth 3:17

NKJV

Ruth 3:17 And she said, "These six ephahs of barley he gave me; for he said to me, "˜Do not go empty-handed to your mother-in-law.' "(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:17 and she added, “He gave me these six scoops of barley and said, ‘Don’t go back to your mother-in-law empty-handed.’” (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth shows Naomi the generous gift Boaz sent with her and explains that he specifically did not want her returning empty-handed. His gift gives Naomi tangible evidence of both his kindness and the seriousness of his intentions.

Verse Explanation

“And she said, ‘These six ephahs of barley he gave me;’”

Ruth now tells Naomi about the barley Boaz had measured out for her before she left the threshing floor. The NKJV supplies “ephahs”, but the Hebrew text simply says “six of barley” without identifying the unit. Whatever the exact quantity, it was substantial enough to be carried in Ruth’s shawl and represented a deliberate act of generosity.

“‘for he said to me, “Do not go empty-handed to your mother-in-law.”’”

Boaz’s gift was not only for Ruth. He specifically thought of Naomi and made sure Ruth did not return to her “empty-handed.” That detail matters because Naomi had earlier described her own life by saying, “I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty” (Ruth 1:21). Now, through Boaz, the emptiness Naomi lamented is beginning to be filled. The story is quietly showing the LORD restoring what Naomi believed she had lost.

Study Notes

The word “ephahs” in the NKJV is supplied because the Hebrew does not name a unit of measurement. Six full ephahs would be an enormous amount-far more than Ruth could reasonably carry in her garment-so the precise quantity cannot be determined from the text. The important point is Boaz’s generous provision, not the exact weight.

The repetition of “empty-handed” creates an important connection with Ruth 1:21. Naomi’s earlier statement that the LORD had brought her back “empty” is being answered gradually through the events of the book. Ruth came into her life, food was provided, and now a redeemer is moving toward action.

Spirit & Life

Boaz’s barley is more than provision for the day; it is a small foretaste of what he intends to do. The redemption is not yet completed, but Naomi receives something she can hold while she waits.

That is often how God works. Before the whole story is visible, He gives enough evidence of His faithfulness to encourage us to trust Him for what remains. Naomi said she had returned empty. God was already filling her life again-one provision, one relationship, and ultimately one act of redemption at a time.

Ruth 3:18

NKJV

Ruth 3:18 Then she said, "Sit still, my daughter, until you know how the matter will turn out; for the man will not rest until he has concluded the matter this day."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 3:18 Then Naomi said to her, “Just be patient, my daughter, until we hear what happens. The man won’t rest until he has settled things today.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi tells Ruth to wait because Boaz has taken responsibility for the matter and will not rest until it is settled. Ruth has done what she can; now she must trust the character and determination of the redeemer.

Verse Explanation

“Then she said, ‘Sit still, my daughter,’”

Naomi tells Ruth not to take any further action. Ruth has approached Boaz, Boaz has accepted his responsibility, and the next step belongs to him. “Sit still” carries the sense of waiting quietly rather than trying to push the situation forward.

“‘until you know how the matter will turn out;’”

There is still genuine uncertainty about the outcome because another relative has a prior right to redeem. Ruth does not yet know whether Boaz will ultimately become her redeemer. She must wait while events beyond her control are resolved.

“‘for the man will not rest until he has concluded the matter this day.’”

Naomi has confidence in Boaz’s character and determination. From what Ruth has reported, she knows he will act promptly rather than leave Ruth’s future unresolved. “This day” is especially important: Boaz intends to deal with the matter immediately. Chapter 4 will show him going directly to the city gate and taking the necessary steps to settle it.

Study Notes

“Sit still” does not mean passivity in a general sense. It is specific to this moment. Ruth has already acted courageously and appropriately; further action from her would not advance the matter because the legal and family responsibility now rests with Boaz.

The chapter ends without resolving the question of who will redeem Ruth. This deliberate pause directs attention away from Ruth’s initiative and toward Boaz’s action. Chapter 4 opens with Boaz already at the gate dealing with the nearer relative.

Spirit & Life

There is a time to act in faith, and there is a time to “sit still.” Ruth’s waiting is not inactivity born of indifference; it follows faithful obedience. She has done what was hers to do, and now she must leave what she cannot do in the hands of another.

There is also a beautiful picture of redemption here. Ruth rests while the redeemer works to settle the matter on her behalf. Without forcing every detail into an allegory, that pattern points naturally toward Christ. Our redemption ultimately rests not on what we can accomplish for ourselves, but on what our Redeemer undertakes and completes for us.
