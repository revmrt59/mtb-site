CLOSE RELATIVE - ( ga'al , Strong's H1350 )

Summary:

The Hebrew term ga'al refers to the act of redeeming, delivering, or acting as a kinsman-redeemer. In the social, economic, and legal structure of ancient Israel, the goel (the active participle of ga'al) was the nearest male relative responsible for protecting the family’s lineage, lives, and property. This role involved buying back ancestral land sold during times of poverty, redeeming relatives from debt-slavery, avenging the blood of a slain relative, and marrying a childless widow to carry on the family name through levirate marriage. In Ruth 4:1, the term refers to the anonymous "close relative" who possessed the primary legal right and responsibility to redeem Elimelech’s land and marry Ruth. His ultimate refusal to fulfill this duty clears the path for Boaz to step in as the redeemer, demonstrating a sacrificial love that points forward to the ultimate redemption found in Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concept of the close relative (goel) is rooted in ancient Israel's clan-based legal and covenantal system. Land was a permanent inheritance given by God to specific tribes and families, and it could not be permanently sold or alienated. If an Israelite fell into poverty and was forced to sell his land or even sell himself into debt-slavery, the family unit was fractured. To prevent this permanent loss, God instituted the law of the kinsman-redeemer. The nearest male relative had the obligation to step in and pay the redemption price. The word picture is one of restorative justice, reclamation, and protection. The goel stands as a safety net for the vulnerable, ensuring that the marginalized are brought back into the security of the family and that a deceased relative's name and inheritance are not erased from Israel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ga'al means to redeem, buy back, deliver, or act as a kinsman-redeemer. The active participle goel functions as a noun referring to the redeemer or the close relative. The semantic range of this root encompasses several vital legal duties:

• Redeeming property: Buying back ancestral land that a poor relative was forced to sell (Leviticus 25:25).

• Redeeming people: Purchasing the freedom of a relative who sold himself into slavery to a foreigner (Leviticus 25:47-49).

• Avenging blood: Acting as the goel haddam (the avenger of blood) to ensure legal justice was carried out if a relative was murdered (Numbers 35:19).

• Preserving the family line: Marrying the widow of a deceased relative to father an heir who would carry on the deceased man's name and inherit his estate (Ruth 4:5-6).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs ga'al to describe both human legal transactions and God's sovereign acts of deliverance.

• God as the Divine Goel: God is frequently depicted as Israel's Redeemer. In Exodus 6:6, God promises to redeem His people from Egyptian bondage "with an outstretched arm." In the book of Isaiah, God is repeatedly called the "Redeemer of Israel," portraying Him not merely as a distant deliverer, but as a close relative who has stepped in to rescue His covenant family from Babylonian exile.

• The Legal Advocate: The goel represents a legal champion. He is the one who enters the public gate of the city to plead the cause of those who cannot speak or pay for themselves. He restores balance, heals loss, and reverses ruin.

4. Theological Implications

The laws of the goel carry profound theological significance, serving as a primary Old Testament type of the redemptive work of Jesus Christ.

• Covenantal Love (Chesed): The duties of a close relative were not driven by financial gain but by covenantal loyalty and sacrificial love. It cost the redeemer his own resources to buy back what his relative lost.

• The Ultimate Goel: Jesus Christ perfectly fulfills the three biblical requirements of a kinsman-redeemer:

• He must be a close relative: Jesus became human, taking on flesh and blood, to become our Brother and next of kin (Hebrews 2:11-17).

• He must have the resources to redeem: The redemption of humanity from sin and death required a price that no ordinary human could pay. Jesus, being sinless and divine, paid the price not with silver or gold, but with His own precious blood (1 Peter 1:18-19).

• He must be willing: Jesus voluntarily laid down His life to redeem us, choosing of His own free will to pay our debt (John 10:17-18).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:1, Boaz goes up to the city gate—the legal court of the ancient world—and sits down. When the "close relative" (goel) of whom he had spoken passes by, Boaz calls him over. This unnamed man is a closer relative to Elimelech than Boaz is, meaning he possesses the primary right of redemption.

The narrative context reveals a sharp contrast in motives. The close relative is initially eager to redeem Elimelech's land because it would increase his own wealth and holdings. However, when Boaz reveals the full scope of the duty—that he must also marry Ruth the Moabitess to raise up an heir for the deceased Mahlon—the man immediately backtracks. He refuses, stating that it would ruin his own inheritance (Ruth 4:6).

This highlights the spiritual theme of the book of Ruth: true redemption requires self-sacrifice. The anonymous relative wanted the land (the blessing) but refused the sacrifice (the widow). Boaz, however, acts as a true goel, gladly taking on the financial cost and the social stigma of marrying a Moabite widow to preserve the family line of Elimelech, ultimately leading to the birth of Obed, the grandfather of King David.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Unnamed Relative (Ploni Almoni): In Ruth 4:1, Boaz addresses the close relative in Hebrew as peloni almoni, translated as "friend" or "such a one." This is an idiom meaning "so-and-so." Because this man refused to redeem Ruth and preserve the name of his deceased relative, his own name is ironically blotted out of the biblical record. He remains anonymous, while the faithful redeemer, Boaz, has his name permanently honored in the genealogy of Jesus Christ.

• The Sandal Ceremony: In Ruth 4:7-8, the custom of transferring the right of redemption is described. The man who refused the redemption pulled off his sandal and handed it to Boaz. In ancient Israel, walking on a piece of land symbolized ownership and the right to claim it. By removing his sandal and giving it to Boaz, the anonymous relative publicly declared that he was relinquishing his right to step on and possess that inheritance, transferring all legal authority to Boaz.
