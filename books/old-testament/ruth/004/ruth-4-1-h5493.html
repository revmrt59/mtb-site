COME ASIDE - ( sur, Strong's H5493 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sur literally means to turn aside, depart, or leave a designated path. In the context of Ruth 4:1, Boaz calls out to the unnamed closer relative to divert his course and sit down at the city gate to resolve a vital legal matter. This physical detour serves as the narrative catalyst for the ultimate redemption of Ruth and Naomi, illustrating how God uses everyday encounters and sudden deviations to work out His grand plan of redemption.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The physical imagery behind the verb sur is that of a traveler who is actively moving in one specific direction but is interrupted or chooses to diverge from that straight line. It describes a change of trajectory—stepping off a path to investigate something, avoid something, or head toward a different destination. In the ancient Near East, the city gate was the central hub for civic, legal, and commercial transactions. To be called to "come aside" at the gate was an invitation to pause one’s personal routine and enter a formal, communal space of legal responsibility.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb sur is a highly active root occurring over 300 times in the Hebrew Bible.

• In the Qal stem (as seen in Ruth 4:1), it means to turn aside, depart, withdraw, or leave the way.

• In the Hiphil (causative) stem, it means to remove, take away, or abolish (often used for removing foreign idols or high places).

• In the context of Ruth 4:1, the imperative surah ("come aside" or "turn aside") is an urgent invitation to halt physical progression, while the subsequent narrative verb wayyasar ("and he came aside") confirms the physical act of changing direction to meet Boaz.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Hebrew Scriptures utilize sur in both literal and metaphorical frameworks:

• Literal redirection: Turning off a path to examine an unusual sight, such as Moses turning aside to inspect the burning bush (Exodus 3:3), or Samson turning aside to look at the lion's carcass (Judges 14:8).

• Ethical and spiritual departure: Walking away from God's commands or turning aside to follow idols and empty things (Deuteronomy 9:12; 1 Samuel 12:21).

• Cessation or removal: The act of taking away garments, ashes, or spiritual strongholds from a designated area (Genesis 35:2).

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of sur in the book of Ruth shines through the theme of divine providence working through human decisions. The unnamed relative did not plan to stop at the gate; he was simply "passing by." However, Boaz’s call to "come aside" interrupted his day. This physical detour forced the relative to face a critical moral and covenantal choice regarding the levirate marriage and land redemption. While the relative physically turned aside to sit with Boaz, he ultimately "turned aside" (shrank back) from his covenant duty to preserve his own inheritance. This highlights the sovereignty of God, who redirects human steps to position the faithful Boaz as the true redeemer.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:1, Boaz is seated at the gate, waiting deliberately. When the closer relative passes by, Boaz calls out, "Come aside, friend, sit down here." The imperative surah is a direct, urgent appeal. The relative's immediate obedience—he "came aside and sat down"—demonstrates the social authority of Boaz and the binding, public nature of gate transactions. The narrative irony is deep: the relative physically turned aside to discuss redemption, but because he valued his own wealth over covenant faithfulness, he chose to depart from his duty. Consequently, his personal name is lost to history (he is addressed only as Peloni Almoni, or "friend"), while Boaz's willingness to step up cements his place in the lineage of David and Jesus Christ.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 3:3: "Then Moses said, 'I will now turn aside and see this great sight, why the bush does not burn.'"

• Deuteronomy 9:12: "They have quickly turned aside from the way which I commanded them; they have made themselves a molded image."

• Judges 14:8: "After some time, when he returned to get her, he turned aside to see the carcass of the lion."

• Proverbs 3:7: "Do not be wise in your own eyes; Fear the LORD and depart from evil."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew narrative, wordplay often highlights deeper spiritual truths. The relative turned aside (wayyasar) to sit with Boaz at the gate, but he ultimately turned away from the opportunity of a lifetime. By attempting to protect his own inheritance from being jeopardized, he lost the very thing he sought to protect—his legacy. Boaz, on the other hand, did not turn aside from his covenant responsibilities, showing that true redemption requires a steadfast refusal to turn aside from God’s moral path.
