BRETHREN - ( 'ach , Strong's H251 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ach fundamentally refers to a male sibling sharing one or both parents, but its semantic range expands broadly to encompass close relatives, kinsmen, clan members, and fellow countrymen. In the covenantal context of Ruth 4:10, 'ach refers to the clan and community of Israelite kinsmen in Bethlehem. Boaz redeems Ruth to ensure that the deceased Mahlon's name and lineage are not erased from among his kinsmen ("brethren") or his place in the civil life of the community at the gate.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, identity, security, and inheritance were fundamentally tribal and familial. The basic unit of society was the household (bet 'av), which nested within the wider clan (mishpachah). While the word 'ach refers first to immediate blood brothers, it also designates the broader circle of male relatives who shared a common covenantal and ancestral boundary. These "brethren" served as the guardians of the family's survival, land, and legacy. To die without an heir meant that a man’s name would be completely erased, or "cut off," from his brethren. This was viewed as a profound tragedy, representing the loss of his place in Israel's history and his inheritance in the Promised Land.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'ach carries several distinct meanings based on its context:

• A blood brother: A male sibling sharing the same father, mother, or both.

• A relative or kinsman: A member of the same extended family or clan, such as an uncle, nephew, or cousin.

• A countryman: A fellow member of the same nation or tribe (the "brethren" of Israel).

• An ally or companion: A person bound by a shared covenant, treaty, or common interest.

• In Ruth 4:10, 'ach functions in its familial-clan sense, denoting the male kinsmen of Elimelech and Mahlon who made up the local community and legal assembly of Bethlehem.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word 'ach is highly relational and highlights covenant solidarity. When Abraham wanted to resolve a dispute with his nephew Lot, he said, "Please let there be no strife... for we are brethren" (Genesis 13:8), utilizing 'ach to establish a bond of peace that exceeded biological siblinghood.

Under the Mosaic Law, the "brother" ('ach) was the object of intense moral duty. Israelites were forbidden from taking interest from their poor brethren, keeping them in perpetual slavery, or ignoring their material needs (Leviticus 25:35). Thus, the word picture of "brethren" evokes safety, mutual responsibility, and protection. In Ruth 4:10, the "brethren" and the "gate" represent the physical circle of legal and social accountability where Mahlon's name is preserved.

### 4. Theological Implications

The preservation of a man's name among his 'ach (brethren) is directly tied to the Abrahamic Covenant. God promised a lasting name and inheritance to His people. Under the laws of the kinsman-redeemer (goel) and levirate marriage, the nearest kinsman was responsible for redeeming the land and raising up heirs for a deceased brother.

Boaz’s actions demonstrate extraordinary covenant faithfulness (chesed). By marrying Ruth, he ensures that Mahlon's name does not perish from among his brethren. This reveals the heart of God, who does not allow the names of His people to be forgotten. Ultimately, this local preservation of a family line serves the grander narrative of redemption, keeping the genealogy intact from Abraham to King David, and ultimately to Jesus Christ.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:10, Boaz stands in the public square at the city gate, declaring his intent to marry Ruth and acquire the estate of Elimelech, Chilion, and Mahlon. He specifies his goal: "that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren."

Here, the "brethren" are the living male relatives of Mahlon’s clan who would continue to possess the surrounding lands of Bethlehem. If Boaz did not step in, Mahlon's portion of the land would pass permanently to another branch of the family, and his memory would be erased from the civil registry of the town. Boaz restores Mahlon's name to its rightful place among his living brethren, ensuring his legacy continues in the community.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 13:8 - "So Abram said to Lot, 'Please let there be no strife between you and me, and between my herdsmen and your herdsmen; for we are brethren.'"

• Leviticus 25:25 - "If one of your brethren becomes poor, and has sold some of his possession, and if his redeeming relative comes to redeem it, then he may redeem what his brother sold."

• Deuteronomy 15:7 - "If there is among you a poor man of your brethren, within any of the gates in your land which the LORD your God is giving you, you shall not harden your heart nor shut your hand from your poor brother."

• Psalm 133:1 - "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The Old Testament concept of 'ach lays the foundational imagery for New Testament discipleship. Jesus expands the definition of "brethren" beyond physical and national lines to encompass all who do the will of God (Matthew 12:50). Furthermore, the author of Hebrews notes that Christ, as our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, entered into our humanity so that He is "not ashamed to call them brethren" (Hebrews 2:11). Just as Boaz redeemed Ruth to secure the name of his deceased brother, so Christ redeemed us to secure our names eternally in the Book of Life.
