INHERITANCE - ( nachalah , Strong's H5159 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word nachalah refers to a permanent possession, heritage, or property handed down through generations, primarily associated with the land allotted by God to the families of Israel. In the book of Ruth, it represents more than physical soil; it is the tangible legacy, identity, and ongoing presence of a family within God's covenant community. By marrying Ruth, Boaz acts as the kinsman-redeemer to preserve the "inheritance" of the deceased Mahlon, demonstrating how covenant love restores what was lost and ensures a future for the vulnerable.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The concept of nachalah is rooted deeply in the soil of the promised land and the covenant structure of Israel. The word derives from the verb nachal, meaning to inherit or take possession. Unlike modern real estate, which is treated as a liquid asset to be bought and sold for profit, an inheritance in ancient Israel was a sacred trust. Yahweh was the true owner of the land; Israelite families were merely tenants and stewards of their allotted portions. Consequently, a family's land allotment—their nachalah—was intended to remain within that specific family and tribe permanently. If poverty or death threatened to alienate the property, the law of redemption stepped in, requiring a close relative to redeem the property and preserve the family line.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term nachalah denotes a possession, property, share, or inheritance. Its semantic range includes:

• An inherited portion of land assigned to a specific family or tribe.

• A possession handed down from ancestors to descendants.

• A heritage or portion assigned by God, whether physical (the land of Canaan) or spiritual (God’s relation to His people).

• The covenant nation of Israel itself, viewed as Yahweh's personal possession.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses nachalah in several rich, figurative, and literal ways:

• The Land of Promise: The most frequent usage refers to the tribal allotments of Canaan distributed under Joshua. The land is a gift from God, yet it must be actively possessed and guarded.

• The Levites' Portion: The tribe of Levi received no territorial allotment. Instead, Yahweh Himself was their nachalah. Their physical sustenance and spiritual calling were directly tied to God’s altar.

• Israel as God's Inheritance: In a beautiful reversal, God refers to the nation of Israel as His own nachalah. Out of all the nations, God claimed Israel as His treasured possession.

• Children as a Heritage: Children are described as an inheritance from Yahweh, emphasizing that family legacy and offspring are divine gifts rather than merely biological occurrences.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theology of nachalah centers on identity, security, and covenant faithfulness.

• Securing Identity: In Israel, to lose one's nachalah was to lose one's place in the covenant community. It meant being cut off from the visible expressions of God's blessing.

• The Grace of Redemption: The laws surrounding the nachalah (such as the Year of Jubilee and the kinsman-redeemer) illustrate God’s heart for restoration. God designed the system so that no family would be permanently disenfranchised or forgotten.

• The Messianic Line: The preservation of Elimelech and Mahlon’s nachalah was not just about saving a family farm; it was about preserving the family line through which David—and ultimately Jesus Christ—would be born.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:10, Boaz stands before the elders at the town gate. The immediate crisis is that Elimelech’s widow, Naomi, has returned from Moab destitute and must sell her late husband's land. A closer relative has the right to buy the land, but he refuses when he learns that he must also marry Ruth, the Moabitess, to perpetuate the name of the dead.

Boaz steps forward as the willing kinsman-redeemer. His declaration in verse 10 links the land (nachalah) directly to the name of the deceased: "to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance." Boaz is not looking to expand his own estate; in fact, redeeming this land and marrying Ruth will cost him financially and complicate his own estate. Yet, out of covenant loyalty (chesed), Boaz rescues Mahlon's nachalah from being lost and ensures that the name of the dead is not cut off. The physical land and the biological lineage are merged into one act of sacrificial redemption.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 18:20 - "Then the LORD said to Aaron: 'You shall have no inheritance in their land, nor shall you have any portion among them; I am your portion and your inheritance among the children of Israel.'"

• Deuteronomy 4:20 - "But the LORD has taken you and brought you out of the iron furnace, out of Egypt, to be His people, an inheritance, as you are this day."

• Joshua 11:23 - "So Joshua took the whole land, according to all that the LORD had said to Moses; and Joshua gave it as an inheritance to Israel according to their divisions by their tribes. Then the land had rest from war."

• Psalm 127:3 - "Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, The fruit of the womb is a reward."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, this Old Testament concept of nachalah expands into a glorious spiritual reality for the believer. Through Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, we are given an inheritance that is "imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you" (1 Peter 1:4). Just as Boaz secured the vulnerable family of Ruth and Naomi by preserving their earthly nachalah, Christ has redeemed us from our spiritual destitution and sealed us with the Holy Spirit, who is the "guarantee of our inheritance" (Ephesians 1:14).
