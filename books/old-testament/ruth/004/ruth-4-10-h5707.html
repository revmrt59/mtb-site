WITNESSES - ( ed , Strong's H5707 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun ed refers to a witness—a person, place, or object that testifies to a transaction, covenant, or historical event. In the legal setting of the ancient Near East, witnesses did not merely observe; they served as the living legal record and active guarantors of covenants, boundaries, and family obligations. In Ruth 4:10, Boaz calls upon the elders and the community at the gate of Bethlehem to serve as official witnesses to his redemption of Elimelech's land and his marriage to Ruth. This act of public testimony secures Ruth’s legal standing, preserves the inheritance of the deceased, and demonstrates how communal accountability undergirds the unfolding of God’s redemptive grace.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, legal transactions, property transfers, and covenant ratifications did not rely on paper documents locked in municipal archives. Instead, legal validity depended on public memory and oral testimony. The city gate functioned as the local courthouse. When a transaction occurred, the elders of the city and the bystanders gathered to observe the proceedings. These observers became edim (the plural form of ed). They were the living archives. If a dispute arose in subsequent generations, these individuals were called upon to repeat what they had seen and heard. The word picture behind a witness is that of a boundary stone or a voice that stands to confirm truth, preventing fraud, and ensuring that covenants are strictly maintained over time.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun ed designates a witness, a recorder, or a testimony. It is closely related to the verbal root 'ud, which carries the primary meaning of going over again, repeating, testifying, warning, or solemnly admonishing. Thus, a witness is fundamentally one who repeats or reiterates a factual occurrence to establish truth in a legal or covenantal setting. In a courtroom context, the ed provides the necessary evidence to convict or acquit. In a covenantal context, the ed stands as a permanent reminder of the promises made between two parties.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term ed is used in several distinct ways throughout the Old Testament:

• Human Witnesses in Legal Matters: Individuals who observe a crime or a civil transaction and are legally obligated to testify. Their testimony is required to establish any matter in court, as the law demands at least two or three witnesses to confirm a charge.

• Inanimate Objects as Witnesses: Non-human entities are frequently personified as witnesses to dramatic covenants. For example, Jacob and Laban erected a heap of stones called Galeed (meaning "heap of witness") to mark their boundary covenant. Moses placed the Book of the Law beside the Ark of the Covenant to serve as a witness against Israel if they turned to idolatry.

• The Song of Moses as a Witness: God commanded Moses to write a song and teach it to the Israelites so that the song itself would serve as a witness for God against them when they fell into apostasy.

• God as the Ultimate Witness: When human witnesses are absent or silent, God Himself is invoked as the sovereign witness who knows the truth of a transaction and will enforce its terms.

### 4. Theological Implications

The concept of the ed underscores the relational, communal, and transparent nature of God’s covenantal dealings with humanity:

• Accountability in Community: God does not design His people to live in isolation. Spiritual commitments, marriages, and redemptive transactions are meant to be witnessed and supported by the faith community. Community validation provides protection for the vulnerable and accountability for the strong.

• The Integrity of Truth: The heavy reliance on witnesses highlights the biblical demand for absolute truth. Bearing false witness is so destructive to the covenant community that it is prohibited in the Ten Commandments and carries severe penalties, reflecting God’s own character as the God of truth.

• Precursor to Gospel Witness: The Old Testament concept of the legal witness lays the foundation for the New Testament calling of the church. Believers are called to be witnesses of Christ’s resurrection and redemptive work, testifying to the world of the ultimate Kinsman Redeemer who purchased our inheritance.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:10, Boaz stands before the elders at the city gate of Bethlehem. He has successfully negotiated with the closer relative who was unwilling to redeem Elimelech’s land because it required marrying Ruth the Moabitess. Boaz declares his intent to buy the land and acquire Ruth as his wife to perpetuate the name of the dead. He immediately charges the crowd: "You are witnesses this day."

The use of ed in this context carries profound pastoral and legal nuances:

• Protection for Ruth: As a foreign widow, Ruth had no legal standing or security. By establishing the transaction before public witnesses, Boaz ensures that no one can ever challenge Ruth's legal status as his wife or the legitimacy of her future children's inheritance.

• Restoration of the Deceased: The witnesses confirm that Mahlon’s name will not be cut off from Bethlehem. The public testimony guarantees that the property remains linked to the deceased’s family line, preserving the covenantal distribution of the Promised Land.

• Communal Blessing: The witnesses do not remain passive observers. In the following verse, they immediately respond, "We are witnesses," and break into a prophetic blessing, praying that Ruth will be like Rachel and Leah who built the house of Israel. The legal witnesses become active spiritual partners in God’s unfolding plan of redemption.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) translates the Hebrew ed in Ruth 4:10 using the Greek word martys. This is the root word from which the English word "martyr" is derived. In the ancient world, a martys was simply a witness who testified to facts in a court of law. However, because early Christian witnesses were so deeply committed to the truth of Christ's resurrection that they were willing to die rather than recant their testimony, the word eventually evolved to mean one who dies for their faith. This connection highlights the solemn, binding, and costly nature of being a witness. Boaz was willing to make a significant financial and social sacrifice to redeem Ruth, and the elders of Bethlehem stood ready to guard that truth with their own reputations and lives.
