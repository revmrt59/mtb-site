WIDOW - ( ishshah , Strong's 802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ishshah primarily denotes a woman or a wife, representing the essential female counterpart to man. In the context of Ruth 4:10, the NKJV renders this word as "widow" to describe Ruth's relationship to her deceased husband, Mahlon. This highlights her legal and relational connection to the family of Elimelech, setting the stage for Boaz to act as her kinsman-redeemer. By marrying Mahlon's ishshah, Boaz restores the family's inheritance and preserves the name of the deceased, beautifully demonstrating the covenant love that transforms a status of grief and emptiness into one of redemption and hope.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word ishshah is the feminine counterpart to the Hebrew word ish (man). In the creation account of Genesis, the ishshah is formed from the rib of the ish, establishing an ontological equality and a complementary partnership. The name itself reflects a deep relational connection; she is called "woman" because she was taken out of "man."

In the ancient Near East, a woman’s identity, protection, and provision were structurally bound to her relationship with a male authority—first her father, and then her husband. When a husband died, an ishshah was left in an incredibly vulnerable position. In the book of Ruth, calling Ruth the ishshah of Mahlon links her directly to the deceased husband's land and lineage, making her welfare the legal and moral responsibility of the next of kin.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term ishshah has a straightforward yet rich semantic range in classical Hebrew:

• Woman: A female human being of any age or status, contrasted with a man (ish).

• Wife: A married woman, often identified in relation to her husband through the grammatical construct state (eshet, meaning "wife of").

• Female: Used occasionally in biological contexts to denote female animals.

In Ruth 4:10, the Hebrew text uses the construct form eshet Mahlon (literally "wife of Mahlon"). Because Mahlon is deceased, the NKJV contextually translates the word as "widow" to accurately reflect her current social status while maintaining her legal connection to her late husband.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, ishshah serves as a focal point for understanding relationship, covenant, and community:

• The Covenant Partner: In Genesis 2:24, the ishshah is the one to whom a man cleaves, establishing the foundational unit of human society.

• The Personification of Wisdom and Folly: In the book of Proverbs, both Wisdom and Folly are personified as women (ishshah), calling out to young men and offering competing paths of life and death.

• The Picture of Israel: God frequently describes His covenant relationship with Israel through the metaphor of marriage, calling the nation His ishshah. Israel's idolatry is depicted as the unfaithfulness of a wife to her husband.

In the book of Ruth, the word picture shifts from loss to restoration. Ruth begins as a grieving ishshah (wife/widow) of a dead man and, through redemption, becomes the beloved ishshah (wife) of Boaz, the redeemer.

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of ishshah in Ruth 4:10 carries profound theological weight:

• Covenant Loyalty (Chesed): Redemption in the Old Testament is never merely about transactions or property; it is fundamentally about people and relationships. Boaz does not simply purchase Elimelech's land; he redeems the ishshah of the deceased to raise up an heir. This illustrates God's redemption of His people, which is motivated by deep relational love and covenant commitment.

• Dignity of the Marginalized: By recognizing Ruth as Mahlon's ishshah rather than just an immigrant outsider, Boaz honors her legal standing and dignity within Israel. God's law consistently advocates for the protection and elevation of those who have lost their earthly protectors.

• The Gospel of the Redeemer: This passage serves as a beautiful type of Christ. Jesus, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, does not merely save us from our debts; He takes us (the Church) as His bride (ishshah), bringing us out of our spiritual poverty and into His eternal inheritance.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:10, Boaz stands before the elders at the city gate to finalize the redemption. The verse contains a striking dual use of ishshah: "Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow [eshet] of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife [ishshah]..."

This structural repetition highlights the transformation taking place. Ruth is no longer defined by her past grief as the wife of the deceased; she is now defined by her secure future as the wife of the redeemer. Furthermore, her identity as "the Moabitess" is brought into covenant Israel. The name of Mahlon, which means "sickly" or "weakness," is preserved and redeemed through Boaz's strength and obedience, demonstrating how God weaves marginalized individuals into the lineage of the Messiah.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:24: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife [ishshah], and they shall become one flesh."

• Genesis 3:6: "So when the woman [ishshah] saw that the tree was good for food, that it was pleasant to the eyes..."

• Proverbs 31:10: "Who can find a virtuous wife [ishshah]? For her worth is far above rubies."

• Ruth 4:10: "Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow [ishshah] of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife [ishshah] to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance..."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

While ishshah is translated as "widow" in Ruth 4:10 to show her connection to her late husband, classical Hebrew has a specific technical term for a widow: almanah (Strong's 490). An almanah was a widow who had no male relative (husband, father, or adult son) to support her, leaving her completely vulnerable and without legal recourse.

By using ishshah ("wife of") instead of almanah ("widow") in this legal transaction, the text emphasizes Ruth's valid legal claim to Mahlon’s family inheritance. She is not presented to the elders merely as a destitute beggar, but as the rightful bearer of Mahlon’s legacy, which Boaz gladly steps in to redeem.
