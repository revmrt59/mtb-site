BUILT - ( banah , Strong's H1129 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb banah means to build, construct, or establish, carrying both a literal sense of physical construction and a rich figurative sense of raising up a family or nation. In Ruth 4:11, the elders of Bethlehem use this term to bless Ruth, praying that she will "build" the house of Boaz just as Rachel and Leah "built" the house of Israel. This word highlights how God works through human relationships, devotion, and fruitfulness to construct His covenant family and advance His redemptive purposes.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical concept of banah involves the intentional process of gathering materials, laying a firm foundation, and assembling a lasting structure. In the ancient Near East, building a physical house and building a family line were conceptually inseparable. This connection is embedded directly within the Hebrew language itself. The Hebrew word for "house" (bayit) refers to both a physical dwelling and a family lineage. Furthermore, the words for "son" (ben) and "daughter" (bat) are derived from the same root as banah. To build a house is to raise up children; to have children is to construct a lasting heritage.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of banah is to build or construct a physical structure, such as an altar, a house, a city, or defensive walls. In its active, literal sense, it describes the physical labor of construction.

• Figuratively, the verb means to establish, restore, or cause to flourish. This includes the establishment of a family or household through childbearing.

• In the passive stems (such as the Niphal), the word can mean to "be built up," which is often used idiomatically for a husband or wife obtaining children and securing their family line through surrogacy or marriage.

• In Ruth 4:11, the verb appears in the Qal perfect plural (banu), describing Rachel and Leah as those who actively "built" the national and biological framework of the entire house of Israel.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses banah to draw a parallel between the visible world of architecture and the invisible world of lineage and covenant:

• The Mother as Builder: A mother "builds" a house not with stones and mortar, but by bringing children into the world and nurturing them. This framing elevates the role of motherhood to that of a master builder of civilization.

• The Divine Architect: Humans may labor to build physical structures or families, but the ultimate success of any building project belongs to God. True building requires divine favor, protection, and sovereign enablement.

• The Living Temple: The family line of Israel is pictured as a grand edifice. Every child born into the covenant line acts as a living stone, strengthening and expanding the house that God is building for His glory.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological depth of banah in the Old Testament rests on God's covenant faithfulness:

• Covenantal Continuity: God’s promise to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob required a physical lineage. By building the "house of Israel" through Rachel and Leah, God demonstrated His faithfulness to multiply His people. By linking Ruth to these matriarchs, the narrative connects her to the unfolding covenant history.

• Redemption of the Barren and Foreigner: Both Rachel and Leah struggled with barrenness and rivalry, yet God opened their wombs to build the nation. Similarly, Ruth is a childless Moabite widow. The use of banah signifies that God’s grace can take a barren foreigner and make her a foundational builder of the line of David.

• Messianic Architecture: God builds a house for David (2 Samuel 7), which ultimately culminates in the Messiah. The building of Boaz's household through Ruth is a vital historical step in constructing the lineage of Jesus Christ.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:11, Boaz has legally redeemed the estate of Elimelech and committed to marry Ruth to perpetuate the name of the deceased. The elders and the townspeople witness this transaction at the city gate and offer a poetic blessing.

• The comparison to Rachel and Leah is highly significant. Rachel was buried near Bethlehem (Ephrathah), making her memory local and vivid to the people of the city.

• By invoking the names of the two matriarchs who "built the house of Israel," the elders are performing a radical act of inclusion. They pray that Ruth, a Moabitess from an enemy nation, will be granted the same status, honor, and fruitfulness as the founding mothers of the Hebrew faith.

• The blessing of "building" looks forward to the birth of Obed, the grandfather of King David, showing that this local marriage was designed by God to build the royal house of Israel.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 16:2 - "So Sarai said to Abram, 'See now, the Lord has kept me from bearing children. Please, go in to my maid; perhaps I shall obtain children [be built up] by her.' And Abram heeded the voice of Sarai."

• Deuteronomy 25:9 - "...then his brother’s wife shall come to him in the presence of the elders, remove his sandal from his foot, spit in his face, and answer and say, 'So shall it be done to the man who will not build up his brother’s house.'"

• Psalm 127:1 - "Unless the Lord builds the house, They labor in vain who build it; Unless the Lord guards the city, The watchman stays awake in vain."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The structural placement of this word at the climax of the book of Ruth serves as a literary bridge. The book begins with emptiness, death, and a lack of heirs in Moab, but ends with the active "building" of a new household in Bethlehem.

• The physical reality of Ruth's labor in the fields is transformed into the spiritual and biological labor of building a lineage. The humble gleaner is recognized by her community as a noble builder of the nation.
