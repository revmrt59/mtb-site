DO WORTHILY - ( chayil , Strong's H2428 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chayil in Ruth 4:11 is a rich, multi-dimensional term signifying strength, moral virtue, capability, and material wealth. In the blessing pronounced by the elders of Bethlehem, it is a prayer for Boaz to act with noble character, achieve prosperity, and maintain a powerful, virtuous influence in his community. This word links Boaz’s external prosperity with his internal integrity, establishing him and Ruth as a household of strength and honor.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete root of chayil relates to a force, an army, or an accumulation of power. It paints a picture of concentrated strength and resources pooled together to achieve an objective. In the ancient agrarian and civic context of Israel, this strength was not merely physical brute force, but the integrated capacity of a person—comprising physical stamina, financial substance, social standing, and moral integrity. When applied to an individual, it suggests a person of substance, someone who stands firm, acts effectively, and commands respect because of their capability and uprightness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of chayil is remarkably broad, adapting to the context in which it is used. It means:

• Strength, power, or might (physical or military force).

• Wealth, substance, or riches (economic capacity).

• Virtue, integrity, or moral worth (character strength).

• Ability, efficiency, or competence (capability in leadership or labor).

In Ruth 4:11, the verbal phrase "may you do worthily" translates the Hebrew idiom asah-chayil, which literally means "to make strength," "to acquire wealth," or "to perform virtuously." It is a comprehensive blessing for successful, honorable, and powerful living.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, chayil describes various kinds of strength:

• Military Might: It is frequently translated as "host" or "army," depicting a disciplined, powerful force gathered for battle.

• Financial Substance: It refers to the wealth and assets that give a person status and security in society.

• Moral Excellence: It describes an individual whose character is sturdy, resolute, and untainted by corruption.

The word pictures associated with chayil show that true strength is not one-dimensional. A man of chayil is not merely rich; he is robust in character. A woman of chayil is not merely capable; she is clothed in strength and dignity.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chayil demonstrates that God-given prosperity is holistic. In the biblical worldview, wealth is not divorced from righteousness. When God blesses a household, He provides both the material resources (chayil as wealth) and the moral stamina (chayil as virtue) to use those resources for His glory. Boaz's life illustrates this beautifully. His wealth is redeemed and sanctified because it is governed by his fear of the Lord and his willingness to protect the vulnerable. The blessing in Ruth 4:11 is a theological acknowledgment that human flourishing is a gift of God, combining righteous character with practical capability.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:11, Boaz stands at the city gate, having just redeemed Ruth and the land of Elimelech. The elders and the people pronounce a double blessing upon him: "may you do worthily (chayil) in Ephrathah and be famous in Bethlehem."

This blessing is deeply intentional. First, it mirrors the description of Boaz in Ruth 2:1 as a "man of great wealth/valor" (chayil) and Ruth in Ruth 3:11 as a "virtuous" (chayil) woman. By using this term, the elders pray that Boaz will continue to live up to his reputation. Second, it links Boaz and Ruth as a perfectly matched pair of chayil. Their union is not built on fleeting passion, but on shared moral strength and covenant faithfulness. Third, the blessing connects Boaz's personal virtue with the building of a historic legacy, which eventually births King David and, ultimately, Jesus Christ.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A literary symmetry exists within the Book of Ruth. It begins with family tragedy, economic ruin, and physical weakness in Moab, but it culminates at the gate of Bethlehem with a prayer for chayil—strength, wealth, and virtue. This structural placement shows that when God restores a family, He turns weakness into chayil. Boaz and Ruth are the only couple in the Old Testament where both the husband and wife are individually designated by this same word, illustrating the profound spiritual and moral equality of their covenant partnership.
