WITNESSES - ( ‘ēd , Strong's H5707 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ‘ēd refers to a witness—either a person who attests to a transaction, covenant, or event, or an object that serves as evidence of an agreement. In the ancient Near East, especially within the legal gates of a city, witnesses were not mere bystanders. They were active participants who legally validated covenants, marriages, and property transactions, binding the parties to their oaths and invoking the community and God as ultimate guardians of the truth.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun ‘ēd is derived from the verbal root ‘ûd, which means to return, repeat, or do again. The word picture of a witness is someone who "re-iterates" or duplicates a testimony, bringing a past event or agreement into the present by speaking it aloud. In ancient Israel, where written documentation was rare and literacy was limited, oral testimony and the collective memory of the community were the primary safeguards of justice. Legal matters were settled at the city gate (ša'ar), the public hub where elders gathered. A witness (‘ēd) stood in this public square to guarantee that an agreement was binding, observable, and memorable.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

A witness is one who has firsthand knowledge of an event, agreement, or truth and formally testifies to it. The semantic range of ‘ēd includes:

• A human witness who testifies in a court of law to establish guilt or innocence.

• A recipient or signatory of a legal contract or covenant transaction.

• An inanimate object (such as a pillar, altar, or written document) that stands as a lasting monument of a covenant.

• God Himself, who observes all human actions and is invoked to testify to the truth of an oath or to judge its violation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs ‘ēd in several distinct ways:

• Legal Protections: The law required multiple witnesses to establish a fact or convict an individual, protecting the community from malicious accusations (Deuteronomy 19:15).

• Inanimate Memorials: Objects often functioned as silent, permanent witnesses. When Jacob and Laban made a covenant, they piled stones as a ‘ēd (Galeed, the "heap of witness"), signifying that neither would cross that boundary to harm the other. Similarly, the Book of the Law was placed beside the Ark of the Covenant to serve as a witness against Israel if they turned to idolatry.

• The Supreme Witness: God is frequently described as the ultimate ‘ēd who witnesses the secret motives of the heart and the quiet transactions of humanity.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of ‘ēd underscores God’s deep commitment to truth, justice, and order. In the biblical worldview, truth is not individual or subjective; it is communal, verified, and grounded in objective reality. The demand for multiple witnesses reflects God's mercy, safeguarding the vulnerable against false testimony. Furthermore, redemption in Scripture is often portrayed in legal terms. Salvation, covenant-making, and inheritance are not arbitrary acts; they are formal, legally binding realities executed in the presence of witnesses. When God redeems His people, He does so in a way that is legally unimpeachable, demonstrated before the assembly of heaven and earth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:11, Boaz stands at the gate of Bethlehem to resolve the redemption of Naomi's land and the future of Ruth the Moabitess. He addresses the elders and the townspeople, saying, "You are witnesses this day" (Ruth 4:10). The crowd responds with the legal declaration, "We are witnesses" (‘ēdîm).

By speaking these words, the elders and the people do three things:

• They legally ratify the transaction, confirming that the closer relative has waived his right of redemption and that Boaz has lawfully acquired the property and the hand of Ruth.

• They commit their collective memory to this event, ensuring that Ruth’s future children will be recognized as the rightful heirs of Elimelech's lineage.

• They transition from legal witnesses to spiritual intercessors. Immediately after saying, "We are witnesses," they pronounce a prophetic blessing of fruitfulness upon Ruth and honor upon Boaz, linking this legal transaction directly to the historical continuity of Israel's covenant.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Israelite jurisprudence, bearing false witness was treated with extreme severity. If a witness was found to have lied in court, the judges were commanded to do to him what he had intended to do to his brother (Deuteronomy 19:16-19). Furthermore, in capital cases, the hands of the witnesses were to be the first raised to execute the sentence. This high bar of personal responsibility ensured that taking the stand as an ‘ēd was never done lightly, as it bound the witness's own life and conscience to the truth of their words.
