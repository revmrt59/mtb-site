FAMOUS - ( shem , Strong's H8034 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shem, translated here as "famous," literally means "name." In the ancient Near East, a person's "name" was not merely a convenient label but represented their character, reputation, authority, and lasting legacy. In the context of Ruth 4:11, the elders of Bethlehem pronounce a blessing of legal and spiritual fruitfulness over Boaz, petitioning that his honorable, self-sacrificing redemption of Ruth and the estate of the deceased Elimelech would establish a lasting reputation and an enduring lineage that would never be forgotten in Israel.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, a name (shem) is deeply tied to existence and character. To have a name is to exist, to have significance, and to leave a trace on the earth. Conversely, to have one's name "blotted out" or "cut off" was the ultimate tragedy, representing complete historical and familial annihilation. The entire plot of the book of Ruth hinges on this reality: the need to perpetuate the "name" of the dead (Mahlon) upon his inheritance so that his family line does not perish. When Boaz steps forward as the kinsman-redeemer, he acts to preserve a name, and in doing so, he is blessed with a lasting name of his own.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun shem designates a name, reputation, fame, glory, memorial, or renown. In Ruth 4:11, the Hebrew text employs a specific idiom: u-qera-shem (literally, "and proclaim a name"). When translated contextually as "be famous," the phrase denotes acquiring an honorable reputation, achieving renown, and establishing a legendary household. Rather than suggesting worldly vanity or seeking personal celebrity, the term signifies the public recognition of righteousness, integrity, and divine favor within the community.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, shem is used to construct several powerful word pictures:

• The Name as a Memorial: A person's name acts as an enduring monument. A righteous life builds a name that outlives the physical body.

• The Fragrant Name: A good name is compared to sweet, costly oil, diffusing a positive influence throughout a community.

• The God-Given Name: True renown is always depicted as a gift of grace and blessing from God, contrasted with the fragile, self-made names constructed by rebellious humanity.

### 4. Theological Implications

The pursuit of a "name" highlights a central biblical tension:

• Self-Exaltation vs. Divine Gift: At the Tower of Babel, humanity attempted to "make a name" (shem) for themselves through pride and self-reliance, resulting in judgment and confusion. In contrast, God promised Abraham that He would "make your name great" as a byproduct of faith and covenant obedience.

• The Reward of Hesed: Boaz's fame is the direct theological consequence of his hesed (covenant loyalty). By willingly risking his own estate and inheritance to preserve the name of a deceased relative, Boaz demonstrates sacrificial love. God honors this selflessness by exalting Boaz's name, illustrating the kingdom principle that those who humble themselves will be exalted.

• The Ultimate Name: This blessing points forward to the ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, Jesus Christ, who emptied Himself and was subsequently given the "name which is above every name."

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:11, the setting is the city gate—the place of legal transactions and elder witness. Boaz has just legally acquired the right to marry Ruth. The elders and the townspeople offer a public blessing consisting of two parallel desires:

• May Ruth be like Rachel and Leah, who "built the house of Israel."

• May Boaz "prosper in Ephrathah and be famous in Bethlehem."

The wordplay is striking. Ephrathah and Bethlehem are ancestral names of the same location. The elders pray that Boaz's household will be established in wealth, strength, and reputation in the very place where he performed this act of redemption. The fulfillment is historical and immediate: Boaz becomes the great-grandfather of King David, forever cementing his "name" in the geographical and redemptive history of Bethlehem.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 11:4 - "And they said, 'Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth.'"

• Genesis 12:2 - "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great; And you shall be a blessing."

• Proverbs 22:1 - "A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches, Loving favor rather than silver and gold."

• Ecclesiastes 7:1 - "A good name is better than precious ointment, And the day of death than the day of one's birth;"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful literary contrast in Ruth chapter 4 between Boaz and the unnamed closer relative. When Boaz first addresses this closer relative in Ruth 4:1, he calls him peloni almoni, translated in the NKJV as "friend," but literally meaning "certain anonymous one" or "Mr. No-Name." This relative refuses to redeem Ruth because he fears it will "ruin" his own inheritance and compromise his own name. In trying to protect his name and estate, he loses both; his actual name is completely omitted from the biblical record. Boaz, who is willing to compromise his own financial standing to show mercy, is rewarded with eternal fame (shem), preserved forever in the genealogy of Jesus Christ.
