BORE - ( yalad , Strong's H3205 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalad fundamentally means to bear, bring forth, beget, or give birth to children. Beyond the physical act of childbirth, it carries deep covenantal, genealogical, and legal significance in the Old Testament. It represents the preservation of a family line, the physical manifestation of divine blessing, and the continuation of God's redemptive history through successive generations.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, the survival of a family, clan, and nation depended entirely on the bearing of children. Thus, yalad is deeply rooted in an agrarian and familial context where fertility was viewed as the ultimate sign of divine favor, and barrenness was seen as a tragic affliction. The word paints a picture of life breaking forth from pain, a physical transition from the hidden darkness of the womb into the light of the family community. It is the crucial link connecting ancestors to descendants, establishing historical continuity, inheritance rights, and the physical preservation of a family's "name" upon the land.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary lexical meaning of yalad is to bear, give birth, bring forth, or beget.

• In the Qal stem, it refers to the mother's action of bearing a child or giving birth.

• In the Hiphil stem, it refers to the father's action of begetting a child, often translated as "begot" in genealogical lists.

• In the Niphal, Pual, and Hophal stems, it expresses the passive action of being born.

• In the Piel stem, it refers to the work of a midwife helping to deliver a child.

The semantic range of the word spans the entire process of conception, labor, physical delivery, and the subsequent genealogical registration of the offspring.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb yalad occurs hundreds of times in the Old Testament, most frequently in the genealogies of Genesis and Chronicles.

• The Anguish of Labor: The physical distress of a woman in labor (yoledah) is frequently used by biblical writers as a vivid metaphor for sudden, overwhelming terror, helplessness, and unavoidable sorrow under divine judgment (as seen in the prophets).

• The Joy of Legacy: Conversely, the completion of yalad is associated with joy, relief, and God's sovereign provision of life.

• Figurative Conception: The word is sometimes used metaphorically to describe the earth bringing forth vegetation, or human hearts conceiving and "bringing forth" wickedness, trouble, or lies.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty over the Womb: In the Old Testament, the power to yalad is never viewed as a purely biological certainty. God is the author of life who opens and closes the womb. The ability to bear children is a gift of His grace.

• Covenant and Seed: The theological progression of the Old Testament is driven by the promise of the "seed" (zera), beginning in Genesis 3:15. Every instance of yalad in the covenant line is a step closer to the fulfillment of the ultimate Seed who would crush the serpent's head.

• Redemption and Levirate Marriage: The word is tied directly to the concept of redemption (goel). In levirate marriage, the firstborn son whom the widow yalad would carry on the name and inheritance of the deceased husband, preventing his name from being blotted out of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:12, the elders and people at the gate offer a blessing to Boaz: "May your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah."

The use of yalad in this context is highly significant:

• The Perez Parallel: Tamar, a foreign woman who faced desperate circumstances after the death of her husband, acted boldly to preserve the family line. Through Judah, she bore (yalda, Qal perfect) Perez. Perez became the founder of the dominant clan of Judah, to which Boaz belonged.

• From Brokenness to Blessing: By invoking Tamar and the child she bore, the elders recognize a parallel in Ruth. Ruth is also a foreign widow seeking to preserve her deceased husband's name. The blessing anticipates that God will allow Ruth to yalad a son who will bring life and restoration, not just to Naomi and Boaz, but to the entire tribe of Judah.

• Covenant Fulfillment: This blessing is answered in Ruth 4:13, where the same verb is used: "the LORD gave her conception, and she bore (yalad) a son." That son, Obed, becomes the grandfather of King David, demonstrating that Ruth’s act of bearing a son was ordained by God to preserve the messianic line.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:1: "Now Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived and bore Cain, and said, 'I have acquired a man from the LORD.'"

• Genesis 16:15: "So Hagar bore Abram a son; and Abram named his son, whom Hagar bore, Ishmael."

• Genesis 30:1: "Now when Rachel saw that she bore Jacob no children, Rachel envied her sister, and said to Jacob, 'Give me children, or else I die!'"

• Ruth 4:13: "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."

• Isaiah 9:6: "For unto us a Child is born, Unto us a Son is given; And the government will be upon His shoulder."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Role of the Midwife: The active participle of the Piel stem of yalad (meyalledeth) is the Hebrew word for midwife. This role is highlighted in the Exodus narrative (Exodus 1:15-21), where the Hebrew midwives feared God and refused to kill the newborn boys, directly participating in the preservation of the nation of Israel.

• LXX Translation: In the Septuagint (LXX), yalad is consistently translated using the Greek verbs gennao (to beget/bear) and tikto (to bring forth). These same Greek words are utilized in Matthew 1 and Luke 1-2 to describe the genealogy and the miraculous birth of Jesus Christ, connecting the physical births of the Old Testament directly to the Incarnation.
