TAMAR - ( Tamar , Strong's H8559 )

Summary:

The name Tamar translates literally to "palm tree," symbolizing uprightness, life-giving fruitfulness, and resilience under pressure. In the context of Ruth 4:12, Tamar is the historical-theological emblem of a miraculous, divinely secured lineage established against impossible human odds. By invoking her name, the elders of Bethlehem pray that Ruth's household will mirror the fruitfulness of Tamar, who, despite abandonment and deception, was vindicated by God to preserve the covenant line of Judah through her son Perez.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun tamar refers to the date palm (Phoenix dactylifera), a tree renowned in the Ancient Near East for its elegance, deep roots, straight stature, and abundant sweetness. In a harsh desert climate, the palm tree represents life, water, shade, and enduring nourishment.

• As a proper name, Tamar embodies these characteristics of resilience and vitality. The most prominent Tamar in Israel's history is the daughter-in-law of Judah. Deprived of her marital rights and financial security after the deaths of her husbands Er and Onan, she took desperate, culturally daring measures to secure an heir. Her actions forced Judah to recognize his failure to provide for her, prompting his confession that she was more righteous than he.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The proper noun tamar derives from the Hebrew noun for a palm tree or date palm. The term designates a tall, upright, fruit-bearing tree.

• In its application to individuals, the name functions as a feminine proper noun representing maternal resilience, the preservation of family inheritance, and the sovereign intervention of God in transforming potential extinction into an abundant lineage.

• The lexical range links the name to themes of physical stature, beauty, and flourishing productivity.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The palm tree (tamar) is a frequent biblical metaphor for moral uprightness and flourishing life. The righteous are described as flourishing like the palm tree, standing tall and remaining fruitful even in old age.

• Architecturally, Solomon’s temple featured prominent engravings of palm trees alongside cherubim, symbolizing the beauty, majesty, and life-giving presence of Eden restored in God's dwelling place.

• As a personal name, Tamar evokes the image of a woman who, like a palm tree in the desert, tapped into deep, hidden sources of life and resilience to bear fruit when all outward circumstances threatened death and barrenness.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereign Grace and Providential Preservation: Tamar’s story in Genesis 38 is a narrative of human failure, deception, and societal vulnerability. Yet, God sovereignly steered these compromised events to preserve the line of Judah. Without Tamar's bold intervention, the tribe of Judah may have dissolved into Canaanite syncretism or died out.

• Inclusion of the Marginalized: Tamar, likely a Canaanite, is one of the few women listed in the genealogy of Jesus Christ. Her inclusion, alongside Ruth (a Moabite), highlights God's expansive grace that transcends ethnic boundaries and redeems scandalous situations to accomplish His redemptive purposes.

• Justification and Vindication: Tamar's story is one of vindication. Though branded a harlot by Judah, she was ultimately declared "more righteous" because she sought to fulfill the covenantal obligation of levirate marriage that Judah had neglected.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:12, the elders and people at the gate bless Boaz by comparing his future house to "the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah." This is a highly deliberate and nuanced comparison.

• Both Tamar and Ruth were foreign widows who lost husbands in Israel, faced the threat of childlessness and erasure, and took bold, unconventional steps to secure a redeemer.

• The comparison is a blessing of fruitfulness. Perez was the ancestor of the Bethlehemite clan, making Boaz a direct descendant of Perez. The elders pray that Boaz’s union with Ruth will be as extraordinarily fruitful as the union of Judah and Tamar, which produced the foundational royal line of Israel.

• By naming Tamar, the elders acknowledge that God specializes in bringing life out of dead, hopeless situations, paving the way for the ultimate King of Israel and the Messiah.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 38:6 - "Judah took a wife for Er his firstborn, and her name was Tamar."

• Genesis 38:24 - "And it came to pass, about three months after, that Judah was told, saying, 'Tamar your daughter-in-law has played the harlot...'"

• 1 Chronicles 2:4 - "And Tamar, his daughter-in-law, bore him Perez and Zerah. All the sons of Judah were five."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The geographical connection: Tamar’s son Perez became the namesake of the clan of Perez, which settled heavily in the region of Bethlehem. Thus, the blessing of Ruth 4:12 is also a localized, historical tribute to the very founders of the community in which Boaz and Ruth lived.

• Literary symmetry: The Book of Ruth begins with tragedy and famine in Moab, mirroring the moral famine and family tragedy of Judah in Genesis 38. Both narratives resolve with a levirate-style redemption that secures the covenant lineage, demonstrating that God is the ultimate Guardian of the vulnerable and the Builder of families.
