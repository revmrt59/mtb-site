LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh (translated as "LORD" in all capital letters in most English versions) is the personal, covenantal, and self-revealed name of the God of Israel. It denotes His eternal self-existence, absolute faithfulness, and active redemption of His people. In the narrative of Ruth, and specifically in Ruth 4:13, the use of this name highlights that the restoration of Naomi's family, the redemption of Ruth, and the birth of Obed are not the products of mere coincidence, but the direct, gracious intervention of the sovereign Covenant God who keeps His promises even during the darkest periods of human history.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The name Yahweh is the tetragrammaton (the four-letter name YHWH) and is the most sacred name of God in the Old Testament.

• Historically, the Jewish people held this name in such high reverence that they avoided pronouncing it aloud to prevent taking it in vain, substituting the word Adonai ("Lord") in its place during public reading.

• This name is deeply connected to God’s self-revelation to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus 3:14, where He identifies Himself as "I AM WHO I AM." It presents God not as an abstract force or a distant deity, but as a personal, accessible, and covenant-making God who enters into relationship with His creation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The name derives from the Hebrew verb hayah, which means "to be" or "to exist."

• Strictly defined, Yahweh means "the self-existent one" or "He who causes to be." It signifies His eternal, uncaused existence; He depends on nothing else for His life, power, or authority.

• In contrast to the generic title Elohim (which emphasizes God's power as Creator), Yahweh is His proper name, denoting His unchanging character, His absolute holiness, and His covenant fidelity. He is the God who is present, active, and reliable.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Yahweh is used over 6,800 times in the Old Testament, making it the most frequent name for God.

• The name is consistently associated with God’s covenant promises, His redemptive acts (such as the Exodus), and His personal relationship with Israel.

• In the book of Ruth, Yahweh is invoked in oaths, blessings, and prayers by various characters, reflecting their underlying faith that the God of Israel is actively monitoring and directing human affairs.

• In Ruth 4:13, the name is used in a powerful word picture of divine enablement: the LORD "gave her conception." The giving of life is pictured not merely as a biological process, but as a direct, hands-on gift from the Author of Life Himself.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty over the Womb: In ancient Israel, barrenness was a source of deep grief and social reproach. By stating that the LORD gave Ruth conception, the text reinforces the theological truth that God is the sovereign giver of life and the healer of barrenness.

• Covenant Faithfulness (Hesed): God's covenant name is attached to this birth because this child is the answer to the prayers of the community and the ultimate fulfillment of God's loyalty to the family of Elimelech. Through this child, a devastated family is redeemed.

• The Orchestration of History: The birth of Obed is a crucial link in the chain of salvation history. The LORD is not just solving a localized family crisis; He is actively raising up the line of David, and ultimately, the Messiah (Jesus Christ).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Throughout the book of Ruth, God’s hand is primarily hidden behind the scenes, working through the "ordinary" events of gleaning, kindness, and legal transactions.

• Ruth 4:13 represents a dramatic shift where the narrator steps forward to attribute a specific event directly to Yahweh. This is one of only two times in the entire book where the narrator explicitly attributes an action to God (the first being Ruth 1:6, where the LORD visited His people to give them bread).

• This deliberate placement highlights that the resolution of the story—moving from emptiness to fullness, from death to life—is ultimately the work of the covenant-keeping God. Boaz redeemed the land, but only the LORD could redeem the lineage by granting conception to a previously barren Moabite widow.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Ruth 1:6: "Then she arose with her daughters-in-law that she might return from the country of Moab, for she had heard in the country of Moab that the LORD had visited His people by giving them bread."

• Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The book of Ruth is set during the dark and chaotic days of the Judges, a time characterized by spiritual decay and national apostasy. Yet, the persistent use of the name Yahweh by the characters in this story demonstrates that even in dark times, there remains a righteous remnant.

• The transition of Ruth from a foreigner to one who is blessed directly by Yahweh illustrates the expansive nature of God's covenant grace, showing that His redeeming love transcends ethnic and national boundaries.
