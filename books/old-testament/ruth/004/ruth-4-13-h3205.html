BORE - ( yalad , Strong's H3205 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalad carries the primary meaning of bearing, bringing forth, or begetting children. In the narrative of Ruth 4:13, it signals the joyful resolution of the book's overarching themes of emptiness, widowhood, and death. By recording that Ruth "bore" a son, the writer showcases the divine restoration of Naomi’s family line and places this personal narrative of redemption directly into the grand lineage of King David and the ultimate Messiah.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite culture, childbirth (yalad) was viewed as much more than a biological event; it was a profound sign of divine blessing, covenantal continuity, and the preservation of a family's heritage. To die without descendants was considered a tragedy, as a family's name would be blotted out from Israel. The legal custom of levirate marriage, which undergirds the final chapters of Ruth, was specifically designed to prevent this tragedy. Therefore, when a woman gave birth, she did not merely bring a child into the world; she brought forth life, hope, and a future for an entire ancestral line. The term yalad evokes the pain of labor, the miraculous intervention of God opening the womb, and the community's shared joy in receiving a new life.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root verb yalad occurs nearly 500 times in the Old Testament. In its basic active form (the Qal stem), it means to bear, bring forth, or give birth (when referring to a mother), and to beget (when referring to a father). In other grammatical stems, it can mean to act as a midwife (Piel), to be born (Pual), or to declare lineage and beget offspring (Hiphil). In Ruth 4:13, the verb appears in the Qal stem, third-person feminine singular with a waw-consecutive (wattēled), translated directly as "and she bore." It denotes the physical, historical reality of Ruth giving birth to a baby boy, cementing her transition from a destitute foreign widow to an honored mother in Israel.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yalad is employed in several distinct ways:

• Genealogical Records: It is the primary verb used to construct Israel's genealogies (often translated as "begot" or "bore"), tracing the historical development of the covenant people from Adam onward.

• Metaphor of Labor Pains: The physical distress of childbirth (yalad) is frequently used by the prophets as a vivid metaphor for intense sorrow, fear, or sudden judgment overtaking a nation (e.g., Micah 4:9-10).

• Divine Agency: The act of bearing children is consistently depicted as dependent on God's sovereignty. Humanity acts, but God is the one who enables conception and successful delivery.

### 4. Theological Implications

The occurrence of yalad in Ruth 4:13 is highly theological because it represents the ultimate reversal of the curse of barrenness and death. The Book of Ruth begins in Moab with the death of three men, leaving Naomi and her daughters-in-law childless and hopeless. When Ruth marries Boaz, the text explicitly states that "the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."

This birth demonstrates that:

• God is the Author of Life: Conception and birth are gifts of divine grace.

• God is Faithful to His Covenant: The birth of Obed ensures that Elimelech’s name is not forgotten in Israel.

• God Works through Ordinary Faithfulness: The birth of this single child in a small village becomes the crucial link in the chain of salvation history, leading directly to David and ultimately to Jesus Christ.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:13, yalad marks the climax of the book's narrative arc. The scene moves rapidly from the legal transaction at the city gate, to the marriage of Boaz and Ruth, to the conception and birth of their son. This swift progression emphasizes that God’s redemptive plan, once set in motion, moves decisively to completion. The nuance here is one of overwhelming restoration. This birth does not just comfort Ruth; it restores Naomi. The community recognizes this in Ruth 4:17, using a passive form of the same verb to exclaim, "There is a son born (yullad) to Naomi," proving that this birth has healed the family's deep-seated grief and emptiness.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:2: "For Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age, at the set time of which God had spoken to him."

• Ruth 4:17: "Also the neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'There is a son born to Naomi.' And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David."

• Isaiah 9:6: "For unto us a Child is born, Unto us a Son is given; And the government will be upon His shoulder. And His name will be called Wonderful, Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), yalad is frequently translated using the verbs tikto (to give birth) or gennao (to beget). These are the exact words used in the New Testament to describe the birth of Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:16, Luke 2:6). Thus, the physical birth of Obed in Ruth 4 foreshadows the miraculous birth of the ultimate Redeemer in Bethlehem, linking the small-town kindness of Boaz and Ruth directly to the global salvation accomplished by Christ.
