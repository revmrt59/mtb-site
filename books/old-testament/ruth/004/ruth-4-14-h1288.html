BLESSED - ( barak , Strong's H1288 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb barak fundamentally means to kneel, salute, praise, or bestow divine favor and prosperity. When directed from humans toward God, especially in the passive participle form, it expresses adoration, thanksgiving, and knee-bending worship. In Ruth 4:14, the women of Bethlehem use this term to declare God's praiseworthiness for His loyal love in redeeming Naomi's family line through Boaz and the birth of Obed.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of barak is closely associated with the Hebrew noun for "knee" (berek). This connection evokes the physical posture of kneeling down to present a gift, show homage, or receive a blessing from a superior. In the ancient Near East, kneeling was an act of submission, honor, and relational alignment. When humans "bless" God, they metaphorically kneel in adoration, recognizing His supreme authority and goodness. Conversely, when God "blesses" humans, He stoops down in grace to bestow life, fertility, and covenantal protection.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary lexical meaning of barak depends on the grammatical stem used in the Hebrew text. In the Qal stem, the verb means to bend the knee or kneel. In the Piel stem (the most frequent verbal form), it means to bless, praise, salute, or greet. When God is the object of this verb, it means to declare Him blessed, to praise, or to adore Him. When God is the subject, it means to endow with power, success, or well-being. In Ruth 4:14, the word appears as a passive participle (baruk), functioning as an adjective meaning "blessed," "praised," or "adored."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes barak to describe a dynamic, multi-directional flow of covenantal relationship:

• God blessing humanity: This is the active impartation of vitality, fruitfulness, and success. It is the divine enablement to flourish.

• Humanity blessing God: This is responsive worship. Because God is already complete, humans do not add to His wealth; instead, they declare His inherent worthiness and kneel in gratitude.

• Humanity blessing humanity: This is the invocation of God's favor upon another person, typically spoken by a father, priest, or leader.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of barak is deeply rooted in God's covenant relationship with His people. Blessing is not a vague, impersonal force; it is the personal, active favor of Yahweh. To declare "Blessed be the LORD" is to acknowledge that He is the ultimate source of all life-giving goodness. It is an act of covenantal alignment, affirming that even in times of difficulty, God's providential care is active and worthy of praise.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:14, the passive participle baruk is used by the women of Bethlehem to praise Yahweh. This represents a profound narrative resolution. In Ruth 1:20-21, Naomi returned to Bethlehem empty and bitter, claiming the Almighty had dealt very bitterly with her. By the end of the narrative, the birth of Obed prompts the community to cry out, "Blessed be the LORD." The blessing highlights that God has not abandoned His covenant loyalty (hesed) to Naomi. The empty lap is now full, and the bitter heart is filled with praise.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:2 - "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great; and you shall be a blessing."

• Genesis 14:20 - "And blessed be God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand."

• Psalm 103:1 - "Bless the LORD, O my soul; and all that is within me, bless His holy name!"

• Psalm 115:15 - "May you be blessed by the LORD, who made heaven and earth."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), the passive participle baruk in Ruth 4:14 is translated with the Greek adjective eulogetos. This is the root of the English word "eulogy," which means to speak well of or to praise. It is the same word used in the New Testament to praise God the Father for the spiritual blessings bestowed upon believers in Christ (Ephesians 1:3).
