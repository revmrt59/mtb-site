LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

In Ruth 4:14, the covenant name of God, Yahweh, is used by the women of Bethlehem to praise the Lord for His sovereign faithfulness and redemptive grace. Having guided Naomi from the emptiness of Moab back to the fullness of blessing in Bethlehem, the LORD reveals Himself not merely as a distant deity, but as the personal Promise-Keeper who restores life and secures the future of His covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The name Yahweh is the tetragrammaton, the four-letter name of God considered so sacred by the ancient Israelites that it was not spoken aloud, substituted instead with Adonai (Lord). It is the unique, personal name of the God of Israel, first fully expounded to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus 3:14. Unlike generic terms for deity, this name represents God in active relationship with His chosen people. It carries the weight of His eternal existence, absolute independence, and unchanging character. It is the signature of the God who enters history to redeem, guide, and sustain those who belong to Him.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yahweh is the proper noun denoting the one true God. Etymologically derived from the archaic Hebrew verb hayah (to be), the name literally means "He is," "He who is," or "He brings into being." It defines God as the self-existent, self-sufficient One who has no beginning and no end. He is the source of all life and existence. Lexically, this name identifies the covenant God of Israel, emphasizing His active presence, absolute faithfulness to His promises, and His sovereign role as Savior, King, and Creator.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The name Yahweh occurs more than 6,800 times in the Old Testament, making it the most frequent designation for God. It is almost exclusively used in contexts involving covenant relationships, sacrifice, divine revelation, and personal salvation.

• The primary word picture associated with Yahweh is that of an immovable, rock-solid foundation. Because He is the self-existent One, His promises are not subject to the shifting shadows of human history or the frailty of human emotion. He is the anchor of hope. To call upon Yahweh is to appeal to the One who is eternally present, whose character cannot change, and whose covenant cannot fail.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Faithfulness (Hesed): The name Yahweh is intrinsically tied to God's hesed—His loyal, covenant love. It reminds the reader that God's commitment to His people is based entirely on His own character, not on their performance.

• Providential Redemption: The use of Yahweh highlights how the sovereign Creator of the universe condescends to involve Himself in the minute details of human lives. He coordinates events, reverses tragedies, and turns mourning into dancing to fulfill His ultimate redemptive purposes.

• Eternality and Immutability: As the "I AM," Yahweh transcends time and circumstance, assuring His people that the God who acted on behalf of their ancestors is the very same God who acts on their behalf today.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:14, the women of Bethlehem cry out, "Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a close relative." This is the climax of Naomi's journey. In chapter 1, Naomi returned from Moab empty and bitter, claiming that Shaddai (the Almighty) had dealt bitterly with her and that Yahweh had testified against her (Ruth 1:20-21).

• By chapter 4, the narrative shifts from Naomi's perceived abandonment to a celebration of Yahweh's active provision. The use of Yahweh here demonstrates that the covenant God was never Naomi's enemy; rather, He was working behind the scenes to provide a kinsman-redeemer (Boaz) and a descendant (Obed) who would restore her life and secure the royal line of David. The name Yahweh in this context highlights His tenderness, His keeping of promises, and His ultimate victory over despair.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:4: "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens,"

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

• Proverbs 18:10: "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; the righteous run into it and are safe."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• While Elohim emphasizes God's power as the transcendent Creator of the universe, Yahweh emphasizes His immanent, personal, and redemptive relationship with humanity. It is highly significant that the Book of Ruth begins with famine and death under the general sovereignty of God, but ends with the community praising Yahweh for the birth of a child. This transition shows that the grand, cosmic Ruler of the universe is also the deeply personal, caring Lord of the household.
