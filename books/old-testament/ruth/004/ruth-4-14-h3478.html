ISRAEL - ( Yisra'el , Strong's H3478 )

Summary:

The name Yisra'el designates the covenant people of God, originating from the name given to Jacob after he wrestled with God at Penuel. In the context of Ruth 4:14, this term expands the horizon of a local family's restoration in Bethlehem to the broader covenant community. The prayer that Obed’s name would be famous "in Israel" highlights the national and ultimately messianic significance of Boaz and Ruth’s child, demonstrating how God's providential care for an individual family serves His larger redemptive plan for His entire chosen nation.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yisra'el is deeply rooted in Israel’s patriarchal history, first appearing in Genesis 32:28. It is a compound word formed from the verb sarah (to contend, strive, or exert power) and the noun El (God). The name translates to "God strives," "one who strives with God," or "ruler with God." It was given to Jacob after his night-long struggle with a divine messenger, marking a pivotal transition from his identity as a supplanter (Ya'aqob) to a prince who prevailed through persistence and faith. The name became the national designation for his descendants, carrying the permanent historical reminder of a people who are defined by their relationship with God—marked by struggles, divine chosenness, and ultimate redemption.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yisra'el serves as a proper noun with several layers of meaning within the Old Testament:

• The Patriarch Jacob: Used as the personal, divinely given name of Jacob himself.

• The Descendants of Jacob: The corporate community of the twelve tribes descended from Jacob's sons.

• The Unified Kingdom: The entire nation under the reigns of Saul, David, and Solomon.

• The Northern Kingdom: After the civil war and division of the nation, the term often specifically designated the ten northern tribes, as distinct from Judah.

• The Spiritual Assembly: The covenant community of Yahweh, defined by faith and commitment to the Mosaic covenant. In the Book of Ruth, which is set during the period of the Judges, it denotes the entire covenant nation of Yahweh’s chosen people.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, Israel is depicted using rich theological metaphors:

• The Vineyard of Yahweh: A picture of a cultivated, treasured possession from which God expects the fruit of righteousness.

• The Firstborn Son: Reflecting a relationship of special inheritance, love, and family obligation before God.

• The Bride/Wife of Yahweh: Highlighting the deep, covenantal intimacy and the profound tragedy of spiritual adultery when the nation turned to idols.

• The Flock: Portraying Israel as helpless sheep dependent upon Yahweh, their Great Shepherd, for guidance, pasture, and protection.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of Yisra'el rests on the doctrine of sovereign election. Israel did not become God's people because of their strength or numbers, but because of Yahweh’s love and His oaths to the patriarchs. The existence of Israel is a testament to God's covenant faithfulness (chesed). Furthermore, Israel was designated to be a kingdom of priests and a holy nation, serving as the channel through which divine revelation, the law, and ultimately the Messiah would come to bless all families of the earth.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:14, the women of Bethlehem pronounce a blessing over Naomi upon the birth of Ruth and Boaz's son, Obed. They pray, "may his name be famous in Israel!"

• From Private Grief to Public Blessing: The story of Ruth begins with famine, death, and emptiness in Moab, but ends with abundance, life, and fullness in Bethlehem. By praying that the child's name would be famous "in Israel," the women elevate this intimate family resolution to national significance.

• The Period of the Judges: The events of Ruth take place when "there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). The mention of "Israel" at the climax of the book provides a beacon of hope. It signals that God was quietly working in the dark periods of national rebellion to preserve and prepare His people.

• Messianic Foreshadowing: The prayer was answered far beyond what the women of Bethlehem could have imagined. Obed became the father of Jesse, who became the father of King David. Through this line came Jesus Christ, the Messiah. The child's name became famous throughout Israel because he was the ancestor of Israel's greatest king and the world's Savior.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 32:28: "And He said, 'Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel; for you have struggled with God and with men, and have prevailed.'"

• Exodus 4:22: "Then you shall say to Pharaoh, 'Thus says the LORD: "Israel is My son, My firstborn."'"

• Deuteronomy 6:4: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!"

• Joshua 24:1: "Then Joshua gathered all the tribes of Israel to Shechem and called for the elders of Israel, for their heads, for their judges, and for their officers; and they presented themselves before God."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The inclusion of Ruth—a Moabitess—into the genealogy of Israel’s ultimate King and Savior showcases the expansive reach of God’s grace. According to the law, Moabites were excluded from the assembly of Israel (Deuteronomy 23:3). However, through faith, covenant commitment ("your people shall be my people"), and the work of a kinsman-redeemer, Ruth is fully grafted into the history and heritage of Israel. This serves as an Old Testament picture of the Gospel, showing that God's covenant blessings in Israel have always been accessible to those who seek refuge under the wings of the Lord.
