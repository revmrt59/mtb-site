FAMOUS - ( qara , Strong's H7121 )

Summary:

In Ruth 4:14, the word translated "famous" is the Hebrew verb qara, which fundamentally means to call, summon, or proclaim. Used here in the passive (Niphal) stem, it literally means "to be called" or "to be named." In the context of ancient Israel, for a person's name "to be called" or "proclaimed" throughout the community meant that their reputation, lineage, and legacy would be celebrated, remembered, and held in high honor. The blessing pronounced upon Obed—the child born to Ruth and Boaz—is a prayer that his name would not fade into the obscurity of Elimelech's nearly extinct family line, but would instead be widely recognized, praised, and established throughout Israel.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root qara is one of the most frequent verbs in the Old Testament, representing an acoustic, vocal event—making a sound to arrest attention, declare a message, or designate a name. In the ancient Near East, a name was not merely a label but an expression of a person's character, destiny, and existence. To have one's name "called out" or "proclaimed" (qara) meant that their life and impact were actively spoken of by the community.

The background of Ruth 4 is the preservation of a family line that was on the brink of extinction. Elimelech and his sons had died, leaving Naomi empty and without an heir. By Boaz marrying Ruth and fathering Obed, the family name and inheritance were redeemed. Therefore, the exclamation "may his name be famous" is a prayer that this restored family line will be highly esteemed, perpetually spoken of, and recognized as a monument of God's restoration.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qara has a broad semantic range depending on its grammatical stem:

• In the active (Qal) stem, it means to cry out, call to someone, summon, read aloud, or bestow a name.

• In the passive (Niphal) stem, which is the form used in Ruth 4:14 (yiqqare), it means to be called, named, summoned, or proclaimed.

• When paired with "name" (shem), the passive construction "let his name be called" functions as an idiom for achieving renown, acquiring a lasting reputation, or becoming famous. It indicates that the person's name will be repeatedly and honorably spoken of in public spaces.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Naming and Ownership: The first biblical use of qara occurs in Genesis 1:5, where God calls the light "Day" and the darkness "Night." This naming establishes order, purpose, and sovereign ownership.

• Proclaiming Character: In Exodus 34:6, God "proclaimed" (qara) His own name and character to Moses. The word picture here is the vocal broadcasting of essential identity.

• The Sound of Renown: In Ruth 4:14, the word picture is a vocal, community-wide celebration. The neighbors and townspeople are not merely thinking about Obed; they are actively speaking his name, praising God for his birth, and spreading his reputation through the gates of Bethlehem.

### 4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Faithfulness (Hesed): The ultimate "famous name" in this lineage is not just Obed, or even David, but Jesus Christ. God takes the empty, nameless, and bitter state of Naomi and transforms it into a legacy of global, eternal renown.

• Redemption of Identity: In biblical theology, to lose one's name is to lose one's inheritance and memory. By applying qara to Obed, God demonstrates that redemption restores lost identity and elevates it to a place of honor.

• Sovereign Provision: The fame of the name is a gift from God. The women of Bethlehem bless Yahweh first ("Blessed be the LORD..."), recognizing that any human renown is a direct result of divine grace and covenant preservation.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:14, the blessing is uttered by the women of Bethlehem to Naomi. The immediate referent of "his name" is the newborn child, Obed.

There is a striking literary contrast in the book of Ruth regarding names. The closer relative who refused to redeem Ruth did so to avoid ruining his own inheritance (Ruth 4:6). Consequently, his actual name is never mentioned in the text; he is referred to only by the placeholder peloni almoni ("Mr. So-and-So" or "friend"). His name is forgotten. Conversely, Boaz, who selflessly redeemed Ruth, and Obed, the son born of this union, have their names eternally proclaimed. The prayer "may his name be famous in Israel" serves as a prophetic bridge. Obed's grandson would be King David, and his ultimate descendant would be Jesus, whose name is exalted above every name.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The passive form (Niphal) of qara in Ruth 4:14 points to a divine passive. While it is the people of Israel who will speak Obed's name, it is God who ultimately establishes his legacy and causes his name to be remembered. Furthermore, the connection between qara (to call/proclaim) and the concept of "calling" or "vocation" is vital. When God names something, He defines its purpose. Praying that Obed's name be "called" or "famous" is a prayer that his God-given purpose in Israel's history would be fully realized and recognized.
