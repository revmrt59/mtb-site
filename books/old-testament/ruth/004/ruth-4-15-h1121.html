SONS - ( ben , Strong's H1121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben fundamentally denotes a male offspring, son, descendant, or builder of the family name. In the ancient Near East, sons represented the continuation of the family line, economic security, and inheritance preservation. In the climax of the book of Ruth (Ruth 4:15), the word occurs in the plural (banim) as the women of Bethlehem declare Ruth to be better to Naomi than "seven sons." This ultimate expression of value highlights how Ruth's covenant-keeping love transcended the traditional ideal of a large, biological male family, culminating in the birth of Obed, who would restore Naomi's life and secure the Davidic lineage.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word ben is closely related to the verbal root banah, which means "to build." From a Hebrew perspective, a son is one who builds up the family house and ensures its continued existence into the next generation.

• In ancient Israel, a household's survival, strength, and legacy depended heavily on the birth of sons. They inherited the family land, worked the fields, defended the estate, and preserved the family name from being blotted out of Israel's memory.

• The number seven represents completeness, perfection, and the ultimate fullness of divine blessing. Therefore, having "seven sons" was considered the absolute pinnacle of earthly security, honor, and maternal blessing in Israelite society.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• In its primary sense, the word refers to a direct male offspring (a son).

• In a broader genealogical sense, it denotes a grandson, male descendant, or descendant in a general sense (e.g., "sons of Israel" to describe the entire nation).

• It is used metaphorically to denote membership in a particular class, guild, or character trait, such as "sons of the prophets" (disciples) or "son of worthlessness" (a wicked person).

• In the plural form (banim), it can refer to children of both genders, but in comparative, genealogical, and inheritance contexts, it refers specifically to male heirs.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Building the House: Because of the linguistic link between ben (son) and banah (to build), to have a son was to have one's "house built up." This word picture is explicitly used in Ruth 4:11, where Rachel and Leah are described as those who "built the house of Israel."

• Arrows in a Warrior's Hand: Scripture pictures sons born in one's youth as arrows in the hand of a warrior, representing protection, strength, and active representation in public affairs at the city gate.

• A Heritage and Reward: Sons are described not as human achievements, but as a heritage and a gift given directly by God to preserve the family line.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenantal Continuity: In the Old Testament, the lineage of the promised seed (originally spoken of in Genesis 3:15) is carried through the line of chosen sons. The birth of a son is never merely a biological event; it is an link in the chain of God's redemptive history.

• Redemption and Grace: In the narrative of Ruth, Naomi's family line was functionally dead. The birth of a son (ben) to Ruth and Boaz represents the redemption of the dead Mahlon's name and Elimelech's land. This points forward to the ultimate Son (ben) of David, Jesus Christ, who redeems humanity's lost inheritance.

• Redefining True Value: By declaring that Ruth is better to Naomi than "seven sons," the biblical text elevates sacrificial, covenant-keeping love (hesed) above standard cultural markers of security. Godly character and loyalty are of greater worth than even the most ideal biological household.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:15, the neighborhood women speak to Naomi at the birth of Obed. The phrase "better to you than seven sons" is a stunning hyperbole in a patriarchal society.

• Naomi had lost her husband and her two biological sons in Moab, leaving her empty and hopeless. The community recognizes that Ruth's voluntary, self-sacrificing love did for Naomi what seven biological sons could never guarantee: she provided a redeemer, food in famine, companionship in grief, and ultimately, a child to nurse in Naomi's old age.

• The comparison underscores that the foreign, Moabite daughter-in-law, through her faith in Yahweh, became the instrument of Naomi's restoration. The son she bore did not just continue her own family, but became the "restorer of life" for Naomi.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The ultimate vindication of the "son" born to Ruth is found in the genealogy at the end of the book (Ruth 4:18-22). The son Obed fathered Jesse, who fathered David.

• The legal transaction in Ruth 4 highlights the concept of the levirate-like redeemer. The son born to Ruth was legally counted to the name of the deceased, yet practically brought joy and life back to the living mother-in-law, showcasing how God's law of redemption works to heal broken families.
