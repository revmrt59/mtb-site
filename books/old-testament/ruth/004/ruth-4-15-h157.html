LOVES - ( ahab , Strong's H157 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ahab expresses a deep, passionate, and binding affection that manifests in loyal, concrete action. While it encompasses emotional warmth, its biblical weight lies in covenant commitment, personal choice, and sacrificial devotion. In the climax of the book of Ruth, this love is highlighted as the driving force behind Ruth's devotion to Naomi—a love that the surrounding community recognizes as far surpassing the cultural ideal of seven sons.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root ahab represents the primary expression of love in the Old Testament. In the ancient Near Eastern world, love was not merely a sentimental feeling or an involuntary emotion. Instead, it was an act of the will, often associated with covenant loyalty, alliance, and choice. To love someone meant to choose them, to bind oneself to them, and to act consistently in their best interest. This word covers the entire spectrum of love: the romantic love between a husband and wife, the familial love between parents and children, the deep bond of loyal friendship, and the covenant love between Yahweh and His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ahab means to love, to desire, or to have a deep affection for. It denotes an intense personal attachment that expresses itself in active, outward devotion. The semantic range includes:

• The emotional and passionate affection between individuals.

• The deliberate, volitional choice to care for and remain loyal to another person, regardless of circumstances.

• The covenantal loyalty owed to a king, benefactor, or to God Himself.

• The act of delighting in abstract concepts such as justice, righteousness, or God's law.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses ahab to paint vivid pictures of relational bonds. It describes the deep familial affection of a father for his son, as seen in Abraham’s love for Isaac, and the romantic attachment of Jacob for Rachel, which made years of hard labor seem like only a few days. It also depicts the profound, soul-knit friendship between Jonathan and David, where love transcended political rivalry and personal ambition. In these contexts, the word functions as a picture of a heart that has found its home in another, resulting in hands that serve, feet that follow, and a life that is willingly laid down for the sake of the beloved.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ahab lies at the very heart of God's relationship with humanity. It is the motivating force behind redemption. God did not set His love on Israel because they were numerous or powerful, but simply because He chose to love them and to keep His covenant oath. Furthermore, ahab is the supreme duty required of God's people; the commandment to love the Lord with all one's heart, soul, and strength is the foundation of the law. In human relationships, ahab acts as the emotional and relational fuel for chesed (covenant faithfulness). While chesed emphasizes the duty and loyalty of a covenant, ahab provides the passionate, willing affection that makes that loyalty beautiful, personal, and life-giving.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:15, the women of Bethlehem pronounce a blessing over Naomi, speaking of Ruth: "for your daughter-in-law, who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons, has borne him."

• This is the only place in the Old Testament where a daughter-in-law's love for her mother-in-law is explicitly described with ahab. It elevates their relationship far beyond mere duty or legal obligation.

• The verb is used in the participle form (ohebet), which indicates a continuous, characteristic, and ongoing state of loving. Ruth’s love was not a fleeting emotion but a steady, active reality.

• The community declares this love to be "better to you than seven sons." In ancient Israel, seven sons represented the absolute pinnacle of family security, honor, and blessing for a widow. By placing Ruth's active, sacrificial ahab above this ideal, the narrative shows that covenant love is more valuable and redeeming than even the greatest natural blessings. Ruth’s love was the human instrument through which God restored life and fullness to Naomi's empty heart.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:2: "Then He said, 'Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah...'"

• Deuteronomy 6:5: "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength."

• 1 Samuel 18:1: "Now it had come to pass, when he had finished speaking to Saul, that the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul."

• Proverbs 17:17: "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity."

• Song of Solomon 1:7: "Tell me, O you whom I love, where you feed your flock, where you make it rest at noon."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound connection exists between ahab (love) and chesed (steadfast love/lovingkindness). In the book of Ruth, chesed is the structural backbone of the story—seen in Boaz's protection and Ruth's loyalty. However, ahab is the heartbeat. The use of ahab at the climax of the book reveals that the loyalty Ruth demonstrated was not born out of cold, legalistic obligation. It was fueled by a warm, deep, and personal affection. True biblical love is never passive; it expresses itself in tangible acts of redemption, provision, and self-sacrifice, ultimately pointing forward to the redeeming love of Jesus Christ.
