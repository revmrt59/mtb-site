BETTER - ( tob , Strong's H2896 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word tob, translated as "better" in Ruth 4:15, communicates supreme value, goodness, and beneficial blessing that surpasses conventional standards. In the context of Naomi’s restoration, Ruth's sacrificial love is declared to be of greater worth than the ultimate patriarchal blessing of seven sons. This word highlights how God's grace often exceeds human expectations, transforming grief into abundant joy through unexpected channels of covenant loyalty.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word tob is deeply embedded in the Hebrew creation narrative. When God creates, He repeatedly pronounces His work as tob (good), indicating that it is beautiful, harmonious, and fully functioning according to His divine design. The word does not merely represent abstract moral goodness but practical, life-giving excellence. In ancient Israelite culture, "seven sons" represented the absolute pinnacle of earthly security, prosperity, and family legacy. Seven was the number of perfection and completeness, and sons were the keepers of the family name and inheritance. To declare that someone is "better" than seven sons is a radical, counter-cultural word picture. It elevates a foreign-born daughter-in-law's faithful love above the highest cultural ideal of patriarchal blessing.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew adjective tob has a broad semantic range, encompassing concepts of goodness, beauty, gladness, prosperity, and moral uprightness. When paired with the comparative Hebrew preposition min (meaning "than"), tob is translated as "better." In this comparative sense, it indicates superior quality, higher value, greater utility, or deeper desirability. Rather than expressing a mere subjective preference, comparative tob denotes an objective, life-enriching worth that genuinely improves the condition of the recipient.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, particularly in Wisdom Literature, tob is used to contrast two paths, choices, or realities. The comparative "better" functions like a balance scale in an ancient marketplace. One side of the scale might hold a highly valued cultural asset—such as wealth, strength, or biological descendants. The other side holds a spiritual or relational reality, such as the fear of the Lord or covenant loyalty. In every case, the scale tips decisively toward the spiritual reality. The word picture suggests that true value is not found in outward quantity or societal status, but in the qualitative weight of godliness and love.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, tob reflects the character of God, who is the source and standard of all goodness. In the book of Ruth, God’s goodness is mediated through human agents who walk in covenant faithfulness (hesed). Ruth’s self-sacrificing love is the earthly expression of God’s covenantal goodness toward Naomi. The declaration that Ruth is "better" than seven sons shows that God's redemptive grace operates outside of rigid cultural expectations. It demonstrates that spiritual devotion and loving-kindness carry eternal weight, transforming a narrative of emptiness and death into one of fullness and life.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:15, the women of Bethlehem speak to Naomi after the birth of Obed. Naomi, who had previously returned to Bethlehem empty and bitter, is now fully restored. The women specifically credit Ruth as the source of this restoration, describing her as "better to you than seven sons." In a patriarchal society, a widow’s survival and security depended entirely on male heirs. By declaring Ruth "better" than seven sons, the community acknowledges that Ruth’s voluntary, sacrificial love provided a security, joy, and legacy that even a perfect biological family could not guarantee. Ruth's love brought life back to Naomi's dead household.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 84:10: "For a day in Your courts is better than a thousand. I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness."

• Proverbs 15:16: "Better is a little with the fear of the LORD, than great treasure with trouble."

• Ecclesiastes 4:9: "Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their labor."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

For pastors and teachers, this passage provides a powerful opportunity to redefine how the church measures blessing and success. While the world—and even religious communities—often measures success by external metrics such as family size, wealth, or social standing, the Holy Spirit measures success through the lens of faithful, self-giving relationships. Ruth, a destitute Moabite widow, became the ultimate channel of divine blessing to Naomi because of her love. This text encourages believers to value quiet faithfulness and covenant relationships over worldly security, reminding us that God's greatest provisions often come through the most unexpected people.
