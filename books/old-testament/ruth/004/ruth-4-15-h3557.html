NOURISHER - ( kul , Strong's 3557 )

Summary:

In Ruth 4:15, the word translated "nourisher" is the Hebrew verb kul, which means to sustain, support, maintain, or provide with food and life's necessities. In this passage, it describes Naomi's newborn grandson, Obed, who would grow up to physically support and care for her in her old age. This term represents the beautiful reversal of Naomi's bitter emptiness, illustrating how God uses family redemption to actively preserve, protect, and sustain His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew root kul fundamentally relates to containing, holding, or measuring out. It carries the physical imagery of a vessel holding liquid or a container holding its contents without spilling or breaking. Over time, this concept expanded to include the idea of holding someone up, bearing them up, or sustaining them so they do not fall or collapse under a heavy burden.

In the ancient Near Eastern world, survival depended heavily on the land and the strength of the family unit. To "nourish" or "sustain" someone meant to actively supply them with the daily physical resources—such as food, water, shelter, and protection—needed to keep them alive and secure. It is a highly practical, concrete word of active care, emphasizing that love and covenant faithfulness are demonstrated through tangible, daily provision.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb kul means to comprehend, contain, sustain, or provide. In its specific grammatical form in Ruth 4:15 (the Pilpel stem), the word means to nourish, provide food for, support, or maintain. It does not refer to a one-time gift, but rather to an ongoing, long-term commitment to care for and preserve another person's life.

Lexically, the word denotes:

• Providing nourishment or food to sustain life.

• Supporting, holding up, or maintaining someone in a state of safety and comfort.

• Bearing the financial and physical responsibility for another person's well-being.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, kul is used in contexts where survival is at stake, illustrating the depth of its protective care.

• It is used of Joseph providing food and sustaining his father and brothers during the extreme famine in Egypt, ensuring their survival.

• It is used of God sending ravens to feed and sustain the prophet Elijah by the brook Cherith during a severe drought.

• It is used of Obadiah hiding one hundred prophets of the Lord in caves and sustaining them with bread and water during Jezebel's persecution.

• It is used of God's forty-year sustaining care of Israel in the wilderness, where they lacked nothing.

The underlying word picture is that of a dependable provider who steps into a situation of lack, weakness, or vulnerability and supplies exactly what is needed to maintain life.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, kul reveals the character of God as the ultimate Sustainer of His creation and His covenant people. God is not a distant deity who merely observes; He is the One who actively holds all things together and provides daily bread.

Furthermore, this word illustrates the beauty of God's grace mediated through human relationships. In the covenant community, God's sustaining care is often delivered through the hands of others. Family redemption is not merely a legal or ceremonial concept; it has physical, daily, and life-giving consequences. When we care for the vulnerable, the widow, and the fatherless, we participate in God’s work of sustaining and nourishing His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the book of Ruth, Naomi's story begins with absolute emptiness. She lost her husband, her sons, her homeland, and her economic security, leading her to declare that the Lord had dealt bitterly with her. However, through the sacrificial love of Ruth and the redemptive action of Boaz, a child is born.

The women of Bethlehem celebrate this birth by declaring that the child, Obed, will be a "nourisher of your old age." The contextual nuance here is a complete reversal of Naomi's bitter widowhood. In ancient Israel, a widow without sons was completely destitute, with no social safety net. By calling Obed a "nourisher," the women are prophesying that this child will legally and physically take on the responsibility to sustain Naomi, ensuring she will never again experience the hunger, vulnerability, and emptiness that defined her past.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 47:12 - "Then Joseph provided his father, his brothers, and all his father's household with bread, according to the number of their families."

• 1 Kings 17:4 - "And it shall be that you shall drink from the brook, and I have commanded the ravens to feed you there."

• Psalm 55:22 - "Cast your burden on the LORD, And He shall sustain you; He shall never permit the righteous to be moved."

• Nehemiah 9:21 - "Forty years You sustained them in the wilderness; They lacked nothing; Their clothes did not wear out And their feet did not swell."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, this concept of physical and spiritual nourishment is mirrored in the way Christ cares for His church. In Ephesians 5:29, believers are reminded that "no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as the Lord does the church."

The Hebrew concept of kul challenges the modern church to think of ministry in holistic terms. True biblical care involves both spiritual restoration and practical, physical sustenance. When the church provides for the needy, supports the elderly, and feeds the hungry, it acts as a "nourisher" in the same spirit that Obed was to Naomi.
