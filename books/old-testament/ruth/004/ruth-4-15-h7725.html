RESTORER - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is a foundational Old Testament word denoting turning, returning, or bringing back. In its causative (Hiphil) form in Ruth 4:15, translated as "restorer," it describes one who causes life, vitality, and joy to return after a season of devastating loss, emptiness, and death. The word represents the reversal of Naomi's bitter exile and bereavement, signifying the covenant faithfulness of God who steps into human despair to renew, refresh, and rebuild what was broken.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The literal, concrete word picture of shub is the physical action of turning back around to go in the opposite direction, or returning to a place of departure. It is used of a traveler returning home, a river flowing back to its source, or a boundary line turning in a specific direction. When applied metaphorically to life circumstances, it paints the picture of a complete turnaround. In the context of a life that has been spiraling downward into grief, poverty, and barrenness—as Naomi’s did—a "restorer" (meshib) is someone who halts that downward trajectory and causes the life force, joy, and security to return to their rightful place.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shub occurs over 1,000 times in the Hebrew Bible, making it one of the most frequently used verbs. In its active, causative (Hiphil) participle form (meshib) used in Ruth 4:15, it means "one who causes to return," "one who brings back," or "one who restores."

• When paired with the Hebrew word nephesh (soul, life, or inner being), the phrase meshib nephesh means "restorer of life" or "one who revives and refreshes the soul."

• It carries the semantic range of returning property to its rightful owner, bringing captives back from exile, reviving the faint with food or water, and restoring spiritual vitality.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Return from Exile: The word is frequently used to describe God bringing His people back to the Promised Land after captivity. It signifies a return to safety, inheritance, and covenant relationship.

• The Revitalization of the Faint: In physical contexts, shub describes reviving someone who is exhausted, hungry, or on the brink of death by giving them sustenance.

• Spiritual Repentance: In the Qal stem, shub is the standard Hebrew verb for "repent"—turning away from sin and returning to Yahweh. In the Hiphil, it can mean bringing back wanderers to the Lord.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of shub as "restorer" touches the very heart of God's redemptive character. God does not merely comfort His people in their ruins; He is a builder and a restorer.

• Covenant Fidelity: God’s restoration is rooted in His covenant promises. When His people suffer the consequences of a fallen world or their own disobedience, God remains ready to restore them upon their return to Him.

• Redemption and Renewal: The ultimate trajectory of biblical history is one of restoration. What was lost in Eden is restored in Christ. The child Obed, called a "restorer," serves as a historical type of Jesus Christ, the ultimate Restorer who redeems our lost inheritance and gives us eternal life.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:15, the women of Bethlehem pronounce a blessing on Naomi at the birth of Ruth and Boaz's son, Obed. Naomi had previously declared, "I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty" (Ruth 1:21, using shub for "brought me home again").

• The birth of Obed is the direct answer to Naomi's emptiness. The child is legally and relationally the "restorer of life" (meshib nephesh) to her.

• Because Obed is born into the line of Elimelech through levirate-like redemption, he restores the family name, guards the land inheritance, and rescues Naomi from old-age destitution. He literally "brings back" her life from the grave of grief and bitterness.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 23:3 - "He restores my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness For His name's sake." (Here, shub in the Polel stem refers to the Shepherd reviving and renewing the believer's inner life).

• Psalm 19:7 - "The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul; The testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple;" (The word translated "converting" is the active participle meshibat, the feminine form of the word in Ruth 4:15, meaning "restoring" or "reviving" the soul).

• Lamentations 1:11 - "All her people sigh, they seek bread; They have given their pleasant things for food to restore life. 'See, O LORD, and consider, For I am scorned.'" (Here, the Hiphil infinitive lehashib is used of food reviving physical life).

• Psalm 80:3 - "Restore us, O God; Cause Your face to shine, And we shall be saved!" (The Hiphil imperative hashibenu petitions God to bring His people back to spiritual and national vitality).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase meshib nephesh (restorer of life/soul) highlights that Naomi's restoration was not merely financial or social; it was deeply psychological and spiritual. The ancient Hebrews did not separate the physical body from the soul; nephesh refers to the entire living being. Therefore, to restore Naomi's nephesh meant to bring her back from a state of walking death—caused by the loss of her husband and sons—into vibrant, hopeful, and functional living.
