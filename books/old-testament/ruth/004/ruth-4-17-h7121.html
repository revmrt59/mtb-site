CALLED - ( qara , Strong's H7121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qara primarily means to call out, summon, proclaim, or bestow a name. In the context of Ruth 4:17, it denotes the specific act of naming Naomi’s grandson ("they called his name Obed"). This communal act of naming signifies the restoration of a devastated family line, transforming Naomi's past emptiness into public joy and establishing a legacy that leads directly to King David and ultimately to Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root qara is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over 700 times. At its physical core, the word depicts the projection of the human voice to get someone's attention, summon an assembly, or make a public announcement. In ancient Near Eastern culture, naming a child (calling a name) was an action of profound significance, representing ownership, authority, prophetic destiny, and the definition of the person's character or role in life.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of qara includes:

• To call out or cry aloud: Projecting the voice to draw attention or express deep emotion.

• To summon or invite: Calling someone to a specific place, a feast, or an official assembly.

• To proclaim or read aloud: Publicly announcing a decree or reading a written text to a gathered group.

• To name or designate: Bestowing a name upon a person, place, or object to establish its identity.

In the Qal stem, when paired with the word shem ("name"), qara functions as the standard Hebrew idiom for naming. It carries the weight of defining a person's life mission and societal placement.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes qara to paint several vivid spiritual pictures:

• Sovereignty in Creation: God speaks and calls the elements of creation by name (Genesis 1:5). By naming the day, night, heaven, and earth, He establishes His absolute ownership and order over the cosmos.

• Covenantal Invitation: God calls His people out of bondage and summons them to Himself. This calling is not a mere suggestion but an authoritative, life-altering invitation into relationship.

• Prophetic Commissioning: God calls prophets by name (such as Moses or Samuel) to set them apart for divine service, imbuing their lives with a specific, unshakeable purpose.

### 4. Theological Implications

The act of calling a name in Scripture is rarely casual; it is deeply theological.

• The Reversal of Emptiness: When Naomi returned to Bethlehem, she demanded to be called Mara ("bitter") because she went out full and returned empty (Ruth 1:20-21). In Ruth 4:17, the calling of the child's name as "Obed" (meaning "servant" or "worshiper") represents God officially overriding Naomi's self-proclaimed bitterness. God replaces her self-applied name of sorrow with a God-given name of hope.

• Providential Lineage: God uses the neighbor women to call the child's name, signaling that this family's restoration is a corporate testimony of God's covenant faithfulness to Israel. Obed's name foreshadows his role as a servant to Naomi in her old age and, more importantly, a servant of Yahweh in the line of the Messiah.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:17, the use of qara is highly unusual because the neighbor women, rather than the parents (Boaz and Ruth), give the child his name. They declare, "There is a son born to Naomi," and they called his name Obed. This unusual naming by the community highlights that the redemption of Elimelech's estate is not merely a private family matter, but a public restoration of Israel's covenant inheritance. The neighborhood women serve as a prophetic choir, recognizing that God has filled Naomi's empty arms and provided a redeemer who will sustain her life and preserve the family name in Israel.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:5: "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night."

• Genesis 17:5: "No longer shall your name be called Abram, but your name shall be Abraham; for I have made you a father of many nations."

• Exodus 3:4: "So when the LORD saw that he turned aside to look, God called to him from the midst of the bush and said, 'Moses, Moses!' And he said, 'Here I am.'"

• Isaiah 43:1: "But now, thus says the LORD, who created you, O Jacob, And He who formed you, O Israel: 'Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; You are Mine.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The entire narrative of Ruth is built around the significance of names. "Elimelech" means my God is king, "Mahlon" means sickly, "Chilion" means pining, and "Naomi" means pleasant. The naming of "Obed" (qara in Ruth 4:17) acts as the resolution of the entire narrative's tension. The transition from death, barrenness, and grief to a named, living child demonstrates that God has the final word over our identities and our lineages.
