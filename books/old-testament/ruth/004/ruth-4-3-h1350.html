CLOSE RELATIVE - ( ga'al , Strong's H1350 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ga'al refers to the act of redeeming, delivering, or acting as a kinsman-redeemer. In the social and legal structure of ancient Israel, the goel (the active participle of ga'al) was the nearest male relative responsible for restoring the rights, property, and lineage of a deceased or impoverished family member. In Ruth 4:3, the word is translated as "close relative," referring to the man who held the primary right and responsibility to buy back Elimelech’s land and perpetuate his family line. This concept ultimately serves as a profound Old Testament picture of Jesus Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, who took on human flesh to redeem His people from their spiritual poverty and debt.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concept of ga'al is deeply rooted in the agrarian and tribal society of ancient Israel. Land was not merely private property; it was a sacred inheritance from God, distributed to the tribes and families of Israel. To prevent the permanent loss of this inheritance due to poverty, God established the law of the kinsman-redeemer in Leviticus 25. The closest male relative (goel) had the legal right and moral duty to step in and restore what had been lost. This included buying back land that had been sold, purchasing the freedom of a relative who had sold himself into slavery, avenging the blood of a murdered family member, and in the case of a deceased brother, marrying his childless widow to carry on the family name and inheritance (levirate marriage). The word picture is one of restoration, protection, and the preservation of a family's legacy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary Hebrew root ga'al means to redeem, buy back, ransom, or act as a kinsman. In its verbal form, it denotes the action of reclaiming or restoring something to its original owner or state. In its noun or active participle form (goel), it refers to the redeemer or next of kin. The semantic range of the word encompasses:

• Reclaiming property that was sold due to poverty.

• Delivering individuals from bondage or captivity.

• Executing justice on behalf of a slain relative.

• Fulfilling familial obligations to preserve the lineage and estate of a deceased relative.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The root occurs over 100 times in the Old Testament, functioning in both civil and theological contexts.

• Legal and Civil Usage: In Leviticus 25, the word describes the precise legal mechanisms for redeeming property and persons. It represents a safety net designed to protect the vulnerable and ensure that no family was permanently disinherited in Israel.

• Divine Redemption: The term is frequently applied to God Himself. God is portrayed as the ultimate Goel of Israel, who redeemed His people from the house of bondage in Egypt by a strong hand (Exodus 6:6) and who would later redeem them from the Babylonian exile (Isaiah 43:1).

• The Avenger of Blood: In Numbers 35, the relative who pursues a murderer to execute justice is called the goel haddam (the avenger of blood), depicting the redeemer as one who restores justice and moral order to the community.

4. Theological Implications

The kinsman-redeemer provides a powerful typological picture of the redemptive work of Jesus Christ. Under biblical law, a goel had to meet three specific qualifications to perform the redemption, all of which are perfectly fulfilled in Christ:

• He must be a close relative: Jesus took on human flesh, becoming our brother, so that He would have the legal right to redeem us (Hebrews 2:11-14).

• He must have the resources to pay the redemption price: The price of our redemption was not silver or gold, but the precious, sinless blood of Jesus Christ (1 Peter 1:18-19).

• He must be willing: Jesus voluntarily laid down His life; no one took it from Him, demonstrating His profound willingness to redeem His bride (John 10:17-18).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:3, Boaz convenes the elders of the city at the gate to address the matter of Naomi's land. He speaks directly to the unnamed "close relative" (goel), informing him that Naomi is selling the piece of land that belonged to their brother Elimelech.

The nuance here is critical: the land could not be sold to an outsider without devastating the family's legacy. While this anonymous relative is initially willing to buy the land as an investment, he refuses when Boaz reveals that the transaction requires marrying Ruth the Moabitess to raise up an heir for the deceased. The relative declines because doing so would jeopardize his own inheritance. This highlights the self-sacrificing nature of true redemption. Boaz, acting as a willing and able goel, gladly accepts the cost, demonstrating the covenant love (hesed) that characterizes the heart of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 25:25 - "If one of your brethren becomes poor, and has sold some of his possession, and if his redeeming relative comes to redeem it, then he may redeem what his brother sold."

• Job 19:25 - "For I know that my Redeemer lives, and He shall stand at last on the earth;"

• Psalm 19:14 - "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in Your sight, O Lord, my strength and my Redeemer."

• Isaiah 43:1 - "But now, thus says the Lord, who created you, O Jacob, and He who formed you, O Israel: 'Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; You are Mine.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful distinction exists between ga'al and padah, another Hebrew word translated as "redeem." While padah emphasizes the payment of a ransom price to transfer ownership from one party to another (such as the redemption of the firstborn in Numbers 18:15), it carries no requirement of kinship. In contrast, ga'al always implies a close family relationship. It is redemption based on kinship and covenant obligation.

Additionally, the anonymous kinsman in Ruth 4 is referred to in the Hebrew text as peloni almoni (translated as "such a one" or "so-and-so"). Because he refused to put his own inheritance at risk to save his brother's line, his name is forgotten in history. Boaz, who sacrificially stepped forward to act as the goel, is forever remembered in the genealogy of King David and the Lord Jesus Christ.
