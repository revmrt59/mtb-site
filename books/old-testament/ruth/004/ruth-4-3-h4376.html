SOLD - ( makar , Strong's H4376 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb makar means to sell, hand over, or surrender ownership of property, goods, or persons in exchange for a price. In the ancient Near East, selling land was not a permanent transfer of real estate as understood today, but rather a lease of the land's productivity until the Year of Jubilee. In Ruth 4:3, the term denotes Naomi's legal and economic vulnerability as she seeks to sell the land of her deceased husband, Elimelech, triggering the necessity of a kinsman-redeemer (goel) to keep the property within the family lineage and rescue the family from destitution.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, land was a divine inheritance, not private property to be liquidated at will. Under the Mosaic Covenant, God owned the land, and families held it as a perpetual stewardship. When a family fell into poverty, they might "sell" (makar) their land, which essentially meant selling the use or harvest of the land for a specific number of years. The word makar conveys the transition of possession, responsibility, and rights from one party to another in exchange for value. It carries an emotional weight of loss, displacement, and economic necessity, particularly when it involves ancestral inheritance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb makar primarily denotes the act of selling or giving up possession of something to another for a price.

• In the active voice (Qal stem), it refers to the literal selling of property, possessions, or individuals into servitude.

• In the passive voice (Niphal stem), it means to be sold or to sell oneself, often describing Israel selling themselves into the hands of their enemies through sin, or being sold into slavery.

• Figuratively, it possesses the meaning of handing over, abandoning, or surrendering someone into the power of another, often used of God surrendering His rebellious people to their oppressors.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Trade and Slavery: Joseph’s brothers sold him to the Ishmaelites (Genesis 37:28), illustrating a cold transaction of human life.

• Debt and Poverty: A poor Israelite might sell his possessions or even himself into servitude to pay off debts (Leviticus 25:25, 39).

• Divine Judgment: God is said to sell His people into the hands of their enemies when they turn to idolatry (Judges 2:14, 4:9), indicating a temporary withdrawal of His protection and ownership as a disciplinary measure.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of makar (selling) stands in direct antithesis to gaal (redeeming). To sell is to alienate and lose; to redeem is to reclaim and restore. The reality of makar highlights human dependency, frailty, and the consequences of a fallen world where inheritance can be lost. It underscores the grace of God’s covenantal laws (such as the Kinsman-Redeemer and Jubilee), which ensured that no "sale" (makar) of familial inheritance was permanent. God designed a system where what was lost or sold could always be recovered, pointing forward to the ultimate redemption of believers from the bondage of sin by Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:3, Boaz states, "Naomi... is selling the piece of land which belonged to our brother Elimelech." The use of makar here represents Naomi's extreme destitution. Returning from Moab as a childless widow, she has no means of cultivating or living off the land. Technically, she is selling the right to use the land. However, because she has no male heirs to inherit it, the land is in danger of permanently leaving Elimelech's family line. This "sale" acts as the legal catalyst for the entire climax of the narrative. It forces the nearest kinsman to make a decision: will he redeem the land and preserve Elimelech’s name, or will he decline, allowing Boaz to step forward as the redeemer?

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 37:28: "Then Midianite traders passed by; so they pulled Joseph up and lifted him out of the pit, and sold him to the Ishmaelites for twenty shekels of silver."

• Leviticus 25:25: "If one of your brethren becomes poor, and has sold some of his possession, and if his redeeming relative comes to redeem it, then he shall redeem what his brother sold."

• Deuteronomy 32:30: "How could one chase a thousand, And two put ten thousand to flight, Unless their Rock had sold them, And the LORD had surrendered them?"

• Judges 4:9: "So she said, 'I will surely go with you; nevertheless there will be no glory for you on the journey you are taking, for the LORD will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The term makar contrasts sharply with modern real estate transactions. In Israel, land was never sold in perpetuity because the ultimate owner of the land was Yahweh: "The land shall not be sold permanently, for the land is Mine" (Leviticus 25:23). Therefore, every makar of land carried an inherent promise and expectation of future redemption.
