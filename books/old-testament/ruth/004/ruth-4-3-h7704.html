COUNTRY - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open field, cultivated land, or a specific geographic territory or country. In Ruth 4:3, it designates the foreign "country" of Moab, representing the place of departure from the Promised Land where Elimelech's family sought refuge during a famine. It highlights the contrast between the wild, foreign fields of Moab and the cultivated, covenant-blessed fields of Bethlehem, marking Naomi's transition from exile back to the place of inheritance and redemption.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun sadeh evokes the vivid imagery of the ancient Near Eastern landscape, specifically the unfenced, open country. Unlike the walled cities (iyr) that represented security, human organization, and defense, the sadeh was the vast expanse where agricultural life took place. It was the realm of the farmer sowing seed, the shepherd grazing flocks, the wild beast roaming, and the soldier engaging in battle. In a broader geographical sense, when sadeh is paired with a proper name (such as Moab or Edom), it pictures the entire plateau, tableland, or regional territory belonging to that nation. It denotes a wilder, more expansive, and less domesticated territory than the immediate fields surrounding an Israelite village.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of sadeh is an open field, pastureland, cultivated ground, or defined territory. It represents:

• Open, uncultivated country where wild beasts live (Genesis 2:19).

• Cultivated land or agricultural fields owned by individuals for crops (Genesis 47:20).

• A specific territory, region, or land belonging to a particular nation or tribe, often translated as "country" (Genesis 32:3; Ruth 1:1).

• In Ruth 4:3, the word is used in the construct state (sedeh Moab) to mean "the country of Moab," designating the specific foreign plateau east of the Dead Sea where Naomi and her family lived during their exile.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sadeh is rich with figurative and narrative significance:

• The Place of Vulnerability: Because it is open and unguarded, the field is a place of exposure. Cain killed Abel when they were out in the sadeh (Genesis 4:8), illustrating a lack of societal protection.

• The Source of Sustenance: The sadeh is the source of life and provision. A neglected field brings poverty, while a cultivated field brings abundance (Proverbs 24:30-34).

• The Boundary of Inheritance: In Israel, a family's sadeh was their sacred heritage, allotted by God. It could not be sold permanently, as it ultimately belonged to Yahweh (Leviticus 25:23-24).

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, sadeh is intimately linked to the land of promise and covenant faithfulness. When Israel was faithful, God blessed their fields; when they rebelled, the fields became barren. Leaving the sadeh of Israel to seek food in the sadeh of Moab (Ruth 1:1) represents a failure of faith—leaving the place of God's covenant presence to find sustenance in a pagan land.

Furthermore, the redemption of the sadeh in Ruth 4 points to the restorative heart of God. The physical land of Israel could not be permanently lost to a family's line. The law of the kinsman-redeemer ensured that what was forfeited in a time of crisis and spiritual wandering could be bought back and restored to its rightful place within the covenant community.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:3, the word sadeh appears twice, creating a profound literary and spiritual contrast. First, Boaz mentions that Naomi has returned from the "country (sadeh) of Moab." Second, he refers to the "piece of land (chelqat hassadeh - portion of the field) which belonged to our brother Elimelech."

This dual usage highlights the journey of redemption. Naomi went to the sadeh of Moab in search of life, only to find death, emptiness, and the loss of her husband and sons. She returns to Bethlehem, destitute, but still possesses a claim to a sadeh of inheritance. The contrast is clear: the foreign "country" of Moab yielded only grave markers, but the covenant "field" of Elimelech in Bethlehem, once redeemed, will yield life, family, and the lineage of the Messiah. Naomi's journey is a physical and spiritual migration from the wild, foreign fields of compromise back to the secure, fruitful fields of covenant promise.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The legal transaction in Ruth 4:3 centers on the redemption of the field according to the laws found in Leviticus 25. If an Israelite became poor and had to sell his property, his nearest relative (goel) had the right and duty to buy it back. Boaz's willingness to redeem Elimelech's sadeh is not merely a financial transaction; it is a redemptive act that preserves the family name and inheritance. It reminds the believer that God is concerned with restoring every area of life that has been lost or devastated by previous seasons of spiritual famine and wandering.
