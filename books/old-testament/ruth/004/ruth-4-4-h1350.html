REDEEM - ( ga'al , Strong's H1350 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ga'al refers to the act of reclaiming, recovering, or buying back a relative or their property to restore them to their original status. Rooted in familial covenant obligations, it describes the duties of a kinsman-redeemer (goel) who steps in to rescue a destitute family member from poverty, slavery, or the loss of ancestral land. In the book of Ruth, and specifically in Ruth 4:4, the word represents the legally binding and deeply personal act of purchasing Naomi's land and marrying Ruth to preserve the family lineage, serving as a profound Old Testament picture of the ultimate redemption provided by Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The cultural background of ga'al is rooted in the ancient Near Eastern laws of kinship. In the agrarian society of ancient Israel, land was a tribal inheritance given by God that was never to be permanently sold or lost. If an Israelite fell into severe poverty and had to sell his ancestral property or even sell himself into slavery to pay off debts, he lost his independence and his legacy.

To prevent the permanent collapse of a family unit, God instituted the law of the goel (the active participle of ga'al, translated as "kinsman-redeemer"). The goel was the nearest male relative who possessed the legal right, the financial capability, and the moral responsibility to step in and buy back what his impoverished relative had lost. This legal safety net preserved family lines, protected vulnerable widows, and ensured that land remained within its designated tribe.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ga'al means to redeem, deliver, rescue, buy back, or act as a kinsman. It denotes the release of a person, property, or inheritance through the payment of an equivalent price by a close relative.

While other Hebrew words for buying or purchasing focus on the commercial transaction itself, ga'al is fundamentally relational. It cannot be separated from the concept of family and covenant. The redeemer must be a blood relative. Therefore, ga'al carries the dual nuance of paying a price and fulfilling a familial duty of protection and restoration.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, the verb ga'al and its derivative noun goel are used in several distinct legal and social contexts:

• Property Redemption: Purchasing a poor relative's sold land to keep it within the family tribe (Leviticus 25:25).

• Personal Redemption: Ransoming a relative who had sold himself into slavery to a resident foreigner due to extreme debt (Leviticus 25:47-49).

• Levirate Marriage: Marrying the childless widow of a deceased brother or close relative to carry on his name and preserve his inheritance (Deuteronomy 25:5-10; Ruth 4).

• The Avenger of Blood: Serving as the goel haddam (avenger of blood) to execute justice and protect the family's honor when a relative was murdered (Numbers 35:19).

• Divine Deliverance: Describing God as the Kinsman-Redeemer of Israel, who rescues His people from Egypt and Babylon not merely as a sovereign Lord, but as a relational Father and Husband (Exodus 6:6; Isaiah 43:1).

### 4. Theological Implications

The theology of ga'al is rich with Messianic significance, pointing directly to the person and work of Jesus Christ. For a redemption to take place under biblical law, three specific criteria had to be met:

• The Right to Redeem: The redeemer had to be a close relative (kinsman). Jesus met this requirement by the Incarnation, becoming a man, taking on our flesh and blood, and qualifying as our Brother (Hebrews 2:14-17).

• The Power to Redeem: The redeemer had to possess the financial resources to pay the redemption price. Humanity was bankrupt in sin, but Jesus possessed infinite spiritual wealth, paying our debt not with silver or gold, but with His own precious blood (1 Peter 1:18-19).

• The Will to Redeem: The redeemer could not be forced; he had to act willingly out of love. Jesus demonstrated this willingness by laying down His life voluntarily for His bride, the Church (John 10:17-18; Ephesians 5:25).

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:4, the word ga'al is used repeatedly in a formal legal transaction at the city gate of Bethlehem. Boaz confronts a closer, anonymous relative (referred to as "Mr. So-and-So" or "such a one") in front of ten elders. Boaz states: "If you will redeem (ga'al) it, redeem (ga'al) it; but if you will not redeem (ga'al) it, then tell me, that I may know; for there is no one but you to redeem (ga'al) it, and I am after you."

The rapid, rhythmic repetition of the word highlights the legal tension of the moment. Initially, the unnamed relative is eager to redeem the land because it will increase his personal estate. However, when Boaz reveals that the redemption of the land also includes marrying Ruth the Moabite to raise up an heir for the deceased Mahlon, the man immediately backs out. He admits that doing so would jeopardize his own inheritance (Ruth 4:6).

This highlights the deep contextual nuance of the word: true ga'al is not a selfish business transaction. It requires self-sacrificial love. The closer relative represents the law—unable and unwilling to redeem at a personal cost. Boaz represents grace—willingly absorbing the cost and the vulnerability to rescue Ruth and Naomi, establishing the lineage of King David and, ultimately, Jesus Christ.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful linguistic contrast exists between the Old Testament Hebrew concept of redemption (ga'al) and the New Testament Greek concepts. New Testament Greek uses words like agorazo (to purchase in the slave market) and lutroo (to release by paying a ransom price). While the Greek terms focus heavily on the payment of the price and the liberation of the captive, the Hebrew ga'al adds the beautiful layer of family restoration. To be redeemed by a goel means not only that your debt has been paid, but that you have been brought back home into the family of God.
