ELDERS - ( zaqen , Strong's H2205 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the "elders" (zaqen) were recognized leaders who possessed wisdom, authority, and responsibility within the community, rather than simply older individuals. In Ruth 4:4, they serve as official witnesses and legal authorities at the city gate, validating Boaz's redemption of Naomi's land and his marriage to Ruth. This term highlights the communal, legal, and orderly nature of redemption within God's covenant community.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root zaqen is closely associated with the growing of a beard or getting old. In the ancient Near East, a beard was a symbol of maturity, dignity, and wisdom. Thus, the noun shifted from a physical description of aging to an official designation of leadership. The elders were the seasoned heads of families and clans who gathered at the city gate—the administrative, commercial, and judicial hub of ancient Israelite towns. They represented the collective wisdom, history, and moral authority of the community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word refers primarily to an old man, but in administrative and legal contexts, it denotes an elder, senator, or community leader. The plural form (zeqenim) designates a formal body of local leaders who exercise judicial functions, witness legal transactions, resolve civil disputes, and maintain social order. In Ruth 4:4, the word signifies the ten specific local leaders Boaz assembled to witness and legitimize the transaction of the kinsman-redeemer (goel).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses zaqen in several key ways:

• Physical Age: Describing individuals advanced in years, representing life experience and longevity.

• Representative Leadership: The official leadership body representing the entire nation of Israel before God, Moses, and subsequent rulers.

• Judicial Authority: Local magistrates who sat at the city gates to hear cases, protect the vulnerable, and execute justice.

The word picture is that of a protective canopy of wisdom over the community, safeguarding justice, preserving covenant integrity, and ensuring adherence to the Law of Moses.

4. Theological Implications

The role of the zeqenim underscores that God's covenant life is deeply communal and orderly. Redemption is not a private, secret transaction; it is a public, legally binding act witnessed and celebrated by the community's representatives. The presence of the elders ensures that the vulnerable (like Ruth and Naomi) are protected and that the land remains within its designated family line. This demonstrates God's faithfulness to His covenant promises through structured, human leadership.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:4, Boaz takes his seat at the gate and gathers ten men of the "elders of the city." By involving the elders, Boaz elevates his personal desire to redeem Ruth into a formal, legal covenant. The elders serve as impartial witnesses to the closer relative's refusal of his redemption rights and Boaz's public commitment to acquire the land and marry Ruth. Their presence guarantees that Boaz's redemption of the estate and his marriage to the Moabite widow are beyond legal or social reproach, establishing a legitimate lineage that ultimately leads to King David and Jesus Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 21:19: "then his father and his mother shall take hold of him and bring him out to the elders of his city, to the gate of his city."

• Joshua 20:4: "And when he flees to one of those cities, and stands at the entrance of the gate of the city, and declares his case in the hearing of the elders of that city..."

• Proverbs 31:23: "Her husband is known in the gates, / When he sits among the elders of the land."

• Lamentations 5:14: "The elders have ceased from the gate, / The young men from their music."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The selection of exactly "ten men" by Boaz was not arbitrary. Ten was the traditional quorum required to establish a local court or a formal assembly in Jewish custom. This ensured the legal validity of the transaction under customary law. The elders' eventual blessing in Ruth 4:11–12 acts as a prophetic validation of the messianic line, comparing Ruth to Rachel and Leah, and anticipating the house of Perez.
