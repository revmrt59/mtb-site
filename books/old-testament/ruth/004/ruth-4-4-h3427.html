INHABITANTS - ( yashab , Strong's H3427 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yashab fundamentally means to sit, dwell, remain, or abide. In the context of Ruth 4:4, translated as "inhabitants" in the NKJV, the term refers specifically to those who are "sitting" as official witnesses at the city gate. This word bridges the everyday reality of dwelling in a community with the formal, legal transactions of ancient Israelite society, highlighting the communal accountability and public nature of redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root yashab is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over 1,000 times. Its concrete, physical meaning is "to sit down," such as sitting on a chair, a throne, or the ground.

• From this physical posture of sitting, the word naturally expanded to denote "dwelling," "residing," or "living" in a place permanently.

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, the city gate was the hub of legal, social, and commercial life. The elders and community leaders did not stand; they sat (yashab) in the gate to conduct business, settle disputes, and witness transactions. Therefore, "those sitting" (hayyoshebim) carried a double meaning: they were the local residents (inhabitants) and, more formally, the active participants or witnesses in a legal assembly.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• To sit, sit down, or remain seated.

• To dwell, inhabit, or live in a specific locality (a house, city, or land) as a permanent resident.

• To abide, stay, or remain in a particular state or place.

• As an active participle (yosheb), it functions as a noun meaning "inhabitant," "resident," or "one who sits."

• In legal and administrative contexts, it refers to those who sit in council or assembly, serving as official observers, judges, or witnesses.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Seat of Authority: Sitting is a posture of rest, authority, and judgment. Kings sit on thrones, and judges sit on the bench. When yashab is used of the community at the gate, it portrays a structured, orderly society where elders sit to preserve justice.

• Dwelling in Covenant Security: The term is frequently used of God "dwelling" among His people or the people "dwelling" safely in the Promised Land. This conveys stability, peace, and permanent relationship.

• Sojourning vs. Dwelling: Unlike gur (to sojourn or dwell temporarily as a stranger), yashab implies permanence, settled ownership, and integration into the community, which is highly relevant to the land redemption laws in Ruth.

4. Theological Implications

• God’s Dwelling with Man: The ultimate expression of yashab is Yahweh dwelling among His people. Covenant faithfulness ensures that Israel can yashab (dwell) in the land securely.

• Public Integrity and Community: Redemption in Israel was never a private, secret affair. By involving the "inhabitants" (those sitting at the gate), God’s law ensured that transactions regarding land and family legacy were done with transparency, consensus, and communal accountability.

• The Goel (Kinsman-Redeemer): The redemption of Ruth and the land required a legal witness to validate the transfer. The "sitting" witnesses represent the community's endorsement of Boaz's righteous act, showing that redemption restores not just individuals, but the whole community.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:4, Boaz tells the closer relative: "Buy it back in the presence of the inhabitants and the elders of my people."

• The "inhabitants" here are not just random citizens walking by, but the specific men sitting in the public square/gate of Bethlehem. Boaz uses their physical posture of sitting (yashab) to establish them as a formal cloud of witnesses.

• By bringing this matter before those "sitting" at the gate, Boaz elevates a private family crisis (Naomi's poverty and Elimelech's lost land) to a matter of public covenant justice. The presence of these inhabitants prevents any future disputes and seals the legitimacy of the redemption.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 13:12: "Abram dwelt in the land of Canaan, and Lot dwelt in the cities of the plain and pitched his tent even as far as Sodom."

• Genesis 19:1: "Now the two angels came to Sodom in the evening, and Lot was sitting in the gate of Sodom."

• Psalm 1:1: "Blessed is the man / Who walks not in the counsel of the ungodly, / Nor stands in the path of sinners, / Nor sits in the seat of the scornful;"

• Psalm 23:6: "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me / All the days of my life; / And I will dwell in the house of the Lord / Forever."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• There is a subtle literary contrast in the Book of Ruth regarding this root. In Ruth 1:1, Elimelech's family went to "sojourn" (gur) in Moab, but they ended up "remaining" (yashab) there (Ruth 1:4), which led to tragedy and loss of inheritance. However, when Naomi and Ruth return to Bethlehem, the "sitting" (yashab) of the elders at the gate signifies a return to covenant order, stability, and the legal restoration of their place in the land.
