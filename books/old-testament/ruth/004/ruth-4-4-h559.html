THOUGHT - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

In Ruth 4:4, the word translated "thought" is the Hebrew verb 'amar, which fundamentally means "to say" or "to speak." Within this narrative context, the word signifies Boaz's internal speech, reflecting his conscious intention, deliberate purpose, and resolved plan to present the legal matter of land redemption to his relative. It bridges his inner integrity and his outer, righteous actions.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• In ancient Hebrew thought, thinking and speaking are not distinct, isolated cognitive functions. To think is to hold an internal conversation; thus, "to think" is frequently expressed as "to say in one's heart."

• The word 'amar represents the fluid transition between internal deliberation and external proclamation. It suggests that actions do not occur in a vacuum but are the direct result of a processed, spoken resolve within the soul.

• Before Boaz speaks to the closer relative at the town gate, he has already processed the moral, legal, and relational implications of redeeming Ruth and Naomi. His "thought" is an internal speech that has matured into a firm intent.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The Hebrew verb 'amar is a primary root occurring over 5,000 times in the Old Testament. Its primary semantic range includes "to speak," "to say," "to declare," "to command," or "to intend."

• While most frequently translated as "said" or "spoke" to denote verbalized communication, it carries the specific nuance of "thought," "intended," or "resolved" when expressing an internal monologue or purpose.

• In Ruth 4:4, the grammatical form is the first-person singular Qal perfect ('amarti), which literally translates as "I said" or "I resolved." Boaz's statement, "I thought to inform you," conveys a settled decision made prior to the conversation, demonstrating foresight and structured preparation rather than a spontaneous impulse.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• 'Amar functions as a broad vehicle for communication: verbal declaration, internal reasoning, and authoritative decree.

• Divine Decree: God "speaks" creation into existence (Genesis 1:3), where His word carries immediate, creative power.

• Internal Monologue: Characters "say in their heart," indicating their deepest, sometimes hidden, motivations and beliefs (such as Esau's intent in Genesis 27:41 or the fool's denial in Psalm 14:1).

• Determined Purpose: It denotes a planned course of action or a firm intention. When a person uses 'amar in this way, they are declaring a decision that has already been weighed and finalized in their mind. In Ruth 4:4, Boaz's "thought" functions as a formal proposal based on this determined purpose.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of 'amar to describe Boaz's thought processes highlights the character of a righteous redeemer. In Scripture, God's thoughts and words are always unified; He speaks what He intends, and He performs what He speaks. Boaz models this divine integrity on a human level.

• His "thought" to inform his relative is not manipulative or self-seeking, but transparent and aligned with the covenant law of God. This reflects the biblical truth that a righteous man's thoughts are governed by God's word, leading to actions that bring life, order, and redemption to the vulnerable.

• It also showcases God's providence operating through the careful, deliberate thinking of godly leadership.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In the context of Ruth 4, Boaz is executing a carefully planned legal strategy at the gate of Bethlehem. He must navigate the legal rights of a closer redeemer (goel) while aiming to secure the redemption of Naomi's land and the hand of Ruth.

• When Boaz tells the relative, "I thought to inform you," he uses the Hebrew idiom 'amarti egleh ozneka, literally meaning, "I said, I will uncover your ear."

• This word picture shows that Boaz's inner resolve ("I thought") directly leads to the intimate sharing of crucial legal information ("to uncover your ear"). His thought was not a passive observation but an active, structured plan to bring hidden matters into the light of day for covenant justice.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 20:11 - "And Abraham said, 'Because I thought, surely the fear of God is not in this place; and they will kill me on account of my wife.'"

• 2 Kings 5:11 - "But Naaman became angry, and went away and said, 'Indeed, I thought, "He will surely come out to me, and stand and call on the name of the Lord his God, and wave his hand over the place, and heal the leprosy."'"

• Psalm 14:1 - "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God.' They are corrupt, They have done abominable works, There is none who does good."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between the "thought" of Boaz and the subsequent reaction of the unnamed closer relative is highly instructive.

• Boaz’s thought was focused on covenant loyalty (hesed) and the preservation of Elimelech's name, regardless of the personal cost.

• The closer relative, upon hearing the full scope of the proposal, immediately changes his mind because it threatens his own inheritance (Ruth 4:6). This reveals that true redemption requires a resolved mind willing to embrace sacrifice, a quality Boaz possessed from the moment he "thought" to bring the matter forward.
