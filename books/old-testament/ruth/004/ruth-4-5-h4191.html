DEAD - ( mût , Strong's H4191 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb mût means to die, perish, or be put to death. In Ruth 4:5, it appears as a substantive participle referring to Mahlon, the deceased husband of Ruth. Within the ancient Israelite covenant context, "the dead" represents more than physical demise; it points to the tragic potential of a family line and its land inheritance being permanently erased. By marrying Ruth, Boaz undertakes the role of the redeemer to "perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance," illustrating God's covenant loyalty (hesed) that overcomes the finality of death to restore life, hope, and legacy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• In ancient Israel, land was not a mere commodity to be bought and sold for profit; it was a permanent covenantal inheritance granted by God to each family. When a man died (mût) without leaving a male heir, his family line faced extinction, and his ancestral land risked permanently passing out of his family's possession.

• To prevent this tragedy, the law of Moses and family customs provided the institution of the kinsman-redeemer (goel) and the levirate-like marriage. The closest male relative was expected to buy back the family land and marry the widow. The firstborn son of this new union would legally carry the name of the deceased husband, ensuring that his memory and portion of the land remained secure in Israel.

• The "dead" (mêt), therefore, refers to the deceased family member whose legal and spiritual presence in Israel was threatened with total deletion unless a living relative stepped forward to act as a savior.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The root verb mût occurs over 800 times in the Old Testament, spanning several grammatical stems:

• In the Qal stem, the verb means to die, expire, or perish (Genesis 25:8).

• In the Hiphil stem, it means to cause to die, slay, or put to death (Numbers 14:15).

• In the Hophal stem, it refers to being put to death or executed under judicial authority (Exodus 21:12).

• In Ruth 4:5, the word is used as a masculine singular active participle functioning as a noun (mêt), meaning "the dead one" or "the deceased." It refers specifically to Mahlon, Naomi's deceased son and Ruth's late husband.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The physical reality: The word describes the natural end of earthly life. It is the return of the physical body to the dust of the earth, as seen in the accounts of the patriarchs who lived, fathered children, and died.

• The judicial reality: Mût is frequently doubled in legal texts (môt yûmāt, literally "dying he shall die" or "shall surely be put to death") to emphasize the absolute certainty and necessity of capital punishment for capital crimes.

• The social and legal entity: In genealogical and inheritance contexts, the "dead" still possesses legal identity. The law of Israel treated the deceased husband as if he still had a claim to his family property, waiting for his name to be revived and attached to that land through a living redeemer.

4. Theological Implications

• Redemption over Death: The kinsman-redeemer provision reveals that God does not abandon the dead to oblivion. It showcases a God who provides a legal and relational way to overcome the finality of physical death, bringing restoration, inheritance, and hope out of a hopeless situation.

• Covenant Faithfulness (Hesed): Boaz's willingness to marry Ruth to raise up the name of the dead is a supreme expression of selfless, sacrificial love. He was willing to spend his own wealth to secure an inheritance that would legally belong to the lineage of the dead man rather than his own personal estate.

• Messianic Typology: This redemption of the dead's inheritance points directly to Jesus Christ. We were spiritually dead, bankrupt, and unable to preserve our inheritance. Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, took on flesh, paid the ultimate price, and redeemed us so that we might receive an eternal inheritance and a new name.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:5, Boaz confronts the closer relative (the unnamed peloni almoni) with the terms of the redemption. The relative was eager to acquire Elimelech's land, but Boaz reveals the catch: "On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance."

• The double mention of "the dead" in this single verse highlights the heavy legal and moral obligation involved. It was not a simple real estate transaction. It required a sacrifice of self-interest.

• The closer relative immediately backs out because raising up an heir for "the dead" would "jeopardize" his own inheritance. He wanted the property for his own legacy, whereas Boaz was willing to lose his own immediate legal primacy to restore the legacy of the dead.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:17: "...but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die."

• Deuteronomy 25:5: "If brothers dwell together, and one of them dies and has no son, the widow of the dead man shall not be married to a stranger outside the family; her husband’s brother shall go in to her, take her as his wife, and perform the duty of a husband’s brother to her."

• Ruth 1:5: "Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband."

• Ruth 1:17: "Where you die, I will die, and there will I be buried. The LORD do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between the two relatives in Ruth 4 is stark. The closer relative seeks to protect his own name and wealth from the threat of "the dead," while Boaz embraces the risk, spending his own resources to honor the dead.

• Ironically, because the closer relative refused to redeem the dead man's name to save his own inheritance, his own name is forgotten in Scripture (he is referred to only as "Friend" or "such a one"). Meanwhile, Boaz, who sacrificed his name for the dead, is immortalized in the genealogy of King David and Jesus Christ.
