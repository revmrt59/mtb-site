INHERITANCE - ( nachalah , Strong's H5159 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun nachalah refers to a permanent possession, heritage, or property handed down through generations, most notably the land allotted to the tribes of Israel by Yahweh. In the context of Ruth 4:5, it denotes the ancestral estate of the deceased Elimelech and Mahlon, which Boaz seeks to redeem. This word highlights the sacred connection between family line, land ownership, and divine covenant, demonstrating that inheritance in Israel was not merely about material wealth but about preserving a family's name and portion within God's covenant community.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, land was not a commodity to be bought and sold on a speculative market. It was a sacred trust. God was the ultimate owner of the land, and He apportioned it to the tribes and families as an enduring possession. This permanent family estate is the nachalah. The word is derived from the root nachal, meaning to inherit, occupy, or take possession. The cultural background of nachalah involves the Jubilee and kinsman-redeemer laws, designed to prevent the permanent loss of family land and the extinction of a family line. If a man died without an heir, his land risked passing to another clan, fracturing the tribal allotment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun nachalah refers to a possession, property, inheritance, or share. It carries the primary sense of an inherited portion that is legally and permanently secured. It is distinct from a temporary possession or a purchase, representing a heritage that is to pass from ancestors to descendants in perpetuity. While it often refers to physical land, it can also describe Israel as God’s personal inheritance, or God Himself as the inheritance of the Levites.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• nachalah is used over 220 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in Numbers, Joshua, and Deuteronomy, where the focus is the division of the Promised Land.

• The Word Picture of Tribal Allotments: In Joshua, the borders of each tribe's nachalah are meticulously detailed, showing that every family had a specific, God-given space where they belonged.

• The Word Picture of Israel as God's Nachalah: God frequently calls the nation of Israel His "inheritance" (Deuteronomy 4:20), picturing a deep, treasured, and permanent relationship where God claims His people as His private, valued possession.

• The Word Picture of the Levites: The Levites received no physical land allocation; instead, God Himself was their inheritance (Numbers 18:20). Their portion was spiritual and relational, sustained by the offerings of the people.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenantal Faithfulness: Nachalah represents God’s faithfulness to His promises. The land given to Abraham's descendants was a physical realization of the covenant.

• Redemption and Security: The preservation of the nachalah shows God's care for the vulnerable (widows, the poor). The kinsman-redeemer acts to restore what was lost, which points typologically to Christ redeeming our lost inheritance.

• Eschatological Hope: The concept of a secure, eternal inheritance shifts in the New Testament to a spiritual inheritance kept in heaven for believers, guaranteed by the Holy Spirit.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:5, Boaz confronts the closer relative with the dual responsibility of redeeming Elimelech's land and marrying Ruth the Moabitess. The purpose of this marriage is to "perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance (nachalah)." The legal-theological tension here is vital: the closer relative was willing to buy the land (adding to his own estate), but when he realized he must also marry Ruth to raise up an heir for the deceased, he declined. If he did so, his own financial estate would be jeopardized because the purchased land would eventually belong to Ruth's son (carrying Mahlon's name), not his own heirs. Here, nachalah represents the intersection of family lineage and physical land. To lose the land was to lose the tangible place of the family in Israel's history; to lose the name was to be blotted out. Boaz's willingness to redeem both the land and the family line demonstrates sacrificial love and covenant faithfulness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 18:20 - "Then the LORD said to Aaron: 'You shall have no inheritance in their land, nor shall you have any portion among them; I am your portion and your inheritance among the children of Israel.'"

• Deuteronomy 4:20 - "But the LORD has taken you and brought you out of the iron furnace, out of Egypt, to be His people, an inheritance, as you are this day."

• Psalm 16:6 - "The lines have fallen to me in pleasant places; Yes, I have a good inheritance."

• Proverbs 13:22 - "A good man leaves an inheritance to his children's children, But the wealth of the sinner is stored up for the righteous."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In contrast with modern real estate, which is an asset to be liquidated for profit, nachalah is non-transferable outside the tribe. It represents stewardship rather than absolute ownership. Naboth’s refusal to sell his vineyard to King Ahab in 1 Kings 21:3 ("The LORD forbid that I should give the inheritance of my fathers to you!") illustrates the sacredness of the nachalah. This illuminates why Boaz's act was so monumental: he did not buy the land for his own gain, but to preserve the nachalah for another man's name.
