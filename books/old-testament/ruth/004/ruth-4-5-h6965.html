PERPETUATE - ( qum , Strong's H6965 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qum in its basic Qal stem means "to arise, stand up, or rise." However, in the Hiphil (causative) stem used in Ruth 4:5, it carries the active meaning of "to cause to stand," "to erect," "to establish," or "to raise up." In the context of levirate marriage, "to perpetuate" (NKJV) signifies the legal and familial act of raising up the name and lineage of a deceased relative so that his memory, household, and inheritance do not perish from Israel. It represents a profound commitment to selflessness, covenant faithfulness, and the preservation of a brother's legacy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root qum is a highly common and versatile Hebrew verb, appearing over 600 times in the Old Testament. In its physical sense, it depicts the action of moving from a prostrate or seated position to an upright, standing posture. It is the word used when a person stands up out of respect, rises to battle, or stands to testify. In the ancient Near East, "standing" was also associated with durability, validation, and permanence. What is "raised up" is established to endure, while what falls or remains prostrate ceases to exist. In the cultural context of Ruth, "perpetuating" or "raising up" the name of the deceased through levirate marriage meant ensuring that a family line which had "fallen" through death was physically and legally set back on its feet.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In the Hiphil stem, qum transitions from "to rise" to "to cause to rise" or "to establish." The semantic range includes:

• To erect or set up a physical object (such as an altar, pillar, or tent).

• To establish, ratify, or confirm a covenant, oath, or promise.

• To raise up or appoint a leader, prophet, or deliverer.

• To restore, rebuild, or re-establish what has been ruined or torn down.

• To perpetuate or preserve a family line and inheritance by providing legal heirs.

In Ruth 4:5, the Hiphil infinitive construct lehaqim specifically means "to cause to stand" or "to establish" the name of the deceased. It expresses the purpose of the redemption marriage: to ensure that Elimelech and Mahlon’s names continue to exist in the tribal records and genealogical lands of Judah.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The physical picture of resurrection and restoration: Just as a collapsed tent is raised up to stand upright again, a family lineage threatened with extinction is "raised up" from the dust of death.

• The covenantal picture of establishing an oath: When God "establishes" His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, or David, He makes it stand firm and secure. Similarly, Boaz’s act of perpetuating Mahlon’s name is an act of establishing covenant love (chesed) within the community.

• The legal picture of standing in court: To make a claim "stand" is to validate it. Perpetuating the name validates the inheritance rights of the family line, ensuring that the property remains within the designated tribe and family clan.

4. Theological Implications

The use of qum in the Book of Ruth highlights several critical theological themes:

• Covenant Faithfulness (Chesed): Perpetuating the name of a deceased relative required great sacrifice. The redeemer had to expend his own resources to purchase the land, but the firstborn son of the union would inherit that land under the deceased father's name, not the redeemer's. This acts as a vivid picture of Christ’s self-sacrificing love, who redeemed us at His own cost to establish our eternal inheritance.

• Redemption and Resurrection: The theological movement from death to life, from falling to rising, is embedded in qum. Ruth, a widowed Moabitess, and Naomi, a grieving mother, are brought from a state of emptiness and death to a state of fullness and life. The raising up of the family line prefigures the ultimate "raising up" of the Davidic line, culminating in the Messiah.

• God’s Providential Preservation: God uses human obedience and sacrificial love to preserve His people and fulfill His promises. Through Boaz's willingness to "perpetuate" the name of the dead, God secures the lineage of King David and, ultimately, Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:5, Boaz presents a dual requirement to the closer relative (the goel): purchasing the field from Naomi also requires marrying Ruth the Moabitess to "perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance." The closer relative was willing to acquire the land to increase his own wealth, but he balked at the requirement to "raise up" the name of the deceased. To do so would mean that the land he bought would eventually belong to Ruth’s son (who would legally be counted as Mahlon's heir), thereby "ruining" his own inheritance (Ruth 4:6). Boaz, however, willingly embraces this financial and personal sacrifice. For Boaz, "perpetuating" the name is not a burden but an act of supreme devotion and covenant love.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:18: "But I will establish My covenant with you; and you shall go into the ark—you, your sons, your wife, and your sons’ wives with you."

• Deuteronomy 25:7: "But if the man does not want to take his brother’s wife, then let his brother’s wife go up to the gate to the elders, and say, ‘My husband’s brother refuses to raise up a name to his brother in Israel; he will not perform the duty of my husband’s brother.’"

• Deuteronomy 28:9: "The Lord will establish you as a holy people to Himself, just as He has sworn to you, if you keep the commandments of the Lord your God and walk in His ways."

• Ruth 4:10: "Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from his position at the gate. You are witnesses this day."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The refusal of the closer relative to "perpetuate" the name of the dead stands in stark contrast to the character of Boaz. The anonymous relative is remembered only as Ploni Almoni ("Mr. So-and-So" or "such a one" in Ruth 4:1), his own name being lost to history because he refused to protect the name of his deceased kinsman. In contrast, Boaz, who selflessly sought to "perpetuate" Mahlon's name, is eternally honored in the genealogy of Jesus Christ. This illustrates a profound biblical paradox: those who seek to save their own name and inheritance often lose it, while those who sacrifice their own interests for the sake of others are established forever by God.
