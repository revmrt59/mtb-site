FIELD - ( sadeh , Strong's 7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open land, cultivated field, plot of ground, or territory. In the ancient Near East, a family's sadeh (field) was not merely real estate or a financial asset; it was a sacred ancestral inheritance (nachalah) given by God to be preserved through generations. In the Book of Ruth, the "field" of Elimelech represents the physical link to the family's legacy, identity, and survival, serving as the stage for redemption and the preservation of a family line that ultimately leads to King David and Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient agrarian culture of Israel, sadeh described the open country, distinct from the walled city (iyr). It was the place of daily labor, sowing, reaping, and pasture. Unlike the wilderness (midbar), which was wild and untamed, the sadeh was cultivated, representing human stewardship and divine blessing. To own a field was to have a place of security, fruitfulness, and covenant belonging. In the book of Ruth, the field is the primary setting where the drama of providence unfolds—first as the place where Ruth gleans under the protection of Boaz, and finally as the legal transaction that secures the family's future.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Sadeh denotes open fields, arable land, a specific plot of ground, or a broader territory.

• It refers to land that can be tilled for crops, such as Boaz's barley and wheat fields.

• It designates pastureland for livestock.

• It can refer to a specific parcel of land owned by an individual or family (as in the field of Elimelech).

• It can describe the broader territory of a nation, such as the "country [field] of Moab."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Field of Providence: The field is a place of divine orchestration. When Ruth goes to glean, she "happens" to light upon the portion of the field belonging to Boaz. What seems like chance to human eyes is the direct guidance of God within the boundaries of the sadeh.

• The Field of Covenant Mercy: Biblical law required landowners not to reap the very corners of their sadeh, leaving the gleanings for the poor, the widow, and the stranger (Leviticus 19:9-10). The field thus becomes a physical manifestation of God's grace and social welfare system.

• The Field of Inheritance: A family's field could not be permanently sold. If poverty forced a sale, the kinsman-redeemer (goel) had the right and duty to buy it back, keeping the land within the tribe and family.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of the sadeh is deeply rooted in the Land Covenant. God is the ultimate owner of the land ("The land is Mine; for you are strangers and sojourners with Me," Leviticus 25:23). Israelite families were stewards of their assigned plots.

• Redemption of the Land: The redemption of Elimelech's sadeh in Ruth 4 illustrates the biblical principle of restoration. God does not want His people to lose their inheritance permanently.

• The Link Between Land and Seed: In the biblical economy, land and posterity are closely bound. The redemption of the field was useless without the continuation of the family name. Therefore, redeeming the field of Elimelech required the redeemer to marry Ruth to raise up heirs for the deceased.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:5, Boaz addresses the closer relative at the city gate: "On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance."

• Here, the sadeh of Elimelech is the catalyst for the legal transaction. The closer relative is eager to acquire the sadeh because it represents an expansion of his own wealth and estate.

• Boaz, however, introduces the covenantal contingency: the field cannot be separated from the family. To redeem the sadeh is to assume responsibility for the widow Ruth.

• This requirement exposes the heart of the closer relative, who values property over people. He declines the redemption because marrying Ruth and fathering a child would split his own inheritance. Boaz steps forward, demonstrating true, sacrificial love by redeeming both the sadeh (the land) and the bride (Ruth).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

7. Other Relevant Insights

The redemption of the field in Ruth 4 provides a beautiful Old Testament type of Christ's redemptive work. Just as Boaz purchased the field to redeem a bride and restore a lost inheritance, Jesus Christ entered the "field" of this world (cf. Matthew 13:44) to redeem His bride, the Church, and restore our lost eternal inheritance. Boaz's redemption was not merely a financial transaction; it was an act of covenant love that restored hope, life, and legacy to a family that seemed completely cut off.
