REDEEM - ( ga'al , Strong's H1350 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ga'al carries the rich legal and familial concept of acting as a "kinsman-redeemer." It refers to the act of delivering or reclaiming a person, property, or inheritance that has been lost, enslaved, or alienated, by paying a price or fulfilling a legal obligation. In the ancient near eastern context, and specifically in the book of Ruth, ga'al describes the duty of the nearest male relative to step in and rescue a vulnerable family member from destitution, preserving the family line and estate. In Ruth 4:6, the closer relative declines this role because of the financial cost and impact on his own inheritance, setting the stage for Boaz to step forward as the ultimate historical picture of redemption, which ultimately points to Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient near eastern agricultural society of Israel, the family land was a sacred trust given by God, intended to remain within the family tribe from generation to generation. If a family member fell into severe poverty, they might be forced to sell their land or even sell themselves into indentured servitude to survive. The role of the kinsman-redeemer (goel, derived from ga'al) was established by God as a social safety net to prevent the permanent loss of family inheritance and the erasure of a family's name from Israel. The redeemer acted not out of personal gain, but out of covenant responsibility and family solidarity, restoring what was broken and reclaiming what had been lost.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ga'al means to redeem, buy back, or rescue. It denotes the act of reclaiming a person, property, or right by paying a required price or fulfilling a legal obligation. The term carries a deeply relational undertone; it is not a mere commercial transaction. It refers specifically to redemption carried out by a close relative who has a rightful claim and duty to intervene. To perform the act of ga'al, three components must be present: the redeemer must possess the legal right of kinship, the financial means to pay the redemption price, and the willing heart to assume the burden of the needy relative.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, ga'al is used to paint a vivid picture of restoration. In the Pentateuch, it describes the legal process of purchasing back field property or freeing a brother from slavery. In the prophets, particularly Isaiah, the word shifts from a human kinsman to Yahweh Himself, who is depicted as the ultimate Goel of Israel, rescuing His captive people from Babylon and redeeming them from bondage. In Job, the word is used as a cry of hope amidst profound suffering, viewing the redeemer as a living advocate who will ultimately stand on the earth to vindicate the sufferer. The word picture is that of a hand reaching down to pull a captive out of a pit of debt and placing them back on their own secure territory.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of ga'al provides the foundational Old Testament imagery for the New Testament doctrine of redemption. It reveals God's heart for the vulnerable, the displaced, and the bankrupt. It serves as a profound messianic type. Jesus Christ fulfilled the role of our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer. He became our close relative through the Incarnation, possessed the infinite wealth of his own sinless life to pay our debt, and willingly laid down His life to restore our lost spiritual inheritance. The theological arc of ga'al travels from the civil laws of Israel to the historical redemption of Ruth, and finally to the cosmic redemption of believers through the blood of Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:6, the word ga'al appears three times in a single sentence, highlighting the legal tension and the pivot of the narrative. When the nameless closer relative is informed that redeeming Elimelech's land also requires him to take Ruth the Moabitess as his wife to raise up an heir for the deceased, he flatly refuses. He states, "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance." His refusal illustrates that redemption is costly. It requires a willingness to jeopardize one's own comfort, wealth, and name for the sake of another. The closer relative represents a self-protective spirit that values personal inheritance over covenant love, contrasting sharply with Boaz, who joyfully embraces the risk and the cost of redemption.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The ancient custom associated with the transfer of redemption rights involved removing a sandal and giving it to the other party (Ruth 4:7-8). This public act symbolized that the closer relative was stepping off the land and relinquishing his right to walk upon it as owner, transferring that legal right entirely to Boaz. Furthermore, there is a powerful spiritual irony in the text: the anonymous relative who refused to redeem out of fear of ruining his own name and inheritance is left completely nameless in the biblical record, known only as "friend" (Ploni Almoni). Meanwhile, Boaz, who sacrificed his own immediate interests to redeem Ruth, is honored throughout history as a key ancestor in the genealogy of King David and the Lord Jesus Christ.
