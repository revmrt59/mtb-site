REDEMPTION - ( geullah , Strong's H1353 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun geullah refers to the right, option, or price of redemption, as well as the actual redemption of family property or persons. Deeply embedded in Israel’s covenant law, this term represents God’s gracious provision to prevent the permanent loss of ancestral land and to rescue family members from poverty and bondage. In Ruth 4:6, geullah designates the legal right of the next of kin to buy back the estate of the deceased Elimelech. When the closest relative abdicates this right to protect his own inheritance, he clears the way for Boaz to step forward as the kinsman-redeemer, beautifully foreshadowing the ultimate redemptive work of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, land was not merely real estate; it was a sacred inheritance granted by Yahweh to each tribe and family, never to be permanently sold. However, extreme poverty could force a family to sell their ancestral fields or even sell themselves into indentured service. To preserve the family line and keep the land within the tribe, God instituted the law of redemption. The geullah represents the legal mechanism of restoration. It paints a picture of a safety net woven from familial responsibility and covenant love, ensuring that no ruin was permanent and no poverty was without hope of restoration.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Geullah is a feminine noun derived from the verb gaal, which means "to redeem" or "to act as a kinsman." The noun specifically denotes:

• The right of redemption (the legal option belonging to the nearest relative to buy back property).

• The price of redemption (the actual financial cost calculated to reclaim what was lost).

• The kin group or family relationship that creates the obligation to redeem.

While the verb gaal emphasizes the action of rescuing or reclaiming, the noun geullah focuses on the legal standing, authority, and means by which that rescue is secured.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The concept of geullah is prominently detailed in Leviticus 25, where God lays down the laws of the Sabbatical Year and the Jubilee.

• It is used as a legal right of reclamation that remains active even after a sale has occurred.

• It serves as a visual demonstration of grace. The law required that a buyer must always allow the original owner or their closest relative the opportunity to buy the property back.

• It represents the restoration of identity and heritage. Without geullah, a family’s name and place in Israel could be erased forever.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of geullah points directly to the person and work of Jesus Christ:

• The Necessity of Kinship: Just as the right of redemption (geullah) belonged only to a near relative, our Redeemer had to become one of us. Jesus took on human flesh to become our close relative so that He would possess the legal right to redeem us.

• The Cost of Redemption: Redemption is never free; it requires a price. Christ did not redeem us with corruptible things like silver or gold, but with His own precious blood.

• The Restoration of Inheritance: Through our spiritual bankruptcy, we lost our inheritance and fell into the bondage of sin. Christ’s act of redemption restores our lost inheritance, granting us eternal life and fellowship with God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:6, the closer relative (the goel who remains anonymous) is presented with the opportunity to buy Elimelech’s land. However, Boaz informs him that acquiring the land also requires marrying Ruth the Moabitess to raise up an heir for the deceased Mahlon. This addition changes the financial and familial equation. The anonymous relative realizes that doing so would jeopardize his own estate and split his inheritance among too many heirs.

Consequently, he declares, "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance. You redeem my right of redemption (geullah) for yourself, for I cannot redeem it." Here, geullah refers to his legal priority and option to buy. By transferring his geullah to Boaz, he legally relinquishes his claim, opening the door for Boaz to demonstrate a self-sacrificing, covenant love (hesed) that values the preservation of Elimelech's line above his own personal wealth.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 25:24 - "And in all the land of your possession you shall grant redemption for the land."

• Leviticus 25:29 - "If a man sells a house in a walled city, then he may redeem it within a whole year after it is sold; within a full year he may have the right of redemption."

• Jeremiah 32:7 - "Behold, Hanamel the son of Shallum your uncle will come to you, saying, 'Buy my field which is in Anathoth, for the right of redemption is yours to buy it.'"

• Jeremiah 32:8 - "Then Hanamel my uncle’s son came to me in the court of the prison according to the word of the Lord, and said to me, ‘Please buy my field that is in Anathoth, which is in the country of Benjamin; for the right of inheritance is yours, and the redemption is yours; buy it for yourself.’ Then I knew that this was the word of the Lord."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast of Hearts: The contrast between the anonymous relative and Boaz is stark. The anonymous relative is concerned with his own legacy and financial security; Boaz is willing to absorb the cost, the social stigma, and the risk to his own estate to redeem Ruth and Naomi. This illustrates that having the geullah (the right to redeem) is useless without the willingness to redeem.

• The Legal Seal: The transaction of transferring the geullah is finalized by the custom of removing a sandal (Ruth 4:7-8). This served as a public, legally binding sign that the right to walk upon and claim the land was officially transferred to Boaz.
