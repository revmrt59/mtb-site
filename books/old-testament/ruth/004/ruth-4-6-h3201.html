CANNOT - ( yakol , Strong's H3201 )

Summary:

In Ruth 4:6, the word translated "cannot" captures more than a mere physical or logical impossibility; it denotes a lack of capability, legal right, or moral willingness due to the costly consequences involved. By declaring "I cannot," the unnamed kinsman reveals the boundary of his resources and his heart, paving the way for Boaz—the willing and able redeemer—to step forward as a type of Christ, who is both able and willing to redeem us.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew verb yakol primarily carries the idea of capacity, power, or ability to perform a task. It describes the capacity to endure, prevail, or overcome. When combined with the negative particle lo ("not"), as seen in Ruth 4:6 (lo ukal), it indicates being powerless, incapacitated, or finding oneself in a position where an action is rendered impossible due to external constraints, internal limitations, or legal and financial barriers.

In the Ancient Near Eastern legal and cultural context of land redemption and levirate marriage, "ability" was not just a matter of physical strength. It was a measurement of financial solvency, legal eligibility, and the willingness to absorb familial and economic risk. To say "I cannot" in a legal assembly at the city gate was a formal declaration of bankruptcy regarding one's capacity or willingness to fulfill a specific covenant obligation.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yakol is a common Hebrew verb meaning to be able, to have power, to be capable of, to prevail, or to overcome. With the negative particle lo, it signifies inability, powerlessness, or prohibition. The semantic range of this root spans several distinct categories of inability:

• Physical capacity: lacking the bodily strength or sensory function to do something.

• Legal or moral capacity: being restricted by law, covenant, or conscience from taking an action.

• Financial capability: lacking the capital, property, or resources required to complete a transaction.

In Ruth 4:6, the verb expresses a conditional, self-imposed inability. The kinsman possesses the legal standing to redeem, but he lacks the financial and familial capacity to do so without jeopardizing his own estate. His "cannot" is a confession of his limits; his self-interest places a hard boundary on his ability.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yakol functions like a scale that measures capacity. To declare lo yakol is to admit that the demands of a situation outweigh one's available resources.

• It is used of a flooded river that cannot be crossed, where the sheer volume of water overcomes human strength.

• It is used of a burden that cannot be borne, where the weight crushes the bearer.

• It is used of an enemy that cannot be overcome, where the military disparity guarantees defeat.

In a legal setting, yakol represents the boundary line of covenant responsibility. When the nearest kinsman uses this word in Ruth 4:6, it serves as a verbal surrender of his priority right of redemption. He draws a circle around his own inheritance and declares that his capacity stops at that line.

### 4. Theological Implications

The contrast between human limitation (lo yakol) and divine sufficiency is a central theme in Scripture. Humanity frequently runs into the wall of "cannot"—we cannot save ourselves, we cannot redeem our lost inheritance, and we cannot keep the covenant perfectly.

This passage presents a profound type of the Law and the Gospel. The nearest kinsman represents the Law or human effort. He is legally qualified to redeem, but when the full cost of redemption is revealed—requiring him to take a foreign bride and raise up an inheritance for another—he must confess, "I cannot." The Law, weak through the flesh, cannot save us (Romans 8:3).

This makes way for Boaz, a type of Jesus Christ. Boaz does not say "I cannot." He is the redeemer who is both fully able and deeply willing. He does not fear "marring" his inheritance; instead, Christ willingly emptied Himself, poured out His life, and endured the cross to purchase His Bride, the Church. Where human institutions and strength fail, the true Redeemer prevails.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:4, the unnamed kinsman initially agreed to buy the field of Elimelech, saying, "I will redeem it." He had the financial capacity to add land to his holdings. However, in Ruth 4:5, Boaz reveals the full scope of the transaction: "On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance."

This disclosure changes the kinsman’s math. Raising up an heir for the deceased Mahlon meant that the land he purchased would eventually belong to Ruth's son, not to his own existing heirs. Furthermore, he would have to support Ruth and Naomi out of his own estate in the meantime. The kinsman's "cannot" in verse 6 is driven by self-preservation. It is not that he literally lacked a single shekel, but that he could not perform this redemption without damaging his own family’s economic future. His "cannot" is ultimately a failure of sacrificial love.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 13:16: "And I will make your descendants as the dust of the earth; so that if a man can [yakol] number the dust of the earth, then your descendants also may be numbered."

• Exodus 18:18: "Both you and these people who are with you will surely wear yourselves out. For this thing is too much for you; you are not able [lo-tukal, root yakol] to perform it by yourself."

• Joshua 24:19: "But Joshua said to the people, 'You cannot [lo-tuklu, root yakol] serve the Lord, for He is a holy God. He is a jealous God; He will not forgive your transgressions nor your sins.'"

• Psalm 139:6: "Such knowledge is too wonderful for me; It is high, I cannot [lo-ukal, root yakol] attain it."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The narrative highlights a stark irony regarding the unnamed kinsman's desire to protect his inheritance. In Hebrew, he is referred to anonymously as peloni almoni, translated as "close relative" or "friend" (literally, "Mr. So-and-So"). Because he was utterly consumed with protecting his own name, legacy, and inheritance, God's providence left him nameless in the eternal pages of Scripture.

Conversely, Boaz, who was willing to risk his own inheritance and wealth to redeem a destitute Moabitess, has his name immortalized forever in the genealogy of King David and the Lord Jesus Christ. Self-preservation led the first kinsman to obscurity, while self-sacrificial love led Boaz to eternal honor.
