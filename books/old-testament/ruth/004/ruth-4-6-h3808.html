CANNOT - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew adverb lo' is the primary particle of absolute negation, which, when joined with the verb yakol ("to be able"), translates as "cannot." In Ruth 4:6, this construction establishes a definitive, objective barrier of inability and refusal. The anonymous close relative uses this term to declare his legal and financial incapacity to perform the duty of the kinsman-redeemer without jeopardizing his own estate. This declaration of "cannot" serves as a critical literary and theological turning point, exposing the limitations of human legalism and setting the stage for Boaz to step forward as the willing and able redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The Hebrew language employs two main negative particles: 'al and lo'. The particle 'al represents subjective, temporary, or injunctive negation (expressing a wish, request, or command, such as "do not"). In contrast, lo' represents objective, absolute, and unconditional negation. It describes an immutable fact or an enduring state of affairs.

• In ancient Near Eastern legal and transactional language, combining lo' with the verb yakol ("to be able") served as a formal, binding disclaimer of capability or right. When a party uttered lo' 'ûḵal ("I cannot"), it was not a mere expression of difficulty or preference; it was a formal declaration of legal and physical insolvency regarding a specific obligation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The adverb lo' expresses absolute negation, meaning "no" or "not."

• When governing the imperfect verb yakol (H3201), the phrase literally means "not able" or "lacking the capacity to prevail."

• In the context of Ruth 4:6, the double occurrence of lo' 'ûḵal ("I cannot redeem... for I cannot redeem") emphasizes an absolute barrier. The speaker does not merely face a difficult task; he is legally and practically blocked from acting due to the prohibitive personal cost that would ruin his own inheritance.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• A Legal Wall of Impossibility: The kinsman-redeemer (goel) had to meet three criteria to redeem land and carry on a deceased relative's name: he had to be a near kinsman, he had to have the financial means, and he had to be willing. The particle lo' acts as an absolute barrier to the third criterion. The closer relative has the family tie and the money, but he lacks the willingness because marrying Ruth would compromise his own children's inheritance. His "cannot" is a moral and financial impossibility.

• Double Negation for Emphasis: The repetition of "cannot" in verse 6 reinforces the finality of his decision. He formally hands over his right of redemption to Boaz, signaling that the door is shut for him but open for another.

4. Theological Implications

• The Inability of the First Redeemer: The anonymous kinsman represents the nearest human option, which historically and typologically represents the Law. The Law has the first right over humanity, yet it "cannot" redeem because it is weak through human self-interest and fleshly limitation.

• The Necessity of a Better Redeemer: Where the first kinsman declares "I cannot," Boaz represents grace and the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ. Christ is the Redeemer who is not only legally related and financially capable (possessing all riches) but is supremely willing to absorb the cost of redemption, even to the point of laying down His life, without fear of losing His inheritance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:4, the relative initially agreed to redeem the land, saying, "I will redeem it." He was eager to acquire Elimelech's property. However, when Boaz adds the condition in verse 5—that acquiring the land requires marrying Ruth the Moabitess to raise up the name of the dead—the relative immediately reverses his decision in verse 6, saying, "I cannot redeem it."

• The transition from "I will" to "I cannot" shows that the introduction of the Gentile outsider, Ruth, exposed the self-interest of his heart. The "cannot" is not a physical inability to pay, but a refusal to allow his own family name and estate to be diluted.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 43:32: "So they set him a place by himself, and them by themselves, and the Egyptians who ate with him by themselves; because the Egyptians could not eat food with the Hebrews, for that is an abomination to the Egyptians."

• Joshua 24:19: "But Joshua said to the people, 'You cannot serve the LORD, for He is a holy God. He is a jealous God; He will not forgive your transgressions nor your sins.'"

• 1 Kings 13:16: "And he said, 'I cannot return with you nor go in with you; neither can I eat bread nor drink water with you in this place.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The literary irony of Ruth 4 is highlighted by this "cannot." The kinsman who was so protective of his own inheritance that he refused to redeem Ruth is left completely nameless in the biblical text (referred to only as Ploni Almoni, or "such a one"). In trying to preserve his name, he lost it. Boaz, who was willing to jeopardize his estate by taking in the Moabite widow, is immortalized in the genealogy of King David and the Messiah.
