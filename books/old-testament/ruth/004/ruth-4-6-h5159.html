INHERITANCE - ( nachalah , Strong's H5159 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun nachalah refers to a hereditary property, possession, or portion of land passed down through generations. Rooted in the covenantal distribution of the Promised Land to the tribes of Israel, it represents far more than mere real estate; it is a permanent, divinely allotted stewardship that must be protected. In the book of Ruth, nachalah highlights the legal and spiritual responsibility of preserving a family's legacy. When the close relative refuses to redeem Elimelech's land because it might "ruin" his own nachalah, the text contrasts his self-preserving fear with Boaz’s sacrificial, covenant love.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, land ownership was fundamentally different from modern real estate. The land belonged to Yahweh, and the families of Israel were His tenants. When the Promised Land was conquered under Joshua, it was divided among the tribes and families as a nachalah—a permanent, inalienable inheritance. It was never to be sold permanently; if poverty forced a family to sell their land, it was to be redeemed by a close relative (goel) or returned in the Year of Jubilee. The word nachalah carries the deep theological weight of God’s faithfulness, family continuity, and covenant security. To lose one’s nachalah was to be cut off from the visible inheritance of God’s people.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun nachalah designates a possession, property, inheritance, portion, or share. It is derived from the verbal root nachal, meaning to inherit, take possession, or bequeath.

Lexically, the term represents:

• Ancestral land holdings handed down through lineage.

• A permanent possession that cannot be legally or permanently alienated from the family.

• Metaphorically, a person's portion or destiny given by God.

• Spiritually, Israel as God's special possession or inheritance among the nations.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs nachalah in several rich ways:

• The Tribal Allotments: The physical geography of Canaan divided among the tribes is repeatedly called their nachalah. This land was their tangible share of God's covenant promise.

• The Levites' Portion: The tribe of Levi received no physical land allotment. Instead, Yahweh Himself—and the offerings made to Him—was their nachalah.

• God’s Heritage: Israel is frequently called Yahweh's nachalah, expressing His deep, personal ownership of and commitment to His people.

• The Heritage of the Righteous: Children are described as a nachalah (heritage) from Yahweh, emphasizing that family is a gift of divine stewardship rather than human achievement.

### 4. Theological Implications

The concept of nachalah connects directly to God’s sovereign grace and covenant faithfulness. He assigns the boundaries of our lives and provides a secure heritage.

Furthermore, nachalah is inextricably linked to redemption (geullah). Because the nachalah belonged to God and was entrusted to specific families, the kinsman-redeemer's duty was to keep the land within the family name. This system of land redemption and Levirate marriage ensured that the poor and vulnerable were cared for, and that no family's name or inheritance was blotted out of Israel. This foreshadows the believer's secure, eternal inheritance in Christ, which is kept and guaranteed by the Holy Spirit.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:6, the unnamed close relative (the goel) states, "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance (nachalah)."

The nuance here is intensely practical and serves as a foil to Boaz's character. The relative was willing to acquire Elimelech's land to expand his own holdings. However, when he learned that he must also marry Ruth the Moabite to raise up an heir for the deceased Mahlon, his perspective shifted. By law, the firstborn son of that union would inherit the redeemed land under Mahlon's name, not his own.

The relative realized that:

• He would have to pay for the land out of his own pocket.

• The land would ultimately go to Ruth's future son, leaving the redeemer with less capital for his own biological children.

• His own family estate (nachalah) would be financially depleted and divided.

Thus, he chose to protect his temporary, earthly nachalah at the expense of covenant love (chesed) and family duty. This highlights the cost of redemption. Boaz, by contrast, willingly risked his own nachalah out of love, demonstrating the selfless spirit of the true Redeemer.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 18:20: "Then the LORD said to Aaron: 'You shall have no inheritance in their land, nor shall you have any portion among them; I am your portion and your inheritance among the children of Israel.'"

• Deuteronomy 4:20: "But the LORD has taken you and brought you out of the iron furnace, out of Egypt, to be His people, an inheritance, as you are this day."

• Joshua 13:14: "Only to the tribe of Levi he had given no inheritance; the sacrifices of the LORD God of Israel made by fire are their inheritance, as He said to them."

• Psalm 127:3: "Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, The fruit of the womb is a reward."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The tragic irony of the close relative's decision is preserved in the literary structure of Ruth. He refused to redeem the land because he wanted to preserve his own name and nachalah. Yet, Scripture does not even record his name; he is referred to only as Ploni Almoni (translated as "friend" or "such a one" in Ruth 4:1). By trying to save his earthly inheritance, he lost his name in sacred history. Boaz, who was willing to jeopardize his own nachalah for the sake of the vulnerable, was blessed with a son named Obed, becoming the ancestor of King David and Jesus Christ.
