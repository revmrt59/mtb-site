RUIN - ( shachath , Strong's H7843 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shachath means to spoil, ruin, destroy, or corrupt. In the context of Ruth 4:6, the nearer kinsman declines to redeem Elimelech's land because marrying Ruth, a Moabite widow, would "ruin" or jeopardize his own estate and inheritance. This term highlights the tension between self-preservation and sacrificial love, contrasting the nameless kinsman's fear of material loss with Boaz's willing, redemptive generosity.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root shachath carries a physical, moral, and legal sense of decay, ruin, or corruption.

• In concrete and agricultural contexts, it refers to a field or vineyard being ruined, a vessel marred in the hands of a potter, or food rotting.

• In covenantal and legal contexts, it depicts the spoiling of a relationship, a lineage, or an estate.

• The word picture is one of complete loss of utility, value, or beauty. What was once whole, productive, and valuable is rendered useless, broken, or depleted.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb is a primary root meaning to decay, ruin, corrupt, or utterly destroy.

• In the Hiphil stem (as used in Ruth 4:6), it means to cause ruin, to spoil, to devastate, or to waste. It describes an action that diminishes value, dismantles structure, or renders something unfit for its original purpose.

• In legal and financial terms, it means to compromise, dilute, or dissipate an inheritance or estate.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical destruction: The verb describes God destroying the earth in the Great Flood, or the physical obliteration of cities like Sodom and Gomorrah.

• Moral corruption: It depicts humanity corrupting its way before God, turning away from moral uprightness into spiritual decay.

• Practical waste: It illustrates a potter's clay vessel being spoiled or marred while on the wheel, requiring the potter to reshape it.

• In Ruth 4:6, the word is used metaphorically for financial and genealogical dilution. The kinsman fears that integrating Ruth's family and future children into his household will drain his financial resources and confuse his family's inheritance lines.

4. Theological Implications

• This term exposes the heart of fallen humanity versus the heart of true redemption. The unnamed kinsman views covenant redemption through the lens of self-interest; to him, helping a vulnerable widow is a threat that will ruin his own legacy.

• It contrasts sharply with the character of God, who enters into our ruined state to redeem us.

• Boaz, as a type of Christ, does not fear the ruin of his own estate. He willingly absorbs the cost, showing that true redemption requires sacrifice, self-giving, and a willingness to lay down one's own rights and resources for the sake of another.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:6, the closer kinsman states, "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance." Under levirate law, the first son born to Ruth would legally carry the name and inherit the estate of her deceased husband, Mahlon, not the redeemer.

• The kinsman's ruin is twofold: First, he would have to spend his own wealth to buy back Elimelech's land, but that land would ultimately go to Mahlon's heir, not his own heirs. Second, if he had only one son with Ruth, his own name and estate could be absorbed into Elimelech's line.

• His refusal because of the fear of ruin sets the stage for Boaz's heroic and selfless love, emphasizing that redemption is never free; it always costs the redeemer something significant.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:12: "So God looked upon the earth, and indeed it was corrupt; for all flesh had corrupted (shachath) its way on the earth."

• Genesis 19:13: "For we will destroy this place, because the outcry against them has grown great before the face of the Lord, and the Lord has sent us to destroy (shachath) it."

• Exodus 12:23: "For the Lord will pass through to strike the Egyptians... and will not allow the destroyer (shachath) to come into your houses to strike you."

• Jeremiah 18:4: "And the vessel that he made of clay was marred (shachath) in the hand of the potter; so he made it again into another vessel..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The unnamed kinsman is referred to in Hebrew as peloni almoni ("Mr. So-and-So"). By refusing to redeem Ruth for fear of ruining his own name and inheritance, he suffered the ultimate irony: his name was forgotten and lost to history, whereas Boaz, who risked his inheritance, is eternally remembered in the genealogy of Jesus Christ.

• This illustrates the spiritual principle of Matthew 16:25: whoever desires to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for Christ's sake will find it.
