REDEEMING - ( geullah , Strong's H1353 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun geullah refers to the right, price, or act of redemption. Rooted in the family laws of ancient Israel, it represents the legal and moral mechanism for restoring lost inheritance, land, or freedom to a vulnerable relative. In Ruth 4:7, geullah designates the formal legal process of securing family land and lineage, serving as a vivid picture of restoration, covenant faithfulness, and the ultimate work of Christ in reclaiming what humanity lost to sin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the agrarian society of ancient Israel, land was not merely private real estate; it was an ancestral inheritance granted by God to specific tribes and families. If a man fell into severe poverty, he might be forced to sell his land or even sell himself into servitude to survive. To prevent the permanent loss of this heritage, God instituted the law of redemption. The noun geullah represents the legal mechanism that kept the inheritance within the family. It was the duty of the closest relative, the goel (kinsman-redeemer), to step forward, pay the necessary redemption price, and buy back what was lost. The word picture is one of restoration: stepping into a situation of loss, debt, and vulnerability, and legally establishing safety, ownership, and inheritance once again.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun geullah designates the legal right of redemption, the price paid for redemption, or the state of being redeemed. It is derived from the root verb ga'al, which means to redeem, act as a kinsman, or deliver from bondage. In the legal framework of Israel, geullah is the formal option or obligation resting on the nearest male relative to purchase back ancestral land or family members who had fallen into poverty or servitude. It implies a legal transaction where a price is paid to release an object or person from a foreign claim, returning them to their rightful place.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The term is primarily used in legal contexts, particularly in Leviticus 25, which details the regulations of the Year of Jubilee and the preservation of ancestral property.

• It paints a picture of active interposition. When an individual is entirely helpless to recover their lost estate, a qualified and willing relative steps into the gap, pays the required valuation, and restores the relative's standing and security.

• In Ruth 4:7, geullah is paired with "exchanging" (temurah), depicting a complete, legally binding transfer of rights and possessions. The visual aid of passing a sandal symbolized that the seller was stepping off the property and transferring the right to walk upon and possess that land to the redeemer.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Preservation: God designed geullah to ensure that no family in Israel was permanently disinherited. It highlights God's deep commitment to preserving the inheritance of His covenant people.

• Grace and Mercy: The redeemer operates out of grace and family solidarity, taking on a financial burden that the debtor could never repay.

• Typology of Christ: The entire system of geullah points directly to the person and work of Jesus Christ. As our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, Jesus became our near relative through the Incarnation, possessed the infinite wealth required to pay our sin debt, and willingly gave His life to buy us back and restore our lost eternal inheritance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:7, the custom of geullah is described as an ancient, honorable practice used to confirm legal transactions in Israel.

• The focus is on the legal transfer of this right of redemption. The anonymous "closer relative," who had the first legal right to redeem Naomi's land, declined because marrying Ruth to raise up an heir for the deceased Mahlon would jeopardize his own inheritance.

• Boaz steps forward to exercise the geullah. He does not bypass legal order; instead, he ensures the transfer is public, validated by ten elders at the city gate. This demonstrates that true biblical redemption is legally perfect, transparent, and motivated by sacrificial love.

Then I knew that this was the word of the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Sandal Custom: In ancient times, taking off a sandal and handing it to another was a recognized legal symbol. Because land was claimed by walking its boundaries, handing over the sandal was a physical declaration of relinquishing one's right to step on and possess that land, transferring that authority entirely to the redeemer.

• Public Accountability: The transaction took place at the city gate in front of witnesses. This shows that the process of geullah was not a private deal done in the dark, but a covenantal act celebrated and validated by the community.
