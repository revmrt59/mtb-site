ISRAEL - ( Yisra'el , Strong's H3478 )

Summary:

• Yisra'el (Israel) means "struggles with God" or "God strives," originating from the historic renaming of the patriarch Jacob after his night-long wrestling match at Peniel. In the Old Testament, the name transitions from an individual designation to a corporate identity, representing the covenant descendants of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. In the book of Ruth, particularly in Ruth 4:7, "Israel" represents the community bound by God’s covenant law, serving as the cultural and spiritual backdrop where redemption, familial responsibility, and divine providence are legally and publicly enacted.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yisra'el is a compound of the verb sarah (to persist, exert oneself, struggle, or rule) and the noun 'el (God). Its origin is rooted in Genesis 32:28, where God renames Jacob after he wrestled with the Angel of the Lord. Instead of the name Jacob (meaning "supplanter" or "deceiver"), he receives a name of honor signifying that he has struggled with God and with men and has prevailed.

Historically, this personal name expanded to define his twelve sons and the subsequent tribes that formed the nation of Israel. In the ancient Near East, where nations were defined by their regional deities, Israel was uniquely defined by its relationship with Yahweh. In the context of the book of Ruth, "Israel" represents more than a geographic territory; it is a sanctified community governed by the Torah, setting it apart from the pagan practices of neighboring Moab.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun Yisra'el means "he strives with God" or "God rules." Lexically, the word operates in three primary categories across the Old Testament:

• The Patriarchal Name: The personal name given to Jacob at Peniel, representing his transformed character and covenant standing.

• The Covenant Community: The collective designation for the twelve tribes descended from Jacob, bound by the Mosaic Covenant.

• The Geopolitical Nation: The physical land and sovereign state, which later experienced a division into the Northern Kingdom (Israel) and the Southern Kingdom (Judah).

In Ruth 4:7, the term functions in the second category. It denotes the covenant community of Yahweh, highlighting the distinctive legal, civil, and moral obligations shared by those who belong to God's people.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, Israel is described using vivid word pictures that illustrate the nation's relationship with God:

• The Vineyard: Planted, tended, and protected by Yahweh to produce spiritual fruit.

• The Firstborn Son: Adopted out of Egypt, carrying the family name, inheritance rights, and the responsibility to represent the Father.

• The Bride: Betrothed to Yahweh in a sacred, exclusive covenant of love and fidelity.

In the book of Ruth, Israel is presented as a community of redemption. The word picture here is a civil and spiritual family tree. When the text speaks of customs "in Israel," it paints a picture of a society where legal transactions are not merely cold business deals, but sacred duties performed in the sight of God and the community to preserve the names and inheritances of the deceased.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of the word "Israel" in Ruth 4:7 carries profound covenantal weight:

• The Rule of Covenant Law: The customs of redeeming (ge'ullah) and exchanging property were not arbitrary cultural traditions; they were rooted in the Levitical law given by God to preserve family lines and land heritages.

• The Scope of Redemption: Ruth, a Moabitess, is brought into "Israel." Her inclusion demonstrates that the covenant boundaries of Israel, while ethnically rooted, were spiritually permeable to those who put their faith in the God of Israel.

• The Messianic Lineage: The legal transaction taking place "in Israel" at the gate of Bethlehem ultimately secures the lineage of King David, and ultimately, Jesus Christ. God uses the civil laws of Israel to bring forth the Savior of the world.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:7, the narrator notes: "Now this was the custom in former times in Israel concerning redeeming and exchanging, to confirm anything: one man took off his shoe and gave it to the other, and this was a confirmation in Israel."

The double emphasis on "in Israel" serves a specific purpose. Writing from a later historical vantage point (likely during the monarchy), the author explains an ancient legal custom to the readers. By framing this custom as something unique to "Israel," the text contrasts the righteous, orderly, and protective laws of Yahweh's people with the chaotic or exploitative practices of pagan nations. The act of removing the shoe was a public, binding testimony of relinquishing one's right to walk upon and claim a piece of land, allowing the kinsman-redeemer (Boaz) to legally step in and redeem both the land and Ruth.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast of Geographies: The journey in the book of Ruth begins with a departure from Israel (Bethlehem) to the fields of Moab due to a famine, resulting in death and emptiness. The return to Israel (Bethlehem) coincides with the harvest, symbolizing a return to God's presence, provision, and life.

• The Spiritual Lesson of the Shoe: In Israel, land ownership was a stewardship from God. Passing the shoe was a physical manifestation of yielding one's step and right of ownership to another. It showed that legal matters in Israel were to be handled with transparency, integrity, and public accountability.
