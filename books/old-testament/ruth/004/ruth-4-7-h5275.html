SANDAL - ( na'al , Strong's H5275 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun na'al refers to a sandal or shoe, typically consisting of a leather or wooden sole bound to the foot with leather straps. In the culture of ancient Israel, the sandal was not only a practical piece of footwear but also a potent legal and symbolic instrument. Removing and handing over a sandal in a public transaction, as depicted in Ruth 4:7, represented the formal, voluntary transfer of property rights, inheritance, or the right of redemption from one party to another. It served as a visual, legally binding confirmation of a completed covenant transaction.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun na'al is derived from the primitive root verb na'al (Strong's H5274), which means "to bar, lock, bolt, or close up," as well as "to bind on sandals." The core word picture of a sandal is something that is bound or fastened to the sole of the foot to protect it from the rough, dusty terrain of the ancient Near East.

Because sandals made direct contact with the ground, they accumulated the dirt and dust of the earth. Consequently, they were removed when entering holy spaces to avoid defiling a sacred area with the dust of the common world. In legal and territorial contexts, walking upon a piece of land with one's sandals symbolized claiming legal possession and dominion over that territory. To step onto a piece of property was to assert ownership. Therefore, removing one's sandal and handing it to another person was a highly physical, symbolic way of stating, "I am stepping off this property and handing my right to tread upon it—and therefore my ownership of it—over to you."

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun refers specifically to a sandal, shoe, or footwear. It denotes the standard footwear worn by men and women, consisting of a simple sole tied with thongs or straps.

The semantic range of na'al includes:

• Ordinary Footwear: Standard gear worn for travel, daily work, or military campaigns, representing preparedness.

• A Token of Low Value: A symbol of something of very little monetary worth or an insignificant bribe.

• A Legal Instrument: A symbolic object used to validate contracts, land transfers, or the relinquishing of legal rights.

• An Emblem of Subjugation: A sign of dominance, where casting a sandal over a territory represents claiming or conquering it.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Legal Exchange: In ancient Near Eastern transactions, land was closely tied to family identity and ancestral inheritance. Because there were no paper deeds, transactions required public, visible actions. Handing over a sandal was the ancient equivalent of signing a deed of transfer. It visually demonstrated that the seller was stepping out of his legal position and allowing the buyer to step into it.

• The Rite of Humiliation: Under the Levirate marriage law outlined in Deuteronomy 25, if a man refused to marry his deceased brother's widow to carry on his name, the widow would publicly pull the sandal off his foot and spit in his face. His household would then be mockingly called "The house of him who had his sandal removed." This was a forced removal of the sandal, symbolizing public shame and a failure of family duty.

• Insignificant Value: Prophets used the sandal to illustrate extreme societal corruption. Selling the needy "for a pair of sandals" described a corrupt justice system where the poor were sold into slavery for the price of cheap, common footwear.

### 4. Theological Implications

• The Grace of the True Kinsman-Redeemer: In Ruth 4, the unnamed closer relative was willing to buy the land of Elimelech but unwilling to redeem Ruth, fearing he would jeopardize his own inheritance. By handing his sandal to Boaz, he legally transferred his right of redemption. Boaz eagerly accepted the sandal, gladly taking on the financial cost and societal responsibility of redeeming Ruth. This points directly to Jesus Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer. Where the law and human self-interest fail to redeem us, Christ steps in. He did not shrink from the immense cost of our redemption but took on our debt to purchase His bride, the Church.

• The Legality of Divine Redemption: The orderly exchange of the sandal at the city gate shows that God's plan of redemption is not arbitrary; it is legally secure, righteous, and fully validated. God ensures that the redemption of His people is legally binding, leaving no room for future claims or condemnation by the accuser.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:7, the narrator includes an explanatory note for the readers: "Now this was the custom in former times in Israel..." This indicates that by the time the book of Ruth was written, this specific sandal-custom was no longer widely practiced.

It is vital to distinguish the sandal exchange in Ruth 4 from the hostile ceremony in Deuteronomy 25. In Ruth, there is no spitting, no public shaming, and the sandal is not snatched away by a bitter widow. Instead, the closer relative voluntarily removes his own sandal and hands it to Boaz. This is a peaceful, cooperative, and respectable legal transaction. The relative recognizes his limitations, and Boaz acts with honor. The sandal serves as a "confirmation" (teudah), making the redemption of Elimelech's estate and the marriage to Ruth legally incontestable before the elders at the city gate.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 25:9 - "...then his brother’s wife shall come to him in the presence of the elders, remove his sandal from his foot, spit in his face, and answer and say, ‘So shall it be done to the man who will not build up his brother’s house.’"

• Joshua 5:15 - "Then the Commander of the LORD’S army said to Joshua, 'Take your sandal off your foot, for the place where you stand is holy.' And Joshua did so."

• Psalm 60:8 - "Moab is My washpot; Over Edom I will cast My shoe; Philistia, shout in triumph because of Me."

• Amos 2:6 - "Thus says the LORD: 'For three transgressions of Israel, and for four, I will not turn away its punishment, because they sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of sandals.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast of Names: The anonymous relative who surrendered his sandal did so to protect his own inheritance and name. Ironically, his name is forgotten in Scripture (referred to only by the Hebrew idiom peloni almoni, translated as "friend" or "such-a-one"). Conversely, Boaz, who took the sandal and sacrificed his own self-interest to redeem Ruth, has his name permanently recorded in the genealogy of King David and Jesus Christ.

• The Sign of Sonship: In the ancient world, slaves went barefoot, while free citizens wore sandals. In the parable of the Prodigal Son, when the repentant son returns, the father commands the servants to "put sandals on his feet" (Luke 15:22). This was not merely for comfort; it was a legal declaration of full restoration to sonship and family inheritance, echoing the legal significance of the sandal in the Old Testament.
