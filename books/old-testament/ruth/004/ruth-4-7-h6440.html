FORMER TIMES - ( lephanim , Strong's H6440 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word lephanim refers to times past, formerly, or ancient days. Derived from the root panim (meaning "face" or "presence"), it literally denotes what lies "in front of" or "before" someone. In Hebrew thought, time is viewed from a perspective where the past is "before" us because it is already known and can be mentally seen, while the future is "behind" us because it is unknown and unseen. In the context of Ruth 4:7, this word marks a historical transition, signaling to the reader that the legal custom of removing a sandal to ratify a covenant was an ancient practice from Israel's history that had fallen out of common use by the time the book of Ruth was written and compiled.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Hebrew mindset, spatial orientation and temporal orientation are deeply connected. The root word panim means "face" or "presence." By adding the prepositional prefix le- (meaning "to" or "toward"), lephanim literally translates to "at the face" or "in front of." Unlike modern Western thinking, which visualizes the future as ahead of us and the past as behind us, Hebrew thought conceptualizes the past as being in front of the observer because historical events have already occurred and can be clearly seen. The future, being unknown, is behind. Thus, lephanim represents the days that have already passed in front of Israel's historical vision.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word lephanim functions as an adverb of time meaning "formerly," "in times past," "of old," or "in former times." It indicates a period of history prior to the current narrative timeline, serving as a temporal marker to introduce an outdated custom, historical footnote, or previous state of affairs. When paired with historical descriptions, it highlights a contrast between ancient Israelite practices and the practices of the writer's contemporary audience.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In physical descriptions, the term indicates physical placement in front of something or someone.

• In chronological descriptions, it transitions the reader back to a prior era, establishing a historical precedent or explaining the origin of a current reality.

• The word picture of "facing" the past emphasizes that Israel's identity, laws, and customs are rooted in historically verifiable acts and covenants that remain visible and instructive to successive generations.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of lephanim demonstrates the progressive nature of God's revelation and the historical development of Israel's covenantal community.

• It reflects a biblical worldview where history is linear, purposeful, and directed by God, meaning that past customs are not meaningless relics but crucial building blocks of present-day covenant faithfulness.

• It underscores the authority of historical precedent in Israelite law. Redemptive practices, such as the levirate-like redemption of land and family lineage, have a long pedigree, tracing back to the foundational periods of the nation's life under God's law.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:7, the narrator uses lephanim ("in former times") to explain a legal formality that might have seemed obscure or obsolete to the contemporary audience reading the narrative.

• The custom of removing a sandal to transfer property or redemption rights was once a standard, legally binding act of confirmation. By the time the author recorded the account of Boaz and the closer redeemer, this physical sign had been superseded by other legal methods.

• This temporal marker provides crucial historical defense for Boaz's legal claim to Ruth and Elimelech's land. It proves that Boaz’s transaction was entirely legitimate, conducted in strict accordance with the ancient, respected traditions of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 2:12 - "The Horites also formerly lived in Seir, but the descendants of Esau dispossessed them..." (Translates lephanim to show historical displacement and God's sovereignty over nations).

• Joshua 14:15 - "And the name of Hebron formerly was Kirjath Arba..." (Translates lephanim to provide historical geographical context).

• Judges 1:10 - "Then Judah went against the Canaanites who dwelt in Hebron. (Now the name of Hebron formerly was Kirjath Arba.)" (Identical geographical and historical usage).

• 1 Samuel 9:9 - "(Formerly in Israel, when a man went to inquire of God, he spoke thus: 'Come, let us go to the seer'; for he who is now called a prophet was formerly called a seer.)" (Double use of lephanim to explain shifting terminology in Israel's spiritual history).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The presence of lephanim in Ruth 4:7 serves as an important internal clue regarding the date of the Book of Ruth. It indicates that the book was written or edited a significant period after the events occurred, likely during the early monarchy (the reign of David or Solomon), when the older judges-era custom of the sandal-exchange was no longer standard legal practice.

• The transition from the sandal-exchange to later legal methods shows how Israel adapted its civil procedures over time while retaining the moral and theological core of the Mosaic Law regarding redemption.
