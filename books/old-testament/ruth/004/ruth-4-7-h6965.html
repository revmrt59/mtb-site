CONFIRM - ( qum , Strong's 6965 )

Summary:

In Ruth 4:7, the Hebrew verb qum is translated as "confirm." Within the context of ancient Israelite legal and covenantal customs, this word refers to the formal ratification, establishment, or validation of a legal transaction. When the anonymous closer relative relinquished his right of redemption to Boaz, the act of transferring his sandal served to make the transaction permanent, legally binding, and socially recognized by the elders at the city gate.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary, concrete root meaning of qum is to arise, stand up, or rise from a seated, prostrate, or inactive position. In the ancient Near East, abstract legal and covenantal terms were regularly rooted in highly physical, concrete imagery. To make an agreement or a transaction "stand" (qum) meant to give it structural integrity, stability, and permanence, preventing it from falling down, collapsing, or being overturned. It is the verbal equivalent of erecting a stone pillar or establishing a foundation. When applied to legal transactions, qum conveys the transition from a tentative proposal to an unalterable, enduring reality that stands firm in the community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qum occurs extensively in the Hebrew Bible. While its basic active Qal stem means to rise or stand up, the causative Piel and Hiphil stems mean to cause to stand, to erect, to establish, to perform, or to make legally binding. In Ruth 4:7, the word appears in the Piel infinitive form (qayyem), meaning to ratify, confirm, or make valid. It refers to the formal process by which an agreement, oath, or transfer of property is legally established as binding and unchangeable. This denotes a decisive act of validation that secures a covenant or contract against future disputes.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture often contrasts "standing" (qum) with "falling" or "failing." An unconfirmed plan or an invalid covenant falls to the ground and turns to dust. Conversely, a confirmed transaction is pictured as a sturdy, upright structure that commands respect and remains standing. In ancient Israel, legal and property transactions were ratified in public spaces, particularly at the city gate, where witnesses could observe the physical signs of ratification. The physical act of handing over a sandal to confirm (qum) the transaction visually demonstrated that the legal right was now standing on new ground, transferred securely to another party.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of qum is deeply tied to covenantal faithfulness. God is the one who ultimately establishes and confirms His promises. When God "establishes" (qum) His covenant with Abraham, Noah, or David, He makes His word stand forever as an immutable reality. In the book of Ruth, the human act of confirming a transaction reflects this divine attribute of faithfulness and order. By legally confirming the redemption of Elimelech’s land and the marriage to Ruth, Boaz operates as a model of righteous covenant-keeping. This legal confirmation ensures that the family line of Elimelech—which eventually leads to King David and the Messiah, Jesus Christ—stands secure in Israel's history.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:7, the writer provides an explanatory parenthesis for later readers, noting the historical custom of confirming (qum) transactions of redemption and exchange. The closer kinsman (goel) is unable or unwilling to redeem Elimelech’s property because it would jeopardize his own inheritance. To formally transfer this redemption right to Boaz, he draws off his sandal and hands it to him. This gesture was the recognized, public sign of relinquishing possession and authority. Within this context, qum carries a highly precise legal nuance: it is the point of no return in the negotiation. Once the sandal is exchanged, the transaction is officially confirmed; the verbal agreement is translated into a binding, public covenant that the elders and townspeople immediately witness and validate.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The public nature of confirming an agreement in ancient Israel served as a safeguard against fraud and boundary disputes. By carrying out the confirmation process (qum) at the city gate in the presence of ten elders, Boaz ensured that the redemption was beyond legal challenge. The removal of the sandal in Deuteronomy 25:9-10 carries a tone of disgrace for refusing levirate duty, but in Ruth 4:7, the custom of exchanging the sandal is purely transactional and legal, serving as a peaceful and honorable way to confirm the transfer of rights.
