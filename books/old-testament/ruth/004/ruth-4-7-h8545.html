EXCHANGING - ( temurah , Strong's H8545 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun temurah refers to the act of making an exchange, a substitution, or the object that is exchanged. Derived from the root verb mura, meaning "to change" or "to exchange," this term carries both commercial and legal weight in the Old Testament. In the context of ancient Israelite society, temurah represents the formalized process of transferring ownership, rights, or property from one party to another. It underscores the value of covenant commitment and legal finality, serving as a tangible expression of substitutionary transactions, whether in the redemption of ancestral land or the dedication of items to the Lord.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun temurah is built upon the verbal root mura, which speaks of altering, changing, or substituting one thing for another. In the ancient Near East, where formal paper contracts did not exist, transactions relied heavily on symbolic actions and public declarations to establish legal permanence. Temurah represents the concrete act of substitution—taking one valuable asset and swapping its ownership or position with another.

This legal background is vividly illustrated in the ancient practice of removing a sandal. To finalize an "exchanging" (temurah), a man would pull off his sandal and hand it to his neighbor. This gesture symbolized the relinquishing of one's right to walk upon and claim a piece of land, transferring that right to the receiver. The sandal became the physical token of the temurah, sealing the legal transaction in the sight of witnesses at the city gate.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Temurah denotes an exchange, a substitute, a recompense, or restitution. In legal and ritual contexts, it identifies either the process of exchanging goods or the actual item that is substituted for another. In wisdom literature, the term expands metaphorically to signify the recompense or "return" a person receives for their actions or investments, representing the ultimate valuation or outcome of a lifestyle.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In the Levitical law, temurah is used to regulate holy dedications. If someone attempted to swap a dedicated animal for another, both the original animal and its temurah (substitute) became holy to the Lord. This prevented people from trying to shortchange God by exchanging a high-quality animal for a lesser one.

• In the Book of Job, temurah takes on a poetic and philosophical dimension. It describes the futility of human wealth compared to wisdom, noting that wisdom cannot be purchased or "exchanged" for vessels of fine gold. It also depicts the tragic recompense of the wicked, whose ill-gotten gains and "exchanges" bring no lasting joy or satisfaction.

### 4. Theological Implications

The concept of temurah points forward to the grand biblical theme of substitution. Levitical law established that a substitute (temurah) carries the same status as the original; what is exchanged becomes bound to the same covenantal reality. This prefigures the substitutionary work of Jesus Christ. In the economy of salvation, a divine exchange occurs: Christ takes our sin and gives us His righteousness.

Furthermore, temurah highlights the sacredness of vows and legal agreements in the biblical worldview. An exchange was not merely a secular business deal but a covenant concluded before God, demanding absolute integrity and faithfulness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:7, temurah appears alongside the word for "redeeming" (geullah) to describe the legal framework governing Boaz’s acquisition of Elimelech's land and his marriage to Ruth.

The "exchanging" here refers to the formal transfer of the right of redemption. The closer relative (the goel) was legally entitled to redeem the land and marry Ruth, but he could not do so without jeopardizing his own inheritance. Therefore, he initiated a temurah—he exchanged his legal rights, transferring them to Boaz. The public removal of the sandal served as the visual confirmation of this temurah. It demonstrated that the transaction was complete, legal, and binding under Israelite custom. This allowed Boaz to step in legally as the kinsman-redeemer, paving the way for the lineage of King David and ultimately the Messiah.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 27:10 – "He shall not substitute it or exchange it, a good for a bad or a bad for a good; and if he at all exchanges beast for beast, then both it and its substitute shall be holy."

• Leviticus 27:33 – "He shall not inquire whether it is good or bad, nor shall he exchange it; and if he exchanges it at all, then both it and its substitute shall be holy; it shall not be redeemed."

• Job 15:31 – "Let him not trust in futile things, deceiving himself, for futility will be his reward."

• Job 20:18 – "He will restore that for which he labored, and will not swallow it down; from the proceeds of his business he will not rejoice."

• Job 28:17 – "Neither gold nor crystal can equal it, nor can it be exchanged for jewelry of fine gold."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The connection between temurah (exchange) and geullah (redemption) in Ruth 4:7 illustrates that redemption often requires an exchange of position. One party steps down so another can step up; one party pays the price so another can receive the inheritance. In Jewish oral law, an entire tractate of the Mishnah and Talmud is named Temurah. This tractate deals extensively with the laws concerning the substitution of consecrated animals, showing the long-lasting legal and religious legacy of this single Hebrew word.
