CONFIRMATION - ( te'udah , Strong's H8584 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word te'udah refers to an established custom, attestation, testimony, or legally binding confirmation. In the ancient world, it represented a public, visible sign or token that legally ratified a transaction, covenant, or agreement, making it binding and recognized within the community. In Ruth 4:7, it describes the public act of removing and exchanging a sandal to confirm a transaction of redemption and inheritance, serving as Israel's formal legal attestation of the transfer of rights.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb of te'udah is ud, which means to return, repeat, warn, bear witness, or testify. While the root verb emphasizes the act of speaking or testifying repeatedly to establish a truth, the noun te'udah refers to the concrete, tangible expression of that testimony or legal proof. In ancient Israel, long before the widespread use of written contracts, legal agreements were ratified through highly symbolic and public actions performed at the city gate in the presence of the elders. The exchange of a sandal was one such custom. Because land was claimed by walking upon it, handing one's sandal to another represented the literal relinquishment of one's right to tread upon or claim ownership of a specific piece of property. The sandal became the visual "confirmation" or physical testimony of the legal transaction.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun te'udah denotes a testimony, attestation, binding decree, or legal confirmation. It refers to the formal witness or the specific instrument—whether a symbolic act or a written document—that certifies the truth and validity of a covenant or transaction. The term carries the weight of an indisputable public record, serving as the ultimate verification that seals an agreement and prevents future disputes.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term te'udah appears only three times in the Old Testament, representing two distinct word pictures:

• Legal Attestation (Ruth 4:7): Here, the word picture is a physical object (a sandal) changing hands. This public gesture served as the visible "confirmation" of a transferred right, establishing a clear boundary of ownership that the entire community recognized.

• Sealed Testimony (Isaiah 8:16, 20): In these passages, the word is translated as "testimony" and is paired with "the law" (torah). The word picture is a scroll containing God's prophetic words that is wrapped up, tied, and sealed. This sealed document serves as a binding legal testimony against those who reject God's word, and as a secure deposit of truth among His disciples.

4. Theological Implications

The use of te'udah highlights several key aspects of God's character and His dealings with humanity:

• God Values Order and Integrity: Redemption and covenant-making are not done in secret or through deceptive practices. God establishes legal, orderly, and public confirmations of His promises so that His people can have absolute assurance.

• The Visibility of Redemption: Just as Boaz's redemption of Ruth required a public "confirmation" to be legally valid, our redemption in Christ is publicly attested. Jesus' death and resurrection were not done in a corner, but were historical, witnessed events.

• The Sealed Covenant: The Holy Spirit acts as the believer's ultimate te'udah—the seal and guarantee of our inheritance, confirming that we have been redeemed and belong to God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4, Boaz meets with the closer kinsman redeemer (goel) at the gate of Bethlehem in the presence of ten elders. While this closer kinsman is willing to buy Elimelech’s land, he refuses to marry Ruth because doing so would jeopardize his own inheritance. To legally transfer his right of redemption to Boaz, the kinsman removes his sandal and hands it to him. The narrator of the book of Ruth, writing for a later generation that was no longer practicing this specific ritual, explains that this physical exchange was the "confirmation" (te'udah) in Israel in former times. This detail assures the reader that Boaz’s acquisition of the land and his marriage to Ruth were completely legal, honorable, and binding. No one could ever contest the legitimacy of Obed’s birth, which ultimately secured the lineage of King David and the Messiah.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 4:7: "Now this was the custom in former times in Israel concerning redeeming and exchanging, to confirm anything: one man took off his sandal and gave it to the other, and this was a confirmation in Israel."

• Isaiah 8:16: "Bind up the testimony, seal the law among my disciples."

• Isaiah 8:20: "To the law and to the testimony! If they do not speak according to this word, it is because there is no light in them."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Historical Transition: The narrator's explanation ("in former times") suggests a historical shift in Israel's legal practices. Over time, oral and symbolic transactions (like exchanging a sandal) gave way to written deeds of purchase, such as those signed and sealed in the days of Jeremiah (Jeremiah 32:9–12).

• Contrast with Deuteronomy 25: The shoe-removal custom in Ruth 4:7 should not be confused with the levirate marriage refusal in Deuteronomy 25:9. In Deuteronomy, if a man refused to marry his deceased brother's widow, the woman would spit in his face and pull off his sandal as a public act of humiliation, branding his family "the house of him who had his sandal removed." In Ruth 4, the transaction is polite, mutual, and honorable. The kinsman voluntarily removes his own sandal to legally transfer his right to Boaz, with no shame attached.
