CLOSE RELATIVE - ( ga'al , Strong's H1350 )

Summary:

The Hebrew term ga'al refers to the act of redeeming, delivering, or acting as a kinsman-redeemer. In the legal and cultural landscape of ancient Israel, the goel (the active participle of ga'al) was the closest male relative who possessed both the legal right and the familial obligation to rescue a relative's person, lineage, or property from loss, bondage, or extinction. In Ruth 4:8, the word is translated as "close relative," identifying the primary candidate for redemption who ultimately surrendered his claim to Boaz, paving the way for the physical and spiritual preservation of Naomi, Ruth, and the messianic line.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The concept of ga'al is deeply embedded in ancient Israel's clan-based socio-economic structure, designed by God to preserve family identity and inheritance.

• The land of Canaan was distributed by tribe and family as an everlasting possession; it could not be sold permanently. If a man fell into poverty and had to sell his ancestral land or sell himself into slavery, his closest male relative—the goel—had the duty to step in and buy back (redeem) the land or the person.

• The goel also had the responsibility to avenge the blood of a murdered relative (the "avenger of blood") and to marry the childless widow of a deceased brother (levirate marriage) to carry on the family name and keep property within the family line.

• The custom of removing the sandal, as seen in Ruth 4:8, was a legal gesture in Israel signifying the transfer of property rights and the relinquishment of one's right to redeem. By drawing off his sandal and handing it to Boaz, the anonymous close relative publicly surrendered his legal claim to Elimelech's estate and the right to marry Ruth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The root ga'al primarily means to redeem, buy back, deliver, or act as a kinsman-redeemer.

• As a verb, it denotes the restoration of persons or property to their original state or owner through the payment of a price or the assertion of a legal right.

• As a noun or substantive participle (goel), it refers to the redeemer, the next of kin, or the close relative who is authorized and obligated to perform these acts of redemption.

• The semantic range encompasses three primary areas of redemption: redeeming property (Leviticus 25:25), redeeming people from slavery (Leviticus 25:47–49), and redeeming blood through justice (Numbers 35:19).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Ga'al is used to describe God's deliverance of Israel from Egypt (Exodus 6:6), where God acts as Israel's national goel, rescuing them from bondage not by paying a price to Pharaoh, but by asserting His sovereign ownership over them.

• In Job 19:25, the word is used in a profound personal sense: "For I know that my Redeemer lives," depicting a living defender who will ultimately vindicate the suffering believer.

• The book of Ruth serves as the premier narrative illustration of ga'al. Boaz acts as the willing and able redeemer, demonstrating that redemption is not merely a legal transaction but an act of covenant love that brings restoration, security, and life out of tragedy.

4. Theological Implications

• Christ as our Ultimate Redeemer: The Old Testament institution of the goel points directly to Jesus Christ. To qualify as a goel, a redeemer had to meet three strict requirements: he must be a close relative (accomplished by Christ's incarnation), he must have the resources to pay the redemption price (accomplished by Christ's sinless life and infinite worth), and he must be willing to redeem (accomplished by Christ's voluntary sacrifice on the cross).

• Redemption of Inheritance: Just as the goel restored lost inheritance and family name, Christ restores our lost spiritual inheritance, redeeming us from the slavery of sin and the debt of death, and adopting us into the family of God.

• Covenant Faithfulness: The concept of ga'al underscores God's absolute commitment to His people. He does not abandon those who have lost everything; He provides a way of rescue and restoration through a Kinsman-Redeemer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 4:8, the noun goel refers to the unnamed relative who was closer in relation to Elimelech than Boaz. In the narrative, this man is referred to anonymously (literally "such a one" in verse 1), contrasting his lost legacy with Boaz's enduring legacy.

• This close relative was willing to redeem the land of Elimelech when he thought it was a simple real estate transaction that would increase his own wealth.

• However, when Boaz informed him that the acquisition of the land also required marrying Ruth the Moabitess to perpetuate the name of the deceased, the man refused, stating that it would ruin his own inheritance (Ruth 4:6). His refusal highlights the self-sacrificial nature of true redemption.

• In verse 8, when this relative says, "Buy it for yourself," and takes off his sandal, he legally transfers the right of redemption to Boaz. The legal obstacle is removed, allowing Boaz to step forward as the willing goel who redeems Ruth and Naomi.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast of Names: The anonymous relative's refusal to redeem Ruth to protect his own inheritance resulted in the loss of his very name in biblical history—he is remembered only as "such a one." Conversely, Boaz, who sacrificed his own resources to redeem Ruth, is eternally remembered in the lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.

• The Sandal Ceremony: Deuteronomic law (Deuteronomy 25:9) dictates that if a man refused to perform his levirate duty, the widow was to spit in his face and pull off his sandal in public disgrace. In Ruth 4:8, the ceremony is modified to a peaceful property transaction where the man himself removes his sandal, showing that Boaz maintained a respectful, legally sound negotiation that avoided public shaming while securing the legal right to redeem.
