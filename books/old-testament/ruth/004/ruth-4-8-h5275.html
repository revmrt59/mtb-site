SANDAL - ( na'al , Strong's H5275 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'al refers to a sandal or shoe, typically consisting of a leather sole bound to the foot by leather thongs. In ancient Israel, the sandal was far more than everyday footwear; it served as a recognized legal and symbolic instrument. Because sandals made direct contact with the ground, they were deeply associated with land, ownership, and walking upon property. In the context of property redemption and levirate marriage, removing and transferring a sandal symbolized the public relinquishment of one's legal right, ownership, or claim to a piece of land. In Ruth 4:8, the transaction between Boaz and the anonymous closer relative is sealed when the relative draws off his sandal and hands it to Boaz, formally and legally transferring his right of redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, sandals were basic necessities for navigating the hot, dusty, and rocky terrain of Israel. They were simple items, usually constructed of a flat piece of cowhide or wooden sole secured with leather straps wrapped around the ankle and foot. Because sandals were the physical barrier between a person and the dirt, they accumulated the dust of the road. Setting one's foot upon a plot of land was a physical gesture of claiming ownership or exercising authority over that territory. Consequently, the sandal became the legal proxy for the foot itself. To take off one's sandal and hand it to another was a vivid, non-verbal way of saying, "I am stepping off this property, and I am handing over the right to step onto it in my place."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term na'al denotes a sandal, shoe, or footwear. The word occurs over twenty times in the Old Testament. Lexically, it refers to:

• The physical footwear worn by ordinary people, soldiers, and travelers.

• A symbol of low material value or a nominal token (as in a "pair of sandals").

• A legal instrument used to ratify transactions, transfers of property, or the renunciation of rights.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Holy Ground: To stand barefoot in the ancient world was a sign of humility, servitude, and reverence. Removing the na'al was required when entering holy space, acknowledging that the ground was sanctified by the presence of Yahweh.

• Mourning and Humiliation: Walking barefoot, stripped of one's sandals, was a sign of intense grief, captive status, or extreme poverty. Captives of war were marched away barefoot to strip them of their dignity.

• Low Value and Bribery: Because sandals were common and relatively inexpensive, the word was used idiomatically to describe things of minimal worth or petty bribes. To sell someone for "a pair of sandals" indicated utter disregard for human dignity in pursuit of cheap material gain.

• Legal Surrender: The physical removal of the sandal served as a binding, public sign of relinquishing legal rights and duties.

4. Theological Implications

The transaction in Ruth 4:8 carries profound covenantal and typological significance. The closer relative (goel) refuses to redeem Elimelech's land because marrying Ruth would jeopardize his own family inheritance. By handing his na'al to Boaz, he publicly and legally yields his right of redemption.

• The Selfless Redeemer: While the anonymous relative protects his own name and inheritance, Boaz willingly steps forward, accepting the financial cost and societal vulnerability of marrying a Moabite widow. This surrender of rights by one party to enable the redemption by another points directly to Jesus Christ. Jesus, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, did not cling to His heavenly rights but emptied Himself, stepping into our poverty to buy us back and secure our eternal inheritance.

• Order and Covenant Integrity: God values public honesty and legal integrity. The public exchange of the sandal demonstrates that the lineage of David—and ultimately the Messiah—was established not through deception or raw power, but through honorable, legally binding, and covenant-keeping actions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The practice of removing the sandal in Ruth 4:8 is closely related to, but distinct from, the law outlined in Deuteronomy 25:5-10. Under the Mosaic Law, if a brother refused to perform the duty of a levirate marriage, the widow would publicly pull his sandal off his foot, spit in his face, and his household would be mockingly called "The house of him who had his sandal removed."

In Ruth 4, however, the tone is cooperative and administrative rather than shameful. The narrator explains that in Israel's history, transferring the sandal was a standard, peaceful custom to confirm any transaction of redeeming and exchanging. The closer relative is not subjected to public disgrace or spitting; instead, he willingly draws off his own sandal and hands it to Boaz. It is a civil, dignified transfer of legal priority, clearing the way for Boaz to act as the redeemer.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Nameless Relative: The closer relative who prioritized his own estate over his family duty remains nameless in the text, referred to only by the Hebrew idiom peloni almoni ("friend" or "so-and-so"). In trying to preserve his name and inheritance by surrendering his sandal, his name was forgotten by history. Boaz, who gave up his wealth to redeem the lost, has his name preserved forever in the genealogy of Jesus Christ.

• Nuzi Tablets: Archaeological discoveries from ancient Near Eastern sites, such as Nuzi, confirm that property transfers and legal agreements were frequently accompanied by symbolic physical gestures, including walking the boundaries of a field or exchanging garments and shoes to symbolize the transfer of possession.
