CHILION'S - ( Kilyon , Strong's H3630 )

Summary:

Chilion’s is a Hebrew proper noun representing one of the two sons of Elimelech and Naomi who died in Moab. Derived from a root meaning to "fail," "pine," or "waste away," his name reflects the tragic and fragile nature of human efforts outside the Promised Land. In Ruth 4:9, the public redemption of Chilion's inheritance by Boaz illustrates the power of God's grace to recover and restore what was seemingly lost to death and spiritual decay.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The setting of the book of Ruth begins during the dark and chaotic period of the Judges, characterized by spiritual decline and physical famine in Israel. Elimelech, seeking to escape the famine in Bethlehem, makes the fateful decision to take his wife Naomi and his two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, to the pagan fields of Moab. The family’s trajectory in Moab is marked by swift tragedy. Elimelech dies, and his two sons marry Moabite women. Instead of finding prosperity, the family encounters utter devastation. The sons' very names foreshadow their brief and fragile lives. While Mahlon suggests "sickness" or "mildew," Chilion carries the heavy connotation of "failing" or "pining away." Within ten years, both young men die childless in a foreign land, leaving Naomi destitute and desperate.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name Kilyon is derived from the Hebrew root verb kalah, which means to be complete, finished, at an end, spent, or to consume and waste away. The proper noun Kilyon translates literally to "pining," "wasting," "destruction," or "failing." It describes a state of physical consumption or a process of drawing to a tragic close. In the semantic landscape of biblical Hebrew, this name represents the vulnerability of human existence and the ultimate end of human strength when separated from the source of life.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The names of Elimelech's sons serve as literary and historical word pictures. In ancient Hebrew culture, names were frequently descriptive of the circumstances surrounding a child's birth or prophetic of their destiny.

• The combination of Mahlon ("sickly") and Chilion ("pining") serves as an outward word picture of Israel's spiritual condition during the era of the Judges.

• The names depict the severe consequences of leaving the place of God's covenant blessing (Bethlehem, meaning "House of Bread") for Moab.

• Their short, childless lives in Moab demonstrate the ultimate end of self-reliance: a slow, agonizing process of pining away until nothing remains.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of Chilion’s name and legacy lies in the reality of human ruin apart from God’s covenant protection, contrasted with the superabounding grace of redemption. To abandon the Promised Land for temporary relief in Moab is to trade covenant abundance for a legacy of "pining" and "wasting."

However, the narrative does not end in Moab’s graveyard. The theological beauty of the book of Ruth is that human failure is not the final word. While Chilion’s life was cut short by death, his legal identity and inheritance are not forgotten by God. The preservation of his name in the genealogical records of Israel proves that God's plan of redemption is capable of reclaiming what was completely lost to the grave.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:9, Boaz stands in the public gate of Bethlehem before the elders of the city and declares, "You are witnesses this day that I have bought all that was Elimelech’s, and all that was Chilion’s and Mahlon’s, from the hand of Naomi."

In this legal transaction, the mentioning of Chilion's name carries profound covenantal weight:

• The Role of the Kinsman-Redeemer: Under Mosaic law, the goel (kinsman-redeemer) was responsible for purchasing the land of a deceased relative to keep the inheritance within the tribe.

• Complete Restoration: By purchasing "all that was Chilion's," Boaz legally erases the shame of the "wasting away" that occurred in Moab.

• Grace Over Death: Although Chilion died in rebellion and foreign exile, his portion of the Promised Land is fully restored. The legal transaction transforms a name synonymous with "pining away" into a monument of covenant faithfulness.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

Ancient Jewish tradition and textual analysis suggest that Chilion was married to Orpah, while Mahlon was married to Ruth (as confirmed in Ruth 4:10, where Ruth is identified as the widow of Mahlon). When Naomi urged her daughters-in-law to return to their homes, Orpah wept, kissed Naomi, and returned to Moab and her false gods. By doing so, Orpah severed her connection to the God of Israel. Chilion’s physical line through Orpah was permanently extinguished.

Nevertheless, through the legal mechanism of the kinsman-redeemer, Chilion's name and property were legally merged into the redemption accomplished by Boaz. This foreshadows the work of Jesus Christ, our ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer, who enters into our state of spiritual ruin, purchases our wasted lives, and restores our lost inheritance.
