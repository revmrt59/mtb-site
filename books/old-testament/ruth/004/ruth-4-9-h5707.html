WITNESSES - ( 'ed , Strong's H5707 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, a witness ('ed) is someone who attests to a fact, event, or covenant, providing legal validation and historical memory. In Ruth 4:9, the term denotes the elders and townspeople gathered at the city gate of Bethlehem, serving as formal observers to Boaz's redemption of Elimelech's land and his marriage to Ruth. This legal testimony secures Ruth's inclusion into the covenant community and establishes a public, binding agreement that ensures the continuity of Elimelech's lineage, ultimately pointing to the legal and redemptive certainty we have in Christ.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, the city gate served as the local courtroom and the hub of civic and economic life. Legal transactions, property transfers, and marriage agreements were not documented on paper and signed in private; rather, they were enacted publicly in the presence of the community. A witness ('ed) was a person who stood as a living archive of the transaction. The noun 'ed is closely related to the verb 'ud, which means to return, repeat, or do again. Thus, a witness is someone who is called upon to "repeat" or "recreate" the truth of a past event to establish justice in the present.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term 'ed carries the basic meaning of a witness, recorder, or testimony. It refers to:

• An individual who has direct knowledge of an event and testifies to it in a judicial context.

• A group of individuals who collectively attest to the validity of a legal transaction, contract, or covenant.

• Inanimate objects (such as heaps of stones, pillars, or written documents) that serve as a lasting memorial or legal proof of an agreement.

In Ruth 4:9, the word appears in its plural form ('edim), representing the legally convened body of elders and citizens who formally acknowledge Boaz’s redemptive transaction.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs 'ed in several rich contexts:

• The Judicial Witness: In the Mosaic legal system, the testimony of witnesses determined life and death. To protect against falsehood, a matter could only be established on the evidence of two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15).

• Inanimate Witness: When Jacob and Laban made a covenant, they set up a heap of stones to serve as a silent witness ('ed) between them, naming it Galeed ("heap of witness") in Genesis 31:47-48.

• The Divine Witness: God Himself is frequently invoked as the ultimate 'ed who searches the heart and testifies to the truth of human actions and oaths (1 Samuel 12:5, Micah 1:2).

### 4. Theological Implications

The concept of 'ed reveals that biblical redemption is not a hidden, arbitrary process, but a public, legal, and orderly transaction. God operates within covenants that are legally binding and publicly attested. Furthermore, the role of the witness underscores the importance of communal accountability and truth-telling within the household of faith. When Boaz redeems Ruth, he does so in complete transparency. This legal uprightness foreshadows the work of Christ, whose redemptive work on the cross was done publicly and is attested to by the Holy Spirit and the testimony of the apostles.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 4:9, Boaz has successfully negotiated the rights of redemption with the closer kinsman. He turns to the elders and the townspeople at the gate and declares, "You are witnesses this day..." By stepping into this role, the people of Bethlehem are not mere spectators; they are the legal guarantors of the transaction. Their response in verse 11 ("We are witnesses") cements Boaz’s legal right to the land and his marriage to Ruth. This public witness removes any potential stigma from Ruth’s Moabite background, formally integrating her into Israel as a legal heir to Elimelech's estate.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

Because ancient Israel did not rely on modern filing systems, the reliability of a witness was paramount. The Decalogue strictly forbids bearing false witness against a neighbor (Exodus 20:16). In the case of Ruth and Boaz, the high status of the ten elders who acted as witnesses guaranteed that the lineage of the future King David (and ultimately Jesus the Messiah) was legally impeccable and beyond reproach.
