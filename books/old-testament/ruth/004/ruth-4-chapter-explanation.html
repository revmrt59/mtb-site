Chapter Study

Ruth 4

Verse-by-Verse
Ruth 4:1
Ruth 4:1
NKJV

Ruth 4:1 Now Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there; and behold, the close relative of whom Boaz had spoken came by. So Boaz said, "Come aside, friend, sit down here." So he came aside and sat down.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:1 Boaz went to the town gate and took a seat there. Just then the family redeemer he had mentioned came by, so Boaz called out to him, “Come over here and sit down, friend. I want to talk to you.” So they sat down together. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz goes up to the city gate, where Israel’s legal business was settled, and sits down just as the close relative he had spoken of passes by. Boaz calls him aside to sit, and the man left unnamed in the story joins him.

Verse Explanation

Boaz goes up to the city gate, where Israel’s legal business was settled, and sits down just as the close relative he had spoken of passes by. Boaz calls him aside to sit, and the man left unnamed in the story joins him.

“Now Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there;”

Boaz keeps his word without delay. He goes “up to the gate,” the entrance of the town where public life, business, and legal matters were conducted. Bethlehem sat on a ridge, so he goes up from the threshing floor. By sitting down at the gate, he signals that he has public business to settle.

“and behold, the close relative of whom Boaz had spoken came by.”

“Behold” marks another well-timed moment. Just as Boaz sits down, the nearer relative he had mentioned to Ruth (3:12) comes along. Once again events unfold with quiet providence.

“So Boaz said, “Come aside, friend, sit down here.” So he came aside and sat down.”

Boaz calls him over and asks him to sit. The man is addressed only as “friend,” and his name is never given. The man who will decline to preserve another man’s name is himself left unnamed, while Boaz’s name endures in the genealogy at the end of the book.

Study Notes

“Friend” (4:1) translates the Hebrew expression peloni ’almoni, an idiom meaning ‘so-and-so’ or ‘such a one,’ used when a person or place is deliberately left unnamed (compare 1 Samuel 21:2; 2 Kings 6:8). Boaz surely knew the man’s name; the narrator chooses not to record it.

The city gate typically included an open area with benches or alcoves where elders sat and legal matters were heard (compare Deuteronomy 21:19; 22:15; Amos 5:15).

Boaz gathers ten of the city’s elders and has them sit down as witnesses. The matter of redemption will be settled openly and lawfully before the community.

“And he took ten men of the elders of the city, and said, “Sit down here.” So they sat down.”

Boaz gathers ten of the town’s elders and asks them to sit as witnesses. The elders were the recognized leaders of the community, and their presence gives the proceedings legal standing. The matter of redemption will be settled openly and lawfully before the community, not privately.

Study Notes

The text does not explain why Boaz gathered ten elders, and it gives no rationale for the number. Later Jewish practice required ten men for certain communal acts, but how that practice relates to Ruth 4 is uncertain.

Ruth 4:3–6 — The Nearer Relative Declines to Redeem

Ruth 4:3
Ruth 4:3
NKJV

Ruth 4:3 Then he said to the close relative, "Naomi, who has come back from the country of Moab, sold the piece of land which belonged to our brother Elimelech.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:3 And Boaz said to the family redeemer, “You know Naomi, who came back from Moab. She is selling the land that belonged to our relative Elimelech. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz tells the close relative that Naomi, who has come back from Moab, sold the piece of land that belonged to their brother Elimelech. He raises the matter first as a question of family land that needs a redeemer.

Verse Explanation

Boaz tells the close relative that Naomi, who has come back from Moab, sold the piece of land that belonged to their brother Elimelech. He raises the matter first as a question of family land that needs a redeemer.

“Then he said to the close relative,”

Boaz now addresses the nearer relative in front of the elders. He begins with the matter of land.

““Naomi, who has come back from the country of Moab,”

Boaz identifies Naomi by her recent return from Moab, a fact well known in Bethlehem (1:19).

“sold the piece of land which belonged to our brother Elimelech.”

The issue is a “piece of land” that belonged to Elimelech, whom Boaz calls “our brother,” meaning their kinsman. Under Israel’s law, land was to remain within the family, because the LORD said the land was His (Leviticus 25:23). When a family member became poor and had to sell land, a close relative had the right to buy it back (Leviticus 25:25). The text does not explain how Naomi held the land during her years away or whether it had already been sold or was being offered for sale; those details remain unclear.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “sold” (4:3) is in a form that can describe a completed act (‘has sold’) or a decision being announced (‘is selling’). Translations differ, and the precise status of the land is not explained.

Jeremiah 32:6–12 records a later example of a relative’s right to redeem family land, with a written deed and witnesses.

But if you don’t want it, let me know right away, because I am next in line to redeem it after you.” The man replied, “All right, I’ll redeem it.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz explains that he wanted the man to have the first opportunity to redeem the land before the witnesses, since he is first in line and Boaz is next. The relative answers, “I will redeem it,” and for a moment it seems the land will pass to him.

Verse Explanation

Boaz explains that he wanted the man to have the first opportunity to redeem the land before the witnesses, since he is first in line and Boaz is next. The relative answers, “I will redeem it,” and for a moment it seems the land will pass to him.

“And I thought to inform you, saying, ‘Buy it back in the presence of the inhabitants and the elders of my people.”

Boaz explains that he wanted the man to know about the land so he could act. The redemption must take place “in the presence of” the townspeople and the elders, so that it will be public and binding.

“If you will redeem it, redeem it; but if you will not redeem it, then tell me, that I may know;”

Boaz lays out the choice plainly. The man may redeem the land, or he may decline, but he must say so publicly.

“for there is no one but you to redeem it, and I am next after you.’ ”

Boaz explains the order of relatives: the man has the first right, and Boaz is next. He respects the rightful order, just as he told Ruth he would (3:12–13).

“And he said, “I will redeem it.”

The relative agrees at once. For a moment it seems that the land will pass to him and that Boaz will have no role. Acquiring more land was attractive, especially if there was no heir to claim it.

Study Notes

“I thought to inform you” (4:4) literally reads ‘I said I would uncover your ear,’ a Hebrew idiom for revealing something to someone (compare 1 Samuel 20:2).

In the clause “if you will not redeem it” (4:4), the traditional Hebrew text known as the Masoretic Text reads ‘if he will not redeem.’ Some Hebrew manuscripts and ancient versions, followed by the NKJV and most translations, read ‘if you will not,’ which fits Boaz’s direct address to the relative. This is a textual and translation choice, not a marked written-and-read variation. Such a marked variation does occur later in the verse, in the form of the verb translated “that I may know,” without affecting the meaning.

Ruth 4:5
Ruth 4:5
NKJV

Ruth 4:5 Then Boaz said, "On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:5 Then Boaz told him, “Of course, your purchase of the land from Naomi also requires that you marry Ruth, the Moabite widow. That way she can have children who will carry on her husband’s name and keep the land in the family.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz adds that in this transaction whoever buys the field from Naomi also takes responsibility for Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, “to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance.” The land and the duty to Ruth come together, and the redeemer must accept both.

Verse Explanation

Boaz adds that in this transaction whoever buys the field from Naomi also takes responsibility for Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, “to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance.” The land and the duty to Ruth come together, and the redeemer must accept both.

“Then Boaz said, “On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi,”

Boaz now adds a condition. Whoever buys the field from Naomi takes on a further responsibility in the same transaction.

“you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead,”

Boaz brings Ruth into the matter, naming her as “Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead.” In this transaction the land and the duty to Ruth come together. Israel’s law treats these concerns separately: a close relative could redeem family land that had been sold (Leviticus 25:25), and a dead man’s brother was to marry his widow and raise up a son in his name (Deuteronomy 25:5–6). Boaz is not Mahlon’s brother, and the text does not fully explain how law and custom combined in this case. Boaz’s own declaration in 4:10 confirms what is involved: he acquires Ruth as his wife. Ruth’s Moabite identity is stated plainly at the very moment that changes the relative’s mind.

“to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance.”

The stated purpose is to keep the dead man’s name alive in connection with his inheritance. Most likely a son born to Ruth would be counted as the heir of Mahlon and Elimelech, so that the land would eventually belong to that son. If so, the redeemer would pay for the land and support Ruth while the property passed to another man’s line, which would explain the relative’s change of mind in the next verse.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of this verse has two well-known difficulties. The written text has ‘I acquire,’ while the traditional reading followed by the NKJV has ‘you acquire.’ In addition, many translations read ‘you also acquire Ruth the Moabitess’ where the NKJV has “buy it from Ruth.” On every reading, the land and the responsibility for Ruth are joined, as 4:10 makes clear.

In Deuteronomy 25:5–10 the marriage duty applies to brothers who dwell together, and a refusal is met with a public shaming ceremony (Deuteronomy 25:9–10). Leviticus 25:23–28 grounds land redemption in the LORD’s ownership of the land. Ruth 4 shares the concerns of both passages without simply reproducing either one.

Ruth 4:6 “Then I can’t redeem it,” the family redeemer replied, “because this might endanger my own estate. You redeem the land; I cannot do it.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The close relative now declines, saying he cannot redeem it for himself lest he ruin his own inheritance, and he passes his right to Boaz. His stated reason is his inheritance, though his reversal once Ruth the Moabitess is named suggests that prejudice against her as a Moabite also played a part.

Verse Explanation

The close relative now declines, saying he cannot redeem it for himself lest he ruin his own inheritance, and he passes his right to Boaz. His stated reason is his inheritance, though his reversal once Ruth the Moabitess is named suggests that prejudice against her as a Moabite also played a part.

“And the close relative said, “I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance.”

The relative now declines. His stated reason is that redeeming the land on these terms would “ruin” his own inheritance. The likely economic concern is that paying for land that would eventually go to Ruth’s son, while also supporting Ruth, could cost him without adding anything permanent to his own estate. The sequence also suggests that prejudice against Ruth as a Moabite played a part, since he changes his mind only after she is named. That is an inference from the sequence, not something the text states, and it should be held alongside his stated concern for his inheritance.

“You redeem my right of redemption for yourself, for I cannot redeem it.”

The relative passes his right to Boaz. Twice he says, “I cannot redeem it.” Boaz, by contrast, is both able and willing. What the nearer relative will not do, Boaz will do.

Study Notes

The text does not explain exactly how redeeming the land with Ruth would ruin the relative’s inheritance. Suggested explanations include the cost of the land and of supporting Ruth, and the division of his estate among his own heirs and a future heir of Mahlon.

The Hebrew verb ga’al, to redeem, and the related noun occur repeatedly in 4:1–8, emphasizing the subject of the scene.

Ruth 4:7–10 — Boaz Redeems Before Witnesses

Ruth 4:7
Ruth 4:7
NKJV

Ruth 4:7 Now this was the custom in former times in Israel concerning redeeming and exchanging, to confirm anything: one man took off his sandal and gave it to the other, and this was a confirmation in Israel.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:7 Now in those days it was the custom in Israel for anyone transferring a right of purchase to remove his sandal and hand it to the other party. This publicly validated the transaction. (NLT)

Main Idea

The narrator explains an earlier custom in Israel for confirming any transaction of redeeming or exchanging: one man took off his sandal and gave it to the other. The explanation shows that the coming gesture legally confirms a transfer of rights.

Verse Explanation

The narrator explains an earlier custom in Israel for confirming any transaction of redeeming or exchanging: one man took off his sandal and gave it to the other. The explanation shows that the coming gesture legally confirms a transfer of rights.

“Now this was the custom in former times in Israel concerning redeeming and exchanging, to confirm anything:”

The narrator pauses to explain an older custom that readers might not know. It concerned transactions of redemption and exchange, and it served to make them binding.

“one man took off his sandal and gave it to the other, and this was a confirmation in Israel.”

The gesture was simple: one party removed his sandal and handed it to the other. Since walking on land could symbolize possession, giving up a sandal may have symbolized giving up one’s right to the property. This explanation shows that the coming gesture legally confirms a transfer of rights.

Study Notes

Deuteronomy 25:9–10 also involves a sandal, when a man refuses to marry his brother’s widow. There the widow removes his sandal and spits in his face as a public disgrace. Ruth 4 describes a different act: a voluntary transfer of rights between two men, without that shaming ritual.

The phrase “in former times” (4:7) implies that the custom was no longer familiar, or no longer practiced, when the book was written, but it does not give a precise date for the book.

Boaz declares to the elders and all the people that they are witnesses that he has bought from Naomi all that belonged to Elimelech, Chilion, and Mahlon. The family’s inheritance is restored through his public, willing act.

“And Boaz said to the elders and all the people, “You are witnesses this day”

Boaz turns to the elders and the crowd gathered at the gate and calls them to witness. A wider audience has now gathered, and the act becomes fully public.

Ruth 4:10
NKJV

Ruth 4:10 Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from his position at the gate. You are witnesses this day."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:10 And with the land I have acquired Ruth, the Moabite widow of Mahlon, to be my wife. This way she can have a son to carry on the family name of her dead husband and to inherit the family property here in his hometown. You are all witnesses today.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz also declares that he has acquired Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, as his wife, so that the dead man’s name will not be cut off from his family and his town. A woman from Moab, a people who had opposed Israel and were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6), now receives marriage, protection, and a place in Israel, a public display of God’s redeeming grace.

Verse Explanation

Boaz also declares that he has acquired Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, as his wife, so that the dead man’s name will not be cut off from his family and his town. A woman from Moab, a people who had opposed Israel and were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6), now receives marriage, protection, and a place in Israel, a public display of God’s redeeming grace.

“Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife,”

Boaz now makes the heart of the matter explicit. He has acquired “Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon,” as his wife. This is the first time the book says which son Ruth had married. Her Moabite identity is named again at the very moment of her redemption. She comes from a people who had opposed Israel and were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6), yet she is now publicly received as the wife of an honored man of Bethlehem. Ruth is not purchased property; Boaz takes on the responsibilities of a husband and redeemer. Her welcome is a public display of God’s redeeming grace toward a woman from a people who had been Israel’s enemies.

“to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from his position at the gate.”

Boaz states his purpose: to keep Mahlon’s name alive in connection with his land, so that it will not disappear from his family or from the public life of the town. A son born to Ruth will carry on Mahlon’s line.

“You are witnesses this day.”

Boaz closes his declaration as he began it, calling everyone present to witness. Boaz’s costly commitment is complete and public. In this he becomes a picture of Jesus Christ, the greater Redeemer, who willingly pays the price to redeem His people and receives them as His own. Christ’s redemption by His blood far exceeds a property and marriage transaction, but Boaz’s willing, public act points toward it.

Study Notes

“Acquired” (4:10) translates the Hebrew verb qanah, the same verb used of buying the land in 4:9. The verb is used of acquiring a wife in a legal sense; it does not imply that Ruth was a slave.

“His position at the gate” literally reads ‘the gate of his place,’ referring to his standing in the public life of his hometown.

The people at the gate and the elders answer, “We are witnesses,” and pray that the LORD will make the woman coming into Boaz’s house like Rachel and Leah, who built the house of Israel. They also pray that Boaz will prosper in Ephrathah and be famous in Bethlehem, placing Ruth beside the mothers of Israel.

“And all the people who were at the gate, and the elders, said, “We are witnesses.”

The people and the elders respond as one: “We are witnesses.” The redemption is now confirmed by the whole community.

“The LORD make the woman who is coming to your house like Rachel and Leah, the two who built the house of Israel;”

The witnesses go further and pronounce a blessing. They pray that the LORD will make Ruth like Rachel and Leah, Jacob’s wives, the mothers of the tribes of Israel. The Moabite woman is placed beside the founding mothers of God’s people.

“and may you prosper in Ephrathah and be famous in Bethlehem.”

They also pray that Boaz will prosper and that his name will be honored in his hometown. “Ephrathah” is the clan and older name associated with Bethlehem (1:2), the area where Rachel was buried (Genesis 35:19).

Study Notes

“Built” (4:11) translates the Hebrew verb banah, which sounds like the Hebrew word ben, son. To ‘build a house’ often means to establish a family through children (compare Deuteronomy 25:9).

“Prosper” translates a Hebrew expression using chayil, the word that described Boaz in 2:1 and Ruth in 3:11.

Verse Explanation

The witnesses pray that Boaz’s house will be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah, through the offspring the LORD will give him by this young woman. They look to the LORD to continue Judah’s line through Ruth, as He once did through another widow.

“May your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah,”

The blessing continues with Perez, son of Judah and Tamar, the ancestor of the clan to which Boaz belonged (4:18). Tamar was a widow in Judah’s family whose son continued the family line when the duty owed to her had been neglected (Genesis 38). The parallel concerns the continuation of Judah’s line through a widow, not identical actions; Tamar’s deception is not Ruth’s pattern.

“because of the offspring which the LORD will give you from this young woman.”

The witnesses look to the LORD to give Boaz children through Ruth. Their prayer recognizes that only the LORD can give the son who will continue the line.

Study Notes

Perez’s birth is recorded in Genesis 38:27–30, and he heads the genealogy in Ruth 4:18–22. The blessing thus anticipates the closing genealogy.

“Young woman” (4:12) translates the Hebrew word na‘arah, the same word used for Ruth in 2:5–6.

Ruth 4:13–17 — The LORD Gives a Son, and Naomi’s Family Is Restored

Ruth 4:13
Ruth 4:13
NKJV

Ruth 4:13 So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:13 So Boaz took Ruth into his home, and she became his wife. When he slept with her, the LORD enabled her to become pregnant, and she gave birth to a son. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz takes Ruth as his wife, and the LORD gives her conception, and she bears a son. The narrator states the LORD’s direct action for only the second time in the book, and the woman who had no child in Moab now receives the gift of life.

Verse Explanation

Boaz takes Ruth as his wife, and the LORD gives her conception, and she bears a son. The narrator states the LORD’s direct action for only the second time in the book, and the woman who had no child in Moab now receives the gift of life.

“So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife;”

Boaz marries Ruth. The marriage that Ruth requested at the threshing floor (3:9) and that Boaz secured at the gate is now a reality. Naomi’s prayer that Ruth would find rest in the house of a husband (1:9) is answered.

“and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son.”

“Went in to her” is the customary expression for the consummation of marriage. Then “the LORD gave her conception.” This is the narrator’s second direct statement of the LORD’s action, matching His gift of bread in 1:6. Ruth had no child during about ten years in Moab; now the LORD gives life. The son she bears is the LORD’s gift and the answer to the family’s emptiness.

Study Notes

“Conception” (4:13) translates the Hebrew noun herayon, a noun that occurs elsewhere only in Genesis 3:16 and Hosea 9:11.

The text says only that Ruth had no child in Moab. It does not say whether she was barren, and her earlier childlessness is not presented as a judgment.

The women of Bethlehem bless the LORD before Naomi because He has not left her that day without a close relative, and they pray that his name will be famous in Israel. Their praise most likely refers to the newborn child as the one who will carry on and restore her family.

“Then the women said to Naomi, “Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a close relative;”

The women of Bethlehem, who once asked “Is this Naomi?” (1:19), now bless the LORD before her. They praise Him because He has not left Naomi without “a close relative,” a redeemer. Most likely they mean the newborn child, who will carry on her family and care for her. Some interpreters understand the redeemer here to be Boaz.

“and may his name be famous in Israel!”

The women pray that the child’s name will become famous in Israel. The prayer will be answered beyond what they could know, for this child will be the grandfather of David.

Study Notes

The close relative of 4:14 is most naturally the child, since 4:15 continues to speak of the one Ruth “has borne.” Some interpreters take it as Boaz, who has acted as the family's redeemer (the Hebrew go’el) in 4:1–10. The grammar allows either reading.

“Child” here is the Hebrew word yeled, the word used of Naomi’s sons in 1:5.

“Also the neighbor women gave him a name, saying, “There is a son born to Naomi.”

The neighbor women give the child his name and celebrate that “a son” has been born to Naomi. They do not mean that Naomi was his biological mother; they mean that her family line has been restored. The emptiness of 1:21 has been answered.

“And they called his name Obed.”

The child is named “Obed.” The book does not explain the name or develop its meaning.

“He is the father of Jesse, the father of David.”

Suddenly the story widens. Obed becomes the father of Jesse, and Jesse the father of David, Israel’s great king. This household’s story now leads to Israel’s king. God’s quiet care for one family in the days of the judges has been part of His larger purpose all along.

Study Notes

Naming by neighbor women is unusual in the Old Testament, where a parent normally names the child.

Obed is commonly understood to mean ‘servant’ or ‘worshiper,’ but the text draws no significance from the meaning.

“Now this is the genealogy of Perez:”

The book closes with a genealogy, a family record. It begins with Perez, the son of Judah and Tamar named in the blessing of 4:12.

“Perez begot Hezron;”

Perez fathered Hezron, and the line of Judah begins to unfold.

Study Notes

“Genealogy” (4:18) translates the Hebrew word toledot, the word used for the family records that structure the book of Genesis (for example Genesis 5:1; 11:27).

The same line appears in 1 Chronicles 2:5–15 and Matthew 1:3–6. Biblical genealogies may be selective, listing representative names rather than every generation, so this list does not by itself establish the length of the period it covers.

Verse Explanation

Obed fathers Jesse, and Jesse fathers David. The book ends by showing that God’s care for one family in the days of the judges led to Israel’s king, and through him, in the larger story of Scripture, to Jesus Christ.

“Obed begot Jesse, and Jesse begot David.”

The book ends with David. A story that began “in the days when the judges ruled” (1:1) closes with the name of Israel’s king. God’s care for one emptied household led to the line of David. In the larger story of Scripture it led further still: Matthew includes Boaz and Ruth in the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David (Matthew 1:5–6, 16). Ruth the Moabitess, welcomed by grace, became part of the family through which the Redeemer of the world was born.

Study Notes

The genealogy contains ten names from Perez to David, with Boaz in the seventh position. Whether the arrangement is deliberate is debated.
