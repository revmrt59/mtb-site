Chapter Study

Ruth 4

Verse-by-Verse
Ruth 4:1

NKJV

Ruth 4:1 Now Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there; and behold, the close relative of whom Boaz had spoken came by. So Boaz said, "Come aside, friend, sit down here." So he came aside and sat down.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:1 Boaz went to the town gate and took a seat there. Just then the family redeemer he had mentioned came by, so Boaz called out to him, “Come over here and sit down, friend. I want to talk to you.” So they sat down together. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz immediately begins the process of settling Ruth’s redemption by going to the city gate, where legal and community matters were handled. When the nearer relative arrives, Boaz calls him aside so the matter can be addressed properly.

Verse Explanation

“Now Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there;”

Boaz does exactly what Naomi expected: he wastes no time. The “gate” was more than an entrance into the town. In ancient Israel, the area at the city gate served as an important public gathering place where elders met, disputes were heard, agreements were witnessed, and community business could be conducted.

Boaz “sat down there” because he intends to handle the matter openly and properly. His desire to redeem Ruth does not lead him to bypass the nearer relative’s rights.

“and behold, the close relative of whom Boaz had spoken came by.”

The timing is striking. Boaz has taken his place at the gate, and the very man he needs to see comes by. “Behold” draws attention to his arrival. The narrative does not describe a miracle, but within Ruth’s larger story it is another ordinary-looking circumstance through which God’s providential care is quietly moving events forward.

This is the “close relative” Boaz mentioned in Ruth 3:12, the man whose claim as redeemer came before Boaz’s.

“So Boaz said, ‘Come aside, friend, sit down here.’”

Boaz takes the initiative. The word translated “friend” does not give us the man’s actual name. It is an indefinite form of address, something like “so-and-so” or “such a one.” The man remains unnamed throughout the account.

“So he came aside and sat down.”

The relative agrees, but Boaz has not yet presented the matter. Before doing so, Boaz will gather ten elders of the city so that what happens will be public and properly witnessed.

Study Notes

The “gate” is important for understanding what follows. Biblical references to someone sitting or acting “in the gate” can involve civic or judicial activity, not simply standing near a doorway. This helps explain why Boaz goes there rather than searching for the relative privately.

The unnamed “friend” creates an interesting contrast in the story. Boaz, who willingly takes responsibility for Ruth and Naomi, has his name preserved and honored, while the nearer redeemer who will decline remains unnamed. The text does not explicitly tell us that this is intended as judgment, so that contrast should not be pressed too far, but it is a noticeable feature of the narrative.

Spirit & Life

Boaz’s integrity is as important as his eagerness. He wants Ruth, but he will not secure her by ignoring what is right. He pursues redemption honorably, openly, and at personal cost.

There is also something beautiful in the movement from Ruth 3:18 to Ruth 4:1. Ruth is told to “sit still,” while Boaz goes to work. What Ruth cannot settle for herself, the redeemer undertakes on her behalf. The story has not yet reached its conclusion, but everything now depends upon the redeemer completing what he has begun-a pattern that finds its fullest expression in Christ.

Ruth 4:2

NKJV

Ruth 4:2 And he took ten men of the elders of the city, and said, "Sit down here." So they sat down.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:2 Then Boaz called ten leaders from the town and asked them to sit as witnesses. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz gathers ten of Bethlehem’s elders to witness the matter he is about to settle. He handles Ruth and Naomi’s redemption publicly and properly, ensuring that the decision will be recognized by the community.

Verse Explanation

“And he took ten men of the elders of the city,”

Boaz brings together “ten men” from among Bethlehem’s elders. These were respected men who carried responsibility in the community and could serve as witnesses in important public matters.

The verse does not explain why Boaz chose exactly ten. What matters here is that he is not arranging a private agreement with the nearer relative. He is establishing a public setting in which the transaction can be witnessed and confirmed.

“and said, ‘Sit down here.’”

Boaz directs the elders to take their places at the gate. Their presence gives the coming discussion a formal and accountable setting.

“So they sat down.”

The simplicity of this statement moves the account quickly forward. Everyone necessary is now in place: Boaz, the nearer relative, and the elders who will witness what happens. Boaz is ready to present the matter.

Study Notes

The “elders of the city” were recognized community leaders rather than simply elderly men. Throughout the Old Testament, elders appear in matters involving leadership, judgment, disputes, family affairs, and public decisions.

Scripture does not state that ten witnesses were universally required for a transaction like this, so we should not read a later legal requirement back into the verse. Here, ten elders provide a substantial and unmistakably public body of witnesses.

Spirit & Life

Boaz continues to show that godly intentions should be carried out in godly ways. He could have viewed the nearer relative as an obstacle to what he wanted, but instead he respects the proper process and brings everything into the open.

That integrity is part of what makes Boaz such a compelling picture of honorable redemption. He is not merely willing to redeem; he is willing to do what redemption requires and to do it rightly.

Ruth 4:3

NKJV

Ruth 4:3 Then he said to the close relative, "Naomi, who has come back from the country of Moab, sold the piece of land which belonged to our brother Elimelech.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:3 And Boaz said to the family redeemer, “You know Naomi, who came back from Moab. She is selling the land that belonged to our relative Elimelech. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz begins his case by telling the nearer relative that Naomi is disposing of the family property that belonged to Elimelech. This gives the nearer relative the first opportunity to exercise his family right of redemption.

Verse Explanation

“Then he said to the close relative,”

With the nearer relative and the ten elders now seated at the gate, Boaz begins presenting the matter. He addresses the man who has a closer family claim than he does and therefore must be given the first opportunity to act.

“‘Naomi, who has come back from the country of Moab,’”

Boaz identifies Naomi and reminds those present of her circumstances. She has returned from Moab after the deaths of her husband, Elimelech, and her sons. Her situation involves not merely property but the future of a family whose male heirs have died.

“‘sold the piece of land which belonged to our brother Elimelech.’”

The NKJV says Naomi “sold” the land, though the Hebrew form can describe an action being undertaken rather than necessarily a transaction already completed. The following verse shows that the opportunity to redeem the property is still open, so Boaz is presenting the land as available for the relative to acquire through redemption.

“Our brother Elimelech” uses “brother” in the broader family sense of a relative or kinsman. Boaz is not necessarily saying that Elimelech was his literal brother.

Study Notes

The wording “sold the piece of land” can initially be confusing because verse 4 proceeds as though the nearer relative can still redeem it. The Hebrew verb can be understood in the sense that Naomi is offering or proceeding to sell the property. The essential point is that Elimelech’s family land is now at issue and the family redeemer has the opportunity to preserve it within the family line.

This reflects the principle behind Leviticus 25:25, where a close relative could redeem family property that had been sold because of hardship. Israel’s land laws were designed to keep ancestral property from being permanently lost from a family.

Spirit & Life

Boaz begins with the responsibility that comes with being a redeemer. Redemption in Ruth is not sentimental; it involves stepping into another person’s loss and willingly assuming a cost to restore what might otherwise be lost.

That gives depth to the Bible’s larger language of redemption. God’s redemption does not merely express compassion from a distance. In Christ, the Redeemer enters our need and bears the cost necessary to bring us back to Himself.

Ruth 4:4

NKJV

Ruth 4:4 And I thought to inform you, saying, "˜Buy it back in the presence of the inhabitants and the elders of my people. If you will redeem it, redeem it; but if you will not redeem it, then tell me, that I may know; for there is no one but you to redeem it, and I am next after you.' " And he said, "I will redeem it."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:4 I thought I should speak to you about it so that you can redeem it if you wish. If you want the land, then buy it here in the presence of these witnesses. But if you don’t want it, let me know right away, because I am next in line to redeem it after you.” The man replied, “All right, I’ll redeem it.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz formally gives the nearer relative the first opportunity to redeem Elimelech’s land. When the man agrees, Boaz is ready to reveal that redeeming the property also carries a responsibility involving Ruth and the continuation of the family line.

Verse Explanation

“‘And I thought to inform you, saying,’”

Boaz explains why he has called the nearer relative aside. Since this man has the first right of redemption, Boaz will not act until he has been given the opportunity to exercise that right.

“‘Buy it back in the presence of the inhabitants and the elders of my people.’”

The transaction is deliberately public. Boaz presents the matter before the elders and others present at the gate so that any decision concerning Elimelech’s property will have recognized witnesses.

“‘If you will redeem it, redeem it; but if you will not redeem it, then tell me, that I may know;’”

Boaz asks for a definite decision. The nearer relative may exercise his right, but if he declines, Boaz is prepared to take his place. Boaz is interested in redemption, but he refuses to disregard the right of the man who stands before him in the family order.

“‘for there is no one but you to redeem it, and I am next after you.’”

This explains the situation clearly. Of those eligible to act, this man has the first right of redemption, while Boaz stands next in line. This is why Boaz could not simply promise Ruth at the threshing floor that he would marry her; another man’s legitimate claim had to be resolved first.

“And he said, ‘I will redeem it.’”

The response creates an immediate complication. The nearer relative wants the property and agrees to redeem it.

At this point, it appears that Boaz may not become Ruth’s redeemer after all. But Boaz has not yet finished explaining what the redemption entails. Verse 5 will introduce Ruth into the matter, and that additional responsibility will change the man’s decision.

Study Notes

The expression translated “I thought to inform you” is literally connected with uncovering or opening the ear-an idiomatic way of saying that Boaz is bringing the matter to the man’s attention.

The exact property arrangements behind the scene are not explained in enough detail to reconstruct every legal step with certainty. The narrative’s essential point is clear: the nearer relative possesses the first right to redeem Elimelech’s property, and Boaz may act only if that man declines.

There is deliberate tension in “I will redeem it.” The story has spent considerable time developing Boaz as Ruth’s potential redeemer, yet the nearer relative initially accepts. The obstacle Naomi and Ruth could not resolve for themselves must now be worked through.

Spirit & Life

Boaz is willing to lose what he desires rather than obtain it wrongly. He honors the nearer relative’s legitimate right even though doing so could cost him Ruth. His integrity is not dependent upon getting the outcome he wants.

There is also a growing emphasis on the willingness of the redeemer. Boaz has already said, “I will redeem you” (Ruth 3:13), and now another man says, “I will redeem it.” The difference between those two commitments will soon become clear. The true cost of redemption has not yet been disclosed, and the question will become not simply who has the right to redeem, but who is willing to bear that cost.

Ruth 4:5

NKJV

Ruth 4:5 Then Boaz said, "On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:5 Then Boaz told him, “Of course, your purchase of the land from Naomi also requires that you marry Ruth, the Moabite widow. That way she can have children who will carry on her husband’s name and keep the land in the family.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz explains that redeeming Elimelech’s property also means taking Ruth as wife so that the deceased man’s family line and inheritance can continue. The nearer relative must now decide whether he is willing to accept the full responsibility of redemption.

Verse Explanation

“Then Boaz said, ‘On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi,’”

The nearer relative has just agreed to redeem the land, but Boaz now explains that the transaction involves more than acquiring property. The field belongs to the family situation created by the deaths of Elimelech and his sons, so the man must consider the responsibilities attached to it.

“‘you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead,’”

Ruth is identified as “the wife of the dead,” referring to her deceased husband, Mahlon. The point is not simply that Ruth comes with the property as though she were property herself. Rather, redemption of the family inheritance is connected with responsibility for the widow and the continuation of the deceased man’s family line.

The wording “Ruth the Moabitess” again reminds us that Ruth is a foreigner. Yet she is now being publicly included within the responsibilities of Israel’s family redemption.

“‘to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance.’”

This explains the purpose. If Ruth bears a son through this arrangement, the family line connected with her deceased husband will not disappear, and the inheritance will remain associated with that family.

The issue, therefore, is larger than land. Redemption means preserving a family name and inheritance that otherwise stood in danger of disappearing.

Study Notes

Ruth 4 combines two related but distinct Old Testament ideas: redemption of family property, described in Leviticus 25:25, and the preservation of a deceased man’s family line through marriage, reflected in Deuteronomy 25:5-10. Ruth’s situation does not fit the Deuteronomy law exactly, since Boaz and the nearer relative are not presented as Mahlon’s brothers. The narrative appears to bring these family responsibilities together according to the accepted customs of Bethlehem.

There is also a textual difficulty in this verse. Some ancient textual evidence supports a reading corresponding to “you must also buy/acquire,” while the traditional Hebrew text can be understood as Boaz saying, in effect, “I acquire Ruth.” The surrounding context strongly supports the NKJV’s basic sense: the nearer redeemer is being told that Ruth is part of the responsibility he must accept if he redeems the property.

Spirit & Life

Verse 4 made redemption appear advantageous: there was land to acquire. Verse 5 reveals the cost. The redeemer cannot simply take what benefits him; he must accept responsibility for the people whose loss created the need for redemption.

That deepens the picture of Boaz as the story moves forward. He is not interested merely in Elimelech’s property. He is willing to embrace Ruth and preserve the family line at personal cost. Redemption is costly because genuine redemption seeks the restoration of the one who needs to be redeemed-a truth ultimately displayed in Christ, who did not redeem us because of what He could gain from us, but gave Himself to make us His own.

Ruth 4:6

NKJV

Ruth 4:6 And the close relative said, "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance. You redeem my right of redemption for yourself, for I cannot redeem it."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:6 “Then I can’t redeem it,” the family redeemer replied, “because this might endanger my own estate. You redeem the land; I cannot do it.” (NLT)

Main Idea

When the nearer relative realizes that redemption includes Ruth and responsibility for preserving the deceased man’s family line, he withdraws because he fears damaging his own inheritance. He therefore passes his right of redemption to Boaz.

Verse Explanation

“And the close relative said, ‘I cannot redeem it for myself,’”

The nearer relative reverses his decision from verse 4. He was willing to redeem the land when that appeared to be the extent of his obligation, but Boaz’s explanation concerning Ruth changes his calculation.

“‘lest I ruin my own inheritance.’”

His concern is financial and familial. If he marries Ruth and a son is born to continue the deceased man’s line, the property being redeemed would ultimately be associated with that line rather than simply increasing the redeemer’s own estate. At the same time, he would assume the expense and responsibility involved in the redemption.

Scripture does not tell us the exact details of his personal circumstances, so it is better not to reconstruct precisely how his inheritance would be endangered. What the text makes clear is that he believes accepting the full responsibility would put his own inheritance at risk.

“‘You redeem my right of redemption for yourself,’”

The nearer relative formally yields his opportunity to Boaz. Since Boaz is next in the family order, the way is now open for him to act as redeemer.

“‘for I cannot redeem it.’”

His repeated “I cannot redeem it” emphasizes his refusal. This does not mean he lacked the legal right to redeem; he has just surrendered that right. Nor does it necessarily mean he was financially incapable. In context, he is unwilling to redeem because of what doing so could cost his own inheritance.

Study Notes

The nearer relative’s concern about “my own inheritance” probably arises from the combination of financial cost and succession. He could spend his own resources redeeming the land while a son born through Ruth could preserve the property in the deceased family’s line. The text gives the principle but not enough information to calculate the exact financial consequences.

There is an important contrast between “I cannot redeem it” and Boaz’s earlier promise, “I will redeem you” (Ruth 3:13). One redeemer has the right but withdraws when he sees the cost; Boaz is willing to assume that cost.

Spirit & Life

The nearer relative is willing to redeem when redemption appears beneficial, but he steps away when redemption threatens something of his own. Boaz will do what the nearer relative will not: accept the cost necessary to restore another person’s future.

That makes the unfolding picture of redemption especially powerful. Ruth’s future depends upon a redeemer who is not merely able to redeem but willing to pay the price. In Christ, we find exactly such a Redeemer. He knew the full cost beforehand and did not turn away: “Christ also has loved us and given Himself for us” (Ephesians 5:2).

Ruth 4:7

NKJV

Ruth 4:7 Now this was the custom in former times in Israel concerning redeeming and exchanging, to confirm anything: one man took off his sandal and gave it to the other, and this was a confirmation in Israel.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:7 Now in those days it was the custom in Israel for anyone transferring a right of purchase to remove his sandal and hand it to the other party. This publicly validated the transaction. (NLT)

Main Idea

The narrator pauses to explain an old Israelite custom: transferring a sandal publicly confirmed the transfer of a right or transaction. This explains the formal act that will confirm the nearer relative’s surrender of his right to Boaz.

Verse Explanation

“Now this was the custom in former times in Israel concerning redeeming and exchanging,”

The story briefly pauses to explain a custom that apparently was no longer familiar when Ruth was written. In earlier Israel, certain transactions involving “redeeming and exchanging” could be formally confirmed through the symbolic transfer of a sandal.

“to confirm anything:”

The sandal was not a casual gesture. In this setting, it served as a visible confirmation that an agreement had been made. With the elders present as witnesses, there would be public evidence that the right of redemption had been transferred.

“one man took off his sandal and gave it to the other,”

The nearer relative will relinquish his sandal as he relinquishes his right to redeem. The physical action makes the transfer concrete and publicly recognizable.

The sandal may have had a natural association with one’s right to tread upon or possess land, but the verse itself does not explain the symbolism, so that connection should not be treated as certain.

“and this was a confirmation in Israel.”

The act formally confirmed what the men had agreed upon. The following verse will show the nearer relative using this custom as he tells Boaz, “Buy it for yourself.” The way is then legally and publicly cleared for Boaz to act.

Study Notes

The phrase “in former times in Israel” is worth noticing. The writer explains the practice because the original audience apparently needed clarification. This is one of those places where the Bible itself explains an older cultural custom for later readers.

This sandal ceremony should be distinguished from Deuteronomy 25:9-10. There, a widow removes the sandal of a brother-in-law who refuses his levirate responsibility, and the act carries public shame. Here the man removes his own sandal and gives it to another as confirmation of a transaction. The customs involve sandals, but they are not identical ceremonies.

Spirit & Life

Redemption in Ruth is being settled publicly and decisively. Ruth does not have to wonder whether Boaz truly has the right to act for her. The obstacle is being formally removed before witnesses.

There is something reassuring in that picture when we think of Christ. Biblical redemption is not merely a hopeful possibility or an expression of good intentions. Jesus accomplished what was necessary to redeem His people. Our confidence rests not in an uncertain arrangement but in a finished work.

Ruth 4:8

NKJV

Ruth 4:8 Therefore the close relative said to Boaz, "Buy it for yourself." So he took off his sandal.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:8 So the other family redeemer drew off his sandal as he said to Boaz, “You buy the land.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The nearer relative formally gives up his right to redeem the property, clearing the way for Boaz to become the redeemer. The removal of his sandal publicly confirms that decision before the witnesses.

Verse Explanation

“Therefore the close relative said to Boaz, ‘Buy it for yourself.’”

The nearer relative now makes his decision explicit. In verse 6 he had told Boaz to exercise the right of redemption in his place; here he formally authorizes Boaz to proceed.

“Buy it for yourself” means that Boaz may now take responsibility for redeeming Elimelech’s property and, as Boaz explained in verse 5, accept the accompanying responsibility involving Ruth and the continuation of the deceased man’s family line.

The obstacle that stood between Boaz and Ruth has now been removed. Boaz is free to do what he had promised Ruth he would do if the nearer relative declined.

“So he took off his sandal.”

The nearer relative performs the customary action explained in verse 7. By removing his sandal, he publicly confirms that he is relinquishing his right in favor of Boaz.

This is more than a private understanding between two men. The transaction takes place at the city gate before the elders, leaving no ambiguity about who now possesses the right to act as redeemer.

Study Notes

The verse does not explicitly say “and gave it to Boaz,” although verse 7 explains that the custom involved one man taking off his sandal and giving it to the other. The abbreviated wording assumes the explanation just given.

It is striking that the nearer relative remains unnamed. He is repeatedly called simply the “close relative.” The man who was concerned about preserving “my own inheritance” (verse 6) disappears from the story without his name being preserved, while Boaz’s willingness to redeem becomes part of the family line that ultimately leads to David. The narrative does not explicitly state that this irony is intentional, but the contrast is difficult to miss.

Spirit & Life

Everything that prevented Boaz from acting as redeemer has now been removed. Ruth herself does nothing in this scene. The matter is being settled entirely by the redeemer acting on her behalf.

There is a beautiful progression through these verses: Boaz was willing to redeem, he had the resources to redeem, and now he has the undisputed right to redeem. The story is moving toward a completed redemption. That provides a fitting window into Christ: our Redeemer is not merely sympathetic toward our need. He is willing, able, and fully sufficient to accomplish the redemption He undertakes.

Ruth 4:9

NKJV

Ruth 4:9 And Boaz said to the elders and all the people, "You are witnesses this day that I have bought all that was Elimelech's, and all that was Chilion's and Mahlon's, from the hand of Naomi.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:9 Then Boaz said to the elders and to the crowd standing around, “You are witnesses that today I have bought from Naomi all the property of Elimelech, Kilion, and Mahlon. (NLT)

Main Idea

With the nearer relative’s claim surrendered, Boaz publicly declares that he has redeemed all the property belonging to Elimelech’s family. The elders and those gathered at the gate serve as witnesses that the redemption is now formally established.

Verse Explanation

“And Boaz said to the elders and all the people,”

Boaz now addresses the assembled witnesses. The matter has been handled openly at the city gate, with the nearer relative present and his prior right formally relinquished.

“‘You are witnesses this day’”

Boaz explicitly establishes those present as witnesses. Their role is important because the redemption is not a private promise between Boaz and Ruth. What Boaz is doing is publicly recognized and can be confirmed by the community.

“‘that I have bought all that was Elimelech’s,’”

With the nearer relative’s right removed, Boaz formally acquires the property associated with Elimelech. “Bought” here belongs to the redemption transaction that has been discussed throughout the scene: Boaz is paying what is necessary to recover and preserve the family property.

“‘and all that was Chilion’s and Mahlon’s, from the hand of Naomi.’”

Boaz carefully identifies the entire family interest involved. Elimelech and both of his sons-Chilion and Mahlon-have died, leaving Naomi as the surviving family representative. Boaz’s action therefore concerns not merely one field but everything belonging to this family estate that is involved in the redemption.

This also prepares for verse 10. Boaz is not simply recovering property; he is acting so that the family name and inheritance will not disappear.

Study Notes

The language “I have bought” should be understood within the redemption setting of the chapter. Boaz is not simply purchasing property as an investment. He is exercising the family redeemer’s role after the nearer relative has formally surrendered his prior claim.

The mention of “Elimelech’s,” “Chilion’s,” and “Mahlon’s” emphasizes the extent of the family loss. All three men introduced in Ruth 1:2 are dead. What appeared in chapter 1 to be a family line collapsing through death is now being restored through redemption.

Spirit & Life

The words “You are witnesses this day” mark a major turning point. What began with Naomi returning to Bethlehem grieving and describing herself as “empty” is now being answered through a public act of redemption. God has been working through ordinary faithfulness, relationships, provision, and seemingly small circumstances to bring the story here.

Boaz also does more than promise redemption. He completes the necessary transaction and publicly declares what he has done. That gives the picture of redemption particular strength: the biblical hope of redemption rests not merely upon a redeemer’s good intentions but upon a work actually accomplished. In Christ, redemption is something He has secured for us at cost to Himself.

Ruth 4:10

NKJV

Ruth 4:10 Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from his position at the gate. You are witnesses this day."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:10 And with the land I have acquired Ruth, the Moabite widow of Mahlon, to be my wife. This way she can have a son to carry on the family name of her dead husband and to inherit the family property here in his hometown. You are all witnesses today.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz publicly declares that he is taking Ruth as his wife so that Mahlon’s family line and inheritance will continue. His redemption embraces both the property and the person, restoring a future to a family that had been left without an heir.

Verse Explanation

“‘Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife,’”

Boaz now publicly declares what has been moving toward fulfillment since Ruth approached him at the threshing floor. He will marry Ruth.

Calling her “Ruth the Moabitess” once again emphasizes her remarkable journey. The woman who entered Bethlehem as a foreign widow is now publicly received into the family through marriage to Boaz. Her Moabite background has not been forgotten, but neither does it prevent her from finding a place among God’s people.

“‘to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance,’”

Boaz explains why the marriage is connected with redemption. His purpose includes preserving the family line of Mahlon, Ruth’s deceased husband. A son born through Ruth would ensure that the deceased family’s name and inheritance did not simply disappear.

Boaz is therefore doing more than acquiring Elimelech’s property or marrying the woman he loves. He is willingly assuming responsibility for restoring the future of a family devastated by death.

“‘that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from his position at the gate.’”

To have one’s name “cut off” meant the family line would disappear from Israel because there was no descendant to carry it forward. Boaz acts so that this will not happen.

The reference to “his position at the gate” connects the family name with its recognized place in the community. The very gate where the matter is now being settled will continue to recognize the family rather than remembering it as a line that vanished.

“‘You are witnesses this day.’”

Boaz ends as he did in verse 9 by calling upon everyone present as witnesses. His commitment to Ruth and to the family he is redeeming is public, definite, and accountable.

There is no secrecy and no uncertainty about what Boaz has undertaken. The redemption has been openly declared before the community.

Study Notes

The expression “I have acquired as my wife” should not be understood as though Ruth were property being purchased. The language belongs to the legal and economic setting of the redemption transaction. Boaz is publicly assuming the responsibilities connected with marrying Ruth and preserving the deceased family’s line.

Ruth’s first husband is explicitly identified here as Mahlon. Ruth 1:4 names Mahlon and Chilion but does not tell us which man married Ruth and which married Orpah. Ruth 4:10 supplies that information.

Boaz’s action resembles the purpose of levirate marriage in Deuteronomy 25:5-6-preserving the name of a man who died without an heir-but it is not a straightforward application of that law, since Boaz is not Mahlon’s brother. Ruth presents a broader family-redemption arrangement in which preservation of the land, widow, and family line are brought together.

Spirit & Life

Boaz’s redemption reaches into everything death had taken from this family. There had been lost husbands, threatened inheritance, an endangered family name, widowhood, poverty, and an uncertain future. Boaz willingly steps into that broken situation and uses what is his to restore what they could not restore themselves.

That makes Boaz one of Scripture’s beautiful anticipations of Christ without requiring us to turn every detail into an allegory. Jesus does not redeem us merely by recovering something around us; He takes us to Himself. Redemption leads to relationship. The outsider is brought near, the hopeless are given a future, and what sin and death had threatened to destroy is answered by the grace and power of the Redeemer.

Ruth 4:11

NKJV

Ruth 4:11 And all the people who were at the gate, and the elders, said, "We are witnesses. The LORD make the woman who is coming to your house like Rachel and Leah, the two who built the house of Israel; and may you prosper in Ephrathah and be famous in Bethlehem.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:11 Then the elders and all the people standing in the gate replied, “We are witnesses! May the LORD make this woman who is coming into your home like Rachel and Leah, from whom all the nation of Israel descended! May you prosper in Ephrathah and be famous in Bethlehem. (NLT)

Main Idea

The elders and people publicly confirm Boaz’s redemption and bless his marriage to Ruth. They pray that God will make Ruth fruitful like Rachel and Leah and give Boaz an honored legacy in Bethlehem.

Verse Explanation

“And all the people who were at the gate, and the elders, said, ‘We are witnesses.’”

The people formally confirm Boaz’s declaration from verses 9-10. The transaction is now publicly witnessed and recognized. The nearer relative has relinquished his right, and Boaz has openly taken responsibility for the property, Ruth, and the continuation of the family line.

“‘The LORD make the woman who is coming to your house like Rachel and Leah,’”

The witnesses move from legal confirmation to blessing. They ask the LORD to make Ruth fruitful, specifically recalling Rachel and Leah, the wives of Jacob through whom, together with their maidservants, the twelve sons who became the tribes of Israel were born.

This is especially striking because Ruth is still identified in the story as a Moabitess. The people of Bethlehem now place this foreign-born woman alongside Israel’s great matriarchs and pray that God will give her a similarly fruitful place in the continuing story of His people.

“‘the two who built the house of Israel;’”

Rachel and Leah “built the house of Israel” through the family that came from them. Here “house” means more than a physical dwelling; it refers to a family or household continuing through descendants.

That language fits Ruth’s situation perfectly. Death had nearly extinguished Elimelech’s household. The people are praying that Ruth, through her marriage to Boaz, will become an instrument through whom that house is rebuilt.

“‘and may you prosper in Ephrathah and be famous in Bethlehem.’”

The blessing now turns toward Boaz. “Ephrathah” is closely associated with Bethlehem and the surrounding clan or region. The people pray that Boaz will act worthily and that his name will become significant in Bethlehem.

The words prove remarkably prophetic. Boaz’s name will indeed endure far beyond his own generation because the family formed through Ruth will eventually produce David, Bethlehem’s great king-and ultimately Jesus the Messiah.

Study Notes

The expression “built the house of Israel” uses “house” in the common biblical sense of a continuing family or dynasty. It is particularly appropriate after Boaz’s concern that “the name of the dead may not be cut off” (verse 10).

The NKJV “prosper in Ephrathah” translates wording that more literally carries the idea of doing worthily or demonstrating strength. The blessing concerns Boaz’s standing and fruitful significance within the community, not merely material prosperity.

“Ephrathah” and “Bethlehem” are closely connected elsewhere in Scripture (Genesis 35:19; Micah 5:2). That connection becomes especially significant in light of the genealogy at the end of Ruth and the later birth of Jesus in Bethlehem.

Spirit & Life

There is a beautiful reversal taking place. Ruth arrived in Bethlehem as a childless Moabite widow with no obvious future in Israel. Now the people of Bethlehem pray that she will become like “Rachel and Leah,” women foundational to the very “house of Israel.” Grace has moved Ruth from outsider to honored member of God’s people.

And the blessing reaches farther than anyone standing at that gate could have understood. They pray that Boaz will “be famous in Bethlehem.” Generations later, his descendant David will be associated with Bethlehem, and ultimately Jesus will be born there. God was answering their prayer on a scale far beyond what they could see.

Ruth 4:12

NKJV

Ruth 4:12 May your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah, because of the offspring which the LORD will give you from this young woman."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:12 And may the LORD give you descendants by this young woman who will be like those of our ancestor Perez, the son of Tamar and Judah.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The people continue their blessing by praying that Boaz and Ruth will have a fruitful and enduring family like Perez, whose descendants included the people of Bethlehem. Their words connect Ruth’s future directly to the continuing family line through which God will eventually bring David-and ultimately the Messiah.

Verse Explanation

“‘May your house be like the house of Perez,’”

The blessing moves from Rachel and Leah in verse 11 to “Perez.” Here “house” means the family line or descendants that will come from Boaz and Ruth.

Perez was a son of Judah through Tamar (Genesis 38:29). He became an important ancestor within the tribe of Judah, and Boaz himself belonged to his family line. The people are therefore praying that the new household beginning with Boaz and Ruth will become similarly fruitful and significant.

“‘whom Tamar bore to Judah,’”

Mentioning Tamar is especially meaningful. Tamar had been left without the offspring that would continue her deceased husband’s family line, and the events of Genesis 38 eventually resulted in the birth of Perez.

Ruth’s circumstances are not identical to Tamar’s, and the two stories should not be flattened into one another. Yet both involve a threatened family line, a woman whose husband has died, and God preserving the family through which His larger purposes will continue.

“‘because of the offspring which the LORD will give you from this young woman.’”

The people recognize that children ultimately come as a gift from “the LORD.” Boaz can redeem the property and marry Ruth, but only God can give life and establish the household they are praying for.

Calling Ruth “this young woman” recalls the way Boaz has spoken of her earlier. The childless widow who came from Moab is now the subject of a public prayer that the LORD will give her offspring and build a lasting family through her.

Study Notes

Perez is particularly appropriate in this blessing because Boaz himself descends from Perez. Ruth 4:18-22 will trace the genealogy from Perez through Boaz and Obed to David. The blessing therefore anticipates the genealogy that closes the book.

The connection with Tamar also adds an important biblical link. Both Genesis 38 and Ruth concern the preservation of Judah’s family line when its continuation had become endangered. Matthew 1:3, 5 later places both Tamar and Ruth within the genealogy of Jesus.

Spirit & Life

The people pray for a child, but God intends to answer with a legacy far larger than they can imagine. Ruth will bear Obed; Obed will become the grandfather of David; and through David’s line will eventually come Jesus Christ.

That is one of Ruth’s great displays of God’s providence. The people at the gate see a widow being redeemed and a family line being preserved. God sees all of that-and generations beyond it. The ordinary faithfulness of Ruth and Boaz has become part of His unfolding purpose to bring the Redeemer into the world.

Ruth 4:13

NKJV

Ruth 4:13 So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:13 So Boaz took Ruth into his home, and she became his wife. When he slept with her, the LORD enabled her to become pregnant, and she gave birth to a son. (NLT)

Main Idea

Boaz and Ruth are married, and the LORD answers the blessing spoken over them by enabling Ruth to conceive and bear a son. After years of loss and childlessness, the story explicitly identifies God as the giver of new life.

Verse Explanation

“So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife;”

The redemption Boaz publicly declared at the city gate now results in marriage. Ruth, who entered Bethlehem as a widowed foreigner with an uncertain future, becomes the wife of Boaz.

The simple statement “Boaz took Ruth” reflects normal biblical marriage language. It describes Boaz taking Ruth as his wife, not treating her as property.

“and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception,”

The language refers naturally and discreetly to the marital relationship. What stands out, however, is the statement that “the LORD gave her conception.” The narrator explicitly brings God’s activity to the foreground.

Ruth had previously been married to Mahlon for about ten years (Ruth 1:4-5), yet no children are mentioned from that marriage. Now the LORD enables her to conceive. The prayer of the people in verse 12-that the LORD would give Boaz offspring through Ruth-is being answered.

“and she bore a son.”

The son is the beginning of the restored future that has been developing throughout the book. Naomi’s family had faced the loss of its men, its inheritance, and its continuing family line. Through this child, that line will continue.

The significance of this son will extend much farther than Ruth and Boaz can yet know. He will be Obed, the father of Jesse and grandfather of David.

Study Notes

“The LORD gave her conception” is one of the clearest direct statements of God’s activity in Ruth. Much of the book portrays God’s providence through ordinary events-Ruth happening to glean in Boaz’s field, Boaz arriving, the nearer relative passing the gate-but here the narrator directly attributes Ruth’s conception to the LORD.

Ruth’s previous ten-year marriage and apparent childlessness make this moment especially striking, although the text never explicitly says Ruth was infertile. It simply records no children from the first marriage and then specifically says that the LORD enabled conception in her marriage to Boaz.

Spirit & Life

The movement of Ruth’s story is remarkable: widowhood gives way to marriage, barrenness to conception, and the threatened end of a family line to the birth of a son. The LORD has been quietly working through the entire story, but here His hand is named plainly: “the LORD gave her conception.”

And God’s gift is larger than anyone in the story yet understands. Ruth sees a son; Naomi will see a family restored; Israel will eventually see David; and the unfolding biblical story will reveal that this family line leads to Jesus. God’s providence often reaches far beyond the immediate blessing we can see.

Ruth 4:14

NKJV

Ruth 4:14 Then the women said to Naomi, "Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a close relative; and may his name be famous in Israel!(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:14 Then the women of the town said to Naomi, “Praise the LORD, who has now provided a redeemer for your family! May this child be famous in Israel. (NLT)

Main Idea

The women of Bethlehem praise the LORD because Naomi’s emptiness has been answered with restoration. Through the birth of Ruth’s son, God has preserved Naomi’s family line and provided a redeemer whose name will continue in Israel.

Verse Explanation

“Then the women said to Naomi,”

The attention shifts from Ruth and Boaz back to Naomi. This is fitting because Naomi’s loss and emptiness have been central to the book from the beginning. The women who surrounded her when she returned to Bethlehem in Ruth 1:19 now appear again as her story reaches its joyful reversal.

“‘Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a close relative;’”

The women give the credit to “the LORD.” The God whom Naomi once believed had dealt bitterly with her has not abandoned her. He has been working through Ruth, Boaz, the redemption of the family property, the marriage, and now the birth of this child.

Here “close relative” refers most naturally to the newborn son as the continuing family redeemer, rather than to Boaz. The child will carry the family forward and become a source of care and restoration for Naomi. Boaz has accomplished the immediate redemption, but the son ensures that its effects continue into the next generation.

“‘and may his name be famous in Israel!’”

The women pray that the child’s name will become renowned in Israel. Their blessing will be fulfilled far beyond what they could have anticipated.

The child will be named Obed, and he will become the father of Jesse and grandfather of David. Through David’s line will eventually come Jesus the Messiah. A child born into what seemed like a small family story in Bethlehem becomes part of God’s purposes for all Israel-and ultimately for the world.

Study Notes

The identity of the “close relative” in this verse can initially be confusing because Boaz has filled that role throughout chapter 4. However, the wording “who has not left you this day without a close relative” follows immediately after the birth in verse 13, and verses 15-17 continue focusing on the child and what he will mean to Naomi. This strongly favors understanding the newborn son as the referent here.

The Hebrew term is the same family of language used for the goel, the redeemer. The child is not replacing Boaz’s redemptive action; rather, his birth ensures the continuation and future security that Boaz’s redemption was intended to accomplish.

Spirit & Life

Naomi once said, “I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty” (Ruth 1:21). Now the women say, “Blessed be the LORD.” The book has not denied Naomi’s suffering or treated her losses lightly. Instead, it has shown that her painful chapter was not the final chapter.

There is also a beautiful change in what Naomi can see. Earlier she interpreted her circumstances primarily through what had been taken from her. Now the women help her recognize what God has given. The LORD had been working even during the season when Naomi could not yet see where His providence was leading.

Ruth 4:15

NKJV

Ruth 4:15 And may he be to you a restorer of life and a nourisher of your old age; for your daughter-in-law, who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons, has borne him."(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:15 May he restore your youth and care for you in your old age. For he is the son of your daughter-in-law who loves you and has been better to you than seven sons!” (NLT)

Main Idea

The women tell Naomi that the newborn son will bring renewed life and care to her in old age. They also remind her that Ruth, whose extraordinary love has already proven more valuable than many sons, is the mother of this child.

Verse Explanation

“‘And may he be to you a restorer of life’”

The women continue speaking about the son born to Ruth and Boaz. They pray that he will bring renewed joy, hope, and vitality into Naomi’s life after the devastating losses with which the book began.

“A restorer of life” does not mean that the child can undo the deaths of Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion. Rather, God has brought new life and a future into circumstances Naomi once regarded as empty and finished.

“‘and a nourisher of your old age;’”

The child also represents future security for Naomi. As he grows, he will belong to the family through which Naomi can be cared for in her later years.

The story that began with Naomi facing old age without her husband or sons now ends with a new generation before her. God has not merely provided for an immediate need; He has given her hope for the future.

“‘for your daughter-in-law, who loves you,’”

The women specifically honor Ruth. “Who loves you” summarizes what Ruth has demonstrated throughout the book-from refusing to abandon Naomi, to leaving Moab with her, working to provide food, and pursuing the family’s redemption.

Ruth’s love has not been primarily expressed through words. Her entire life in Bethlehem has demonstrated it.

“‘who is better to you than seven sons, has borne him.’”

This is an extraordinary statement in a culture where sons were deeply valued for continuing the family line and providing family security. “Seven” often represents fullness or completeness, so the women are saying that Ruth’s faithful love has proven of surpassing worth to Naomi.

The statement is especially powerful because Ruth is a Moabite daughter-in-law. The woman who might naturally have been considered an outsider has become Naomi’s greatest earthly source of faithful love and has now borne the son through whom the family continues.

Study Notes

“Restorer of life” carries the idea of causing life to return or bringing renewed vitality. In context, it describes the reversal of Naomi’s earlier experience of emptiness and hopelessness rather than literal restoration from death.

The expression “better to you than seven sons” is deliberately emphatic. Seven commonly communicates completeness in Scripture. The women are not making a mathematical comparison; they are declaring the extraordinary value of Ruth’s devoted love to Naomi.

Spirit & Life

One of the most beautiful details in Ruth is that God’s provision for Naomi had been standing beside her before Naomi could recognize it. When Naomi said, “the LORD has brought me home again empty” (Ruth 1:21), Ruth had actually come home with her. Now the women explicitly identify Ruth as one of God’s richest gifts to Naomi-“better to you than seven sons.”

Sometimes God’s restoration begins before we recognize it as restoration. Naomi was looking at what she had lost; God was already working through the faithful woman walking beside her. By the end of the story, what once looked like emptiness has become a place of renewed life, relationship, provision, and hope.

Ruth 4:16

NKJV

Ruth 4:16 Then Naomi took the child and laid him on her bosom, and became a nurse to him.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:16 Naomi took the baby and cuddled him to her breast. And she cared for him as if he were her own. (NLT)

Main Idea

Naomi receives Ruth’s son into her arms and takes a special place in his life. The woman who returned to Bethlehem feeling empty is now holding tangible evidence that God has been quietly restoring what grief had taken from her.

Verse Explanation

“Then Naomi took the child”

The child is Ruth and Boaz’s newborn son, Obed. Yet the verse deliberately focuses on Naomi. Ruth’s marriage and the birth of Obed have brought restoration not only to Ruth but also to Naomi, whose husband and two sons had died in Moab.

“and laid him on her bosom”

This is a tender picture of Naomi holding the baby closely against herself. It expresses affection, belonging, and personal attachment. The child born through Ruth has become deeply connected with Naomi’s own restored family line.

“and became a nurse to him.”

“Nurse” here does not necessarily mean that Naomi breastfed Obed. The idea can refer more broadly to caring for, nurturing, or tending a child. Naomi assumes an active, loving role in Obed’s upbringing, much like what we would call a caregiver.

Study Notes

The movement from Ruth 1 to Ruth 4 is striking. Naomi had said, “I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty” (Ruth 1:21). Now the book gives us Naomi with a child held against her. The story does not erase her losses-Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion remain gone-but God has brought new life into the very place where Naomi believed only emptiness remained.

Obed is not merely a source of personal joy. He will become the grandfather of David (Ruth 4:17), placing this quiet domestic scene inside the unfolding line that ultimately leads to Jesus Christ.

Spirit & Life

There is something beautiful about the final picture of Naomi. Earlier she asked to be called Mara, “Bitter,” because of what she had suffered. Now she is holding Obed close to her heart. God did not restore her by simply recreating the life she had lost; He brought restoration in a form Naomi could not have imagined when she returned to Bethlehem.

God’s restoration does not always mean getting back exactly what was taken. Sometimes He writes something new-something that does not deny yesterday’s sorrow but reveals that sorrow did not have the final word.

Ruth 4:17

NKJV

Ruth 4:17 Also the neighbor women gave him a name, saying, "There is a son born to Naomi." And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:17 The neighbor women said, “Now at last Naomi has a son again!” And they named him Obed. He became the father of Jesse and the grandfather of David. (NLT)

Main Idea

The women of Bethlehem celebrate Naomi’s restoration by recognizing Ruth’s son as belonging to her family, and the child is named Obed. The verse then reveals something far larger than anyone in that Bethlehem neighborhood could have seen: this child would become the grandfather of King David.

Verse Explanation

“Also the neighbor women gave him a name, saying, ‘There is a son born to Naomi.’”

The women who had witnessed Naomi’s sorrow when she returned to Bethlehem now witness her restoration. Their words do not mean that Naomi literally gave birth to Obed; Ruth is his mother. They are celebrating what his birth means for Naomi-her family line has been preserved, and she once again has a son within her household and family.

“And they called his name Obed.”

The name Obed is commonly connected with the idea of “servant” or “one who serves.” The text unusually emphasizes the neighborhood women in connection with his naming, continuing their role in recognizing what God has done for Naomi.

“He is the father of Jesse, the father of David.”

With these words, the significance of the entire story suddenly expands. Obed will father Jesse, and Jesse will father David, Israel’s great king. What began as the story of Naomi’s loss and Ruth’s faithfulness has become part of God’s much larger work in Israel’s history.

Study Notes

The statement “There is a son born to Naomi” should be read in light of the family-redemption theme running through the chapter. Obed provides continuation for the family that Naomi feared had come to an end. The women are celebrating restoration, not confusing Naomi with the child’s biological mother.

David’s appearance at the end also helps explain why this seemingly small family story has such importance in Scripture. Ruth, the Moabite widow who came to Bethlehem with Naomi, becomes David’s great-grandmother-and, through David’s line, an ancestor of Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5-6).

Spirit & Life

Naomi could see a baby; God could see David. And beyond David, the unfolding biblical story leads eventually to Christ. God was doing far more through Ruth and Boaz than any of them could have understood at the time.

That gives this verse a remarkable perspective on ordinary faithfulness. Ruth’s loyalty, Boaz’s integrity, and Naomi’s restoration belonged to a purpose larger than their own lives. Much of what God accomplishes through faithful obedience may reach farther than we will ever live to see.

Ruth 4:18

NKJV

Ruth 4:18 Now this is the genealogy of Perez: Perez begot Hezron;(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:18 This is the genealogical record of their ancestor Perez: Perez was the father of Hezron. (NLT)

Main Idea

The book now traces the family line from Perez toward David. This genealogy shows that the story of Ruth and Boaz belongs to a much larger history of God’s work through generations.

Verse Explanation

“Now this is the genealogy of Perez:”

Perez was the son of Judah and Tamar (Genesis 38:29), making him part of the tribe of Judah. He had already appeared in the blessing spoken over Boaz and Ruth, when the elders prayed that their family would be “like the house of Perez” (Ruth 4:12). The genealogy now returns to Perez and traces the family line forward.

“Perez begot Hezron;”

“Hezron” is the next generation in this family line. The word “begot” identifies descent and does not require us to assume that every generation must necessarily be listed in every biblical genealogy. The emphasis is on establishing the family line that ultimately leads to David.

Study Notes

Perez is significant because he belongs to the line of Judah. Genesis 49:10 associates Judah with future royal rule, and Ruth closes by showing the line through which David, Israel’s great king from Judah, would eventually come.

This genealogy also appears within the genealogy of Jesus in Matthew 1. Matthew traces the line through “Perez and Zerah by Tamar,” then Hezron, and eventually through Boaz, Ruth, Obed, Jesse, and David (Matthew 1:3-6). Ruth therefore ends with a genealogy that reaches David, while the New Testament carries that same family line forward to Jesus Christ.

Spirit & Life

A genealogy can look like little more than a list of names, but each name represents another generation through which God continued His purpose. Perez could not see David, and David’s ancestors could not yet see Christ, but God was steadily carrying His plan forward through them.

Ruth has shown that same truth on a smaller scale. Ordinary decisions of faithfulness became part of something far larger than the people making them could see. God often works across generations, joining lives and acts of obedience into purposes whose full significance becomes visible only later.

Ruth 4:19

NKJV

Ruth 4:19 Hezron begot Ram, and Ram begot Amminadab;(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:19 Hezron was the father of Ram.Ram was the father of Amminadab. (NLT)

Main Idea

The genealogy continues through Hezron, Ram, and Amminadab, quietly tracing the family line that will eventually lead to David-and ultimately to Jesus Christ.

Verse Explanation

“Hezron begot Ram,”

Hezron was a descendant of Perez in the tribe of Judah. The genealogy continues the line introduced in the previous verse, moving steadily from Judah’s family toward the royal line of David.

“and Ram begot Amminadab,”

Amminadab continues that same family line. He appears elsewhere in Israel’s genealogies as an ancestor of Nahshon, an important leader of Judah during Israel’s wilderness period (Numbers 1:7; 2:3). Ruth is therefore connecting the family of Boaz with the established history of the tribe of Judah.

Study Notes

The genealogical line Perez → Hezron → Ram → Amminadab is also found in 1 Chronicles 2:9-10 and Matthew 1:3-4. This consistency helps us follow the Judahite family line across different parts of Scripture.

Amminadab also provides an important historical marker. His son Nahshon was “leader of the children of Judah” during the wilderness generation (Numbers 2:3). The genealogy is moving from the earlier history of Judah toward the period in which Ruth and Boaz lived, and then onward to David.

Spirit & Life

There is no dramatic event recorded here-only one generation following another. Yet God’s purposes were advancing through each of them.

Scripture repeatedly shows that God works not only through extraordinary moments but through generations of lives that may appear ordinary. Ruth and Boaz could not see everything their faithfulness would produce, but their lives became part of a line through which David would come and, ultimately, Christ Himself.

Ruth 4:20

NKJV

Ruth 4:20 Amminadab begot Nahshon, and Nahshon begot Salmon;(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:20 Amminadab was the father of Nahshon.Nahshon was the father of Salmon. (NLT)

Main Idea

The genealogy moves from Amminadab to Nahshon and then to Salmon, bringing the family line closer to Boaz. These names connect Ruth’s family story with the larger history of the tribe of Judah.

Verse Explanation

“Amminadab begot Nahshon,”

Nahshon was not an obscure figure in Israel’s earlier history. During the wilderness period he was the recognized leader of the tribe of Judah (Numbers 1:7; 2:3). His presence here anchors the genealogy in Judah, the tribe from which David’s royal line would emerge.

“and Nahshon begot Salmon;”

Salmon is the next named ancestor in the line. The following verse identifies him as the father of Boaz, bringing the genealogy directly toward the people at the center of Ruth’s story.

Study Notes

Nahshon appears prominently in Numbers as “leader of the children of Judah” (Numbers 2:3) and as Judah’s representative in bringing an offering at the dedication of the tabernacle altar (Numbers 7:12).

Matthew 1:4-5 follows the same line-Amminadab, Nahshon, Salmon, and Boaz-and adds that Salmon fathered Boaz by Rahab. Ruth itself does not mention Rahab or identify Salmon’s wife, so that information comes from Matthew’s genealogy.

Spirit & Life

These closing verses allow us to see something the people within Ruth’s story could not yet see. God was carrying a purpose through generations, and Ruth’s decision to remain with Naomi would become woven into that continuing story.

The line is moving toward David, but Scripture later shows that it was moving beyond David to Jesus. God’s work in one generation can become part of His blessing to generations that follow.

Ruth 4:21

NKJV

Ruth 4:21 Salmon begot Boaz, and Boaz begot Obed;(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:21 Salmon was the father of Boaz.Boaz was the father of Obed. (NLT)

Main Idea

The genealogy reaches Boaz, Obed, and Jesse, showing that the child born from Ruth and Boaz becomes the crucial link carrying this family line toward David.

Verse Explanation

“Salmon begot Boaz,”

The genealogy now arrives at Boaz, one of the central figures of Ruth. What the book has presented as the story of a faithful and honorable man caring for Ruth and acting as a family redeemer is now placed within the larger family line leading to Israel’s future king.

“and Boaz begot Obed,”

Obed is the son born to Boaz and Ruth (Ruth 4:13, 17). His birth answered the immediate need within the story by preserving the family line connected with Naomi, but the genealogy shows that his significance reaches much farther. Obed becomes the next link in the line toward David.

“and Obed begot Jesse,”

Jesse is Obed’s son and therefore the grandson of Ruth and Boaz. His importance becomes clear in the next verse: Jesse will become the father of David.

Study Notes

Matthew 1:5 adds information not stated here: “Boaz begot Obed by Ruth.” Matthew deliberately includes Ruth by name in the genealogy of Jesus, along with several other women whose stories stand out in Israel’s history.

The genealogy also creates a remarkable connection between Ruth and 1 Samuel. Ruth ends with Jesse and David; 1 Samuel later introduces Jesse and his sons when God sends Samuel to Bethlehem to anoint the king He has chosen (1 Samuel 16:1-13). The same Bethlehem in which Ruth met Boaz becomes the hometown of David and, generations later, the birthplace of Jesus.

Spirit & Life

Obed’s birth seemed immediately to be God’s gracious provision for Ruth and Naomi. Yet God was doing something far larger through that child. Obed would lead to Jesse, Jesse to David, and David’s royal line ultimately to Jesus.

Ruth beautifully shows how God can weave ordinary faithfulness into purposes extending far beyond one lifetime. Ruth and Boaz simply walked faithfully before God in the circumstances given to them; they could not have seen how far the consequences of that faithfulness would reach.

Ruth 4:22

NKJV

Ruth 4:22 Obed begot Jesse, and Jesse begot David.(NKJV)

NLT

Ruth 4:22 Obed was the father of Jesse.Jesse was the father of David. (NLT)

Main Idea

Ruth ends by reaching the destination toward which its closing genealogy has been moving: David. The story of Ruth, Boaz, Naomi, and Obed becomes part of the family history of Israel’s great king-and ultimately of Jesus Christ.

Verse Explanation

“and Obed begot Jesse,”

Obed, the son whose birth brought such joy and restoration at the end of Ruth’s story, became the father of Jesse. The child held in Naomi’s arms would grow up and continue the family line into another generation.

“and Jesse begot David.”

David is the final name in the book, and that ending is enormously significant. Ruth has taken us from famine, death, widowhood, and apparent family extinction to the family line of Israel’s future king. David would become the Lord’s chosen king and the beginning of the royal dynasty that holds a central place in the rest of the biblical story.

Study Notes

Ending with David helps reveal one major reason this family story mattered to Israel. Ruth is not merely an account of how two widows survived difficult circumstances or how Ruth married Boaz. It explains part of David’s ancestry and shows that Ruth the Moabitess became his great-grandmother.

The genealogy of Matthew carries the line beyond David to Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5-6, 16). Jesus is repeatedly identified as the “Son of David” in the New Testament. Ruth therefore closes at David, but from the perspective of the whole Bible, David is not the end of the line. The story ultimately participates in the history leading to Christ.

Spirit & Life

The final word of Ruth is “David.” That changes how we see everything that came before it. Ruth could not have known, when she refused to abandon Naomi, that her faithful decision would become part of the story of Israel’s greatest king. Boaz could not have known how far his kindness, integrity, and obedience would reach.

And even David was not the final destination. Through this family line would eventually come Jesus. Ruth begins with people who cannot see how their story could possibly turn out well and ends by showing that God was accomplishing something far greater than any of them could see. Faithfulness in the small places of life can belong to purposes of God that reach far beyond our own lifetime.
