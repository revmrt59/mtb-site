Book Overview

At a Glance

Song Of Solomon
Author

Solomon, by traditional attribution: “The song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (1:1). The Hebrew phrase can mean by, for, or concerning Solomon, and authorship is debated.

Audience

No recipients are named. Within the poem the bride speaks to the “daughters of Jerusalem,” a listening company drawn into her love for the beloved (1:5; 5:8–6:1).

Date

If Solomon is the author, the tenth century BC, during his reign (about 970–930 BC); the book itself gives no date.

Historical Setting

Israel in the days of its kings, from the vineyards of En Gedi and the slopes of Gilead to Lebanon, Tirzah, and Jerusalem. Shepherds, gardens, harvests, and the royal court supply the poem’s images.

Purpose

To sing the greatest of songs: the love of the beloved and his bride, read as the love of Christ, the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom, for His people and their answering love as they become wholly His.

Central Message

Song of Solomon, “The song of songs” (1:1), sings of the love between a beloved shepherd and king and the bride he desires, through delight, invitation, separation, searching, and belonging. Read as the Spirit’s portrait of Christ, the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom, and His people, it traces the journey of intimacy and becoming wholly His: His love awakens, assures, tests, and matures the bride’s love until, leaning on her Beloved and sealed by a love “as strong as death,” she belongs to Him with her whole life and longs for His coming.

Key Verse

Song of Solomon 8:6: “Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm; For love is as strong as death.”

Key Themes

Love that awakens and welcomes; beauty in His eyes; the Beloved’s excellence; invitation and response; seeking and finding; belonging; the garden offered for His pleasure; protecting love’s time; love stronger than death; longing for His coming.

Book Flow

  • I. Love Awakened and the King’s Welcome (Song of Solomon 1:1–4). Desire for the beloved and the king’s gracious welcome into his chambers.

  • II. Dark but Lovely: Assurance at His Table and Under His Banner (Song of Solomon 1:5–2:7). The bride who knows her need learns at his table and under his banner that she is loved.

  • III. The Call Beyond Familiar Safety (Song of Solomon 2:8–3:5). The beloved calls her to come away; her hesitation gives way to seeking and finding.

  • IV. The King’s Glory and the Garden Offered to Him (Song of Solomon 3:6–5:1). The king’s wedding procession and the bride’s garden offered to both winds for his delight.

  • V. Love Tested, Sought, and Declared (Song of Solomon 5:2–6:3). Comfort delays her response, she searches and is wounded, and her praise of him becomes witness.

  • VI. Beautiful in His Eyes: Love Centered on the Bridegroom (Song of Solomon 6:4–8:4). Assured of her beauty in his eyes, she invites him into the fields and vineyards.

  • VII. Leaning on the Beloved and Sealed with the Flame of Love (Song of Solomon 8:5–14). She leans on her beloved, asks to be set as a seal on his heart, and longs for his coming.

Go Deeper

Background

Author

The book opens with its title and a name: “The song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (1:1). The church and the synagogue have long received the Song as Solomon’s. That fits what Scripture says of him, for Solomon “spoke three thousand proverbs, and his songs were one thousand and five” (1 Kings 4:32). The Hebrew phrase in 1:1 can also mean a song for Solomon or about Solomon, and some scholars think the book reached its final form later, so its authorship is debated. Solomon himself also appears inside the poem, in the royal procession of chapter 3 and the vineyard of chapter 8 (3:7–11; 8:11–12). By attaching the Song to Israel’s wisest king, the title places it among the wisdom writings of Scripture.

Audience

The Song names no recipients. Inside the poem the bride often turns to address the “daughters of Jerusalem” (1:5; 2:7; 5:8, 16). They listen, ask questions, and finally want to join her search: “Where has your beloved turned aside, That we may seek him with you?” (6:1). The Song draws its hearers into the love it celebrates. It was first received in Israel, a people who would later hear their prophets speak of the LORD’s love in the language of marriage: “I will betroth you to Me forever” (Hosea 2:19).

Date

The Song gives no date. If Solomon wrote it, it comes from his reign in the tenth century BC, about 970–930 BC, the period of Israel’s greatest peace and wealth. Those who doubt Solomonic authorship place it later. Its message does not depend on settling the question.

Historical Setting

The Song’s world is the land of Israel at its height under its kings. Its images reach from one end of the land to the other: the vineyards of En Gedi by the Dead Sea (1:14), the plain of Sharon (2:1), the goats on Mount Gilead (4:1), the heights of Lebanon, Senir, and Hermon in the north (4:8), the pools of Heshbon (7:4), and Mount Carmel (7:5). The bride is even compared to the cities of Tirzah and Jerusalem (6:4). The whole land seems to be gathered into the praise of love.

Two settings alternate through the poem. One is the countryside, with flocks and shepherds’ tents, vineyards that must be kept, gardens of spices, and the coming of spring when “the winter is past” (2:11). The other is the royal court of Solomon, with his guarded couch, his palanquin of cedar, silver, gold, and purple, and the crown his mother placed on him “On the day of his wedding” (3:7–11). The beloved belongs to both worlds. He feeds his flock among the lilies, and he is also the king.

The poem reflects ordinary life in ancient Israel: brothers who made their sister the keeper of the family vineyards (1:6), brothers who watch over a younger sister (8:8–9), and watchmen who guarded the city at night (3:3; 5:7). Its many spices and perfumes, myrrh, frankincense, spikenard, saffron, cinnamon, and aloes, were luxuries, and they give the poem much of its fragrance.

Occasion

The Song does not say why it was written. Its title says what it is. “The song of songs” is a Hebrew way of saying “the greatest song,” just as “the holy of holies” means the most holy place. Of Solomon’s many songs, this one is named the song above all others, and the NLT renders the title, “Solomon’s song of songs, more wonderful than any other” (1:1). It is the story unlike any other: the love of the incomparable Bridegroom and the bride He makes wholly His.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

On its surface, the Song is a sequence of love poems. A woman longs for her beloved, delights in his name, and rejoices that “The king has brought me into his chambers” (1:4). He praises her beauty again and again, and she praises his. He calls her to come away with him into the spring, and twice she searches for him through the city by night. Her garden is offered to him, and he comes to receive its fruit. When she delays at his knock, she loses him, is wounded by the watchmen, and still declares to the daughters of Jerusalem that “he is altogether lovely” (5:16). The poem ends with her leaning on her beloved, asking to be set as a seal upon his heart, and calling him to come quickly. Three refrains, the charge not to awaken love “Until it pleases” (2:7; 3:5; 8:4), mark resting points along the way.

The beloved appears in three roles, as a shepherd who feeds his flock, a king who brings her to his table and chambers, and a bridegroom on the day of his wedding. They are best read as one beloved seen from different sides.

Read as a whole, the Song is the love of Christ for His people and their answering love. The shepherd, king, and bridegroom is Jesus Christ, the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom, and the woman is His people, together and one by one. The poem does not state this in so many words, but Scripture gives its marriage language this meaning. The LORD calls Himself His people’s husband (Isaiah 54:5), Jesus calls Himself the Bridegroom (Matthew 9:15), and Paul says the union of husband and wife speaks “concerning Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:32). Read in that light, the Song’s images become a coherent spiritual story.

That story is the journey of intimacy and becoming wholly His. The bride is welcomed by grace before her love is mature. At first her love centers largely on what she receives from Him: His kisses, His table, His shade, His embrace. Through invitation, hesitation, searching, testing, and wounding, her love grows until it centers on Him. The three declarations of belonging trace that change. Early she says, “My beloved is mine, and I am his” (2:16). After testing she says, “I am my beloved’s, And my beloved is mine” (6:3). At last she says, “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me” (7:10). She no longer begins with what she has, but with whose she is.

Why This Book Matters

The Song of Solomon is Scripture’s great song of love. God placed in His Word a book that delights in love between a man and a woman, in beauty, desire, faithfulness, and belonging. That alone honors the love He designed from the beginning, when a man would “be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh” (Genesis 2:24).

Its deepest contribution is to show the heart of Christ toward His people. The beloved cannot stop praising his bride: “Behold, you are fair, my love!” (1:15); “You are all fair, my love, And there is no spot in you” (4:7); “You have ravished my heart” (4:9). Many believers know they are forgiven but struggle to believe they are wanted. The Song answers that struggle. In it the bride learns how beautiful she is in His eyes and how great He is, and that assurance frees her to give herself to Him.

The Song also shows that love must be tested to mature. The bride’s prayer, “Awake, O north wind, And come, O south!” (4:16), is a sincere surrender, yet in the very next scene her comfort keeps her from opening to his knock (5:2–6). Her failure leads to a painful search, and out of that pain comes her richest description of him (5:10–16), a testimony that moves others to seek him with her (6:1). Later she is the one who says, “Let us get up early to the vineyards” (7:12). Few books of the Bible picture so fully how love for Christ grows from receiving to giving.

Book Flow

Song of Solomon 1:1–4: Love Awakened and the King’s Welcome

The title names this “The song of songs” (1:1). At once the woman longs for the beloved’s kisses, finds his love “better than wine,” and delights in his name as “ointment poured forth” (1:2–3). Then she rejoices, “The king has brought me into his chambers” (1:4). Love begins in desire, but the king’s welcome comes before her love is mature; he draws her, and she and others run after him.

Song of Solomon 1:5–2:7: Dark but Lovely: Assurance at His Table and Under His Banner

Before the daughters of Jerusalem she confesses, “I am dark, but lovely,” darkened by the sun, burdened by her brothers’ anger, and aware that “my own vineyard I have not kept” (1:5–6). The beloved directs her to the flock, praises her beauty, and brings her to his table, to his shade under the apple tree, and to “the banqueting house,” where “his banner over me was love” (1:7–2:4). The movement closes in his embrace and the first charge not to “stir up nor awaken love Until it pleases” (2:6–7). In the Song’s spiritual story, the bride who knows her fallen condition learns at His table and under His banner that she is welcome and loved.

Song of Solomon 2:8–3:5: The Call Beyond Familiar Safety

The beloved comes “Leaping upon the mountains” and calls, “Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away” (2:8, 10), longing to see her face and hear her voice in “the secret places of the cliff” (2:14). She asks that the little foxes be caught, then sends him away until the day breaks (2:15–17). By night she searches the city until she finds him and will not let him go (3:1–4). His call beyond familiar safety exposes her fear and hesitation, and her search teaches her to pursue Him.

Song of Solomon 3:6–5:1: The King’s Glory and the Garden Offered to Him

“Who is this coming out of the wilderness” (3:6) opens a royal procession, and the daughters of Zion are called to see King Solomon crowned “On the day of his wedding” (3:11). The beloved praises her, “there is no spot in you” (4:7), calls her to come with him from the lions’ dens and the mountains of the leopards (4:8), and delights in her as “A garden enclosed” (4:12). She answers, “Awake, O north wind, And come, O south!” and “Let my beloved come to his garden” (4:16), and he comes to receive its fruit (5:1). This exchange is the high point of the first half of the Song: the bride offers her life to hard and pleasant seasons alike for His delight.

Song of Solomon 5:2–6:3: Love Tested, Sought, and Declared

By night he knocks, “Open for me, my sister, my love” (5:2), but she hesitates in her comfort, and when she rises to open, “my beloved had turned away and was gone” (5:6). Searching, she is struck and wounded by the watchmen. Yet when the daughters of Jerusalem ask what makes her beloved different, she praises him from head to foot and concludes, “he is altogether lovely” (5:16). Her testimony moves them to seek him with her, and she affirms, “I am my beloved’s, And my beloved is mine” (6:3). Tested love becomes witness.

Song of Solomon 6:4–8:4: Beautiful in His Eyes: Love Centered on the Bridegroom

The beloved returns to praise, with no word of reproach for her delay: she is “as beautiful as Tirzah” and “Awesome as an army with banners,” “My dove, my perfect one” and “the only one” (6:4, 9). The Shulamite is called to return so that she may be seen (6:13), and his praise continues, “How fair and how pleasant you are” (7:6). Now she answers, “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me,” and she herself proposes, “Let us go forth to the field” (7:10–11). Assured of her beauty in His eyes, the bride initiates shared life and work with Him; the movement closes with embrace and the third charge (8:3–4).

Song of Solomon 8:5–14: Leaning on the Beloved and Sealed with the Flame of Love

“Who is this coming up from the wilderness, Leaning upon her beloved?” (8:5) presents the bride transformed by love. This is the Song’s climax. She asks, “Set me as a seal upon your heart,” for “love is as strong as death,” its flame is “A most vehement flame,” and “Many waters cannot quench love” (8:6–7). Love then turns outward in care for a little sister and stewardship of the vineyard (8:8–12), and the Song ends not with closure but with longing: “Make haste, my beloved” (8:14).

Key Themes to Watch For

  • Love that awakens and welcomes. The king draws her and brings her in before her love is mature (1:2–4).

  • Beauty in His eyes. The beloved’s repeated praise teaches the bride how He sees her (1:15; 4:1–7; 6:4–9; 7:1–6).

  • The Beloved’s excellence. She learns, and declares, how great He is (2:3; 5:10–16).

  • Invitation and response. His calls to come away are answered at last by her own invitation (2:10–13; 4:8; 5:2; 7:11–12).

  • Seeking and finding. Two night searches end differently, one in finding and one in wounding and witness (3:1–4; 5:6–6:3).

  • Belonging. Three declarations mark love’s growth (2:16; 6:3; 7:10).

  • The garden. Her life becomes a garden offered for His pleasure (4:12–5:1; 6:2).

  • Love’s own time. The refrain protects love from being forced (2:7; 3:5; 8:4).

  • Love stronger than death. The seal and the flame express love that cannot be quenched (8:6–7).

  • Longing for His coming. The Song ends with the call, “Make haste, my beloved” (8:14).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

Cast of Characters

Each figure is described first as the poem presents them, then as read in the Song’s spiritual story. Where a speaker is uncertain, see Speakers under Helpful Notes.

  • The bride, the Shulamite. The poem tells almost nothing about her life. She is darkened by the sun from keeping her brothers’ vineyards (1:5–6) and at home among flocks and vineyards, yet the king brings her into his chambers (1:4) and calls her “My dove, my perfect one” (6:9). The designation Shulamite occurs only in 6:13, where it appears twice. Its derivation is debated; it is read here as the feminine counterpart of Solomon’s name, so that she bears an identity that belongs with his and shares his life, though she appears to be an ordinary woman with no public standing of her own. In the spiritual reading she is Christ’s people together and each believer’s personal share in that life, a bride whose love is real but still maturing (Ephesians 5:30–32).

  • The beloved: shepherd, king, and bridegroom. The man she loves is a shepherd who feeds his flock among the lilies (1:7; 2:16), a king who brings her to his table and chambers and who appears in the royal procession as King Solomon (1:4, 12; 3:7–11), and a bridegroom “On the day of his wedding” (3:11; 4:8–12). These are one beloved, not rivals for her heart. In the spiritual reading He is Christ: Shepherd in His care and leading, King in His authority and glory, Bridegroom in His affection and belonging (John 10:11; Revelation 19:16; Matthew 9:15).

  • The daughters of Jerusalem. The women the bride addresses throughout the poem (1:5; 2:7; 3:5; 5:8; 8:4). They ask, “What is your beloved More than another beloved?” (5:9), and after her answer they want to seek him with her (6:1). The virgins of 1:3 already love him. In the spiritual reading they are believers whose love is real but less developed, and the bride’s testimony awakens them to seek Him too.

  • Her brothers. “My mother’s sons were angry with me; They made me the keeper of the vineyards,” so that her own vineyard went untended (1:6). In the spiritual reading they picture pressures from familiar or religious relationships that burden the believer and crowd out the care of her own life with Christ, until His voice teaches her a stewardship that answers to Him (Galatians 1:10). The speakers who plan for a little sister in 8:8–9 are often taken to be the same brothers, but that closing scene has its own concern and is read on its own terms. The NKJV heading also gives them 2:15, an assignment other translators dispute (see Speakers under Helpful Notes).

  • Shepherds and the flock. When she asks where the beloved feeds his flock, she is told to “Follow in the footsteps of the flock” and feed her goats “Beside the shepherds’ tents” (1:7–8). In the spiritual reading the flock’s footsteps and the shepherds’ tents picture faithful pastoral care and believers who have learned to follow the Shepherd, so that her journey includes the company of His people (John 10:27; 1 Peter 5:2–4).

  • The watchmen. Guards who go about the city at night. In 3:3 they simply find her, and she asks whether they have seen the one she loves. In 5:7 they strike and wound her, and “The keepers of the walls” take her veil. In the spiritual reading they represent religious opposition and misunderstanding from those who should have protected her. Their wrongdoing remains wrong; the Song does not excuse those who harm people under their care.

  • The little sister. “We have a little sister” (8:8): a young sister, not yet grown, for whom the speakers plan protection and adornment (8:9). She carries the bride’s closing concern for the growth and protection of others. Her precise spiritual identification remains open.

  • Other voices. Solomon’s “sixty valiant men” guard the royal couch (3:7–8), the daughters of Zion are called to see the king crowned (3:11), the queens and concubines praise the bride (6:8–9), and companions appear beside the flocks and in the gardens (1:7; 8:13). These figures fill out their scenes rather than carry fixed meanings of their own.

Images and Expressions

  • Reading the images. Almost every concrete image in the Song can carry spiritual meaning. The key is the quality the image and its meaning share. Ointment is sweet and healing, so the beloved’s name “poured forth” (1:3) speaks of the sweetness and healing of knowing Jesus. Shade gives rest, so sitting in his shade (2:3) speaks of rest in Christ’s saving work. A banner declares whose people gather under it, so “his banner over me was love” (2:4) speaks of His love declared over her life. Fire burns with consuming strength, so the “vehement flame” of 8:6 speaks of a love that takes hold of the whole life. Each image is then read within its own scene, the Song’s larger story, and the teaching of the rest of Scripture.

  • “Dark, but lovely” (1:5–6). On the surface, the woman’s skin is darkened from working in the sun. In the spiritual reading, she bears both the damage of humanity’s fallen condition and the dignity of a person created in God’s image. Her shame is real, but it does not have the last word, because the beloved declares her lovely.

  • The refrain (2:7; 3:5; 8:4). “Do not stir up nor awaken love Until it pleases.” Naturally, the charge protects love from being forced before its time. In the Song’s spiritual story it guards the work of love and honors God’s timing in communion with Him.

  • The seal and the flame (8:6). A seal was pressed into clay or wax to mark ownership and authenticity, and it could be worn on a cord or as a ring on the hand (compare Genesis 38:18; Jeremiah 22:24). The bride asks to be set as a seal on her beloved’s heart and arm. Where the NKJV prints “A most vehement flame,” its footnote gives the literal reading, “a flame of Yah,” using a short form of the LORD’s name. That reading points to the divine source and consuming strength of this love.

Christ and Life

The beloved of the Song is the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom, and in Jesus Christ the three come together. He is “the good shepherd” who “gives His life for the sheep” (John 10:11). He is “KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS” (Revelation 19:16). He is the Bridegroom whose presence makes His friends rejoice (Matthew 9:15), and Paul says he has “betrothed you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin to Christ” (2 Corinthians 11:2). The Song shows what that love is like from the inside: tender, delighted, patient, and determined to have a bride who is wholly His.

The relationship begins with grace. “The king has brought me into his chambers” (1:4) before the bride has done anything to earn a place there. Believers are welcomed in the same way, “having boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus” (Hebrews 10:19). At His table and under His banner (1:12; 2:4) she is nourished by what His love provides. In Christ that table holds the benefits of the cross: reconciliation, forgiveness, propitiation, redemption, justification, and adoption. The banner of love over her life becomes her assurance in hardship, for nothing “shall separate us from the love of Christ,” and “in all these things we are more than conquerors through Him who loved us” (Romans 8:35, 37).

The bride who confessed “I am dark” hears the beloved say, “You are all fair, my love, And there is no spot in you” (4:7). That is how Christ speaks of the church He is cleansing, which He will present to Himself “not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing” (Ephesians 5:27). Christ does not overlook sin. He gave Himself to remove it, and He delights in the people He is making holy. To be holy is to belong wholly to God: “And you shall be holy to Me, for I the LORD am holy, and have separated you from the peoples, that you should be Mine” (Leviticus 20:26). The Song pictures that holiness as a bride whose heart, choices, and service become His.

Such love has to be tested. The bride is called to the mountains, beyond familiar safety; she offers her garden to the north wind as well as the south; she delays at His knock and is wounded in her search. Each experience exposes some fear, comfort, or divided desire, and each draws her love further toward Him. Out of her wounding comes her fullest praise of Him, and others come to seek Him with her. Christ’s love does not spare His people every hard season, but it uses every season to bring forth the fragrance and fruit He desires.

The Song’s climax gathers all of this. The bride comes up from the wilderness “Leaning upon her beloved” (8:5), and she asks to be set as a seal upon His heart, “For love is as strong as death” (8:6). Believers are “sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise” (Ephesians 1:13), and “the love of God has been poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit” (Romans 5:5). The flame of Yah is love that comes from God and kindles an answering love in His people, strong enough to let go of self-rule. “We love Him because He first loved us” (1 John 4:19). Paul describes that love as the power of a surrendered life: “the love of Christ compels us,” so that “those who live should live no longer for themselves, but for Him who died for them and rose again” (2 Corinthians 5:14–15). Love for Christ is what makes death to self possible: “it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” (Galatians 2:20).

The Song ends with longing: “Make haste, my beloved” (8:14). The bride who has been sealed in love still wants more of His presence. The Bible ends the same way. “The Spirit and the bride say, ‘Come!’” and the church answers its Lord’s promise, “Even so, come, Lord Jesus!” (Revelation 22:17, 20).

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Read the poem first, then the spiritual story. The Song is real love poetry, and its scenes, voices, and images should be understood as poetry before their spiritual meaning is drawn out. The two readings belong together; the poem is the vehicle through which the love of Christ is portrayed.

Remember that the Song is a poem, not a timetable. Its experiences recur and deepen, and it does not fix a wedding date, a biography of the lovers, or a sequence every believer must pass through in the same order. Speaker labels in many Bibles are editorial aids added by translators.

Read its frank love language with reverence. The poem honors married love as God’s good gift, and when read as the love of Christ it speaks of holy intimacy with Him. Remember, too, that the bride’s suffering is not presented as punishment, and the watchmen’s violence is not presented as right.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

  • Titles. The book is called Song of Solomon, Song of Songs (from its first words), and sometimes Canticles, from its Latin title.

  • “The holy of holies.” The Mishnah (Yadayim 3:5) records Rabbi Akiva’s judgment that all the Writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies.

  • Speakers. The Hebrew text marks speakers mainly through masculine and feminine forms rather than headings. Interpreters differ at some points. The NKJV heading at 2:15 assigns the foxes to “Her Brothers,” while other translators give the line to the woman, and the speaker of 8:5b is debated.

  • “The only one” (6:8–9). The beloved sets “sixty queens And eighty concubines, And virgins without number” beside his dove, who “Is the only one.” In this Song, love is exclusive.

  • Lebanon (4:8). The NKJV reads “Look from the top of Amana”; other translations render the verb “come down” or “descend.” Either way, the beloved calls the bride to come with him from the high and dangerous places of the north.

Historical Approaches and Our Reading

Many interpreters read the Song as poetry celebrating human love, desire, and marriage between a man and a woman. They differ about whether it tells one continuous story or gathers a collection of related love poems joined by shared refrains and images.

Jewish interpreters have long heard in the Song the love between God and Israel, though their readings vary across the centuries. Rashi, the medieval commentator, explains that every “Solomon” in the Song refers to God, the King to whom peace belongs, and he hears the opening longing for kisses as Israel in exile recalling the love God showed when He gave the Torah and spoke with His people directly. These are readings within Jewish tradition, not Christian conclusions.

Christian interpreters have read the Song as the love of Christ for His church and for the believer. John Gill hears “the church and bride of Christ” speaking to Christ in 1:2 and takes the kisses she desires as “the sweet and comfortable doctrines of the gospel.” John of the Cross wrote his Spiritual Canticle as the soul, the bride, seeking her Beloved, the Son of God. Hudson Taylor’s Union and Communion treats the book as the believer’s communion with Christ, “the Bridegroom of the soul.” These writers differ in method and detail and divide the book in their own ways. Some Christian readers carry the allegory through the whole Song, while others explain it chiefly as a poem about human marriage and then draw an analogy to Christ and the church from the rest of Scripture.

A separate question concerns how the poem is arranged and who its characters are. Some readers see the shepherd, the king, and the bridegroom as one beloved. Others reconstruct a drama in which King Solomon tries to win a country woman who remains loyal to a shepherd she loves, so that king and shepherd are rivals. This is a question about the poem’s cast and arrangement rather than a separate religious tradition.

Our reading attends carefully to the human love poetry and hears its story as a sustained portrait of Christ’s love for His people and their answering growth toward becoming wholly His. The shepherd, king, and bridegroom are one beloved, the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom. The grounds come from the marriage language of the rest of Scripture (Isaiah 54:5; Hosea 2:19–20; Matthew 9:15; Ephesians 5:25–32). The poem does not name Christ; the wider Scriptures establish the theology its images express, which is why the spiritual reading runs through the whole book rather than being saved for a closing application.

Natural poetry, this adopted reading, and further personal application remain distinct. Each image is first understood as poetry, and its spiritual correspondence follows the quality it shares with what it portrays, its own scene, the movement of the whole book, and the teaching of Scripture, as described under Reading the images. That keeps the reading coherent without turning the Song into a dictionary in which every detail has a fixed meaning.
