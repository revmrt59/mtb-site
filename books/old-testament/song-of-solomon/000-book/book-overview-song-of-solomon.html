Book Overview

At a Glance

Song Of Solomon
Author

Solomon, by traditional attribution: “The song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (1:1). The Hebrew phrase can mean by, for, or concerning Solomon, and authorship is debated.

Audience

No recipients are named. Within the poem the bride speaks to the “daughters of Jerusalem,” a listening company that she joins at the beginning and whose love she later awakens (1:3–5; 5:8–6:1).

Date

If Solomon is the author, the tenth century BC, during his reign (about 970–930 BC); the book itself gives no date.

Historical Setting

Israel in the days of its kings, from the vineyards of En Gedi and the slopes of Gilead to Lebanon, Tirzah, and Jerusalem. Shepherds, gardens, harvests, city streets, and the royal court supply the poem’s images.

Purpose

To sing the greatest of songs: the love between a King and the bride He draws to Himself, read as Christ’s love for His people and the journey into intimacy by which they become wholly His.

Central Message

Song of Solomon, “The song of songs” (1:1), sings of the love between a beloved shepherd and king and the bride he desires, through delight, invitation, separation, searching, and belonging. Read as the Spirit’s portrait of Christ, the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom, and His people, it traces the journey of intimacy and becoming wholly His: His love awakens, assures, tests, and matures the bride’s love until, leaning on her Beloved and sealed by a love “as strong as death,” she belongs to Him with her whole life and longs for His coming.

Key Verse

Song of Solomon 8:6: “Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm; For love is as strong as death.”

Key Themes

Grace that comes first; love received and returned; His calls and her answers; shame answered by cleansing; surrender and its testing; three declarations of belonging; restoration and shared work; the secret place; union, the seal, and the flame; longing for His coming.

Book Flow

  • I. Called, Favored, and Awakened (Song of Solomon 1:1–4). The King’s welcome comes first, and her longing for Him is its fruit.

  • II. Dark but Lovely: Off Track, Called Back, and Assured (Song of Solomon 1:5–2:7). Ashamed and burned out, she is brought back to His table, His banner, and His embrace.

  • III. The Call to Follow (Song of Solomon 2:8–3:5). He calls her to come away; not feeling clean enough, she turns Him away, then seeks and finds Him.

  • IV. The King Revealed and the Garden Offered (Song of Solomon 3:6–5:1). He cleanses her, calls again, and draws her to offer her garden to every wind.

  • V. Tested After Surrender: Comfort, Absence, Wounding, and Witness (Song of Solomon 5:2–6:3). Comfort delays her, she can no longer sense His nearness, and after the watchmen wound her, her praise moves others to seek Him with her.

  • VI. Restored Without Reproach: Beautiful in His Eyes, Partner in His Work (Song of Solomon 6:4–8:4). Restored and resting in His desire, she invites Him out into His fields.

  • VII. Leaning, Sealed, and Longing (Song of Solomon 8:5–14). She leans on Him in union, asks for His seal, keeps her own vineyard, and longs for His coming.

Go Deeper

Background

Author

The book opens with its title and a name: “The song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (1:1). The church and the synagogue have long received the Song as Solomon’s. That fits what Scripture says of him, for Solomon “spoke three thousand proverbs, and his songs were one thousand and five” (1 Kings 4:32). The Hebrew phrase in 1:1 can also mean a song for Solomon or about Solomon, and some scholars think the book reached its final form later, so its authorship is debated. Solomon himself also appears inside the poem, in the royal procession of chapter 3 and the vineyard of chapter 8 (3:7–11; 8:11–12). By attaching the Song to Israel’s wisest king, the title places it among the wisdom writings of Scripture.

Audience

The Song names no recipients. Inside the poem the bride often turns to address the “daughters of Jerusalem” (1:5; 2:7; 5:8, 16). At the beginning she joins a company that already loves the king: “Rightly do they love you” (1:4). Later the daughters listen, ask questions, and finally want to join her search: “Where has your beloved turned aside, That we may seek him with you?” (6:1). The Song draws its hearers into the love it celebrates. It was first received in Israel, a people who would later hear their prophets speak of the LORD’s love in the language of marriage: “I will betroth you to Me forever” (Hosea 2:19).

Date

The Song gives no date. If Solomon wrote it, it comes from his reign in the tenth century BC, about 970–930 BC, the period of Israel’s greatest peace and wealth. Those who doubt Solomonic authorship place it later. Its message does not depend on settling the question.

Historical Setting

The Song’s world is the land of Israel at its height under its kings. Its images reach from one end of the land to the other: the vineyards of En Gedi by the Dead Sea (1:14), the plain of Sharon (2:1), the goats on Mount Gilead (4:1), the heights of Lebanon, Senir, and Hermon in the north (4:8), the pools of Heshbon (7:4), and Mount Carmel (7:5). The bride is even compared to the cities of Tirzah and Jerusalem (6:4). The whole land seems to be gathered into the praise of love.

Two settings alternate through the poem. One is the countryside, with flocks and shepherds’ tents, vineyards that must be kept, gardens of spices, and the coming of spring when “the winter is past” (2:11). The other is the royal court of Solomon, with his inner chambers, his guarded couch, his palanquin, a royal carriage made “Of the wood of Lebanon” with silver, gold, and purple (3:9–10), and the crown his mother placed on him “On the day of his wedding” (1:4; 3:7–11). The beloved belongs to both worlds. He feeds his flock among the lilies, and he is also the king.

The poem reflects ordinary life in ancient Israel: brothers who made their sister the keeper of the family vineyards (1:6), unnamed family members who plan to protect a younger sister (8:8–9), watchmen who guarded the city at night (3:3; 5:7), and a vineyard leased to keepers, each of whom was to bring a thousand silver coins for its fruit (8:11). Its many spices and perfumes, myrrh, frankincense, spikenard, saffron, cinnamon, and aloes, were luxuries, and they give the poem much of its fragrance.

Occasion

The Song does not say why it was written. Its title says what it is. “The song of songs” is a Hebrew way of saying “the greatest song,” just as “the holy of holies” means the most holy place. Of Solomon’s many songs, this one is named the song above all others, and the NLT renders the title, “Solomon’s song of songs, more wonderful than any other” (1:1). It is the story unlike any other: the love of the incomparable Bridegroom and the bride He makes wholly His.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

On its surface, the Song is a sequence of love poems. A woman longs for her beloved and rejoices that “The king has brought me into his chambers” (1:4). He praises her beauty again and again, and she answers with praise of her own. He calls her to come away with him into the spring and later to come with him from the mountains of Lebanon. Twice she searches for him through the city by night. Her garden is offered to him, and he comes to receive its fruit. When she delays at his knock, she loses him and is wounded by the watchmen, yet she still declares to the daughters of Jerusalem that “he is altogether lovely” (5:16). The poem ends with her coming up from the wilderness leaning on her beloved, asking to be set as a seal upon his heart, and calling him to come quickly. Three charges not to awaken love “Until it pleases” (2:7; 3:5; 8:4) mark resting places along the way.

The beloved appears as a shepherd who feeds his flock, a king who brings her to his table and chambers, and a bridegroom on the day of his wedding. These are best read as one beloved seen from different sides.

Read as a whole, the Song is the love of Christ for His people and their answering love. The shepherd, king, and bridegroom is Jesus Christ, the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom, and the woman is His people, together and one by one. The poem does not state this in so many words, but Scripture gives its marriage language this meaning. The LORD calls Himself His people’s husband (Isaiah 54:5), Jesus calls Himself the Bridegroom (Matthew 9:15), and Paul says the union of husband and wife speaks “concerning Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:32). Read in that light, the Song’s images become a coherent spiritual story.

That story begins with grace. The King welcomes the bride into His chambers before her love is mature, and her longing for His kisses is the fruit of what she has already tasted (1:2–4). From there the Song follows how love grows. She is ashamed of her darkness and worn out by demands that were never His. She is called to follow Him and holds back, first because she does not feel clean enough, then before the dangers of the heights, and later out of comfort. Each time He answers her: He cleanses her, He keeps speaking to her, He lets her go for a time without sensing His nearness, and He restores her without reproach. Three declarations of belonging trace the change. Early she says, “My beloved is mine, and I am his” (2:16). After testing she says, “I am my beloved’s, And my beloved is mine” (6:3). At last she says, “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me” (7:10). Her love no longer begins with what she has, but with whose she is, and finally rests in His desire for her.

Why This Book Matters

The Song of Solomon is Scripture’s great song of love. God placed in His Word a book that delights in love between a man and a woman, in beauty, desire, faithfulness, and belonging. That alone honors the love He designed from the beginning, when a man would “be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh” (Genesis 2:24).

Its deepest contribution is to show the heart of Christ toward His people. The beloved cannot stop praising his bride: “Behold, you are fair, my love!” (1:15); “You are all fair, my love, And there is no spot in you” (4:7); “You have ravished my heart” (4:9). Many believers know they are forgiven but struggle to believe they are wanted. The Song answers that struggle. It shows a King whose favor comes before the bride has anything to offer, and whose delight in her does not change after she fails.

The Song also shows how love for Christ matures, and it does so with unusual honesty about what holds believers back. The bride hesitates three times for three different reasons: she feels too unclean to follow (2:17), she faces real danger on the heights (4:8), and she is too comfortable to rise when He knocks (5:3). No other book of the Bible pictures so fully how love grows from receiving to giving, or how patiently the Lord answers each kind of hesitation.

Finally, the Song speaks directly to people worn out in the name of God. At the beginning the bride confesses that her brothers made her “the keeper of the vineyards, But my own vineyard I have not kept” (1:6). At the end she says, “My own vineyard is before me” (8:12). The same work that once burned her out becomes faithful stewardship when her own heart is kept before Him first. Love, not pressure, is what finally carries her into a surrendered life: “love is as strong as death” (8:6).

Book Flow

Song of Solomon 1:1–4: Called, Favored, and Awakened

The title names this “The song of songs” (1:1). The bride’s first words are a cry, “Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth— For your love is better than wine” (1:2), and verse 4 explains it: “The king has brought me into his chambers.” The reader meets her longing first and only then learns its cause: she longs for him before the poem tells how he welcomed her. In the order of their relationship, his welcome comes first, as a king once showed favor to Esther, and her desire is its fruit. She joins those who loved him before her and agrees from her own experience, “Rightly do they love you” (1:4).

Song of Solomon 1:5–2:7: Dark but Lovely: Off Track, Called Back, and Assured

Before the daughters of Jerusalem she confesses, “I am dark, but lovely,” ashamed to be seen and burned out from keeping her brothers’ vineyards, for “my own vineyard I have not kept” (1:5–6). She asks where he feeds his flock, and he brings her back (1:7–8). He praises her and seats her at his table, where her spikenard, a costly perfume, “sends forth its fragrance” in his presence (1:12). He becomes her bundle of myrrh, shades her under the apple tree, and brings her to “the banqueting house,” where “his banner over me was love” (1:13–2:4). Their mirrored praise shows love received and returned, and the movement closes in his embrace and the first charge not to “stir up nor awaken love Until it pleases” (2:5–7).

Song of Solomon 2:8–3:5: The Call to Follow

The beloved comes “Leaping upon the mountains,” stands near “behind our wall,” announces that “the winter is past,” and calls, “Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away” (2:8–13). He seeks her face and voice “in the secret places of the cliff” (2:14). After the first declaration of belonging, “My beloved is mine, and I am his,” she turns him away “Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away” (2:16–17); in the Song’s spiritual story she does not feel clean enough to follow. Missing him, she seeks him by night, finds that the watchmen cannot take his place, holds him, and brings him to her mother’s house before the second charge (3:1–5).

Song of Solomon 3:6–5:1: The King Revealed and the Garden Offered

“Who is this coming out of the wilderness” (3:6) opens a vision of the king in his glory, crowned “On the day of his wedding” (3:11). He praises her and takes up the words she used when she turned him away: “Until the day breaks… I will go my way to the mountain of myrrh And to the hill of frankincense” (4:6). In the Song’s spiritual story the mountain of myrrh pictures Christ’s suffering at the cross, and the hill of frankincense His prayer for His people. Then he declares, “there is no spot in you” (4:7), and calls again, more specifically, “Come with me,” into the heights of Lebanon and the lions’ dens (4:8). She does not answer at once, but his words draw her until she yields, “Awake, O north wind, And come, O south!”, her garden becomes “his garden,” and he comes to receive its fruit (4:16–5:1). This surrender is the high point of the first half of the Song.

Song of Solomon 5:2–6:3: Tested After Surrender: Comfort, Absence, Wounding, and Witness

The surrender is tested at once. He knocks by night, “Open for me, my sister, my love” (5:2), but she answers from her comfort, and when she opens, “my beloved had turned away and was gone” (5:6). Struck and wounded by the watchmen yet still lovesick, she answers the daughters of Jerusalem with her fullest praise of him, “he is altogether lovely” (5:16). Her testimony moves them to seek him with her, and she declares, “I am my beloved’s, And my beloved is mine” (6:3).

Song of Solomon 6:4–8:4: Restored Without Reproach: Beautiful in His Eyes, Partner in His Work

He returns with no word of reproach and repeats his earlier praise: she is “the only one” (6:9), who “looks forth as the morning” (6:10), and called back so that she may be seen; what they see in her is like “the dance of the two camps” (6:13). Resting in the third declaration, “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me,” she now takes the initiative she lacked before: “Come, my beloved, Let us go forth to the field” (7:10–11). She has fruit laid up for him (7:13) and longs to bring him into her mother’s house and give him spiced wine (8:1–2); in the spiritual story, the one who was fed at his table now wants to give to him. The movement closes with his embrace and the third charge (8:3–4).

Song of Solomon 8:5–14: Leaning, Sealed, and Longing

“Who is this coming up from the wilderness, Leaning upon her beloved?” (8:5) presents the bride transformed and in union with him, brought back to the apple tree where love was awakened. This is the Song’s climax. She asks, “Set me as a seal upon your heart,” for “love is as strong as death,” and “Many waters cannot quench love” (8:6–7). Mature love then cares for a little sister (8:8–10). The king entrusts his vineyard to keepers who must bring him its fruit, and the bride, who once neglected her own vineyard, now tends it before him and gives him its fruit: “My own vineyard is before me” (8:11–12). He still asks to hear her voice for himself, even while companions listen to her: “Let me hear it!” (8:13). The Song ends not with closure but with longing: “Make haste, my beloved” (8:14). Where she once said “Turn, my beloved” and sent him away (2:17), she now calls him to come quickly.

Key Themes to Watch For

  • Grace that comes first. The king brings her into his chambers before her love is mature (1:2–4).

  • Love received and returned. He speaks first, and she answers in kind (1:15–16; 2:2–3; 4:10; 5:10–16).

  • His calls and her answers. “Come away” and “Come with me” are finally answered by her own “Come, my beloved” (2:10; 4:8; 7:11; 8:14).

  • Shame answered by cleansing. The woman who said “I am dark” hears “there is no spot in you” (1:5–6; 2:17; 4:6–7).

  • Surrender and its testing. A sincere surrender is followed at once by a fresh test (4:16–5:6).

  • Belonging. Three declarations mark love’s growth (2:16; 6:3; 7:10).

  • The secret place. He asks to hear her voice in the hidden place and still asks at the end (2:14; 8:13).

  • The vineyard. Neglected under others’ demands, shared with him, and kept at last (1:6; 7:12; 8:12).

  • Love as strong as death. The seal and the flame express love that many waters cannot quench (8:6–7).

  • Longing for His coming. The Song ends with the call, “Make haste, my beloved” (8:14).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

Cast of Characters

Each figure is described first as the poem presents them, then as read in the Song’s spiritual story. Where a speaker is uncertain, see Speakers under Helpful Notes.

  • The beloved: shepherd, king, and bridegroom. The man she loves is a shepherd who feeds his flock among the lilies (1:7; 2:16), a king who brings her into his chambers and to his table and who appears in the royal procession as King Solomon (1:4, 12; 3:7–11), and a bridegroom “On the day of his wedding” (3:11; 4:8–12). These are one beloved, not rivals for her heart. In the spiritual reading He is Christ: Shepherd in His care, King in His authority and favor, Bridegroom in His affection and desire (John 10:11; Matthew 9:15; Ephesians 5:25–32).

  • The bride, the Shulamite. The poem tells almost nothing about her life. She is darkened by the sun from keeping her brothers’ vineyards (1:5–6), yet the king brings her into his chambers (1:4) and calls her “My dove, my perfect one” (6:9). The designation Shulamite occurs only in 6:13, where it appears twice. Where the name comes from is debated; it is read here as the feminine counterpart of Solomon’s name, so that an ordinary woman bears an identity that belongs with his and shares his life. In the spiritual reading she pictures the church together and also each individual believer. She moves from shame and a neglected heart toward surrender, rest in His desire, shared work, and union.

  • The virgins and the daughters of Jerusalem. “The virgins love you” (1:3): others loved the king before she did, and she joins them. The daughters of Jerusalem are the women she addresses throughout (1:5; 2:7; 3:5; 5:8; 8:4). They ask, “What is your beloved More than another beloved?” (5:9), and after her answer they want to seek him with her (6:1). In the spiritual reading they are believers whose love is real but less developed. She first learns from those who love Him; later her testimony awakens others.

  • Her brothers. “My mother’s sons were angry with me; They made me the keeper of the vineyards,” so that her own vineyard went untended (1:6). In the spiritual reading they picture familiar and religious demands that drive the believer into burnout and cause her to neglect her own relationship with Christ. Their pressure is different from the fruitful service she later shares with Him (7:11–12; 8:12). The unnamed speakers who plan for a little sister in 8:8–9 should not simply be assumed to be these brothers.

  • Shepherds and the flock. She is told to “Follow in the footsteps of the flock” and feed her goats “Beside the shepherds’ tents” (1:8). In the spiritual reading these picture His provision through faithful pastoral care and believers who have learned to follow the Shepherd. Leaders help her follow Him; they cannot replace Him. At the end she honors “those who tend its fruit” (8:12).

  • The watchmen. Guards who go about the city at night. In 3:3 they simply find her, and she asks whether they have seen the one she loves; she finds him only after she passes them (3:4). In 5:7 they strike and wound her, and “The keepers of the walls” take her veil. In the spiritual reading they show that no religious guardian can take Christ’s place, and they picture opposition from those who should have protected her. Their wrongdoing remains wrong; the Song does not excuse those who harm people under their care.

  • Her mother. In 3:4 she brings her beloved “to the house of my mother, And into the chamber of her who conceived me.” In 8:2 she says what she would do: “I would lead you and bring you Into the house of my mother, She who used to instruct me.” In the spiritual reading the mother’s house pictures the place of new birth and the Spirit’s teaching (John 3:5–6; 14:26). This is a picture of the Spirit’s work, not a title for the Spirit.

  • The little sister. “We have a little sister” (8:8): a young sister, not yet grown, for whom the speakers plan protection and adornment (8:9). She brings mature love’s care for younger believers into view. Her precise spiritual identification remains open.

  • Other voices. Solomon’s “sixty valiant men” guard the royal couch (3:7–8), the daughters of Zion are called to see the king crowned (3:11), the queens and concubines praise the bride (6:8–9), and companions appear beside the flocks and in the gardens (1:7; 8:13). These figures fill out their scenes rather than carry fixed meanings of their own.

Images and Expressions

  • Reading the images. Almost every concrete image in the Song can carry spiritual meaning. The key is the quality the image and its meaning share. Ointment is sweet and healing, so the beloved’s name “poured forth” (1:3) speaks of the sweetness and healing of knowing Jesus. Shade gives rest, so sitting in his shade (2:3) speaks of rest in Christ’s saving work at the cross. A banner declares whose people gather under it, so “his banner over me was love” (2:4) speaks of His love declared over her life. Each image is then read within its own scene, the Song’s larger story, and the teaching of the rest of Scripture.

  • “Dark, but lovely” (1:5–6). On the surface, the woman’s skin is darkened from working in the sun. In the spiritual reading she bears both the damage of humanity’s fallen condition and the dignity of a person created in God’s image. Her shame is real, but it does not have the last word, because the beloved declares her lovely.

  • “Our wall” and the lattice (2:9). “Behold, he stands behind our wall; He is looking through the windows, Gazing through the lattice.” In the spiritual reading the wall pictures the limits of our present, mortal state. Christ has come near, but His full glory is still partly hidden. A lattice hides, yet it lets the light in: “For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face” (1 Corinthians 13:12). Through these openings she can recognize His loving attention, His eyes on her, even while she sees Him only in part.

  • The declarations of belonging. “My beloved is mine, and I am his” (2:16) begins with what she has. “I am my beloved’s, And my beloved is mine” (6:3) begins with whose she is. “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me” (7:10) drops “mine” altogether and rests in His desire. The Hebrew noun translated “desire” in 7:10 is the same noun used in Genesis 3:16 and 4:7. After the fall the woman heard, “Your desire shall be for your husband, And he shall rule over you” (Genesis 3:16). Here it is the Bridegroom’s desire that is toward His bride, a picture of redemption reversing the broken pattern of the fall.

  • The refrain (2:7; 3:5; 8:4). “Do not stir up nor awaken love Until it pleases.” In the poem itself, the charge protects love from being forced before its time. In the Song’s spiritual story it marks resting places in the journey and guards the work God is doing in her.

  • The seal and the flame (8:6). A seal was pressed into clay or wax to mark ownership and authenticity, and it could be worn on a cord or as a ring on the hand (compare Genesis 38:18; Haggai 2:23). The bride asks to be set as a seal on her beloved’s heart and arm. The heart is the inner person; the arm is strength and visible action. She asks to be bound to Him in His deepest feelings and His visible life, permanently and publicly. Where the NKJV prints “A most vehement flame,” its footnote gives the literal reading, “a flame of Yah,” using a short form of the LORD’s name. That reading points to a love whose source is God Himself: intense, purifying, and unstoppable.

Christ and Life

The beloved of the Song is the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom, and in Jesus Christ the three come together. He is “the good shepherd” who “gives His life for the sheep” (John 10:11). He is “KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS” (Revelation 19:16). He is the Bridegroom whose presence makes His friends rejoice (Matthew 9:15). The Song shows what His love is like from the inside: it comes first, it delights, it calls, it waits, and it is determined to have a bride who is wholly His.

The relationship begins with His favor. “The king has brought me into his chambers” (1:4) before the bride has done anything to earn a place there, as Esther “obtained grace and favor” in the king’s sight and he “held out to Esther the golden scepter” (Esther 2:17; 5:2). Believers are welcomed in the same way: “No one can come to Me unless the Father who sent Me draws him” (John 6:44), and we come “boldly to the throne of grace” (Hebrews 4:16). Her longing for more of Him is the fruit of that welcome, not the price of it.

At His table and under His banner (1:12; 2:4) she is nourished by what His love provides. In Christ that table holds the benefits of the cross. The shade of the apple tree points to the tree on which Christ “Himself bore our sins in His own body” (1 Peter 2:24), and the bundle of myrrh she keeps close “all night” (1:13) recalls the myrrh brought for His burial (John 19:39): His suffering love is her comfort in the night. Her spikenard, the costly perfume released at the king’s table, pictures worship rising in His presence. It recalls Mary of Bethany, who poured “very costly oil of spikenard” on Jesus’ feet while “Martha served” (John 12:2–3). Restored from burnout to worship, the bride learns that service flows from sitting with Him. His banner of love is what gives her victory: “in all these things we are more than conquerors through Him who loved us” (Romans 8:37).

Christ does not wait for His people to make themselves clean before He calls them. When the bride turns Him away because she does not feel clean enough (2:17), He answers with her own words, “Until the day breaks,” and goes “to the mountain of myrrh And to the hill of frankincense” (4:6). The mountain of myrrh pictures His suffering at the cross; the hill of frankincense pictures His prayer for His people, for “He always lives to make intercession for them” (Hebrews 7:25). He deals with the darkness she could not remove, and then declares, “there is no spot in you” (4:7). Only then does He call again. Peter did the same thing when Jesus first called him: “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord!” Jesus answered, “Do not be afraid,” and Peter left everything and followed Him (Luke 5:8–11). “The blood of Jesus Christ His Son cleanses us from all sin” (1 John 1:7), and He presents His church to Himself “not having spot or wrinkle” (Ephesians 5:27).

To be holy is to belong wholly to God: “you shall be holy to Me, for I the LORD am holy, and have separated you from the peoples, that you should be Mine” (Leviticus 20:26). The Song pictures that holiness as “A garden enclosed” (4:12) that becomes “his garden” (4:16), offered to the north wind of hardship as well as the south wind of blessing. His discipline is real, and Scripture is honest about its cost: “no chastening seems to be joyful for the present, but painful; nevertheless, afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11). Even when she can no longer sense His nearness (5:6), He has not left her. He is in His garden, at work in her life (6:2). The watchmen’s blows were wrong, yet the Lord brought something good out of her suffering: after they wounded her, her praise of Him moved the daughters of Jerusalem to seek Him with her (5:7–6:1). When He returns, it is without reproach. Jesus met Peter after his denial the same way, restoring him and giving him work: “Feed My sheep” (John 21:17).

The Spirit’s work runs through the bride’s story. The house of wine where His banner is raised (2:4) recalls Paul’s words, “do not be drunk with wine… but be filled with the Spirit” (Ephesians 5:18). Her mother’s house, “the chamber of her who conceived me” (3:4), pictures the new birth: “that which is born of the Spirit is spirit” (John 3:6). Her garden becomes “A well of living waters” (4:15), as Jesus promised that “out of his heart will flow rivers of living water,” which “He spoke concerning the Spirit” (John 7:38–39).

The Song’s climax gathers all of this. The bride comes up from the wilderness “Leaning upon her beloved” (8:5): reliance, closeness, and union. “He who is joined to the Lord is one spirit with Him” (1 Corinthians 6:17). She asks to be set as a seal upon His heart, and believers are “sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise” (Ephesians 1:13). The flame of Yah is love that comes from God and kindles an answering love in His people, and love is the only power strong enough to make death to self possible: giving up living for oneself in order to live for Christ. “We love Him because He first loved us” (1 John 4:19). “The love of Christ compels us,” so that “those who live should live no longer for themselves, but for Him who died for them and rose again” (2 Corinthians 5:14–15). “It is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” (Galatians 2:20).

The bride’s awakening also brings Christ joy. If she is the one speaking in 8:5, “I awakened you under the apple tree,” she is what Isaiah foresaw: “He shall see the labor of His soul, and be satisfied” (Isaiah 53:11). His love came first, yet her response to grace moves Him. “He will rejoice over you with gladness… He will rejoice over you with singing” (Zephaniah 3:17).

The Song ends with longing: “Make haste, my beloved” (8:14). The bride whose own vineyard is kept can long for His coming without fear, for “when He appears, we may have confidence and not be ashamed before Him at His coming” (1 John 2:28). The Bible ends the same way: “And the Spirit and the bride say, ‘Come!’” and the church answers its Lord’s promise, “Even so, come, Lord Jesus!” (Revelation 22:17, 20).

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Read the poem first, then the spiritual story. The Song is real love poetry, and its scenes, voices, and images should be understood as poetry before their spiritual meaning is drawn out. The two readings belong together; the poem is the vehicle through which the love of Christ is portrayed.

Remember that the Song is a poem, not a timetable. Its experiences recur and deepen, and it does not fix a wedding date, a biography of the lovers, or a sequence every believer must pass through in the same order. The bride’s growth is never the price of her welcome; she is received first and matures afterward.

Read its frank love language with reverence. The poem honors married love as God’s good gift, and when read as the love of Christ it speaks of holy intimacy with Him. Remember, too, that the bride’s suffering is not presented as punishment, and the watchmen’s violence is not presented as right. Speaker labels in many Bibles are editorial aids added by translators.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

  • Titles. The book is called Song of Solomon, Song of Songs (from its first words), and sometimes Canticles, from its Latin title.

  • “The holy of holies.” The Mishnah, an early collection of Jewish teaching, records Rabbi Akiva’s judgment (Yadayim 3:5) that all the Writings, the third part of the Hebrew Bible, are holy, but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies.

  • Speakers. The Hebrew text marks speakers mainly through masculine and feminine forms rather than headings, and interpreters differ at some points. The NKJV heading at 2:15 assigns the foxes to “Her Brothers,” while other translators give the line to the woman or the beloved. The speaker of 6:11 is debated, though the beloved is the more likely. The speaker of 8:5b is open. The Hebrew words for “you” there address a man, so some hear the bride speaking to her beloved; others hear Christ reminding her where her life began. At 8:12 most hear the bride; a few hear the beloved.

  • “The secret places of the cliff” (2:14). The KJV renders the phrase “the secret places of the stairs.” The Hebrew word means a steep place or ascent, which is why the hidden place is also pictured as the way up: the bride learns to follow Christ higher through time alone with Him in prayer.

  • “The only one” (6:8–9). The beloved sets “sixty queens And eighty concubines, And virgins without number” beside his dove, who “Is the only one.” In this Song, love is exclusive.

  • Lebanon (4:8). The NKJV reads “Look from the top of Amana”; other translations render the verb “come down” or “descend.” Either way, the beloved calls the bride to come with him from the high and dangerous places of the north. Senir is another name for Hermon (Deuteronomy 3:9).

  • The two camps (6:13). The Hebrew word is Mahanaim, the name Jacob gave the place where God’s angels met him (Genesis 32:1–2). The shared word allows the comparison without suggesting that the Song describes Jacob’s location.

Historical Approaches and Our Reading

Many interpreters read the Song as poetry celebrating human love, desire, and marriage between a man and a woman. They differ about whether it tells one continuous story or gathers a collection of related love poems joined by shared refrains and images.

Jewish interpreters have long heard in the Song the love between God and Israel, though their readings vary across the centuries. Rashi, the medieval commentator, explains that every “Solomon” in the Song refers to God, the King to whom peace belongs, and he hears the opening longing for kisses as Israel in exile recalling the love God showed when He gave the Law (the Torah) and spoke with His people directly. These are readings within Jewish tradition, not Christian conclusions.

Christian interpreters have read the Song as the love of Christ for His church and for the believer. John Gill hears “the church and bride of Christ” speaking to Christ in 1:2 and takes the kisses she desires as “the sweet and comfortable doctrines of the gospel.” John of the Cross wrote his Spiritual Canticle as the soul, the bride, seeking her Beloved, the Son of God. Hudson Taylor’s Union and Communion treats the book as the believer’s communion with Christ, “the Bridegroom of the soul.” These writers differ in method and detail and divide the book in their own ways. Some Christian readers read the whole Song as a picture of Christ and His people, while others explain it chiefly as a poem about human marriage and then draw an analogy to Christ and the church from the rest of Scripture.

A separate question concerns how the poem is arranged and who its characters are. Some readers see the shepherd, the king, and the bridegroom as one beloved. Others reconstruct a drama in which King Solomon tries to win a country woman who remains loyal to a shepherd she loves, so that king and shepherd are rivals. This is a question about the poem’s cast and arrangement rather than a separate religious tradition.

Our reading attends carefully to the human love poetry and hears its story as a sustained portrait of Christ’s love for His people. The King’s favor comes first and awakens the bride’s love. Through calling, hesitation, cleansing, testing, and restoration, her love moves from what she has to whose she is to rest in His desire, until she leans on Him in union and longs for His coming. The shepherd, king, and bridegroom are one beloved, the Shepherd-King-Bridegroom. The grounds come from the marriage language of the rest of Scripture (Isaiah 54:5; Hosea 2:19–20; Matthew 9:15; Ephesians 5:25–32). The poem does not name Christ; the wider Scriptures establish the theology its images express, which is why the spiritual reading runs through the whole book rather than being saved for a closing application.

Natural poetry, this adopted reading, and further personal application remain distinct. Each image is first understood as poetry, and its spiritual correspondence follows the quality it shares with what it portrays, its own scene, the movement of the whole book, and the teaching of Scripture, as described under Reading the images. That keeps the reading coherent without turning the Song into a dictionary in which every detail has a fixed meaning.
