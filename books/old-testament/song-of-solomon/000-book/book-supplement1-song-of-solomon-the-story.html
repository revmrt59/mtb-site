Introduction

The Song of Solomon tells the story of a King who draws an ordinary woman into His presence. He welcomes her before she has anything to offer, then patiently leads her through delight, failure, testing, and wounding until she belongs to Him with her whole life. Scripture uses the language of marriage for Christ and His people (Ephesians 5:32), so her story is ours too. She stands for the church and for each believer in it. This is the journey to intimacy with Jesus. This is the journey to maturity with Jesus. This is the journey of becoming wholly His. This is the journey of bride of Christ. This is your journey.

Each chapter below summarizes the story and then shows where it touches our lives. The Song’s scenes recur and deepen rather than following a strict timeline, and not every believer meets them in the same order. Read it looking for the place where you are standing now.

Chapter 1: Drawn, Off Track, and Called Back

The Song opens with longing: “Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth— For your love is better than wine” (1:2). Her hunger comes from what she has already tasted: “The king has brought me into his chambers” (1:4). She joins those who loved Him before her and agrees, “Rightly do they love you.” But she soon gets off track. She is ashamed to be seen, “Do not look upon me” (1:6), and worn out from serving angry brothers: “They made me the keeper of the vineyards, But my own vineyard I have not kept” (1:6). So she asks where He feeds His flock (1:7). He sends her to the shepherds’ tents, tells her she is beautiful, and seats her at His table, where her worship rises like spikenard (1:12). Through the night she keeps His suffering love close, like a bundle of myrrh (1:13).

Our story. Grace comes first. We do not have to work up enough desire to earn His welcome; desire grows from tasting His love, so the cure for dryness is coming back to receive. Many believers also know the brothers’ vineyards: serving driven by approval, guilt, or fear of disappointing someone. We keep serving while our own walk with God dries up, and we start avoiding His gaze. He does not answer with a better plan for the vineyards. He seats us at His table. Keeping our own vineyard begins by sitting down.

A question to carry: Whose vineyards are you working in, and what has happened to your own?

Chapter 2: Rest, Then the Call to Follow

Now love is received and returned. He says, “Like a lily among thorns” (2:2), and she answers, “Like an apple tree among the trees of the woods” (2:3). She rests in His shade, in the tree that points to the cross, and is filled in His house of wine, where “his banner over me was love” (2:4). She ends held in His embrace (2:6). Then He comes leaping on the mountains, looks in through the lattice, declares that winter is past, and calls: “Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away” (2:10). He wants to meet her in the secret place, “Let me see your face, Let me hear your voice” (2:14), and warns about the little foxes that spoil tender vines (2:15). She says, “My beloved is mine, and I am his” (2:16), and in the next breath sends Him away: “Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, Turn” (2:17). She does not feel clean enough to follow.

Our story. Our victory comes from the love He has declared over us, not from our performance. Then He calls us to follow, usually before we feel ready: real time alone with Him, something to surrender, a step of obedience that costs. Many of us answer, “Not yet. When I’ve cleaned up my life.” It sounds humble, but it is a refusal. He cleanses those He calls. Watch the little foxes too: a nursed resentment, a screen that fills every quiet moment, a delay that becomes a habit, a need for approval.

A question to carry: What has He been calling you to that you keep postponing until you feel ready?

Chapter 3: Seeking Him, and Seeing the King

Having sent Him away, she misses Him: “By night on my bed I sought the one I love; I sought him, but I did not find him” (3:1). She rises and searches the city. The watchmen cannot give Him to her, but just past them she finds Him: “Scarcely had I passed by them, When I found the one I love. I held him and would not let him go” (3:4). She brings Him into her mother’s house, the place of new birth, and God’s work in her is given time to grow (3:5). Then a procession rises out of the wilderness, fragrant with myrrh and frankincense, and she sees the King crowned “On the day of his wedding, The day of the gladness of his heart” (3:11).

Our story. When we send Him away, we feel His absence, and we often go to the watchmen: a leader, a conference, a new church. Good leaders help, but no one can take His place. We have to go past every helper until we find Him and hold on. Notice, too, how He moves her forward. He does not scold; He shows her His glory. A fresh sight of Jesus, who “for the joy that was set before Him endured the cross” (Hebrews 12:2), does more to get us moving than any amount of guilt.

A question to carry: Are you looking to someone or something to give you what only Christ can give?

Chapter 4: Made Clean, Called Again, and the Garden Offered

He praises her from head to toe (4:1–5). Then He takes up her own words of refusal: “Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, I will go my way to the mountain of myrrh” (4:6). She waited for her darkness to leave; He goes to the cross to deal with it Himself. So His verdict is, “You are all fair, my love, And there is no spot in you” (4:7). Only then does He call again, more specifically: “Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse” (4:8), into heights where lions and leopards live. She hesitates, and He keeps speaking: “You have ravished my heart With one look of your eyes” (4:9). He calls her “A garden enclosed” (4:12), a life set apart for Him. At last she yields: “Awake, O north wind, And come, O south!” She welcomes hardship and blessing alike: “Let my beloved come to his garden” (4:16).

Our story. The order matters. He cleanses us before He calls us. Many Christians reverse it and follow in order to become clean, so they never feel they have arrived. The second call still costs: telling the truth at work, taking a stand in the family, serving where there is real opposition. Fear is normal, and He answers it with love, saying “with me” twice. Most of us pray for the south wind of blessing. Few ask for the north wind. When we do, “my garden” becomes “his garden,” and our time, gifts, family, and future become His.

A question to carry: Which part of your life is still “my garden”?

Chapter 5: Tested by Comfort, Silence, and Wounds

He comes to His garden and shares its fruit: “Eat, O friends!” (5:1). Then, in the night, He knocks: “Open for me, my sister, my love” (5:2). This time comfort holds her back: “I have taken off my robe; How can I put it on again?” (5:3). When she finally rises, His myrrh is on the lock, but “my beloved had turned away and was gone” (5:6). She calls, and He does not answer. The watchmen strike and wound her and take away her veil (5:7). Yet when the daughters ask what makes her beloved better than any other (5:9), she gives the fullest praise of Him in the whole Song, ending, “Yes, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, And this is my friend” (5:16).

Our story. A real surrender will be tested. Shame kept us from following when we felt dirty; comfort keeps us from rising when we feel clean. He knocks at inconvenient hours: a person who needs us, a prompting to pray, a call that would disturb a well-arranged life. Some seasons bring silence in prayer. That silence is not rejection or the loss of His love. Some believers are wounded by the very people who should have protected them. That wrong remains wrong. But out of her wounds came her clearest sight of Him, and many people have come to Christ by watching someone suffer and still say, “He is altogether lovely.”

A question to carry: Where is comfort keeping you from answering His knock?

Chapter 6: Restored Without Reproach

Her testimony awakens others: “That we may seek him with you?” (6:1). She knows He is in His garden, her own life, and she says, “I am my beloved’s, And my beloved is mine” (6:3). Belonging now comes first. He returns without a word about her delay: “O my love, you are as beautiful as Tirzah” (6:4). He repeats almost word for word the praise He spoke before she failed (6:5–7). She is “the only one” (6:9), the one who “looks forth as the morning” (6:10). The woman who once said “Do not look upon me” is now called back to be seen: “Return, return, O Shulamite” (6:13). They see in her “the dance of the two camps,” earth moving in step with heaven.

Our story. After failure we expect a lecture or a probation period. He repeats His praise instead, the way Jesus restored Peter at the breakfast fire and gave him work. The test now is whether we will believe what He says about us. Hardship often moves the center of our love from what we have to whose we are. And restoration leads somewhere: into a life that moves when He moves, in step with the Spirit (Galatians 5:25).

A question to carry: Is there a failure you still expect Him to bring up?

Chapter 7: Resting in His Desire, Sharing His Work

This time His praise begins with her feet: “How beautiful are your feet in sandals, O prince’s daughter!” (7:1). She is no longer hiding; she is walking. Then comes the third mark of belonging: “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me” (7:10). “Mine” has dropped out. She rests in being wanted, the reverse of the broken pattern after the fall (Genesis 3:16). Out of that rest she does what she could not do before. She invites Him: “Come, my beloved, Let us go forth to the field” (7:11). She goes back to the vineyards, now with Him: “There I will give you my love” (7:12). She has stored up fruit for Him, “new and old” (7:13).

Our story. Knowing we are wanted frees us from grasping for assurance. Then our attention turns outward to what He cares about: a new believer who needs someone to walk with, a neighbor who does not know Him, a ministry that needs workers. Notice that she returns to the same kind of place that burned her out in chapter 1. The work was not the problem; the center was. Believers who once burned out in ministry do not have to quit serving. They can go back into the vineyards with Him, out of rest instead of demand.

A question to carry: Can you say, “His desire is toward me”? What would change if you believed it?

Chapter 8: Leaning, Sealed, and Longing

Onlookers marvel: “Who is this coming up from the wilderness, Leaning upon her beloved?” (8:5). The story returns to the apple tree, where her new life began. She asks, “Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm; For love is as strong as death” (8:6), His in her inner life and her visible life. “Many waters cannot quench love” (8:7); no trial she passed through could put out the flame. Mature love now cares for a younger sister (8:8–9). The woman who once could not keep her own vineyard says, “My own vineyard is before me” (8:12). Even as others listen for her voice, He still says, “Let me hear it!” (8:13). The Song ends with longing: “Make haste, my beloved” (8:14).

Our story. Maturity in Christ looks like more dependence, not less. The strongest believers have come up out of their own wilderness leaning on Him. The seal asks that our private devotion and our public life both belong to Him, and only love makes that possible: “the love of Christ compels us” (2 Corinthians 5:14). Mature believers build up and protect younger ones instead of loading them with demands. The fruitful never outgrow the secret place. And the more we know Him, the more we want Him to come, as the whole Bible ends: “Even so, come, Lord Jesus!” (Revelation 22:20).

A question to carry: What wilderness are you coming up out of, and are you leaning on Him in it?
