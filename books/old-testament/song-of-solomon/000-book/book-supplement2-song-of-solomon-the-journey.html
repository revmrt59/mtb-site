Introduction

The Song of Solomon calls itself “The song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (1:1), the song above every other song. Read as a portrait of Christ and His people, it tells the story of a King who draws an ordinary woman into His presence, extends His favor to her before she has anything to offer, and then patiently leads her, through delight, failure, testing, and wounding, until she belongs to Him with her whole life.

The Song is real love poetry, and its images come from vineyards, gardens, flocks, mountains, city streets, and a royal court. Scripture uses the language of marriage for God and His people: the LORD calls Himself His people’s husband (Isaiah 54:5), Jesus calls Himself the Bridegroom (Matthew 9:15), and Paul says the union of husband and wife speaks “concerning Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:32). Read in that light, the Song becomes a sustained portrait of Christ, who shepherds, reigns over, and loves His people, and the bride He is making wholly His.

The Song is not a sequential series of events. Its scenes recur and deepen, the way the Christian life does. But it has a clear direction. It moves from a love that mostly receives to a love that is secure in His love, shares His work, and stays close to Him in every step. That direction is the journey into intimacy and becoming wholly His. Along the way are places where He meets her, words He speaks over her, trials that expose her heart, blessings that strengthen her, and three declarations of belonging that mark how far her love has come.

Her Story Is Our Story

The woman in the Song has no name until late in the poem, and even then she is called only “the Shulamite” (6:13). That suits the way the Song works. She portrays Christ’s people as a whole and each believer within that people, so her story is meant to be recognized, not just observed. Many believers know her moments from the inside: the first taste of His love that made everything else seem flat; the years of serving hard in someone else’s vineyard while their own walk with God dried up; the day they knew He was calling them to something more and answered, in effect, “Not yet. Not until I’m cleaner.” Many also know the night when they prayed and heard nothing, and the people who should have helped them hurt them instead.

The Song does not give a timetable every believer must follow, and her scenes do not come to everyone in the same order. What it gives is a portrait of how Christ loves and how love grows in the one He loves. So this supplement tells her story and then, at each stage, asks where that story touches ours: what it looks like in a believer’s life today, what holds us back, and what He does about it. Read slowly. Somewhere in these pages you will likely find the place where you are standing right now, and you will find that He has already spoken to it.

The Story

The King draws her, and she comes into His chambers, where He welcomes her in private. There He extends His favor, as a king once held out the golden scepter to Esther. His welcome makes her want to know Him more: “Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth” (1:2). She finds that others loved Him before she did, and now she knows from her own experience that “Rightly do they love you” (1:4).

She starts well, but she soon gets off track. She is ashamed of her own darkness and shrinks from being seen. She has also spent herself under the demands of angry brothers, working the vineyards they assigned her. That work felt like devotion and passed for growth, but it only burned her out, and her own vineyard, her own heart before the King, went untended. So she turns and asks Him where He feeds His flock. He brings her back. He tells her how beautiful she is and seats her at His table. Her worship rises like the scent of spikenard, a costly perfume. Through the night, she keeps the memory of His suffering love close, like a bundle of myrrh. She rests in the shade of the tree that points to the cross, is filled in His house of wine, lives under His banner of love, and rests in His embrace.

Then He calls her to follow Him: “Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away” (2:10). The season has changed, and He wants to meet her in the quiet place of prayer where she learns to follow Him. She claims Him as hers, but she turns Him away. She doesn’t feel clean enough to follow: until the day breaks and her shadows flee, she can’t go. Now she misses the One she sent away. She searches for Him by night, finds that the watchmen cannot take His place, and finds Him just past them. She holds Him and brings Him into the place where the Spirit brought her to new birth, where God gives her time to grow in love.

In that place He keeps revealing Himself. She sees the King in His glory on His wedding day. He tells her what she means to Him. He takes up her own words, “until the day breaks,” and goes to the mountain of myrrh to deal with the darkness she could not remove. Then He declares, “There is no spot in you” (4:7), and calls again, more specifically: “Come with me” (4:8), into heights where lions and leopards live. She does not answer at once. He keeps speaking, telling her how deeply she moves Him and comparing her life to a fragrant garden, until she yields: “Awake, O north wind, And come, O south!” (4:16). She welcomes both hardship and blessing so that others may come to know Christ through her life. “My garden” becomes “his garden,” and He comes to receive its fruit.

That surrender is tested at once. He knocks by night, and comfort keeps her in bed. When she rises, He is gone. She calls and He does not answer. The watchmen strike and wound her. Yet out of her wounds comes her fullest praise of Him, and others want to seek Him with her. She finds that He was in His garden, her own life, all along, and she now speaks first of belonging to Him: “I am my beloved’s”.

He returns without a word of reproach and repeats His praise of her. She is chosen above all, shining like the morning, called back so that she may be seen, and pictured in a dance that shows her learning to move in step with God. She begins to care about what He wants to grow in others and joins Him in His work. Now resting in His desire for her, she invites Him: “Come, my beloved, Let us go forth to the field” (7:11). She brings out fruit, new and old, laid up for Him, and serves Him at her own table.

At last she comes up from the wilderness, so changed that the onlookers marvel. She leans on her Beloved, stays close to Him, and depends on Him for every step. She asks to be set as a seal on His heart and arm, bound to Him in His deepest feelings and visible life. Love from the LORD burns in her like a fire no flood can put out. That love enables her to give up living for herself and live for Him. She cares for a younger sister, keeps her own vineyard before Him, and finds peace in His eyes. The one who once sent the Gazelle away to the mountains of separation now shares His freedom to walk the heights, and she calls Him to the mountains of spices: “Make haste, my beloved” (8:14).

Our Story

Everyone who belongs to Christ began where she began: drawn. We did not find our way to Him by our own searching. He drew us, and the first thing we tasted was favor we had not earned. It may have come at an altar, in a hospital waiting room, or alone in a parked car, but at some point His love stopped being something we had heard about. It became better than wine, and we wanted more of Him. We also found that others had loved Him before us, and we ran with them.

Then, for many of us, came the vineyards. We got busy. We joined teams, filled roles, and tried to meet the expectations of people who seemed to know what a serious Christian looks like. Some of it was good work, but much of it ran on approval, guilt, or the fear of letting someone down. It passed for growth. Inside, we were burning out, and the one vineyard that mattered most, our own heart before the Lord, went untended. Like her, we grew ashamed to be seen by Him. We assumed He was as disappointed in us as the people we were trying to please.

He did not answer that with a heavier load. He called us back to His table and told us we were beautiful to Him. The cross became sweet again, no longer only a doctrine we affirmed but the shade where we finally sat down. He filled us with His Spirit, and we learned that His banner over us was love, not performance.

Then He called us to follow, and that was harder. Most believers who have walked with Jesus for long remember the moment He asked for more: real time alone with Him, a habit to surrender, a step of obedience that would cost something. Many of us answered, “Not yet.” We loved Him, but we felt too unclean and too unfinished to go with Him, as if we had to clean ourselves up first. Then we missed Him. Meetings, leaders, and good books could not take His place. We had to go past all of them to find Him and hold on.

He showed us what He had done about our darkness. He did not wait for us to remove it. He went to the cross Himself and declared us clean. Then He called again, this time toward the lions and leopards: real opposition and real cost. We hesitated, and He kept telling us how He saw us until love outweighed fear. We told Him to send whatever wind He chose, hard or pleasant, and our life became His garden.

That surrender was tested soon. Some of us grew comfortable and did not want to get up when He knocked. Some went through long stretches when prayer met only silence. Some were wounded by the very people who should have protected us. Yet many found, in that pain, that they saw Him more clearly than ever, and others began to seek Him because of how they spoke of Him. He had been in His garden, at work in our lives, the whole time.

When we came back from our failures, He did not lecture us. He spoke the same words over us that He had spoken before. Restored, we began to care about what He cares about, the growth of other people, and we went back into the vineyards. This time the work did not burn us out, because we were doing it with Him, out of rest in His desire for us, instead of for someone else’s approval.

That is where the Song is taking every believer: up out of the wilderness, leaning on Him with our whole weight, sealed to Him in heart and in life, caring for those coming behind us, keeping our own vineyard before Him, and longing for Him to come. Wherever you find yourself in that story, the One who drew you at the beginning is still drawing you. He began this work, and He intends to finish it (Philippians 1:6).

The Journey to Intimacy

Intimacy is mutual knowing and belonging. She receives His love and loves Him in return. As she learns what matters to Him, she gives her life to Him and comes to care more about what He desires than about what she needs.

Intimacy is not a feeling she achieves. It begins in His welcome and deepens as the assurance of His welcome shapes how she lives. Its end is union: the two become one (Ephesians 5:31–32).

How intimacy grows: love received and returned

Throughout the Song, He speaks first and she answers. Her love grows as she receives His.

  • “Behold, you are fair” (1:15) is answered by “Behold, you are handsome” (1:16).

  • “Like a lily among thorns” (2:2) is answered by “Like an apple tree among the trees of the woods” (2:3).

  • “Your love is better than wine”, her first words (1:2), are returned to her by Him: “How much better than wine is your love” (4:10).

  • His head-to-toe praise of her (4:1–5) is answered, out of her wounds, by her head-to-toe praise of Him (5:10–16).

  • The gaze she feared, “Do not look upon me” (1:6), becomes the gaze that moves Him, “You have ravished my heart With one look of your eyes” (4:9), and the place where she rests, “in his eyes As one who found peace” (8:10).

The three marks

The three declarations of belonging are the turning points of the journey. Each shows where the center of her love lies.

Mark The center What it shows
1. “My beloved is mine, and I am his” (2:16) What she has She speaks first of possessing Him. The belonging is real, but in the next breath she turns Him away because she doesn’t feel clean enough to follow (2:17). Intimacy is genuine but centered on herself: He is mine.
2. “I am my beloved’s, And my beloved is mine” (6:3) Whose she is After testing, absence, wounding, and witness, the order reverses. She speaks first of belonging to Him. Intimacy has become surrender: I am His.
3. “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me” (7:10) His desire “Mine” drops out entirely. She rests in His desire for her and at once invites Him into the fields (7:11). Intimacy has become rest and partnership: I live in His desire and share His work.

The Hebrew word for “desire” in 7:10 occurs elsewhere in the Old Testament only in Genesis 3:16 and 4:7. After the fall the woman heard, “Your desire shall be for your husband, And he shall rule over you” (Genesis 3:16). In the Song, it is the Bridegroom’s desire that is toward His bride. The third mark pictures redemption reversing the broken pattern of the fall.

The seal on all three

The seal and the flame (8:6–7) are not a fourth declaration. They bring all three declarations to their fullest expression. She asks to be made His in His deepest feelings and visible life, bound permanently and publicly as His beloved. The flame of Yah, or the LORD, pictures the love that purifies her and survives every trial. Only love gives her the strength to stop living for herself and live for Him.

What mature intimacy looks like

“Who is this coming up from the wilderness, Leaning upon her beloved?” (8:5). Leaning gathers reliance, closeness, and union. She is close enough to rest her whole weight on Him, and they move together.

The three marks in our lives

Most believers can hear themselves in all three sentences. The first is where many live for years: He is mine. We know He loves us, we stand on His promises, and we come to Him for what we need. None of that is wrong. It is the honest faith of someone who has truly received Him. But a love centered on what we have will still tell Him “not yet” when following costs too much, as she did in the very next verse (2:17).

The second sentence usually comes through hardship. Something we could not control, such as a loss, a long silence in prayer, or a wound from people we trusted, moves the center from what we have to whose we are. “I am my beloved’s” is what a believer says after learning that Jesus is worth holding on to even when He does not feel near.

The third sentence is rest: “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me” (7:10). The believer who can say it no longer grasps for assurance or works to keep His attention. Knowing that we are wanted, we are free to go out with Him into the fields. It is worth asking which sentence comes most naturally to you right now. The answer is not a grade on your spiritual life. It shows where His love is leading you next.

The Seven Movements in the Song of Solomon

I. Called, Favored, and Awakened (1:1–4)

The Song names itself “The song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (1:1): the song above every other song, the story unlike any other.

The poem gives us her longing before it tells us how the King awakened it. Her first words are a cry: “Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth— For your love is better than wine” (1:2). This isn’t the longing of someone who has only heard about Him. She has tasted His love and found it better than the best this world offers. She knows the fragrance of His name: “Your name is ointment poured forth” (1:3). Her hunger comes from what she has already received.

Verse 4 tells us what happened: “The king has brought me into his chambers.” He drew her. She came, and He brought her into the privacy of His own rooms and extended His favor. The chambers here aren’t first a marriage bed. The wedding day comes at 3:11 and the consummation imagery at 4:16–5:1. This is the King’s inner room, a place of access and favor.

Esther shows the same picture: an ordinary young woman brought into the king’s palace, who “obtained grace and favor in his sight more than all the virgins” (Esther 2:17). Later, when she stood before him, he “held out to Esther the golden scepter” (Esther 5:2). Psalm 45 sings of the royal bride: “She shall be brought to the King… With gladness and rejoicing they shall be brought; They shall enter the King’s palace” (Psalm 45:14–15). The Song echoes it closely: the King, being brought, the virgins, “We will be glad and rejoice in you” (1:4).

So grace comes first. She is welcomed before she is mature, before she has anything to offer, and her desire is the fruit of His welcome, not the price of it. “With lovingkindness I have drawn you” (Jeremiah 31:3). “No one can come to Me unless the Father who sent Me draws him” (John 6:44). Her prayer, “Draw me away!” (1:4), is the prayer of someone who has felt the pull of His love and wants more of it. We come boldly because He has opened the way (Hebrews 4:16; 10:19).

She also finds she isn’t the first to love Him. “Therefore the virgins love you” (1:3). Others loved Him before she did, drawn by His name. Now, having tasted for herself, she agrees: “Rightly do they love you” (1:4). Their love was right, and now it’s hers too. So she runs with them: “We will run after you.” The journey begins personally, but she isn’t alone. She joins a company who already love Him. Later she passes on what she received: others will learn to love Him through her testimony (6:1).

Images along the way

  • “The song of songs”: the greatest of songs, the story unlike any other.

  • The kisses of His mouth: His personal word and nearness, not secondhand knowledge.

  • Wine: the best joys of this world; His love is better.

  • Ointment poured forth: His name, Jesus, sweet and healing to the whole person.

  • “Draw me away”: His drawing comes before her running.

  • The chambers: the King’s inner presence; access and favor by grace.

  • The virgins: those who loved Him first; the company she joins.

Her Story, Our Story

The order of this opening matters for every believer. Many Christians live as if desire has to come first: if I want God enough, pray hard enough, and prove I am sincere, He will draw near. The Song turns that around. Her longing is the fruit of His welcome. She wants more because she has already tasted.

That is good news for anyone who feels spiritually dry. Dryness does not mean you must manufacture hunger before you are allowed to come. The way back to desire is the way she found it in the first place: coming into His presence and receiving again what He freely gives. “Draw me away!” (1:4) is a prayer anyone can pray, even someone who feels very little. It asks Him to do again what He did at the beginning.

The opening also corrects a lonely kind of faith. She learns that others loved Him before she did, and she runs with them: “We will run after you.” Our life with Christ is personal, but it is never solitary. Most of us came to Him through people who loved Him first: a grandmother who prayed, a pastor who preached, a friend who kept inviting us. Part of growing up in Christ is gladly agreeing with them, “Rightly do they love you,” and in time becoming that person for someone else.

Where Are You in the Story?

  • When did His love first become more to you than something you had heard about? What did it taste like?

  • Do you tend to think you must want God enough before He will welcome you? How does “The king has brought me into his chambers” (1:4) answer that?

  • Who loved Him before you did and helped you run after Him? Who might be learning to love Him through you?

II. Dark but Lovely: Off Track, Called Back, and Assured (1:5–2:7)

The welcome was real, but she soon gets off track. Before the daughters of Jerusalem she admits, “I am dark, but lovely, O daughters of Jerusalem, Like the tents of Kedar, Like the curtains of Solomon” (1:5). She is like the black goat-hair tents of the desert nomads, and also like the fine curtains of the king. Both are true of her at once.

She longs to be near Him but wonders how she can stand being seen: “Do not look upon me, because I am dark, Because the sun has tanned me” (1:6). Part of the darkness is her own: the fallen condition every believer still carries. The rest of the image points to the toll of working under her brothers’ demands.

“My mother’s sons were angry with me; They made me the keeper of the vineyards, But my own vineyard I have not kept” (1:6). Her brothers put her to work for them. Their anger drove her, and their vineyards consumed her. It looked like devotion and passed for growth, but it only scorched her. Working for others left her with no time to care for her own relationship with the King. She was busy doing what others called devotion, but their demands wore her out.

Martha was “distracted with much serving” while Mary “sat at Jesus’ feet and heard His word” (Luke 10:39–40). The church at Ephesus labored faithfully, yet the Lord said, “you have left your first love” (Revelation 2:4). Paul asks, “Having begun in the Spirit, are you now being made perfect by the flesh?” (Galatians 3:3). The remedy is to “Keep your heart with all diligence” (Proverbs 4:23). Her own vineyard has to be kept.

Then she turns back to Him. “Tell me, O you whom I love, Where you feed your flock, Where you make it rest at noon” (1:7). She is weary in the heat of noon and wants His rest. She won’t wander like a veiled stranger “By the flocks of your companions.” She wants Him, not a substitute.

His answer sends her to His people: “Follow in the footsteps of the flock, And feed your little goats Beside the shepherds’ tents” (1:8). He meets her through faithful shepherds and the company of believers who have learned to follow Him.

Then He looks at her, and the gaze she feared becomes a gaze of delight. He compares her to a filly among Pharaoh’s chariots (1:9). She is noticed, beautiful and strong. He says her cheeks and neck are lovely with ornaments and promises, “We will make you ornaments of gold With studs of silver” (1:10–11). Her beauty is His gift.

She comes near: “While the king is at his table, My spikenard sends forth its fragrance” (1:12). At His table she enjoys fellowship with Him because He has made a way for her to come near (1 Corinthians 10:16; Psalm 23:5). There her worship rises like perfume. Centuries later Jesus sat at a table in Bethany. While “Martha served,” Mary “took a pound of very costly oil of spikenard, anointed the feet of Jesus… And the house was filled with the fragrance of the oil” (John 12:2–3). The woman who had chosen to sit at His feet poured out her worship at His table. This movement holds both sides of that story: worn out from serving, then restored to worship in His presence.

“A bundle of myrrh is my beloved to me, That lies all night between my breasts” (1:13). Myrrh was a gift at His birth (Matthew 2:11), was offered to Him at the cross (Mark 15:23), and was brought for His burial (John 19:39). His suffering love becomes precious to her, and she keeps it close all night. In the night seasons, the fragrance of what He suffered for her is her comfort. “My beloved is to me a cluster of henna blooms In the vineyards of En Gedi” (1:14): fragrance and life at an oasis in the desert.

Now love is received and returned, and their words mirror each other. He says, “Behold, you are fair, my love! Behold, you are fair! You have dove’s eyes” (1:15), and she answers, “Behold, you are handsome, my beloved!” (1:16). She calls herself an ordinary wildflower, “the rose of Sharon, And the lily of the valleys” (2:1), and He answers, “Like a lily among thorns, So is my love among the daughters” (2:2). She answers again: “Like an apple tree among the trees of the woods, So is my beloved among the sons” (2:3). He speaks first, and she echoes Him. She begins to receive that she is beautiful in His eyes, and at the same time His beauty becomes more precious to her. That is how intimacy grows: love received and returned.

She speaks of a home they share: “our bed is green. The beams of our houses are cedar, And our rafters of fir” (1:16–17). It is flourishing and lasting, a dwelling with Him (John 14:23).

Then she rests. “I sat down in his shade with great delight, And his fruit was sweet to my taste” (2:3). The apple tree is where she finds rest, and it points to the cross. Christ “Himself bore our sins in His own body on the tree” (1 Peter 2:24). Under that tree she rests from the heat that scorched her in her brothers’ vineyards, and she feeds on the sweetness of what He has done. At the end of the Song the same tree returns: “I awakened you under the apple tree” (8:5). The journey begins and ends at the tree.

“He brought me to the banqueting house, And his banner over me was love” (2:4). Again He is the one who brings her. The house of wine is the house of the Spirit. Paul sets the two side by side: “do not be drunk with wine… but be filled with the Spirit” (Ephesians 5:18). In His house she is filled with His joy and His life.

His banner over her is love, and His love is what brings us victory. A banner went up over an army and marked whose side it was on. The LORD is “My Banner” (Exodus 17:15). Her victory doesn’t come from her strength, her performance, or her brothers’ approval. It comes from His love: “in all these things we are more than conquerors through Him who loved us” (Romans 8:37). That banner goes with her into every hardship ahead.

His love overwhelms her: “Sustain me with cakes of raisins, Refresh me with apples, For I am lovesick” (2:5). Then He holds her: “His left hand is under my head, And his right hand embraces me” (2:6). She came into this movement scorched, weary, and ashamed of being seen. She ends it held, supported, and at rest.

The movement closes with the first charge: “Do not stir up nor awaken love Until it pleases” (2:7). This is the first rest. Love can’t be forced, and He gives rest in its time.

Her Story, Our Story

The brothers’ vineyards are easy to find in church life. They look like the ministry treadmill: the volunteer who cannot say no, the leader who measures spiritual health by attendance and activity, the believer who serves out of fear of disappointing a pastor, a parent, or a reputation. The brothers in the Song are “angry,” and that is the giveaway. Work driven by someone’s displeasure, or by fear of it, drives instead of draws. It can produce a great deal of fruit for others while our own heart goes dry.

Notice what she says first: “Do not look upon me.” Burnout and shame tend to travel together. When our inner life with God has been neglected, we assume He looks at us the way the angry brothers did, with disappointment and demand. So we avoid Him, and the dryness deepens. Many believers in this condition are not backsliding in any obvious way. They are still serving. They have simply stopped sitting at His table.

His remedy is striking. He does not give her a better plan for the vineyards or tell her to try harder. He tells her she is beautiful, seats her at His table, and lets her rest under the tree. For us, that means coming back to the simple places where Christ feeds His people: the Lord’s Table, unhurried prayer, the Word read to know Him rather than to finish a reading plan, and the company of believers who are following Him well (1:8). It means letting the cross become shade again, the place where we stop trying to earn what has already been given. Keeping our own vineyard does not begin with more discipline. It begins with sitting down.

The banner matters for what lies ahead. Victory in the hard places of this life will not come from our performance or from other people’s approval. It comes from the love He has declared over us. Believers who know that banner can serve hard without being consumed, because their service flows from His love instead of trying to secure it.

Where Are You in the Story?

  • Whose vineyards are you working in right now? What drives that work: love for Him, or fear of someone’s disapproval?

  • What has happened to your own vineyard, your personal life with God, while you have been busy?

  • Where do you shrink from His gaze? What does He say to you instead (1:15; 2:2)?

  • What would it look like this week to sit at His table instead of only serving at it?

III. The Call to Follow (2:8–3:5)

The bride has been welcomed, fed, and given rest. Now the Beloved comes in a new way: “The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes Leaping upon the mountains, Skipping upon the hills” (2:8). The heights are where He moves freely. He comes right up to the wall and looks in through the lattice (2:9). The wall pictures the limits of our mortal life. Christ is near, though we cannot yet see Him in His fullness. The openings in the wall let her catch a glimpse of Him watching over her with love. One day His people will see Him face to face.

He announces a new season before she feels ready for it: “For lo, the winter is past, The rain is over and gone” (2:11). Flowers appear, the time of singing has come, the turtledove’s voice is heard, and the fig tree and vines show their first, tender fruit (2:12–13). The season of growth and love has begun. Winter may mean barrenness, hiddenness, or hardship; whatever it was, He declares it over.

Then comes the first call to follow: “Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away” (2:10, 13). This is the Song’s form of Jesus’ call to His disciples: “Follow Me” (Matthew 4:19). The relationship can’t stay only at the table and under the banner. Love that has been received is now invited to walk with Him. The call rests on what He declares, not on what she feels.

He knows where she is: “O my dove, in the clefts of the rock, In the secret places of the cliff, Let me see your face, Let me hear your voice; For your voice is sweet, And your face is lovely” (2:14). She is hidden, like a dove sheltering in the rocks. He wants to meet her in the secret place, “the secret places of the stairs,” as the older translations put it. The Hebrew word behind “cliff” describes a steep place. The image connects the climb with the daily time she spends alone with Him in prayer, where He sees her face and hears her voice. The clefts of the rock are also where God hid Moses to show him His glory (Exodus 33:22), and “that Rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4). Her life is “hidden with Christ in God” (Colossians 3:3). When the LORD passed by Moses and declared His name, Moses saw Him only from behind. The image can call to mind the wounds Christ bore for us.

New growth is tender, and small things can ruin it: “Catch us the foxes, The little foxes that spoil the vines, For our vines have tender grapes” (2:15). The danger at this stage isn’t lions but foxes: small compromises, distractions, resentments, delays, and the need for human approval that quietly spoil what He is growing. “A little leaven leavens the whole lump” (Galatians 5:9). The more tender the growth, the more easily it is spoiled. “Catch us… our vines”: He calls her to deal with them together with Him.

Then comes the first mark of intimacy: “My beloved is mine, and I am his” (2:16). It’s true, and she means it, but she speaks first of what is hers.

And then she turns Him away: “Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, Turn, my beloved, And be like a gazelle Or a young stag Upon the mountains of Bether” (2:17). She doesn’t feel clean enough to follow. In effect she is saying, “Until my darkness is gone, I can’t go with You.” The woman who said “I am dark” and “Do not look upon me” (1:5–6) still sees the shadows on herself. She thinks she has to be clean before she can follow, so she sends Him on ahead.

That is the oldest instinct of shame: “I was afraid because I was naked; and I hid myself” (Genesis 3:10). Peter did the same when Jesus first called him: “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord!” Jesus answered, “Do not be afraid. From now on you will catch men,” and Peter left everything and followed Him (Luke 5:8–11). Isaiah, seeing the King, cried, “Woe is me… I am a man of unclean lips.” Only after his sin was purged did he say, “Here am I! Send me” (Isaiah 6:5–8). She has yet to learn that He cleanses those He calls. She doesn’t have to clean herself before she follows.

She misses the One she sent away. “By night on my bed I sought the one I love; I sought him, but I did not find him” (3:1). This time she rises: “I will rise now… And go about the city; In the streets and in the squares I will seek the one I love” (3:2).

She asks the watchmen, the guardians of the city, “Have you seen the one I love?” (3:3). They can’t give Him to her. “Scarcely had I passed by them, When I found the one I love” (3:4). No guardian, leader, or ministry can take His place in her relationship with Him. “There is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5). She had to go past them to find Him.

When she finds Him, she holds on: “I held him and would not let him go” (3:4). Jacob held on through the night until the day broke and said, “I will not let You go unless You bless me!” (Genesis 32:26). She brings Him “to the house of my mother, And into the chamber of her who conceived me.” She brings Him into the place where she was born, the place of the Spirit’s work in her (John 3:5–6).

Then comes the charge: “Do not stir up nor awaken love Until it pleases” (3:5). God is doing a work in her. Don’t disturb it. “He who has begun a good work in you will complete it” (Philippians 1:6). It is the second rest.

Images along the way

  • Leaping on the mountains: His freedom on the heights.

  • The wall and lattice: the limits of our mortal life. Christ is near and lets us see enough to know He is watching over us.

  • Winter past; flowers, singing, turtledove, figs, vines: the new season of growth and love that He declares.

  • “Rise up… And come away”: the call to follow (Matthew 4:19).

  • The clefts of the rock, the secret places of the stairs: time alone with Him in prayer, where she learns to follow Him.

  • “Let me see your face, Let me hear your voice”: He wants her presence and her voice. Prayer goes both ways.

  • The little foxes: small tolerated things that spoil tender growth; dealt with together.

  • Feeding among the lilies: He is found among His people.

  • “Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away”: she won’t follow until her darkness is gone. She doesn’t feel clean enough.

  • The mountains of Bether: perhaps “separation”; the meaning is uncertain.

  • The bed by night and the city streets: missing Him, then rising to seek Him.

  • The watchmen: guardians who can’t take His place.

  • Holding Him and not letting go: Like Jacob, she holds on until she receives what she seeks.

  • Her mother’s house, the chamber of her who conceived me: the place of new birth, the Spirit’s work in her.

  • The gazelles and does: God is working; don’t disturb it.

Her Story, Our Story

Every believer eventually hears “Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away.” Grace welcomed us as we were, but love does not leave us there. The call often comes quietly: a nudge to spend real time alone with Him, to surrender something we have been holding, to forgive someone, or to take a step of obedience that will cost comfort or control. And it usually comes before we feel ready. He announces the new season; we do not wait until we sense it.

Her answer is one many of us have given. “Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away… Turn.” In our words: I’ll follow You when I’ve got my life together, when I’ve beaten this habit, when I’m a little more spiritual. It sounds humble, but it is a refusal, and it rests on a mistaken idea, that we must become clean before we can follow. Peter and Isaiah show the opposite. He cleanses those He calls.

The little foxes belong at this stage. What spoils new growth is rarely a dramatic sin. It is the small things we tolerate: a resentment we nurse, a screen that fills every quiet moment, a delay that hardens into a habit, a need for people’s approval. They seem too small to matter, and that is exactly what makes them dangerous to tender vines. He says “Catch us,” and the vines are “our vines.” We deal with the foxes together with Him, not alone.

When we send Him away, we feel His absence. Many believers then do what she did and go to the watchmen. They look to a leader, a conference, a counselor, or a new church to give them what only Christ can give. Good leaders help, and the Song never despises them. But no one can take His place. Sometimes the most important thing a pastor can do is point past himself, and the most important thing a believer can do is keep going past every helper until she finds Him and holds on.

Where Are You in the Story?

  • What has He been calling you to that you keep postponing until you feel ready?

  • Have you ever told Him, in effect, “Not until I’m cleaner”? How does His answer in 4:6–7 speak to that?

  • What little foxes are loose in your vineyard?

  • Are you looking to a person, ministry, or experience to give you what only Christ can give?

IV. The King Revealed and the Garden Offered (3:6–5:1)

In the place where God is working, He keeps drawing her by revealing Himself. “Who is this coming out of the wilderness Like pillars of smoke, Perfumed with myrrh and frankincense, With all the merchant’s fragrant powders?” (3:6). A procession rises out of the wilderness like the pillar that led Israel (Exodus 13:21). It is fragrant with myrrh, His suffering love, and frankincense, worship and prayer (Psalm 141:2).

“Behold, it is Solomon’s couch, With sixty valiant men around it… Every man has his sword on his thigh Because of fear in the night” (3:7–8). The armed guard shows the protection the King provides even while the dangers of the night are real: “You shall not be afraid of the terror by night” (Psalm 91:5). His palanquin, a royal carriage, is made of the wood of Lebanon, with pillars of silver, a support of gold, a seat of purple, and “Its interior paved with love” (3:9–10).

Then the daughters of Zion are called out to see the King Himself: crowned “On the day of his wedding, The day of the gladness of his heart” (3:11). For the first time the Song shows Him crowned on His wedding day. His wedding joy points to Christ’s joy in bringing His people into a lasting bond with Him. “Your eyes will see the King in His beauty” (Isaiah 33:17). Jesus, “for the joy that was set before Him endured the cross” (Hebrews 12:2).

Then He speaks pleasantly to her and tells her what she means to Him. He begins with the words of 1:15, “Behold, you are fair, my love! Behold, you are fair!”, and describes her from head to toe (4:1–5). Her eyes are doves’ eyes behind her veil. Her lips are “like a strand of scarlet.” Her neck is “like the tower of David… On which hang a thousand bucklers.” Her teeth are like shorn sheep, “Every one of which bears twins, And none is barren among them.” He sees fruitfulness and strength where she saw only darkness.

Then comes the answer to her refusal. She had said, “Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, Turn” (2:17): she couldn’t follow until her darkness was gone. Now He picks up her own words: “Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, I will go my way to the mountain of myrrh And to the hill of frankincense” (4:6). She waited for her shadows to leave. He goes to deal with them Himself. The mountain of myrrh is the way of His suffering love, the cross, and the hill of frankincense is the place where He prays for her. What she couldn’t do for herself, He does.

So the next words are His verdict: “You are all fair, my love, And there is no spot in you” (4:7). The reason she gave for refusing Him has been removed. “The blood of Jesus Christ His Son cleanses us from all sin” (1 John 1:7). He presents her to Himself “not having spot or wrinkle… holy and without blemish” (Ephesians 5:27). She isn’t clean enough because she finally cleaned herself. She is clean because He went to the mountain of myrrh.

Only then does He call again, and this second call is more specific: “Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse, With me from Lebanon. Look from the top of Amana, From the top of Senir and Hermon, From the lions’ dens, From the mountains of the leopards” (4:8). For the first time He calls her “my spouse.” The call in chapter 2 was simply “come away.” Now He names the places and the dangers. Lebanon is the high mountain range on Israel’s northern border. Amana is a peak in the Anti-Lebanon range. Senir is the Amorite name for Hermon (Deuteronomy 3:9), the highest, snow-capped mountain of the region. These are wild heights, the haunts of lions and leopards. From their tops one could see far across the land. So the call holds a view she could never have from below, and real danger she will have to face: opposition and spiritual warfare in following Him. But He says “with me” twice. She will not go alone.

She doesn’t answer at once. Shame no longer stands in the way, but the lions and leopards are real. We are afraid to follow Him into freedom. “Where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty” (2 Corinthians 3:17), and “perfect love casts out fear” (1 John 4:18).

So He keeps speaking and lets His love do its work. “You have ravished my heart, My sister, my spouse; You have ravished my heart With one look of your eyes” (4:9). The woman who feared His gaze now moves His heart with hers. “How much better than wine is your love” (4:10): her own first words are returned to her (1:2). “Your lips, O my spouse, Drip as the honeycomb… And the fragrance of your garments Is like the fragrance of Lebanon” (4:11).

He compares her life to a fragrant garden set apart for Him. She is “A garden enclosed… A spring shut up, A fountain sealed” (4:12), a life set apart for Him, which is holiness: becoming wholly His. Her garden is full of fruit and spices: pomegranates, henna, spikenard, saffron, cinnamon, frankincense, myrrh, and aloes (4:13–14). The spikenard of her worship and the myrrh of His suffering love now grow in her. She is also “A fountain of gardens, A well of living waters, And streams from Lebanon” (4:15). The Spirit’s life flows out of her: “Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water,” which Jesus “spoke concerning the Spirit” (John 7:38–39).

At last she comes to the point of surrender: “Awake, O north wind, And come, O south! Blow upon my garden, That its spices may flow out. Let my beloved come to his garden And eat its pleasant fruits” (4:16). She accepts both the hardships and the blessings He allows her to face. The south wind is pleasant: refreshing and blessing. The north wind is cold and hard: hardship and His discipline. “No chastening seems to be joyful for the present, but painful; nevertheless, afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11). She welcomes both, so that others may come to know Christ through her life (2 Corinthians 2:15), because that is what pleases Him. In one verse “my garden” becomes “his garden.” That is the heart of becoming wholly His.

He comes at once: “I have come to my garden, my sister, my spouse; I have gathered my myrrh with my spice; I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey; I have drunk my wine with my milk” (5:1). He receives everything and calls it His. Then He turns to others: “Eat, O friends! Drink, yes, drink deeply, O beloved ones!” The fruit of a surrendered life feeds others: “through us [He] diffuses the fragrance of His knowledge in every place” (2 Corinthians 2:14).

This is the high point of the first half of the Song. For the first time her love gives, not only receives.

Images along the way

  • Coming out of the wilderness like pillars of smoke: God’s presence leading His people.

  • Myrrh and frankincense: His suffering love; worship and prayer.

  • The sixty valiant men with swords “Because of fear in the night”: the King’s security against the terrors of the night.

  • The palanquin “paved with love”: The royal carriage pictures His provision, and love fills it.

  • The crown on His wedding day: the King in His glory, the covenant, the gladness of His heart.

  • The head-to-toe praise: He sees her whole person, fruitful (twins, none barren) and strong (the tower of David).

  • “Until the day breaks… I will go my way to the mountain of myrrh”: He goes to the cross and the place of intercession to deal with the darkness she couldn’t remove.

  • “No spot in you”: the cleansing that answers her refusal at 2:17.

  • “Come with me… my spouse”: the second, more specific call, with a new name.

  • Lebanon, Amana, Senir, Hermon: the heights of the north, and the view from them.

  • The lions’ dens and leopards’ mountains: real opposition and spiritual warfare, faced with Him.

  • “You have ravished my heart With one look of your eyes”: She once feared being seen. Now her gaze moves His heart.

  • “Better than wine is your love”: her first words returned to her.

  • Honeycomb and the fragrance of Lebanon: the sweetness and fragrance He finds in her.

  • The garden enclosed, the spring shut up, the fountain sealed: set apart for Him; holiness.

  • The orchard of spices: what she received now grows in her.

  • The fountain and well of living waters: the Spirit’s life flowing out.

  • The north and south winds: She welcomes both hardship and blessing from His hand.

  • “My garden” becomes “his garden”: her life given over to Him.

  • “Eat, O friends!”: her fruit feeds others.

Her Story, Our Story

Look at how He draws her after her refusal. He does not scold her or simply repeat the command. He shows her His glory and tells her what she means to Him. That is still how Christ moves His people forward. Believers rarely grow because they are pressured. They grow because they see Him more clearly and begin to believe what He says about them. One fresh sight of the King, crowned with gladness on His wedding day, does more to get us moving than any amount of guilt.

The order here is vital. He deals with her darkness (4:6) and declares her spotless (4:7) before He calls her again (4:8). Many Christians reverse that order. They think their cleansing depends on their following, so they work to become acceptable and never feel they have arrived. The gospel puts the cross first. We do not follow in order to be made clean. We are made clean, and that frees us to follow.

That does not make the second call easy. “Come with me” leads past lions’ dens and leopards’ mountains. For one believer that means stepping into a ministry that will draw spiritual opposition. For another it means telling the truth at work, taking a stand in the family, or obeying God in a way that costs friends or money. Fear is a normal response, and the Song does not shame her for hesitating. What it shows is that He answers fear by speaking love, not by withdrawing the call. He says “with me” twice. We do not climb alone.

Then comes the prayer of a surrendered life: “Awake, O north wind, And come, O south!” Most of us gladly ask for the south wind of blessing. Few ask for the north wind. But a believer who has seen His love can say, “Send what You know is best, hard or pleasant, so that the fragrance of Christ comes out of my life.” That is when “my garden” becomes “his garden.” Our time, gifts, family, and future stop being things we manage as our own and become His. What He receives, He shares: “Eat, O friends!” A surrendered life feeds other people.

Where Are You in the Story?

  • Is there something you still believe about yourself that He settled at the cross? Read 4:7 slowly.

  • Where is He saying “Come with me” toward a place that frightens you?

  • Have you ever invited the north wind? What would it mean to pray 4:16 honestly?

  • Which part of your life is still “my garden” rather than “his garden”?

V. Tested After Surrender: Comfort, Absence, Wounding, and Witness (5:2–6:3)

The surrender of 4:16 was real, and it is tested at once. The garden has been offered, the Beloved has come and eaten, and she has gone to rest. Then, in the night: “I sleep, but my heart is awake; It is the voice of my beloved! He knocks, saying, ‘Open for me, my sister, my love, My dove, my perfect one; For my head is covered with dew, My locks with the drops of the night’” (5:2).

Her heart still knows His voice, but she is half asleep. He comes with every tender name He has given her, and He has been out in the night; His hair is wet with dew. In chapter 2 He called her to the mountains. Now He stands at her own door: “Behold, I stand at the door and knock” (Revelation 3:20). Some see His night-soaked head as an echo of Gethsemane, where He prayed in agony (Luke 22:44). The text doesn’t say so, but it fits the mountain of myrrh He went to in 4:6.

Her answer shows the test: “I have taken off my robe; How can I put it on again? I have washed my feet; How can I defile them?” (5:3). This time the problem isn’t that she doesn’t feel clean enough. She is clean, comfortable, and at rest, and she doesn’t want to be disturbed. Earlier she wouldn’t follow because she felt too dirty. Now she won’t rise because she doesn’t want to get dirty again. Shame held her back at 2:17. Comfort holds her back here. A real surrender can be followed by a fresh test of what it means in practice. Will she let Him interrupt her rest?

“My beloved put his hand By the latch of the door, And my heart yearned for him” (5:4). Her heart is moved, and she rises: “I arose to open for my beloved, And my hands dripped with myrrh, My fingers with liquid myrrh, On the handles of the lock” (5:5). He has left myrrh on the lock where His hand was. Even as He withdraws, the fragrance of His suffering love is on the door. The test itself carries the mark of the cross. She is being invited into “the fellowship of His sufferings” (Philippians 3:10).

“I opened for my beloved, But my beloved had turned away and was gone. My heart leaped up when he spoke. I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer” (5:6). This is the hardest place in the Song so far. She opens, and He is gone. She calls, and He doesn’t answer. This isn’t rejection. He hasn’t stopped loving her. She no longer senses His nearness. In the silence she learns to keep seeking and trusting Him. Job knew this: “I go forward, but He is not there… But He knows the way that I take; When He has tested me, I shall come forth as gold” (Job 23:8–10). “Who walks in darkness And has no light? Let him trust in the name of the LORD” (Isaiah 50:10).

She goes out searching again, and this time it costs her: “The watchmen who went about the city found me. They struck me, they wounded me; The keepers of the walls Took my veil away from me” (5:7). In chapter 3 the watchmen couldn’t help her. Now they hurt her. The guardians who should have protected her strike the one most passionately seeking Him. They don’t understand her love, and they strip away her covering. Their wrong stays wrong; the Song never excuses those who wound the people in their care. Following Him sometimes means going “outside the camp, bearing His reproach” (Hebrews 13:13).

Wounded and exposed, she still won’t stop: “I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, If you find my beloved, That you tell him I am lovesick!” (5:8). In 2:5 she was lovesick from His fullness. Now she is lovesick from His absence. Her love has survived His silence and the watchmen’s blows.

The daughters ask the question that changes everything: “What is your beloved More than another beloved, O fairest among women? What is your beloved More than another beloved, That you so charge us?” (5:9). Why is He worth all this?

Her answer is the fullest praise of Him in the whole Song (5:10–16). In chapter 4 He described her from head to toe. Now, out of her wounds, she describes Him from head to toe. Love received has become love returned, even in pain. “My beloved is white and ruddy, Chief among ten thousand” (5:10). His head is like the finest gold, and His hair is wavy and black as a raven (5:11). His eyes are like doves by the rivers of waters (5:12). His cheeks are a bed of spices, and His lips are lilies “Dripping liquid myrrh” (5:13); the myrrh is on His words. His hands are rods of gold set with beryl, and His body is carved ivory inlaid with sapphires (5:14). His legs are pillars of marble on bases of fine gold, and His countenance is like Lebanon, “Excellent as the cedars” (5:15). “His mouth is most sweet, Yes, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, And this is my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem!” (5:16).

She has never seen Him so clearly. The testing has helped her see more of who He is. The woman who once only enjoyed what He gave her now declares who He is, and she calls Him not only “my beloved” but “my friend.”

Her testimony awakens others: “Where has your beloved gone, O fairest among women? Where has your beloved turned aside, That we may seek him with you?” (6:1). At the beginning she learned from those who rightly loved Him (1:3–4). Now others learn to seek Him through her. God “comforts us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort those who are in any trouble” (2 Corinthians 1:4).

She also knows where He is: “My beloved has gone to his garden, To the beds of spices, To feed his flock in the gardens, And to gather lilies” (6:2). He hasn’t gone far. He is in His garden, the garden she gave Him in 4:16, which He called “my garden” in 5:1. The One she thought was gone is at work in her own life.

Then comes the second mark of intimacy: “I am my beloved’s, And my beloved is mine. He feeds his flock among the lilies” (6:3). The order has reversed. At 2:16 she began with what was hers. Now she begins with whose she is. Testing, absence, wounding, and witness have moved the center of her love from having Him to belonging to Him.

Images along the way

  • “I sleep, but my heart is awake”: devotion that is real but drowsy.

  • His knock and “Open for me”: He comes to her own door (compare Revelation 3:20).

  • “My sister, my love, My dove, my perfect one”: He calls with every tender name.

  • His head covered with dew, His locks with drops of the night: He has been out in the night. Some connect this to Gethsemane (Luke 22:44)

  • The robe taken off and the feet washed: comfort and settled rest; she is unwilling to be disturbed.

  • His hand at the latch, her heart yearning: He stirs her even as He waits.

  • Myrrh on the handles of the lock: His suffering love left on the door; the test carries the mark of the cross. Everything He touches is marked with the cross.

  • He turned away and was gone; no answer: She no longer senses His nearness and learns to trust Him through the silence.

  • The watchmen strike and wound her: religious misunderstanding and opposition from those who should have protected her.

  • Her veil taken away: exposed and stripped of her covering.

  • Lovesick (5:8): love from His absence, compared with 2:5, love from His fullness.

  • “What is your beloved More than another beloved?”: the question that calls out her witness.

  • White and ruddy, chief among ten thousand: He is beyond all others.

  • His head of gold: His supreme worth and authority.

  • His eyes like doves by the rivers: purity and gentleness.

  • His lips dripping myrrh: His words carry His suffering love.

  • His hands of gold, His body of ivory and sapphire, His legs of marble on gold: His strength, worth, and steadiness.

  • His countenance like Lebanon and the cedars: majesty.

  • “Altogether lovely… my beloved… my friend”: love and friendship together.

  • “That we may seek him with you”: others awakened through her testimony.

  • He has gone to His garden: He was at work in her life all along.

Her Story, Our Story

This movement surprises many believers. We assume that after a real surrender things should get easier. Instead, her surrender is tested almost at once, and the test is not shame this time but comfort. She is clean, settled, and at rest. When He knocks in the night, getting up feels like too much.

That is the quiet danger of a maturing Christian life. The obvious sins have been dealt with. Life is orderly, and our devotions have a comfortable shape. Then He knocks at an inconvenient hour: a person who needs us, a prompting to pray when we would rather sleep, a call to give or go that would disturb a well-arranged life. “I have taken off my robe; How can I put it on again?” Most of us know that sentence. Shame kept us from following when we felt dirty. Comfort keeps us from rising when we feel clean.

Then comes the silence. She opens, and He is gone. She calls, and He does not answer. Many faithful believers walk through times like this, when prayer feels like speaking into an empty room. The Song is careful here, and we should be too. His withdrawal is not rejection. She has not lost His love or her place with Him. He left myrrh on the lock. In the silence she learns to seek Him for Himself and not only for the sense of His nearness.

Some of the deepest wounds believers carry come from the watchmen: pastors, leaders, or church members who should have protected them and instead misunderstood, shamed, or harmed them. The Song does not excuse what the watchmen did, and neither should we. Their wrong remains wrong, and those who have been hurt this way should hear that clearly. But notice what the wounded bride does. She keeps seeking Him, and out of her wounds comes the fullest praise of Him in the whole book. Pain did not turn her against Him. It gave her eyes to see Him. And her testimony drew others: “That we may seek him with you?”

That is how many people come to Christ. They are not won by a believer who explained Him well in an easy season. They are won by watching someone suffer and still say, “He is altogether lovely.” And then, like her, we discover that He was never far. He was in His garden, at work in our own lives, the whole time.

Where Are You in the Story?

  • Where is comfort keeping you from answering His knock?

  • Have you walked through a time when He seemed silent? What did you learn about Him there?

  • If you have been wounded by those who should have cared for you, what would it look like to keep seeking Him without excusing what they did?

  • If someone asked what makes your Beloved better than any other, what would you say (5:9–16)?

VI. Restored Without Reproach: Beautiful in His Eyes, Partner in His Work (6:4–8:4)

She has just declared, “I am my beloved’s” (6:3). Now He speaks, and the first surprise is what He doesn’t say. There is no reproach for her delay at the door and no mention of 5:3. Instead: “O my love, you are as beautiful as Tirzah, Lovely as Jerusalem, Awesome as an army with banners! Turn your eyes away from me, For they have overcome me” (6:4–5).

Tirzah was a royal city whose name is often explained as “delight” or “pleasing.” Jerusalem was the city of God. And now there is a new note: “Awesome as an army with banners.” The woman who was struck and wounded in the streets now carries the strength of an army marching under its banners. The banner of love (2:4) has become her standard. She once shrank from His gaze. Now the look in her eyes moves Him deeply.

Then He repeats, almost word for word, what He said to her in chapter 4, before her failure: her hair like a flock of goats going down from Gilead, her teeth like sheep, “Every one bears twins, And none is barren among them,” her temples like a piece of pomegranate behind her veil (6:5–7; compare 4:1–3). His view of her hasn’t changed. Her delay didn’t lower her worth in His eyes. “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever” (Hebrews 13:8). “He does not retain His anger forever, Because He delights in mercy” (Micah 7:18).

Peter knew this restoration. The man who once said “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man” (Luke 5:8), and later denied Him, was met after the resurrection with breakfast and a question: “Do you love Me?” Jesus didn’t rehearse the failure. He restored Peter and gave him work: “Feed My sheep” (John 21:15–17). The same pattern runs through this movement: restoration without reproach, then partnership.

“There are sixty queens And eighty concubines, And virgins without number. My dove, my perfect one, Is the only one, The only one of her mother, The favorite of the one who bore her” (6:8–9). The Esther picture comes back. Among all the women in the palace, she is the chosen one, the only one. The one who brought her to new birth delights in her. And the women who once stood above her now honor her: “The daughters saw her And called her blessed, The queens and the concubines, And they praised her” (6:9).

Then comes another “who is” question: “Who is she who looks forth as the morning, Fair as the moon, Clear as the sun, Awesome as an army with banners?” (6:10). At 2:17 she waited for the day to break before she could follow. At 4:6 He went to the mountain of myrrh “Until the day breaks.” Now she herself “looks forth as the morning.” The day has broken, and it is breaking in her. She is fair as the moon, which shines with light it receives, and clear as the sun. “The righteous will shine forth as the sun” (Matthew 13:43). “The path of the just is like the shining sun, That shines ever brighter unto the perfect day” (Proverbs 4:18).

“I went down to the garden of nuts To see the verdure of the valley, To see whether the vine had budded And the pomegranates had bloomed” (6:11). Most likely the Beloved is the one going down to check His garden. He looks for growth and fruit.

What has changed is that she now shares His concern. She wants to see the same growth He looks for. In 7:12 she uses almost the same words: “Let us see if the vine has budded… And the pomegranates are in bloom.” What He went to look for, she now wants to look for with Him. Earlier she couldn’t keep her own vineyard (1:6). For much of the journey her attention was on her own condition: her darkness, her shame, her fear, her comfort. Now she cares about His fruit and His work, and she joins Him in it. “I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit” (John 15:16).

“Before I was even aware, My soul had made me As the chariots of my noble people” (6:12). The verse is hard to translate. One way to read the image is that, as she came to care about the fruit He sought, she found herself taking part in the King’s work before she realized what had happened.

“Return, return, O Shulamite; Return, return, that we may look upon you! What would you see in the Shulamite— As it were, the dance of the two camps?” (6:13). This is the first time she is called the Shulamite, the feminine counterpart of Solomon’s name, sharing His identity and life. And the woman who once said “Do not look upon me” (1:6) is now asked to come back so she can be seen.

The Hebrew for “the two camps” is Mahanaim, the name Jacob gave the place where God’s angels met him on his road: “This is God’s camp” (Genesis 32:2). Two camps met there: Jacob’s and heaven’s. What they see in her is the dance of those two camps: earth moving in step with heaven. She is moving to heaven’s rhythm. She is dancing with Jesus, in step with the Spirit. “If we live in the Spirit, let us also walk in the Spirit” (Galatians 5:25); the Greek word Paul uses for “walk” there means to keep in line or keep in step. Her life now answers the prayer “Your will be done On earth as it is in heaven” (Matthew 6:10). The woman who once hid in the clefts, sent Him away, and stayed in bed is now in step with Him. She moves when He moves.

Then comes His fullest praise of her (7:1–9). This time it begins at her feet: “How beautiful are your feet in sandals, O prince’s daughter!” (7:1). His earlier praise began with her eyes and face (4:1). Now it begins with her feet. She is no longer hiding in the clefts; she is walking. “How beautiful upon the mountains Are the feet of him who brings good news” (Isaiah 52:7). She is a “prince’s daughter,” royal by His choice. The praise rises from her feet to her head. Her waist is “a heap of wheat Set about with lilies” (7:2): harvest and fruitfulness. Her neck is “like an ivory tower,” and her eyes are like the pools in Heshbon (7:4). Her head crowns her “like Mount Carmel,” and “A king is held captive by your tresses” (7:5).

“How fair and how pleasant you are, O love, with your delights!” (7:6). Her stature is like a palm tree, and He says, “I will go up to the palm tree, I will take hold of its branches” (7:7–8). He comes to take her fruit. Her love is like “the best wine” that “goes down smoothly for my beloved” (7:9). Her love satisfies Him.

Then comes the third mark of intimacy: “I am my beloved’s, And his desire is toward me” (7:10). “Mine” is gone. At 2:16 she began with what was hers. At 6:3 she began with whose she was. Now she doesn’t mention what she has at all. She rests in His desire for her. The Hebrew word for “desire” appears elsewhere in the Old Testament only in Genesis 3:16 and 4:7. After the fall the woman heard, “Your desire shall be for your husband, And he shall rule over you.” Here it is His desire that is toward her. She doesn’t grasp at Him. She rests in being wanted.

Out of that rest comes what she couldn’t do before: she invites Him. “Come, my beloved, Let us go forth to the field; Let us lodge in the villages. Let us get up early to the vineyards; Let us see if the vine has budded, Whether the grape blossoms are open, And the pomegranates are in bloom. There I will give you my love” (7:11–12). At 2:17 she sent Him away because she didn’t feel clean enough. At 3:4 she held Him in the inner place of the Spirit’s work. At 5:3 comfort kept her in bed. Now she proposes the journey, goes out into His fields, and rises early.

These are the fields and villages of His harvest. “Lift up your eyes and look at the fields, for they are already white for harvest!” (John 4:35). And the vineyards she once worked until she burned out for her brothers (1:6) she now visits with Him. It is the same kind of work with a different center: not driven by angry demand, but shared with the One she loves. “There I will give you my love.” Her work has become an expression of intimacy, not a replacement for it.

“The mandrakes give off a fragrance, And at our gates are pleasant fruits, All manner, new and old, Which I have laid up for you, my beloved” (7:13). She has been storing up fruit for Him: old fruit from earlier seasons and new fruit from this one. “Every scribe instructed concerning the kingdom of heaven is like a householder who brings out of his treasure things new and old” (Matthew 13:52). Everything she has lived through, she offers to Him.

Then she longs for open love: “Oh, that you were like my brother… If I should find you outside, I would kiss you; I would not be despised” (8:1). She wants to love Him openly, in public, without shame and without the scorn her brothers once showed her.

“I would lead you and bring you Into the house of my mother, She who used to instruct me. I would cause you to drink of spiced wine, Of the juice of my pomegranate” (8:2). Once again she brings Him to her mother’s house, the place of the Spirit’s work. Now it is “she who used to instruct me,” the Spirit who teaches (John 14:26). And this time she is the one who gives. She serves Him spiced wine and pomegranate juice. The one who was once fed at His table now sets a table for Him.

“His left hand is under my head, And his right hand embraces me” (8:3). It is the same embrace as 2:6, but she is not the same woman. The movement closes with the third charge: “Do not stir up nor awaken love Until it pleases” (8:4). It is the third rest.

  • “Beautiful as Tirzah, Lovely as Jerusalem”: a royal city and the city of God; beauty that pleases Him (the name Tirzah is often explained as “delight”).

  • “Awesome as an army with banners”: strength and authority; the banner of love has become her standard.

  • “Your eyes… have overcome me”: her gaze moves His heart.

  • The praise repeated from chapter 4: His view of her didn’t change after her failure.

  • Sixty queens, eighty concubines, virgins without number; “the only one”: chosen above all (Esther 2:17).

  • “The only one of her mother… the one who bore her”: the delight of the One who brought her to new birth.

  • The daughters, queens, and concubines praise her: those once above her honor her.

  • “Looks forth as the morning”: The light she once waited for can now be seen in her life.

  • “Fair as the moon, Clear as the sun”: shining with reflected light, then with clear light (Matthew 13:43).

  • The garden of nuts: the Beloved checks His garden for growth. She now shares His concern and joins Him in it (7:12).

  • “The chariots of my noble people”: The verse is difficult to translate. In this reading, as she comes to care about what matters to Him, she finds herself sharing in the King’s work.

  • “Return, return, O Shulamite… that we may look upon you”: her identity with Him; once ashamed to be seen, now called to be seen.

  • “The dance of the two camps” (Mahanaim): earth and heaven meeting (Genesis 32:2). She moves to heaven’s rhythm, dancing with Jesus in step with the Spirit (Galatians 5:25).

  • “How beautiful are your feet in sandals”: she now goes out to serve (Isaiah 52:7).

  • “O prince’s daughter”: royal by His choice.

  • The palm tree and its clusters; “I will take hold of its branches”: He comes to take her fruit.

  • Mandrakes and pleasant fruits, new and old, laid up for Him: everything she has lived through, stored up and offered.

  • “Oh, that you were like my brother… I would not be despised”: a longing for open, unashamed love.

  • Her mother’s house, “she who used to instruct me”: the Spirit who teaches.

  • Spiced wine and pomegranate juice: now she gives to Him.

  • The embrace (8:3): the same embrace as 2:6, but a changed bride.

Her Story, Our Story

After her failure at the door, the first thing He does is praise her, in nearly the same words He used before she failed. Many believers find this harder to receive than correction. We expect a lecture. We assume that after letting Him down we will be on probation for a while, allowed back but not quite trusted. The Song says otherwise. His view of her did not drop. Like Peter at the breakfast fire, she is restored without reproach and then given work. If you carry an old failure, Christ is not waiting for you to earn your way back. The test now is whether you will believe what He says about you.

What follows is a mark of maturity: her attention turns outward. For most of the Song she has been absorbed in her own condition: her darkness, her shame, her fear, her comfort. Now she cares about what He cares about, whether the vine has budded and the pomegranates are in bloom. In a believer’s life this shows up as real concern for the growth of others: a new believer who needs someone to walk with them, a neighbor who does not know Him, a ministry that needs workers. Like her, we may find ourselves in the King’s work before we realize what has happened.

The dance of the two camps describes what that life looks like. She moves when He moves. This is not frantic activity. It is the rhythm of the Spirit, a life in step with heaven that answers the prayer “Your will be done On earth as it is in heaven” (Matthew 6:10).

Notice where she goes: the vineyards. The same kind of place that burned her out in chapter 1 is where she now goes gladly. Many believers who once burned out in ministry decide the answer is never to serve again. The Song gives a better answer. The work was not the problem; the center was. Driven by angry demand, the vineyard scorched her. Shared with the One she loves, it becomes the place where she says, “There I will give you my love.” When our work flows from rest in His desire for us, service becomes an expression of intimacy instead of a substitute for it.

Where Are You in the Story?

  • Is there a failure you still expect Him to bring up? What do His words in 6:4–9 say to you?

  • Whose growth do you care about right now? Where is He inviting you into His fields?

  • If you have burned out in ministry before, what would it look like to return to the vineyards with Him instead of for others?

  • Can you say, “His desire is toward me”? What would change if you believed it?

VII. Leaning, Sealed, and Longing (8:5–14)

The third rest is over, and the Song asks its last “who is” question: “Who is this coming up from the wilderness, Leaning upon her beloved?” (8:5). At 3:6 the question introduced the King’s procession and His glory revealed to her. At 6:10 it showed the bride shining like the morning. Now it shows the bride coming up out of the wilderness, leaning on Him.

The question shows surprise. Onlookers can hardly believe what they see. This is the woman who said “I am dark” and “Do not look upon me” (1:5–6). She was the one burned out in her brothers’ vineyards, hiding in the clefts, sending Him away, staying in bed when He knocked, and wounded in the streets. Now she comes up out of the wilderness close to Jesus.

She is hard to recognize. She is a changed person. “If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; old things have passed away; behold, all things have become new” (2 Corinthians 5:17). The wilderness is where God humbles and tests His people “to know what was in your heart” (Deuteronomy 8:2). It holds every hard place of her journey: the heat of her brothers’ vineyards, the clefts where she hid, the night streets, the watchmen’s blows, the silence after the knock. The wilderness that tested her didn’t destroy her. It revealed what His love has made of her.

Leaning means reliance, closeness, and union. She rests her whole weight on Him: “My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:9). She is near enough to lean. And the two move as one. “The two shall become one flesh. This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:31–32). “He who is joined to the Lord is one spirit with Him” (1 Corinthians 6:17). This is the whole portrait of the bride: no longer walking at a distance, behind or ahead of Him, but one with Him. The woman who once sent Him away until the day broke now won’t take a step without Him.

“I awakened you under the apple tree. There your mother brought you forth; There she who bore you brought you forth” (8:5). The speaker could be the bride or her Beloved. Some hear Christ speaking to the bride. He reminds her of the exact place and condition in which her spiritual life began, which guards her against spiritual pride as she reaches full maturity. “Look to the rock from which you were hewn” (Isaiah 51:1). “What do you have that you did not receive?” (1 Corinthians 4:7). The Hebrew wording addresses a man, which leads other readers to hear the bride speaking to her Beloved. Either way, the story comes back to the tree. At 2:3 she rested in His shade and tasted His fruit: rest and nourishment at the cross. Now the apple tree is named as the place where love was awakened and new life was brought forth. The journey began at the tree and comes back to it. Everything she has become was born there.

If the bride is speaking, her words open three further truths.

The reward of the cross. When the bride comes to life under the apple tree, the cross, she is what Isaiah foresaw: “He shall see the labor of His soul, and be satisfied” (Isaiah 53:11). Her spiritual awakening stirs His love, because she is the prize He endured the cross to win. Jesus, “for the joy that was set before Him endured the cross” (Hebrews 12:2). His love came first; “We love Him because He first loved us” (1 John 4:19). But when the believer steps into the life He purchased, His affections are stirred in response. “There will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents” (Luke 15:7).

His inheritance in the saints. By pointing back to her origin, the bride confidently declares who she is. She is not merely a rescued beggar. She is part of “the riches of the glory of His inheritance in the saints” (Ephesians 1:18). Her new birth brought Him part of His inheritance. In effect she says to the King, “My awakening brought You Your reward.”

The delight they share. The bride recognizes that her response to grace moves Him. When she was awakened to His love under the apple tree, His own heart was “awakened” in joyful, triumphant satisfaction over her. “He will rejoice over you with gladness… He will rejoice over you with singing” (Zephaniah 3:17). She now lives confident that her very existence as a redeemed soul brings Him continuous delight.

Then comes the climax of the Song: “Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm” (8:6). A seal was a personal signet, worn on a cord over the heart or as a ring on the hand (Genesis 38:18). It marked ownership and authenticity. God told Zerubbabel, “I… will make you like a signet ring; for I have chosen you” (Haggai 2:23). In Hebrew the heart is the inner person: affections, desires, and will. The arm is strength and visible action. So her request means: make me yours in your deepest feelings and in your visible life; bind me to you permanently and publicly as your beloved. Believers are “sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise” (Ephesians 1:13). What He gave at the beginning, she now asks for with her whole heart.

“For love is as strong as death, Jealousy as cruel as the grave; Its flames are flames of fire, A most vehement flame. Many waters cannot quench love, Nor can the floods drown it” (8:6–7).

His love went to death for her: the mountain of myrrh (4:6). Nothing “shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:38–39). And that love has kindled an answering love in her. Only love gives her the strength to stop living for herself and live for Him. Shame couldn’t push her into following Him. Duty didn’t get her out of bed. Her brothers’ demands only burned her out. What carries her now is love: His love for her, and the love it has lit in her. “We love Him because He first loved us” (1 John 4:19). “The love of Christ compels us… that those who live should live no longer for themselves, but for Him who died for them and rose again” (2 Corinthians 5:14–15). “I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” (Galatians 2:20). “If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow Me” (Luke 9:23). At 2:17 she wouldn’t follow, and at 5:3 she wouldn’t rise. Now love makes the cross bearable.

“Jealousy as cruel as the grave” describes love that asks for her whole heart. The LORD’s “name is Jealous” (Exodus 34:14). He will have all of her, and she now wants all of Him.

Where the NKJV prints “A most vehement flame,” its footnote gives the literal reading, “a flame of Yah,” a short form of the LORD’s name. The flame of Yah is intense: love from God Himself, burning at full strength. “The love of God has been poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit” (Romans 5:5). It is purifying: “He is like a refiner’s fire… He will purify… And purge them as gold and silver” (Malachi 3:2–3). His love purifies her, burning away what keeps her heart from Him. And it is unstoppable: “Many waters cannot quench love, Nor can the floods drown it” (8:7). Every trial she passed through was water thrown on the flame: shame, fear, comfort, absence, wounding. None of those trials could put out the flame. “When you pass through the waters, I will be with you” (Isaiah 43:2). “Our God is a consuming fire” (Hebrews 12:29).

“If a man would give for love All the wealth of his house, It would be utterly despised” (8:7). Love can’t be bought. She couldn’t buy His love at the beginning, because it came as grace, as favor extended. He can’t be bought off from loving her either. His love comes “without money and without price” (Isaiah 55:1).

Then love turns outward: “We have a little sister, And she has no breasts. What shall we do for our sister In the day when she is spoken for?” (8:8). Here is someone younger and not yet grown. Who speaks isn’t certain, but the concern is clear: the mature now look after the growth of the immature. Once others burdened her. Now love asks, “What shall we do for her?”

“If she is a wall, We will build upon her A battlement of silver; And if she is a door, We will enclose her With boards of cedar” (8:9). If she is a wall, steady and settled, they will build on her and adorn her. If she is a door, open and vulnerable, they will protect her. Mature love doesn’t drive younger believers the way her brothers drove her. It builds them up and guards them. Paul cared for his converts “just as a nursing mother cherishes her own children” (1 Thessalonians 2:7). The little sister’s exact spiritual identity is left open, but her place in the story is clear.

The bride answers from her own experience: “I am a wall, And my breasts like towers; Then I became in his eyes As one who found peace” (8:10). She is no longer a door anyone can push through. She is a wall: steady, mature, able to stand. In His eyes she has found peace. The gaze she feared at the beginning is now where she finds rest. The Hebrew for “peace” (shalom) sounds like her name, Shulamite, and His, Solomon. Whether the names actually come from shalom is debated, but the wordplay fits. She has become the one who found peace in the Prince of Peace.

Then comes the vineyard: “Solomon had a vineyard at Baal Hamon; He leased the vineyard to keepers; Everyone was to bring for its fruit A thousand silver coins” (8:11). The King’s vineyard is entrusted to keepers who are answerable for its fruit, the way Jesus told of a landowner who “planted a vineyard… And he leased it to vinedressers” (Matthew 21:33).

“My own vineyard is before me. You, O Solomon, may have a thousand, And those who tend its fruit two hundred” (8:12). This is where her vineyard story ends. At the beginning she said, “My own vineyard I have not kept” (1:6). Now: “My own vineyard is before me.” She keeps her own heart and life before Him, and she gives Him the full return of its fruit. “It is required in stewards that one be found faithful” (1 Corinthians 4:2). She also honors those who help tend it: “those who tend its fruit two hundred.” She doesn’t despise the shepherds and workers who serve: “The laborer is worthy of his wages” (1 Timothy 5:18). She now cares for what He has entrusted to her. Her work for Him grows out of her life with Him, and she keeps tending that life as she serves.

He still wants her voice: “You who dwell in the gardens, The companions listen for your voice— Let me hear it!” (8:13). She now dwells in the gardens. She isn’t hiding in the clefts or working in someone else’s vineyard. Others listen for her voice. And the Beloved asks for what He asked at the beginning of the call: “Let me hear your voice” (2:14). Even now that others listen to her, He still wants her to speak to Him in private prayer and worship.

The Song ends with her answer: “Make haste, my beloved, And be like a gazelle Or a young stag On the mountains of spices” (8:14). Her words recall 2:17, but her invitation is different now. Then she said, “Turn, my beloved, And be like a gazelle… Upon the mountains of Bether”: go away until my darkness is gone. Now she says, “Make haste”: come quickly. Then the mountains were Bether, perhaps “separation.” Now they are mountains of spices, the mountains He went to for her (4:6).

And the heights are no longer a place she fears. All through the Song the Beloved is the gazelle on the mountains (2:9, 17), free on the heights she wouldn’t climb. But along the way He described her with the gazelle’s young: “like two fawns, Twins of a gazelle” (4:5; 7:3). The one who couldn’t follow Him has been given His nature. “He will make my feet like deer’s feet, And He will make me walk on my high hills” (Habakkuk 3:19). She was made to live on the heavenly heights with Him: He “raised us up together, and made us sit together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus” (Ephesians 2:6). She was made to rule and reign with Him: “If we endure, We shall also reign with Him” (2 Timothy 2:12); “To him who overcomes I will grant to sit with Me on My throne” (Revelation 3:21). The mountains of spices are no longer a place of separation, but the shared home of the bride and her King.

The Song doesn’t end with closure. It ends with longing. A sealed, mature, leaning bride still wants more of Him. That is how the Bible ends too: “And the Spirit and the bride say, ‘Come!’… Even so, come, Lord Jesus!” (Revelation 22:17, 20). The journey into intimacy doesn’t finish on earth. It reaches toward the day He comes, when the bride will be with Him forever.

Her Story, Our Story

The final question, “Who is this coming up from the wilderness, Leaning upon her beloved?” is the question God intends people to ask about us. The goal of the Christian life is not a believer who no longer needs Christ but one who leans on Him with her whole weight. Maturity in the Song looks like more dependence, not less. The strongest believers you know are probably like this. They have walked through their own wilderness, whether grief, long illness, betrayal, or years of unseen faithfulness, and come out closer to Him instead of harder.

Every wilderness place, for her and for us, was water thrown on the flame. Shame, fear, comfort, silence, and wounding all pressed against her love, and none of them could put it out. That is a reason to keep going when your own wilderness feels long. It cannot quench what He has kindled.

The seal shows what we were made for: to be His in our inner life and in our visible life, heart and arm. Many believers keep the two apart. They give Him private devotion while their public life runs on its own terms, or they serve visibly while the heart is somewhere else. The bride asks to be His in both. Love is the only power that makes that possible. Duty never got her out of bed, and her brothers’ demands only burned her out. When believers finally lay down their lives for Christ, it is because “the love of Christ compels us” (2 Corinthians 5:14).

Mature love also turns toward the younger. “What shall we do for our sister?” The one who was once driven by others now builds up and protects those coming behind her. In a church, that looks like mature believers who make room for new ones, strengthening the steady and sheltering the vulnerable, instead of loading them with demands.

And she still keeps her own vineyard. The woman who once said “My own vineyard I have not kept” now says “My own vineyard is before me.” The most fruitful believers have not outgrown the secret place. Even when the companions listen for her voice, He still says, “Let me hear it!” Public fruitfulness never replaces private prayer.

The Song ends with longing, and so does a healthy Christian life. A believer who is sealed and leaning on Him still says, “Make haste, my beloved.” The more we know Him, the more we want Him to come.

Where Are You in the Story?

  • What wilderness are you coming up out of? Are you leaning on Him in it?

  • Is there a gap between your heart and your arm, between your private devotion and your public life?

  • Who is your “little sister,” someone younger in the faith whom you can build up or protect?

  • Has fruitfulness crowded out your secret place? When did He last hear your voice alone?

  • Do you long for His coming? What makes that longing grow or fade in you?

The seven movements in our lives

  • I. Called, Favored, and Awakened: We are drawn and welcomed before we have anything to offer. Desire for Him grows from tasting His love.

  • II. Dark but Lovely: We burn out serving others’ expectations and shrink from His gaze. He brings us back to His table, His cross, and His banner of love.

  • III. The Call to Follow: He calls before we feel ready. We tell Him “not yet” because we feel unclean, then miss Him and seek Him past every substitute.

  • IV. The King Revealed and the Garden Offered: He cleanses us at the cross, calls us into costly places, and loves us into surrender until our life becomes His garden.

  • V. Tested After Surrender: Comfort, silence, and wounds test our surrender. Out of pain comes praise that draws others to Him.

  • VI. Restored Without Reproach: He restores us after failure without a lecture and brings us into His work, now flowing from rest instead of demand.

  • VII. Leaning, Sealed, and Longing: We come up from our wilderness leaning on Him, sealed to Him in heart and life, caring for the younger, keeping our own vineyard, and longing for Him to come.

What holds us back, and what He does

  • Shame (2:17): “I can’t follow until I’m clean.” He goes to the mountain of myrrh and declares, “There is no spot in you” (4:6–7).

  • Fear (4:8): The lions and leopards are real. He says “with me” twice (4:8) and keeps speaking love until love outweighs fear (4:9–16).

  • Comfort (5:3): “How can I put it on again?” (5:3) He knocks, leaves myrrh on the lock, and lets her feel His absence until she rises and seeks Him (5:4–6).

  • Failure (after 5:3): We expect reproach. He repeats His praise and gives her work (6:4–9; 7:11–12).

The marks, the rests, and the “who is” questions
