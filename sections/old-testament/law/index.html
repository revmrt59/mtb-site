Old Testament — The Five Books of Moses

Canonical Context

(Law, Torah, Pentateuch)

The Five Books of Moses, often called the Law, the Torah, or the Pentateuch, stand at the beginning of Scripture because here God establishes relationship with His people based on covenant.

A covenant is a binding relationship initiated by God. It is His committed promise to His people, joined with His call for faithfulness in return. It is more than rules. It is a sacred bond.

In these books, God reveals who He is and defines how His redeemed people are to live with Him.

These books are not merely ancient regulations. They are the foundation of redemptive history. Here we meet the Creator, the covenant-making God, the Holy One who forms a people for Himself.

Before God gives the law at Sinai, there is Genesis. Genesis tells us how everything began. Creation, goodness, image-bearing, marriage, worship, work, rebellion, and the promise of Christ. It all starts here. We learn that the world was made good. We learn that sin is not the original design, but a rebellion of self-will that fractures what God made good. We learn that God moves toward fallen humanity with covenant promise of the seed which would crush the serpent’s head.

The Law reveals who God is before it tells us what to do. He is sovereign. He is holy. He is faithful. He redeems before He commands. The deliverance in Exodus comes before God gives the Law at Sinai. Deliverance precedes instruction. Grace precedes law.

What the Law Is Doing in Scripture

The Law establishes:

• The goodness of God’s created order
• The holiness of God
• The seriousness of sin
• The necessity of sacrifice
• The structure of covenant relationship
• The pattern of worship
• The promise of a coming Redeemer

It teaches us that God dwells with a people only through atonement. The blood, priesthood, altar, and tabernacle are not just ritual details; they are shadows pointing forward to Jesus.

The Law does not compete with the gospel. It prepares us for it.

How the Law Forms Us

The Law trains reverence. It confronts casual approaches to God. It dismantles the idea that God is manageable. It teaches boundaries, purity, obedience, and covenant faithfulness.

It also trains gratitude. The commands are given to a redeemed people, not to slaves trying to earn freedom.

The Law exposes the human heart. It reveals that external compliance is not enough. It presses inward. It uncovers the need for transformation, not merely instruction.

The Law produces a longing.

  • Longing for a better sacrifice.

  • Longing for a faithful High Priest.

  • Longing for a circumcised heart.

That longing finds fulfillment in Christ.

Common Misreadings to Avoid

Do not read these books as:

• Mere moralism
• A failed system replaced by grace
• Irrelevant ritual
• A checklist for righteousness

The Law is holy, just, and good. It reveals the character of God, the seriousness of covenant life, and His goodness toward mankind. It must be read through its fulfillment in Christ.

Christ in the Law

The Law points forward to Jesus.

  • The sacrifices anticipate the Lamb.

  • The priesthood anticipates the Mediator.

  • The tabernacle anticipates God dwelling among us.

  • The Mosaic covenant anticipates a new and better covenant written on the heart.

The story that begins in Genesis and unfolds through Moses finds its fulfillment in Christ.
