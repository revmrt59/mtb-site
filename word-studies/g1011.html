PLAN, PLOT, CONSIDER - (bouleúō, Strong's G1011)

Summary

The Greek verb bouleúō means to think a matter through and come to a decision: to consider, deliberate, plan, or resolve. A person may do it alone, as when a king sits down to weigh his chances in war, or with others, as when a council works out what to do. The word carries no moral color of its own. Whether a plan is wise or wicked depends on who is planning and what they intend.

The word appears only a handful of times in the New Testament, yet its uses cover a wide range of human planning. Jesus uses it to tell would-be disciples to think soberly about what following Him will cost (Luke 14:31). The religious leaders use it to plot the deaths of Lazarus and the apostles, and both times they are responding to resurrection life (John 12:10; Acts 5:33). Paul uses it three times in one verse to defend his honesty in ministry, and he grounds that honesty in God's own faithfulness in Christ (2 Corinthians 1:17). Taken together, these passages show that human planning can never stand on its own. It is either set against God's purposes or brought under them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bouleúō belongs to the same word family as boulē, "counsel" or "purpose." It describes the inner work of weighing circumstances and options until a course of action is settled. In the New Testament it appears in Luke, John, Acts, and 2 Corinthians. The settings are ordinary ones, such as war, travel, sailing, and courtroom anger, and that ordinariness is part of what makes the word useful. It describes the kind of planning every person does every day.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has two closely connected senses:

1. To deliberate or consider: to think a situation through before acting. The king in Jesus' parable must "sit down first and consider" whether he can win (Luke 14:31).

2. To resolve or plan: to settle on a course of action, whether honorable or hostile. The sailors "planned to run the ship" onto a beach (Acts 27:39), and the chief priests "plotted to put Lazarus to death" (John 12:10).

The word describes the act of planning. It does not promise that the plan will succeed.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The English renderings change with the moral setting, while the Greek word stays the same. When the plan is murder, the NKJV says "plotted." When it is a ship's survival or an apostle's travel, it says "planned" or "determined." When Jesus calls for reflection, it says "consider." The same human ability to think and decide can be used for discipleship, for practical wisdom, or for rebellion.

Acts 15:37 has a textual difference worth knowing about. The Greek text behind the NKJV reads bouleúō, "determined," while many modern translations follow texts that read the related verb boulomai, "wished" or "wanted." In that verse the difference in meaning is small.

4. Theological Significance

In every New Testament use of bouleúō, the one doing the planning is a human being. People deliberate, scheme, disagree, and adjust. Some of their plans are wise and some are wicked, and some are overturned by circumstances or by God. The related noun boulē is used in passages such as Acts 2:23, 4:28, and Hebrews 6:17 for the settled counsel of God. God does not sit down and weigh His odds the way the king in the parable does. His purpose stands.

This contrast clarifies what all human planning is. Every human plan is finite, and it runs either with God's purpose or against it. Gamaliel saw this when he warned the council that a plan "of men" would come to nothing, but if it was of God, "you cannot overthrow it—lest you even be found to fight against God" (Acts 5:38–39). The wisest human planning is planning that has been brought under the counsel of God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Luke 14:25–33: Thinking honestly about the cost of discipleship. Great crowds were following Jesus, and He turned and told them something that would thin the ranks: whoever does not hate father and mother and even his own life, and whoever does not bear his cross, "cannot be My disciple" (vv. 26–27). He then gives two pictures. The first is a builder who must "sit down first and count the cost" (v. 28). The second is a king who must "sit down first and consider whether he is able with ten thousand to meet him who comes against him with twenty thousand" (v. 31). Bouleúō is the word for the king's careful thinking.

Jesus wants this kind of deliberation, and He does not treat it as doubt. He would rather have a disciple who has looked honestly at what following Him means than a crowd carried along by excitement. The king's conclusion matters as well. He realizes he cannot win, so he "sends a delegation and asks conditions of peace" (v. 32). Jesus then draws the point: "whoever of you does not forsake all that he has cannot be My disciple" (v. 33). Honest reckoning leads a person to see that he cannot meet God on his own terms, and so he surrenders everything.

John 12:10 and Acts 5:33: Plotting against life. After Lazarus was raised, crowds came to see him, and "on account of him many of the Jews went away and believed in Jesus" (John 12:11). The chief priests responded by deciding that Lazarus must die too. Years later, when the apostles were preaching the risen Christ and their ministry was confirmed by healings, the council "were furious and plotted to kill them" (Acts 5:33). In both cases the leaders were looking at clear evidence of God's life-giving power, and their response was to plan a death. There is a dark absurdity in a plan to kill a man Jesus had already raised from the grave. Their planning exposes a heart that will not yield to God's truth and that plots against it when it is confronted. Psalm 2 had already described this: "the rulers take counsel together, against the LORD and against His Anointed" (Psalm 2:2). They "plot a vain thing" (v. 1).

2 Corinthians 1:15–20: Planning that reflects God's "Yes." Paul had changed his travel plans, and some in Corinth took this as proof that he could not be trusted. He answers with bouleúō three times in one sentence: "when I was planning this, did I do it lightly? Or the things I plan, do I plan according to the flesh, that with me there should be Yes, Yes, and No, No?" (v. 17). Planning "according to the flesh" means saying whatever suits the moment, promising to please people, backing out to avoid trouble, and adjusting one's word to protect oneself.

Paul's defense does not rest on his organizational skill. It rests on God. In the verses that follow (vv. 18–20), he says that God is faithful, that the Son of God preached among them was not "Yes and No," and that all God's promises find their "Yes" and "Amen" in Christ. Because the gospel Paul preaches is God's unwavering Yes, the way he makes and communicates his plans must not undercut it. His calendar is part of his witness. When a minister's word cannot be trusted, people begin to wonder whether his message can be trusted either.

Acts 15:36–40: Godly people, opposing plans. "Barnabas was determined to take with them John called Mark" (v. 37). Paul was just as determined not to take Mark, because Mark had left them earlier. Both men were Spirit-filled apostles, and both had thought the matter through. Their sharp disagreement led them to part ways. Luke records this frankly and does not tell us who was right. Careful, godly deliberation does not guarantee agreement, and even apostolic planning is not infallible. Yet the outcome was two missionary teams instead of one, and Paul later valued Mark's ministry (2 Timothy 4:11). God's purpose went forward through a disagreement He did not prevent.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The plot against Lazarus did not stand alone. The word "also" in John 12:10 shows that the leaders were already set on putting Jesus Himself to death (John 11:53). Here the plotting rulers of Psalm 2 are seen moving against the Lord's Anointed. Yet Acts 2:23 says Jesus was delivered up "by the determined purpose and foreknowledge of God." The most wicked plan in human history was carried out inside God's counsel and became the means of salvation. The early church prayed in exactly these terms, quoting Psalm 2 and confessing that Herod, Pilate, and the people had done what God's hand and purpose had determined beforehand (Acts 4:25–28).

The story moves from human plotting that cannot succeed to God's purpose that cannot fail, and it reaches its center in Christ. In Him every promise of God is "Yes" (2 Corinthians 1:20). Because of this, the believer's planning has a firm foundation. Our plans are shaky, but they rest on a God whose word does not shift.

7. Spirit & Life

Jesus does not ask anyone to follow Him without thinking. He invites people to sit down and reckon honestly. Those who do will reach the king's conclusion: they cannot stand against God on their own terms. That realization opens the way to surrender, and it is a gift. Faith that has counted the cost holds up better under pressure than faith that has never done so.

Second Corinthians 1 gives ordinary Christian planning real weight. Promises to a friend, commitments to a church, and the way we explain a change of plans all show something about the God we serve. A believer whose word is unreliable makes the gospel harder to believe. A believer who plans carefully, keeps his word, and explains himself honestly when plans must change reflects the faithfulness of Christ. Faithful planning still allows for change. Paul did change his plans. What he refused to do was change his word for the sake of convenience or self-protection.

Acts 15 brings relief to anyone who has prayed, thought hard, and still disagreed with a fellow believer. Disagreement among godly people is not necessarily sin, and God is not stopped by it. Acts 5 brings sober comfort as well. No council, however angry, can overturn what God is doing.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Acts 27:39. After two weeks in a storm, the sailors "observed a bay with a beach, onto which they planned to run the ship if possible." This is plain, practical planning under pressure, and the word "if possible" admits how uncertain it is. Paul had already told them that God would spare every life aboard (Acts 27:22–25), and their desperate plan became the means by which that promise was kept.

The other occurrences are developed in Section 5.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G1012 (boulē), lexical relative: the noun "counsel, purpose," used of God's determined purpose (Acts 2:23; Hebrews 6:17).

  • G1014 (boulomai), related verb meaning "to wish, want," read in place of bouleúō in many modern texts of Acts 15:37.

  • Canonical pairing: Psalm 2:1–2, where rulers "take counsel together" against the Lord's Anointed, gives the background for the plots of John 12:10 and Acts 5:33.
