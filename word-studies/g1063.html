FOR, BECAUSE, INDEED - (gár, Strong's G1063)

Summary

Gár is the Greek conjunction most often translated "for." It connects what comes next to what was just said, usually by supplying the reason behind a statement, the support for a claim, or a piece of background the reader needs. It appears 1,054 times in the New Testament, and the NKJV renders it "for" in the large majority of those places. It also appears in some questions and sharp replies, where English uses "Why" or "indeed." Gár does not open a new topic. It points backward and asks the reader to see how the two thoughts fit together. When you notice it, a string of verses stops looking like a list of separate statements and starts reading as a connected line of thought. That makes it one of the most practical words in the Greek New Testament for following what an author is actually saying.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Gár joins two thoughts so that the second explains, justifies, or fills out the first. It is "postpositive," which means it never stands first in its Greek clause. It normally sits in second position, even though English translations put "for" at the very front. In John 3:16 the Greek begins with "so" and gár comes second, while the NKJV opens with "For." Gár does not always state a strict cause. Sometimes it gives evidence for a claim, sometimes it adds an explanatory aside, and sometimes it gives a question or reply its edge. In every case it looks back to what precedes it, so context must identify exactly what is being explained.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Giving the reason for a statement or action (for, because)

The most common use tells why something is said or done. In Matthew 1:21 the angel instructs Joseph, "you shall call His name JESUS, for He will save His people from their sins." Gár ties the name to its purpose. The child is called Jesus because of the rescue He will accomplish.

Supporting a claim and building an argument (for)

In the letters, gár often links a chain of reasons, each one supporting the one before it. Romans 1:16–17 reads: "For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ, for it is the power of God to salvation for everyone who believes… For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith to faith." Paul's readiness to preach in Rome (v. 15) rests on his confidence in the gospel. That confidence rests on the gospel's power, and its power rests on what it reveals. The later "for everyone" and "for the Jew" in verse 16 translate different Greek words, not gár.

Supplying background in a narrative (for)

Gospel writers use gár to pause the story and give the reader information. At the well in Samaria, John explains in an aside that Jesus' disciples had gone into the city to buy food, which accounts for Jesus being alone with the woman (John 4:8, paraphrased). Mark notes that the stone at the tomb had been rolled away and then adds that it was very large (Mark 16:4, paraphrased). These asides do not argue a point. They help the reader see the scene clearly.

Sharpening a question or reply (Why, indeed)

In lively speech, gár can carry surprise, protest, or emphasis. Pilate answers the crowd: "Why, what evil has He done?" (Matthew 27:23). The healed man tells the Pharisees, "Why, this is a marvelous thing, that you do not know where He is from" (John 9:30). When Paul refuses a quiet release from the Philippian jail, his "No indeed!" (Acts 16:37) uses gár with a negative to give a firm refusal.

3. Practical Reading Insight

An old piece of Bible-study advice applies here: whenever you see "for," ask what it is there for. John 3:16 is often quoted as if it stood alone, but its opening "For" ties it to what Jesus has just said. The Son of Man must be lifted up so that whoever believes in Him may have eternal life (John 3:14–15, paraphrased). Verse 16 then explains why that lifting up happens: God so loved the world that He gave His Son. The best-known verse in the Bible is the reason behind the cross Jesus has just described.

Romans 1 rewards the same attention. Paul's chain of "for" clauses continues into verse 18, where he explains that God's wrath is revealed from heaven against ungodliness (paraphrased). Interpreters discuss exactly how verse 18 attaches to verse 17, but the connection itself is clear. Paul does not move from good news to bad news by accident. The gospel is God's power to save precisely because humanity stands under His just judgment. Following gár traces Paul's reasoning from his travel plans down to its deepest foundation.

4. Reading Guidance

A "for" at the start of a verse is not a fresh beginning. Verse numbers came later and can hide the connection, so read backward to find what is being explained. Remember also that English "for" translates several Greek words. Hóti (G3754) is often rendered "for" or "because," and prepositions appear as "for" too, as in Romans 1:16. Gár shows that a connection exists, and context decides what kind.
