FOR, BECAUSE, INDEED - (gár, Strong's G1063)

Summary

Gár is the Greek word behind most of the "for" statements in the New Testament. It gives the reason behind what was just said. An angel names a child, and gár tells us why. Paul makes a claim, and gár brings the support. A Gospel writer pauses the story, and gár supplies the background a reader needs. Less often it adds force to a reply or a question, as in "No indeed!" or "Why, what evil has He done?" Noticing gár turns the reader into a careful listener. Each "For" invites the question, "What is this explaining?" Asking it keeps verses tied to the reasoning that gives them their meaning.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Gár joins a new statement to something already said and shows that the new statement explains, grounds, or confirms the earlier one. In Greek it is postpositive, which means it normally stands second in its clause. English cannot follow that order, so translators move "for" to the front. The word is very common in the New Testament, and the NKJV renders it "for" or "For" in the great majority of its uses. It also appears as "because," "since," "indeed," and "Why."

The word points backward. It rarely introduces a brand-new subject. Instead, it opens a window into the speaker's reasoning. Sometimes that reasoning is a strict cause. At other times it is a looser explanation or supporting comment.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Giving the reason for a statement or command (for, because, since)

This is the word's most common use. In Matthew 1:21 the angel tells Joseph, "you shall call His name JESUS, for He will save His people from their sins." The name is not arbitrary. Gár ties the command to its reason, and that reason is the heart of the gospel.

John 3:16 works the same way: "For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son." The verse explains what came just before it. Verses 14–15 say the Son of Man must be lifted up so that believers may have eternal life. Gár tells us why such a gift exists: God loved the world.

Supplying background in a story (for)

Gospel writers often use gár to step aside from the action and give the reader information. Mark explains that the women found the stone rolled away and adds that it was very large (Mark 16:4, paraphrased). John explains that Jesus was alone at the well because His disciples had gone into town to buy food (John 4:8, paraphrased). In these places gár does not argue a point. It fills in what the reader needs to follow the scene.

Adding force to replies and questions (indeed, Why)

In lively speech, gár can sharpen a response. Paul refuses a quiet release from the Philippian jail: "And now do they put us out secretly? No indeed!" (Acts 16:37). Pilate, pressed by the crowd, asks, "Why, what evil has He done?" (Matthew 27:23). The healed blind man answers the Pharisees: "Why, this is a marvelous thing, that you do not know where He is from" (John 9:30). Here gár ties a speaker's reaction to what has just been said. It carries protest, surprise, or exasperation.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Paul often stacks gár clauses one on another, so that each "For" supports the statement before it. Romans 8 shows this clearly.

  • Paul has just said that believers suffer with Christ so that they may also be glorified with Him.

  • Then: "For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us" (8:18).

  • Why should we believe that glory is coming? "For the earnest expectation of the creation eagerly waits for the revealing of the sons of God" (8:19).

  • Why is creation waiting? "For the creation was subjected to futility, not willingly" (8:20).

  • And the whole argument is confirmed by what we can see: "For we know that the whole creation groans and labors with birth pangs together until now" (8:22).

Read as a chain, these verses form one steady argument for hope. Present suffering is real, but it sits inside a larger story that God is bringing to glory. Lift any one verse out on its own and the reader loses the reason it was written. When gár appears, trace the line back. You will usually find the claim the verse was meant to prove.

4. Reading Guidance

Not every English "for" represents gár. In Romans 8:19, creation "waits for" the revealing, and that "for" is part of the English rendering "waits for" (one Greek verb); it does not translate gár. In Romans 8:20–21, the words "because of" and "because" translate other Greek expressions. Gár also does not always look back only to the sentence right before it. Sometimes it supports a larger idea built up over several verses, so read the whole paragraph.
