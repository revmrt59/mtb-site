JUDGMENT, MIND, PURPOSE, CONSENT - (gnṓmē, Strong's G1106)

Summary

Gnṓmē is a Greek word for a mind that has reached a decision. It names a settled conclusion: a considered opinion, a piece of advice, a firm intention, or an agreement shared by more than one person. The NKJV translates it with several words ("decided," "judgment," "advice," "consent," "mind," "purpose") because the word follows whoever has reached the conclusion. That may be a traveling apostle choosing a safer road, a pastor giving counsel, a church member giving his free agreement, a coalition of kings, or God Himself.

The word appears only nine times, and its uses gather around a few large truths. Paul gives his gnṓmē on questions where the Lord had given no direct command, and he gives it as a man the Lord had made trustworthy. He pleads with a divided church to be joined together in the same judgment. He refuses to keep Onesimus without Philemon's consent, because he wants Philemon's goodness to be willing. In Revelation 17 the same word describes both the united will of kings who hand their power to the beast and the purpose of God that stands behind and above them. The word shows what God does with human resolve. He forms it in His people, He honors it in their relationships, and He rules over it even when it is set against Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gnṓmē belongs to the family of Greek words built on the verb for knowing (ginōskō). The word's meaning comes from how it is used, not from that family resemblance. Where knowing can be simple awareness, gnṓmē describes knowledge that has been weighed and turned into a position: "this is what I think," "this is what I intend," or "this is what we have agreed."

The New Testament uses it in three settings: once in narrative (Acts), five times in Paul's letters to churches and to a friend, and three times in a single chapter of Revelation. The range runs from everyday decisions about travel to the purposes behind world history.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, gnṓmē is a settled mind on a matter. Context determines whether that mind is an intention, an opinion offered to others, or an agreement held in common.

1. A decision or purpose: a resolved intention to act, as when Paul "decided to return through Macedonia" (Acts 20:3), or when God's purpose is carried out (Revelation 17:17).

2. An opinion, judgment, or advice: a considered view offered to guide others, as in Paul's "judgment" about virgins (1 Corinthians 7:25) or his "advice" about giving (2 Corinthians 8:10).

3. Consent or common mind: agreement given or shared, as in Philemon's "consent" (Philemon 1:14) or kings who are "of one mind" (Revelation 17:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the word changes its weight. When Paul uses it of himself in 1 Corinthians 7 and 2 Corinthians 8, gnṓmē is counsel offered to others, and he deliberately sets it beside the commands of the Lord that already existed on those topics. In Philemon, the gnṓmē belongs to someone else, and Paul waits for it. In 1 Corinthians 1:10 and Revelation 17:13, the word describes a mind held in common by a group, once as Paul's hope for the church and once as the agreement of God's enemies.

One pairing is worth noticing. In 1 Corinthians 1:10, "the same mind" and "the same judgment" stand side by side. The first points to a shared outlook, and the second, gnṓmē, points to shared conclusions. Paul wants the Corinthians to think alike and also to come out in the same place.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God cares about the inner resolve of a person, not merely the outward act. He gives wisdom to His servants so that their judgments carry spiritual weight. He values free agreement among His people and does not want their goodness pried out of them. Their unity is meant to reach their convictions, not just their manners.

These passages also show that human resolve is never independent of God. The most determined human agreement in the New Testament, a league of kings united against the Lamb, turns out to serve God's purpose. The same God who forms the judgment of the faithful governs the judgment of the rebellious. Wisdom, freedom, and sovereignty meet in this one small word.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Corinthians 7:25, 40 — Judgment given by a trustworthy servant. Paul carefully distinguishes an existing command of the Lord on a topic from his own apostolic counsel where no command of Jesus is recorded. On divorce he writes, "yet not I but the Lord" (7:10). A little later he says, "I, not the Lord, say" (7:12). On the question of virgins he admits, "I have no commandment from the Lord; yet I give judgment as one whom the Lord in His mercy has made trustworthy" (7:25). At the chapter's end he gives his judgment that a widow is happier if she remains unmarried, adding that he thinks he also has the Spirit of God (7:40, paraphrased).

Paul does not claim a recorded command of Jesus on this specific question, and he does not treat his counsel as a casual opinion either. Mercy made him trustworthy, and the Spirit was at work in him. Many believers want an explicit command for every decision. Here God shows another way He guides His people: through the Spirit-formed judgment of mature, faithful servants, offered honestly as judgment and received with respect.

1 Corinthians 1:10 — The same judgment. Chloe's household had reported "contentions" (1:11). Believers were saying, "I am of Paul," "I am of Apollos," "I am of Cephas," and even "I am of Christ" as a party label (1:12). Paul pleads "by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" that they be "perfectly joined together in the same mind and in the same judgment" (1:10). His next questions show where that shared judgment must come from: "Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you?" (1:13).

Paul is asking for more than a truce between camps that keep their separate loyalties. He wants believers to reach the same settled convictions because they are looking at the same crucified Lord. Personality-driven division collapses once the church remembers who was crucified for it.

Philemon 1:14 — Consent instead of compulsion. Paul wanted to keep Onesimus, now a believer and a help to him in prison. He had the standing to command Philemon, and earlier in the letter he says so (vv. 8–9, paraphrased). Instead he writes, "without your consent I wanted to do nothing, that your good deed might not be by compulsion, as it were, but voluntary." An apostle holds back his authority so that a brother's kindness can be his own. Paul wanted Philemon's goodness to grow out of his heart. He could have extracted it by pressure, but that would not have been the same gift.

Revelation 17:13, 17 — Two purposes in one chapter. Ten kings "are of one mind, and they will give their power and authority to the beast" (17:13). This is evil at its most organized, with the rulers of the earth united in purpose. Then verse 17 pulls back the curtain. John explains that God put it into their hearts to carry out His purpose, to be of one mind, and to give their kingdom to the beast until God's words are fulfilled (paraphrased). The Greek uses gnṓmē for the kings' shared mind and for God's purpose in the same sentence.

The kings' agreement is real, and they are responsible for it. Yet their resolve becomes the very means by which God's purpose is done, and the judgment on Babylon that follows is His. The united will of God's enemies sits inside His will. Their plan carries out His decree. Proverbs 21:1 and Genesis 50:20 express the same truth about kings' hearts and evil intentions.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word's uses trace a line from the church to the end of history. In Corinth, the cure for division is a shared judgment formed around Christ crucified. In Revelation, the final rebellion is a shared judgment formed against the Lamb, as the surrounding chapter makes clear when the kings make war with Him and are overcome (17:14). Humanity will finally reach one mind, either in Christ or against Him.

The cross is the clearest example of what Revelation 17 describes. There, too, rulers agreed together against God's Anointed, and God used their resolve to accomplish redemption (Acts 4:27–28, which uses different words). Revelation 17 applies the same pattern to the end of history. The story does not end with rival wills in balance. It ends with God's words fulfilled.

7. Spirit & Life

Because God forms judgment in His servants, believers do not need to freeze in place where Scripture gives no command. Paul's pattern in 1 Corinthians 7 frees us to seek mercy-made trustworthiness: to grow in the Spirit, weigh matters honestly, and offer counsel with humility about its status. It also teaches us to listen to mature believers whose judgment has been tested.

Philemon shows how God's people treat one another. Leaders who could press for compliance may choose to appeal instead, because a willing gift honors both the giver and the Lord. The same spirit appears when Paul says each one should give "as he purposes in his heart, not grudgingly or of necessity; for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7).

Revelation 17 brings steadiness. When powerful people seem perfectly united against God's ways, His purpose is still being done, even through them. The believer can choose now what the kings never chose: to bring our resolve into willing agreement with God's purpose instead of having it overruled.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 20:3 — When a plot formed against him, Paul "decided to return through Macedonia." The word describes ordinary, prudent resolve in the middle of real danger.

  • 2 Corinthians 8:10 — "And in this I give advice." Paul encourages the Corinthians to finish the collection they had eagerly begun a year earlier. He speaks as counsel, which fits his concern that their giving be willing.
