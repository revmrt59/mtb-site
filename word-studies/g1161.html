AND, BUT, NOW, THEN - (dé, Strong's G1161)

Summary

Dé is one of the most frequent connecting words in the Greek New Testament. Its job is to move the reader from one statement to the next. Sometimes it simply carries the story or argument forward, and the NKJV renders it "and," "now," "then," or "so." Other times it turns the reader toward a different subject or a contrasting idea, and the NKJV renders it "but," "yet," or "however." The word itself does not say which kind of move is happening. Context decides. Readers who notice dé begin to see how Scripture's stories and arguments fit together. It may open a new topic, change the speaker, set one truth beside another, or complete an "on the one hand… on the other" pairing. It also teaches a simple caution: every English "But" is not a sharp collision of ideas. Many are a gentle turn in a steady line of thought.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Dé connects clauses and sentences. It normally stands second in its Greek clause, after the first word or phrase. From there it signals that what follows belongs to the flow of what came before, and that something has shifted. The shift may be small, such as the next step in a narrative or the next generation in a family line. It may be larger, such as a new topic in a letter, a new person entering a scene, or an idea set against the previous one.

This is why English translations render it so many different ways. English forces a choice between "and" and "but." Dé can do both kinds of work. It is best understood as a word that marks a new step and leaves the size and direction of that step to the surrounding text.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Continuation and transition ("and," "now," "then," "so")

Here dé moves the discourse forward without any sense of opposition. In Matthew's genealogy it links each generation to the next: "Abraham begot Isaac, Isaac begot Jacob, and Jacob begot Judah and his brothers" (Matthew 1:2). In Greek, dé also stands at the "Isaac begot Jacob" link, although the NKJV leaves it untranslated there. The line simply advances one name at a time. In the letters, the same word can open a new subject. Paul turns to a question the Corinthians had raised with "Now concerning spiritual gifts, brethren, I do not want you to be ignorant" (1 Corinthians 12:1). The "Now" does not oppose anything. It signals that Paul is beginning a new section of his letter.

Contrast or change of focus ("but," "yet," "however")

Often dé sets a new statement against the previous one. In Romans 6:23 it divides two destinies: "For the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." Here the contrast is unmistakable. Wages are earned; a gift is given. In Matthew 6:33 Jesus has just described how the nations anxiously pursue food and clothing (6:31–32). He then redirects His disciples: "But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you." The contrast here is between two ways of living, and dé is the point where the turn happens.

Paired contrast (with men)

Dé often completes a balanced pair begun by the particle men, giving the sense "on the one hand… on the other." John the Baptist uses this pattern to set his ministry beside Christ's: "I indeed baptize you with water unto repentance, but He who is coming after me is mightier than I" (Matthew 3:11). The "indeed" reflects the first half of the pair, and dé brings in the second. The construction holds the two ministries side by side so that the greater one stands out.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Following dé helps a reader trace how a passage is built. In Romans 6:23 the whole verse turns on it. Paul places humanity's earned wage and God's free gift on either side of one small hinge, and the reader is meant to feel the distance between them. Death is what sin pays. Eternal life comes from God, and only "in Christ Jesus our Lord."

Matthew 3:11 shows a different kind of turn. John is not contradicting himself or diminishing his own work. The men… dé pattern lets him honor what God gave him to do while pointing past himself to the One coming after him. Grammar and humility work together in that sentence. John's baptism is real, but it prepares the way for the One who baptizes "with the Holy Spirit and fire."

Even the genealogy rewards attention. Generation after generation is joined by the same plain connector. The word itself only says "next." The passage as a whole shows God carrying His promise forward, name by name, toward Jesus.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume every English "But" signals a hard reversal. Dé often marks only a change of speaker, scene, or topic, and translators sometimes choose "But" where "And" or "Now" would serve equally well. Let the paragraph decide how strong the contrast is. Dé is also generally a softer contrast than alla (G235), which English also renders "but." The same English word can therefore stand for two different kinds of turn.
