MIND, UNDERSTANDING, IMAGINATION - (diánoia, Strong's G1271)

Summary

Diánoia is the Greek word for the mind as the part of a person that thinks things through. It covers reasoning, understanding, and the settled outlook that grows from them. In the NKJV it is usually translated "mind," and sometimes "understanding" or "imagination." The word itself is morally neutral. It names a human faculty, and that faculty can be proud and darkened, or enlightened and devoted to God.

Scripture uses diánoia to tell the whole story of the human mind. Jesus commands us to love God with all our mind. Paul describes the mind before Christ as driven by fleshly desire, darkened, and hostile toward God. Hebrews quotes God's promise to put His laws into the minds of His people. And the apostles call believers to keep that renewed mind alert and focused. The thinking life is one of the places where God does His saving work, and it is one of the ways we love Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Diánoia belongs to the same family of Greek words as nous, the common word for "mind." It describes the mind at work: thinking a matter through, weighing it, and arriving at an understanding or attitude. It appears thirteen times in the New Testament. Its uses fall into a few clear settings: the greatest-commandment dialogues in the first three Gospels, Mary's song in Luke, Paul's descriptions of life before and after Christ in Ephesians and Colossians, the new covenant promise in Hebrews, and pastoral exhortations in Peter's letters and 1 John.

Paul's longer discussions of the mind in Romans and Corinthians use nous instead. Romans 12:2 is an example: "be transformed by the renewing of your mind." The two words cover similar ground, so readers should not draw large conclusions from which one appears.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The faculty of thinking and understanding. This is the mind as the capacity to reason and comprehend, as in "the eyes of your understanding being enlightened" (Ephesians 1:18).

2. A way of thinking or disposition. This is the mind's settled attitude or inclination, sometimes expressed as imagination or inner reasoning, as in "the proud in the imagination of their hearts" (Luke 1:51).

Neither sense guarantees good or bad thinking. Context determines whether the mind is darkened or enlightened.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's surroundings decide its moral color. When diánoia is linked to "the flesh," as in Ephesians 2:3, to darkness (Ephesians 4:18), or to hostility (Colossians 1:21), it describes the mind in rebellion. When God acts on it, it becomes a renewed mind. God enlightens it in Ephesians 1:18, the Son gives it in 1 John 5:20, and God writes His laws on it in Hebrews 8:10.

The word often appears beside "heart." Luke 1:51 speaks of the diánoia "of their hearts," and Hebrews pairs mind and heart in parallel lines. In biblical thinking, the mind is part of the heart's inner life. It is not a separate machine sitting above the emotions.

4. Theological Significance

These passages reveal that God never treats the human mind as a neutral observer standing outside the conflict between sin and grace. Paul includes the mind in our bondage: "we all once conducted ourselves in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind" (Ephesians 2:3). The mind itself had desires, and they ran away from God. Ephesians 4:18 traces the root of this darkness to "the blindness of their heart." Colossians 1:21 joins inner hostility to outward conduct: "enemies in your mind by wicked works." Romans 8:7 states the same reality in Paul's other vocabulary: "the carnal mind is enmity against God."

Mary's song shows how God answers proud thinking. "He has scattered the proud in the imagination of their hearts" (Luke 1:51). The proud build their plans in their own reasoning, and God simply disperses them.

So the mind needs rescue as much as any other part of us. Colossians 1:21 does not stop at hostility. It continues, "yet now He has reconciled." The God who saves sinners saves their thinking too.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Matthew 22:37; Mark 12:30; Luke 10:27 — Loving God with the mind. Deuteronomy 6:5 calls Israel to love the LORD with heart, soul, and strength. In each Gospel account the mind is named as well: "with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength" (Mark 12:30). In Luke 10:27 the lawyer answering Jesus places the mind last, and Jesus affirms his answer in verse 28. Jesus' words in Matthew and Mark and His endorsement in Luke leave no faculty out. Thinking, learning, remembering, and reasoning are ways of loving God. A believer who loves God warmly but never thinks hard about Him has not yet given Him everything the command asks. The same is true of one who studies God carefully but never loves Him.

Hebrews 8:10 and 10:16 — The law written on the mind. Hebrews quotes Jeremiah's promise twice: "I will put My laws in their mind and write them on their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be My people" (Hebrews 8:10). Jeremiah's Hebrew speaks of God's law placed in their "inward parts." The Greek translation of the Old Testament that Hebrews follows rendered that phrase with diánoia. The promise reaches the very faculty Ephesians and Colossians describe as darkened and hostile. God does not merely post His commands outside a person, as He once did on tablets of stone. He writes them where thinking and desiring begin, so that His will becomes the believer's own inclination.

Notice what happens when Hebrews repeats the promise in 10:16: "I will put My laws into their hearts, and in their minds I will write them." The order is reversed. Mind and heart trade places, because God's work covers both. Scripture does not let us split "head knowledge" from "heart knowledge." The heart wants what the mind grasps of God, and the mind understands what the heart has come to love. When God renews one, He is renewing both.

1 John 5:20 — Understanding as the Son's gift. John ends his letter with assurance grounded in something given: "the Son of God has come and has given us an understanding, that we may know Him who is true." The purpose of this understanding is relationship. John wants believers to know Him, and he goes on to say that they are in Him, "in His Son Jesus Christ." Understanding God truly is a gift from Christ, and the gift exists so that we can know God personally.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of diánoia moves from Eden's rebellion to the new covenant. Jeremiah looked ahead to a day when God would write His law inwardly. Hebrews announces that this day has come through Christ, the mediator of a better covenant, whose once-for-all sacrifice opened the way for that inward work (Hebrews 10:14–18). The promise of a rewritten mind is anchored in the cross.

Ephesians traces the same movement within one letter. Paul describes a former life governed by the desires "of the mind" (2:3) and an understanding darkened and "alienated from the life of God" (4:18). Even so, he prays early in the letter that the eyes of their understanding would be enlightened to know "the hope of His calling" (1:18). The darkness he describes has already been broken into by grace. John completes the picture: the Son Himself has come and given understanding. Jesus does more than command us to love God with all our mind. He makes that obedience possible by giving us a new mind.

7. Spirit & Life

Grace does not leave the redeemed mind idle. Because God has given understanding, the apostles call believers to use it with purpose. Peter tells his readers to prepare their minds for action, borrowing the picture of a person gathering up long robes and tucking them into a belt in order to run or work (1 Peter 1:13). A mind left loose trips over every distraction. In 2 Peter 3:1, Peter says he writes to stir up their minds by way of reminder. Even sincere minds need to be awakened again to truths they already know.

This has practical weight. Worry, cynicism, and pride are not merely moods. They are ways of thinking that the gospel confronts. God has written His laws on the believer's mind, and so the believer can deliberately think God's thoughts after Him: rehearsing His promises, testing ideas against His Word, and refusing to let the old reasoning of the flesh set the agenda. Bible study, careful reflection, and honest questions can all be acts of love toward God. They answer His command to love Him with the mind and receive His gift of understanding.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 1:51 — Mary rejoices that God "has scattered the proud in the imagination of their hearts." Proud human reasoning cannot stand against the Lord who lifts up the lowly.

  • Ephesians 4:18 — Unbelievers have "their understanding darkened, being alienated from the life of God." Darkness of mind and distance from God go together.

  • Colossians 1:21 — Those once "enemies in your mind by wicked works" are now reconciled. Christ's work reaches the hostility within.

  • Ephesians 1:18 — Paul prays for "the eyes of your understanding being enlightened" so believers may know their hope and inheritance.

  • 1 Peter 1:13; 2 Peter 3:1 — Peter calls believers to ready their minds and to keep them stirred up by reminder.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G3563 (nous), "mind" — A lexical relative from the same family. It is Paul's usual word in Romans and Corinthians, for example, "the renewing of your mind" (Romans 12:2).

  • G2588 (kardia), "heart" — A canonical pairing. It is joined to diánoia in Luke 1:51 and set in parallel with it in Hebrews 8:10 and 10:16.

10. Other Relevant Insights

In Ephesians 1:18, many modern translations read "the eyes of your heart," following Greek manuscripts that have kardia there. The NKJV follows manuscripts that read diánoia. Either way, the verse shows the close partnership between mind and heart that runs through this word's uses.
