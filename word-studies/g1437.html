IF, WHOEVER, WHATEVER, THOUGH - (eán, Strong's G1437)

Summary

Eán is one of the main Greek words the New Testament uses for "if." It usually introduces a situation that could really happen, or one that keeps happening in ordinary life. It does not usually picture something everyone knows is untrue. The "if" clause sets the condition, and the rest of the sentence says what follows: "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us." When eán joins words like "who," "what," or "where," it widens the statement to whoever, whatever, or wherever, so the words apply to anyone who fits. Sometimes it pushes a case to an extreme, and the NKJV renders it "though" or "even if." Watching this word helps a reader see exactly what hangs on what, and how far a promise or warning reaches.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

A conditional sentence has two halves: the condition ("if this…") and the result ("…then that"). Eán opens the first half. It treats the situation as open and real. The thing might happen, often does happen, or will keep happening. Greek writers normally pair eán with a verb form that expresses possibility rather than plain fact.

When Jesus says, "For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you" (Matthew 6:14), He is not wondering whether forgiveness is likely. He is stating a principle that holds whenever the condition is met. Eán sets up the case, and the main clause tells the reader what God does in that case.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Open or general condition: "if"

This is by far the most common use. In 1 John 1:9 John writes, "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." Confession is the condition. Forgiveness and cleansing are the result. The result rests on God's own character, since He is "faithful and just." Matthew 6:14, quoted above, works the same way as a standing principle Jesus lays down for His disciples.

Widening the scope: "whoever," "whatever," "wherever"

Paired with a relative word, eán turns a specific statement into an open one. "Whoever therefore breaks one of the least of these commandments, and teaches men so, shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:19). The warning names no one in particular because it covers anyone who does this. Mark 11:23 uses the same pattern to extend a promise: "he will have whatever he says." The same widening appears with place in Matthew 26:13, where Jesus says the woman's deed will be told wherever the gospel is preached.

Concession and extreme cases: "though," "even if"

Sometimes eán grants a condition for the sake of argument, even an extreme one, to show that the outcome still does not change. "Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I have become sounding brass or a clanging cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13:1). Paul goes on to grant prophecy, knowledge, mountain-moving faith, and total self-sacrifice (13:2–3). Galatians 1:8 reaches even further: "But even if we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel to you than what we have preached to you, let him be accursed." Paul imagines the most unlikely messengers possible so that no messenger, however impressive, can be allowed to change the gospel.

3. Practical Reading Insight

First John 1:6–10 is built almost entirely on eán. John moves through a sequence of cases. "If we say that we have fellowship with Him, and walk in darkness, we lie" (1:6). "But if we walk in the light as He is in the light, we have fellowship with one another, and the blood of Jesus Christ His Son cleanses us from all sin" (1:7). "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" (1:8). "If we confess our sins…" (1:9). "If we say that we have not sinned, we make Him a liar" (1:10).

These are not abstract puzzles. They are real claims people were making in the church. John sets each one beside its true outcome, so the reader can see which road leads where. The false claims end in lying and self-deception. The honest response ends in fellowship and cleansing. John places the reader inside each "if" and asks, in effect, which of these describes you. The good news is that the door of confession stands open. Its result depends on a faithful God, not on how well the person confessing has performed.

First Corinthians 13 shows the word's other edge. Paul stacks up the greatest gifts imaginable as granted conditions, and every one of them collapses into nothing without love. The "though" clauses take away every excuse.

4. Reading Guidance

In English, "if" can sound doubtful. Eán by itself does not signal that something is unlikely, so do not read hesitation into Jesus' or John's conditions. Equally, a "whatever" promise such as Mark 11:23 still has its stated conditions ("does not doubt in his heart, but believes") and must be read within its passage. The New Testament also uses another Greek word, ei, that English translates "if." Do not build a doctrine on which of the two a writer chose. Not every English "whoever" represents eán either.
