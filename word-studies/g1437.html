IF, WHOEVER, WHATEVER, THOUGH - (eán, Strong's G1437)

Summary

Eán is the Greek word most often translated "if." It sets out a situation that may arise and then states what follows when it does. Jesus uses it for real-life cases, such as "if your brother sins against you." John uses it to attach a promise to a condition: "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us." Combined with words like "who," "what," or "where," eán widens a statement to "whoever," "whatever," or "wherever," so a promise or warning reaches anyone who fits the description. It can also introduce an extreme case that does not change the outcome, as in "Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels." Readers who notice eán can see who a statement applies to, what it depends on, and how far it reaches. They are also less likely to hear doubt where the text expresses none.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Eán introduces the "if" half of a conditional sentence and joins it to the result. It usually looks at circumstances that may come about, often in the future or as a general rule: "if this happens, then this follows." Such sentences are not idle speculation. Many of them describe what believers will actually face, such as a brother's sin, a prayer offered, or a voice heard at the door. Eán also attaches to relative words to make them open-ended, turning "who" into "whoever." Greek has another common word for "if," ei. It often treats a condition as a point granted for argument. New Testament writers do not keep the two strictly apart, so context remains the surer guide.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Setting a condition ("if")

This is the word's most frequent use. In 1 John 1:9, the condition comes first: "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." The word ties the assurance of cleansing to the act of confession.

In Matthew 18:15, Jesus uses eán twice to lay out steps for restoring a relationship: "if your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault between you and him alone. If he hears you, you have gained your brother." The first "if" names a situation every church will face. The second shows what success looks like at the first step.

Widening the scope ("whoever," "whatever," "wherever")

When eán follows a relative pronoun or adverb, it makes the statement open-ended. Jesus tells Peter, "whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven" (Matthew 16:19). The word does not limit the statement to a list of named cases. It covers anything that falls within the binding and loosing Jesus describes.

The same pattern produces "whoever desires to become great among you" in Matthew 20:26, and "wherever he goes in" in Mark 14:14, where the disciples do not yet know which house they will enter. A close relative of this use is eán with "anyone": "If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20).

Granting an extreme case ("though," "even if")

Sometimes eán introduces a case that, even if true, would not change the conclusion. Paul begins his great chapter on love this way: "Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I have become sounding brass or a clanging cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13:1). He repeats the pattern through verse 3, stacking up prophecy, faith, and sacrifice, and none of them counts without love.

Galatians 1:8 pushes the extreme even further: "But even if we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel to you than what we have preached to you, let him be accursed." Paul does not expect an angel to preach a false gospel. He names the most unlikely source imaginable to show that no messenger outranks the message.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Eán shows who a promise is for and what it depends on. Revelation 3:20 is addressed to the church in Laodicea, a church Jesus has just called lukewarm. Yet the promise is not reserved for a select few within it. "If anyone hears My voice and opens the door" leaves the door open to every listener, and the promise of fellowship follows as surely as the condition is met. The same structure runs through 1 John 1:9. Forgiveness rests on God's faithfulness and justice, and confession is the way a believer receives it.

The concessive use carries its own lesson. In 1 Corinthians 13 and Galatians 1, eán lets Paul imagine the most impressive gifts and the most exalted messengers. He then shows that neither can replace love or the true gospel. The word sets up the test, and the main clause delivers the verdict.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume "if" signals doubt. In John 14:3, Jesus uses eán when He speaks of going to prepare a place, though His going was certain. In 1 John 3:2, the NKJV renders eán as "when" for Christ's appearing. Avoid the opposite error too: treating a conditional promise as automatic. Finally, the grammatical type of condition alone cannot carry the weight of a doctrine. Let the surrounding passage decide.
