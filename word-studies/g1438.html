MYSELF, YOURSELF, HIMSELF, THEMSELVES, OWN - (heautoû, Strong's G1438)

Summary

Heautoû is the Greek New Testament's ordinary reflexive pronoun. It points an action or a possession back to the person who is doing it. When Jesus warns that "whoever exalts himself will be humbled," heautoû marks the man who lifts himself up. When Paul says Christ "humbled Himself," the same word shows that the Son was the one who chose the lowering. In the plural it can describe a group acting within its own circle: disciples reasoning "among themselves," or believers forgiving "one another." The word helps the reader see who is acting on whom. Scripture uses it to expose self-exaltation and self-trust, and also to show the willing self-giving of Jesus Christ.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

A reflexive pronoun turns a sentence back on itself. The subject does something, and the receiver of that action turns out to be the subject too. Heautoû does this work in Greek, and English normally renders it with words ending in "-self" or "-selves." It also appears with nouns to mark what belongs to the subject, which the NKJV often renders "own," "his," or "their." In plural settings it can describe what happens inside a group, so that "themselves" or "one another" marks members acting toward each other. In every case the word tracks the same person or group named in the clause. It keeps the reader from imagining some outside party.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Self-directed action ("himself," "themselves," "Himself")

This is the word's most frequent use. The subject acts upon his or her own person. Jesus' warning in Matthew 23:12 turns on it twice: "And whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted." The reflexive shows that each man is the one doing the lifting or the lowering. God then reverses the outcome.

Paul uses the same construction of Christ in Philippians 2:8: "He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross." Jesus was not simply humbled by others. He took the lower place Himself. Jesus' call to discipleship uses the same pattern. In Matthew 16:24, the disciple must deny himself.

Reflexive belonging ("own," "his," "their")

Here heautoû marks something as belonging to the subject. Paul turns this around for believers in 1 Corinthians 6:19: "you are not your own." The Corinthians imagined their bodies were theirs to use as they pleased. Paul answers that they belong to God, and the Holy Spirit dwells in them.

Action within a group ("among themselves," "one another")

With plural subjects, heautoû often describes conversation or action kept within a group. In Matthew 16:7, after Jesus warns about the leaven of the Pharisees, the disciples "reasoned among themselves." They talk the matter over with each other instead of asking Jesus, and they miss His point.

The same word can describe healthy mutual ministry. Colossians 3:16 calls believers to be "teaching and admonishing one another." Ephesians 4:32 is instructive because it uses two different Greek expressions: "And be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another." The first "one another" translates allēlōn, the usual Greek reciprocal word. The second, attached to forgiving, translates heautoû. The church forgives within its own body, as members of one people who have all been forgiven in Christ.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The reflexive draws attention to the self-giving of Christ. Galatians 1:4 says Christ "gave Himself for our sins." Titus 2:14 says He "gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people." In these verses Jesus is both the giver and the gift, and He gives for the sake of others.

This does not set Christ against His Father. Galatians 1:4 adds in the same breath that this happened "according to the will of our God and Father." The Son's willing gift and the Father's purpose stand together.

The same word exposes the opposite instinct in human hearts. Luke 18:9 introduces the Pharisee and the tax collector by describing "some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous." When the rulers mocked Jesus at the cross, they said in effect that He saved others but could not save Himself (Mark 15:31). He would not save Himself, because He had come to give Himself.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not treat every "himself" or "own" in English as heautoû. English "himself" also translates a different Greek word, autos, when it adds emphasis ("He Himself"). Be careful, too, with plural uses. "Among themselves" can mean private discussion or genuine mutual ministry, and context decides which. Finally, the pronoun shows who receives an action. The weight of meaning comes from the verb and the surrounding passage.
