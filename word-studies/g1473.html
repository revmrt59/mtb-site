I, ME, MY, WE, US, OUR - (egṓ, Strong's G1473)

Summary

Egṓ is the Greek first-person pronoun. It is the word a speaker uses for himself ("I," "me," "my") and, in its plural forms, for a group that includes the speaker ("we," "us," "our"). Most of its uses are ordinary. It marks who receives an action, as in the leper's plea, "You can make me clean" (Mark 1:40), or whose something is, as in "Our Father in heaven" (Matthew 6:9).

Its most important use is easy for English readers to miss. A Greek verb already shows its subject, so a writer does not need egṓ to say "I." When the pronoun does appear as the subject, it usually stresses the speaker: "I, in contrast," or "I myself." This is the force behind Jesus' repeated "But I say to you" in the Sermon on the Mount. It also lies behind His "I AM" sayings in John. Noticing this stress helps readers hear where authority, identity, and personal claims fall in the text.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Egṓ points back to whoever is speaking or writing. English needs "I" in every sentence, but Greek does not. A verb like "I say" is complete without a separate pronoun. That makes the explicit subject form meaningful. It puts the speaker forward, often to set him against someone else or to make his identity the point of the sentence.

The pronoun's other forms do the everyday work of first-person reference:

  • "me" and "us" mark the speaker as the one acted upon.

  • "me" and "us" also serve as the object of a preposition.

  • "my" and "our" mark possession or relationship.

Its plural forms mean "we," "us," and "our," referring to a group that includes the speaker. So the word covers both a single speaker and a speaking community, such as the apostles, the church, or Israel.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Emphatic or contrastive subject (NKJV: "I," "we")

The explicit pronoun stresses the speaker, usually against a contrast nearby.

  • Matthew 5:22. Jesus says, "But I say to you that whoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment." In the verse before, He recalls what "was said to those of old" (paraphrase of 5:21). The emphatic "I" sets His own word beside that inherited teaching. He does not appeal to another teacher's authority. He speaks on His own.

  • Galatians 2:20. Paul writes, "it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me." The stressed "I" is deliberately set aside so that Christ can stand in its place.

Self-identification with "I am" (NKJV: "It is I," "I am He," "I AM")

Paired with the verb "to be," egṓ forms the expression egō eimi.

  • Matthew 14:27. The phrase can be a simple identification. Jesus calls across the storm, "Be of good cheer! It is I; do not be afraid."

  • John 8:58. In John the expression carries more weight. Jesus says, "before Abraham was, I AM." The sentence has no completing word, and His hearers immediately try to stone Him (paraphrase of 8:59). Both details show they understood a claim far greater than a statement of age. Many readers hear in it an echo of God's self-naming in Exodus 3:14.

Possession and relationship (NKJV: "my," "our," "mine," "ours")

These forms tie a person or thing to the speaker.

  • Matthew 6:9. Jesus teaches His disciples to pray, "Our Father in heaven." From its first word, the prayer belongs to a family praying together.

  • Romans 1:8. Paul writes, "I thank my God through Jesus Christ." The possessive expresses personal belonging, not ownership of God.

Object of a verb or preposition (NKJV: "me," "us")

These forms mark the speaker as the one who receives an action.

  • Mark 1:40. The leper's request is simple: "make me clean."

  • Galatians 2:20. Paul speaks of the Son of God "who loved me and gave Himself for me." The sweeping work of Christ is received as something done for one particular person.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Paying attention to the emphatic "I" changes how we hear Jesus. In Matthew 5 He does not simply offer a better reading of the commandments. The stressed pronoun places His own word at the center. That is a remarkable claim for any teacher to make, and Matthew's audience felt it.

Galatians 2:20 shows the pronoun working from the other direction. Paul uses the strong "I" in order to give it up: "no longer I who live." Then he lets the humbler forms carry the gospel to him personally: Christ lives "in me," loved "me," and gave Himself "for me." The grammar mirrors the change Paul describes. The self that once stood at the center gives way, yet the person is not erased. He is loved by name.

The plural forms make a quieter point. Much of the New Testament's language of faith is "our" language: "Our Father," "our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). Faith is personal, but it is never private.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume that every "I" in an English Bible stands for a separate Greek egṓ. Often the "I" comes only from the verb. Emphasis should be claimed only where the pronoun is actually present.

Also, the pronoun alone does not tell you who the "I" is. The repeated "I" of Romans 7:14–25 is a good example. Paul says, "For what I am doing, I do not understand" (7:15). Interpreters debate whether this describes Paul before conversion, Paul as a believer, or a representative figure. The answer comes from the flow of Romans 6–8, not from the pronoun.

Similarly, "I am He" in John 8:24 is read either as a messianic claim or as an echo of the divine name. The surrounding chapter, not the grammar alone, guides that decision.
