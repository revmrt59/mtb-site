TO, INTO, FOR, TOWARD - (eis, Strong's G1519)

Summary

Eis is one of the most common prepositions in the Greek New Testament. Its basic job is to point an action somewhere: toward a place, a person, a purpose, or a point in time. The wise men come into the house where Jesus is. Paul calls the gospel the power of God to salvation. Christ's blood is shed for the remission of sins. Each case has a sense of direction, of something moving toward an end. Once readers notice this, many verses become clearer, because the word often answers a quiet but important question in the sentence: where is this going? Sometimes the answer is a city. Sometimes it is righteousness, salvation, or the endless reign of the Son of David.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Eis links an action to its destination. With verbs of going, sending, throwing, or leading, it marks the place entered or reached. The same sense of direction carries over into less physical settings. It can name the goal or result an action produces, the person an attitude is aimed at, or the time an action extends to. NKJV translators render it in many ways, most often "to," "into," "in," and "for," because English has no single word that covers every use. The common thread is orientation toward an end point. Context decides whether that end point is a location, an outcome, a person, or the future. In New Testament Greek, eis sometimes appears where we might expect a word for simple location ("in"). Not every occurrence should be pressed to mean active motion.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Movement into or toward a place (into, to)

This is the most familiar use. Matthew writes that the wise men, "when they had come into the house, they saw the young Child with Mary His mother" (Matthew 2:11). Here eis marks the threshold they crossed. In "Then Jesus was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil" (Matthew 4:1), it names the destination of the Spirit's leading. The same idea extends figuratively in the Lord's Prayer: "And do not lead us into temptation" (Matthew 6:13). Temptation is pictured as a place one could be brought into.

Goal, purpose, or result (to, for, unto)

Eis often points to what an action aims at or brings about. Paul says the gospel "is the power of God to salvation for everyone who believes" (Romans 1:16). Salvation is the outcome God's power works toward. At the Last Supper Jesus says His blood "is shed for many for the remission of sins" (Matthew 26:28). In that verse, "for many" and "for the remission" come from different Greek words. Only the second is eis, and it states what the shedding of His blood accomplishes. A related pattern marks what something becomes. In Matthew 19:5, for example, the two "shall become one flesh," and eis introduces the new reality into which husband and wife are joined.

Direction toward a person, in favor or in offense (toward, against)

Attitudes and actions can be aimed at someone. "But God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). God's love has a target, and it is people who had done nothing to attract it. The same preposition can mark the person wronged. In Luke 15:18, the prodigal son resolves to confess that he has sinned against heaven and before his father (paraphrase). There the direction is hostile rather than kind.

Extent in time, especially "forever" (forever, until)

Eis can point to a limit in time. It also appears in a fixed expression, literally "into the ages," which English renders "forever." The angel tells Mary, "And He will reign over the house of Jacob forever, and of His kingdom there will be no end" (Luke 1:33). The English "forever" represents the whole Greek phrase, not eis alone. The idiom pictures time stretching ahead without a stopping point. That is exactly the kind of reign promised to David's greater Son.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Seeing the direction in eis helps readers follow where a sentence is heading. Romans 10:10 is a clear example: "For with the heart one believes unto righteousness, and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation." Paul does not present belief and confession as ends in themselves. Each leads somewhere. Heart-belief issues in righteousness, and spoken confession issues in salvation. The preposition traces the line from the response God calls for to the gift God gives.

A second pattern rewards attention in John's Gospel. John repeatedly speaks of believing "in" Jesus, as in John 3:16, using eis rather than a simpler construction. The phrase directs faith toward a Person, not merely toward a set of statements about Him. This should not be overstated. Other New Testament writers express real faith with different prepositions, and the preposition alone does not define saving faith. Still, John's steady habit of pointing believing toward Christ fits the whole aim of his Gospel, which was written that readers would believe and have life in His name.

4. Reading Guidance

The English word "for" can look backward ("thanked for the gift") or forward ("saving for a house"). When "for" translates eis, it normally points forward to a goal or result. Acts 2:38 is a well-known example: "be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins." Most grammarians read eis here in its usual forward-looking sense. Some interpreters argue for a backward-looking ("because of") sense, though that use of eis is rare at best. A single preposition cannot settle the wider question of how baptism relates to forgiveness. That question must be weighed from the whole of Scripture.
