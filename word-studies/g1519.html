INTO, TO, FOR, IN - (eis, Strong's G1519)

Summary

Eis is a Greek preposition that points toward a goal. Most often it marks where someone is going, as when the wise men "had come into the house" (Matthew 2:11). The same forward-pointing sense carries over into other areas. Eis can name the purpose an action serves, the new state or relationship a person enters, or the point in time something reaches. That is why the NKJV renders it in many ways: "to," "into," "in," "for," "until," and, in a fixed phrase, "forever." Noticing eis helps readers see where a sentence is headed. The gospel is God's power aimed at salvation. Faith is directed toward Jesus Himself. Some of the New Testament's richest phrases, such as being "baptized into Christ Jesus" (Romans 6:3), are built on this word.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Eis is one of the most frequently used prepositions in the New Testament. It governs a noun and points the action of the sentence toward that noun as a destination, goal, or limit. The word itself does not change, but its role shifts with the verb it accompanies. After a verb of motion, it marks a place. After a word of purpose, it marks an aim. With expressions of time, it marks how far something extends. The NKJV reflects this range by rendering it "to," "into," "in," and "for," along with other wordings. The direction is constant. The English word changes because English needs different words for different kinds of "toward."

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Destination and direction ("into," "to," "toward")

This is the most common use. Jesus "was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness" (Matthew 4:1). Eis names where the Spirit's leading took Him. The word does not always mean going inside, though. On Easter morning, the other disciple "came to the tomb first" (John 20:4), but the next verse says he stopped and looked in without entering. Eis marks arrival at a point. Context decides whether that point is crossed.

Goal, purpose, or result ("to," "for")

Here the movement is no longer physical. Paul writes that the gospel "is the power of God to salvation for everyone who believes" (Romans 1:16). Eis tells us what the gospel's power is aimed at and what it accomplishes. Its power is directed toward rescuing people.

Entering a relationship ("in," "into")

With verbs of believing and baptizing, eis points to a person. "Whoever believes in Him" (John 3:16) uses eis, so faith is pictured as directed toward Jesus as its object. Paul speaks of those "baptized into Christ Jesus" and "baptized into His death" (Romans 6:3). Here eis expresses being joined to Christ and sharing in what His death accomplished.

Extent of time ("until," "forever")

Acts 4:3 uses eis twice. The apostles were put "in custody until the next day." The first marks the condition they were placed into, and the second marks how long it lasted. In a fixed phrase, literally "into the ages," eis becomes the NKJV's "forever." Gabriel promises that Jesus "will reign over the house of Jacob forever" (Luke 1:33). The single English word renders the whole Greek phrase, not eis alone. The phrase reaches past every horizon a reader can imagine.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Eis keeps asking a useful question: where is this going? When Paul says the gospel is God's power "to salvation," he is not describing a general religious force. He is naming its target. When John repeatedly writes that people believe eis Jesus, the grammar places Jesus at the end of faith's direction. Saving faith in John's Gospel is not a vague confidence. It has a Person as its object.

Romans 6:3 takes this further. Paul does not merely say believers were baptized near Christ or in honor of Him. They were baptized "into Christ Jesus" and "into His death." His argument in the chapter depends on this union. Those joined to Christ's death cannot casually go on living in sin, because they now belong to Him. The preposition carries the weight of belonging. Believers have been brought into Christ, and their lives are now oriented toward Him.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not press "into" so literally that every eis means going inside. John 20:4 shows arrival without entry. In this period of Greek, eis also sometimes overlaps with en ("in") and can simply mark location. In John 1:18, the Son "is in the bosom of the Father," and building a theology of motion on that one preposition goes beyond the evidence.

Range of meaning also matters in Acts 2:38, "for the remission of sins." Most read eis there as goal. Others point to Matthew 12:41, where Nineveh "repented at the preaching of Jonah," to argue for a looser "with reference to." The preposition alone cannot settle that debate. Context and the wider teaching of Scripture must decide.
