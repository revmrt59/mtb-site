OUT OF, FROM, OF, BY - (ek, Strong's G1537)

Summary

The Greek preposition ek points to where something comes from. Its basic sense is "out of" or "out from." It can describe a voice coming from heaven, a person singled out of a group, a life born of God, or a right standing that rests on faith rather than on works. English translations render it many ways, most often "of," "from," and "by." Behind each of these renderings is the same question: what is this coming out of? Asking that question clarifies several key passages. In John, it shows whether a person's life comes from God or from the world. In Paul, it shows that justification rests on faith, not on law-keeping. In the Gospels, it marks the painful truth that Jesus' betrayer came from inside the circle of the Twelve.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Ek connects a person, thing, or event to its point of origin. Sometimes that origin is a physical place, and the word traces movement out of it. Often the origin is personal or spiritual, such as a family line, a birth, a group, or a source of character. In theological arguments it can also name the ground a result rests on. In those cases NKJV often uses "by," as in "justified by faith." The original sense of coming "out of" a place helps explain these wider uses, but each use must be read on its own terms. When Scripture says someone "is of the earth" or "born of God," the word identifies what that person's life comes from. It does not describe a physical location.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Movement out of a place or source (from, out of)

At Jesus' baptism, "a voice came from heaven" (Mark 1:11). Ek marks heaven as the place the Father's words come from. Matthew applies the same idea to geography when he quotes Hosea: "Out of Egypt I called My Son" (Matthew 2:15).

One fixed expression belongs here. The common phrase rendered "at My right hand" or "on the right" (for example Matthew 22:44; 26:64) uses ek. Greek pictures position as seen "from" a side, while English describes being "at" or "on" it. The difference comes from Greek idiom, so it carries no hidden meaning.

Origin, birth, and belonging (of, from)

John uses ek to name the source of a person's life. Believers are those "who were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God" (John 1:13). John lists three human sources and sets each one aside, then names God as the true source.

John 3:31 uses the same preposition to describe character: "he who is of the earth is earthly and speaks of the earth." Origin shapes how a person speaks. A person who comes from the earth talks in earthly terms.

Part of a larger group (of, among)

Ek can single out one member of a group. At the Passover meal Jesus said, "one of you will betray Me" (Matthew 26:21). Luke says Satan entered Judas, "who was numbered among the twelve" (Luke 22:3). Here the word stresses that the betrayer came from inside Jesus' closest company, not from among His enemies.

The basis or ground of a result (by, from)

In Paul's letters, ek often names what a result rests on. "By the deeds of the law no flesh will be justified" (Romans 3:20). Paul writes that "we might be justified by faith in Christ and not by the works of the law" (Galatians 2:16). He sets two possible foundations side by side and rejects one of them. Romans 1:17 uses the word twice: righteousness is revealed "from faith to faith," and "The just shall live by faith."

3. Practical Reading Insight

Paul and John both use ek to ask what a life grows out of. Paul's question is the ground of acceptance with God. His answer is never law-keeping. It is faith in Christ. John's question is the source of new life. His answer is God Himself, and that is why he can write that "Whoever has been born of God does not sin, for His seed remains in him" (1 John 3:9). A life that comes from God takes on its Father's likeness. Jesus asks the Father to keep His disciples from the evil one, while saying they are not of the world (John 17:15–16, paraphrased). They remain in the world, but their lives come from somewhere else.

The same preposition appears in the great commandment, which calls us to love God "with all your heart" (Mark 12:30; Luke 10:27). In Greek, that love comes out of the whole heart. God asks for love that springs from within, not love applied from the outside.

4. Reading Guidance

The English word "of" does not always mean possession. When it renders ek, it usually means "coming from" or "part of." The English word "by" can also hide different Greek words. In Romans 3:20 the first "by" renders ek, but the second, "by the law is the knowledge of sin," does not. Paul also uses other prepositions with faith, so do not build a doctrine on ek alone.
