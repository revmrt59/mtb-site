THAT, THOSE, HE - (ekeînos, Strong's G1565)

Summary

Ekeînos is the Greek word for "that" or "those." It points at a particular person, time, place, or thing and sets it apart from what lies closer at hand. Sometimes it modifies a noun, as in "that day" or "those days." Often it stands alone as "that one," and the NKJV then renders it "He," "She," or "they." It also fixes events to a precise moment, which the NKJV can render "the same day" or "that very hour." The word does not tell the reader who or what is meant. The context does that, and the context sometimes holds surprises. In John 1:8, "He was not that Light" refers to John the Baptist, not to Christ. In Jesus' words about the Spirit in John 14–16, the same pointer rests again and again on the One who will teach, guide, and glorify Christ. Reading well means asking each time, "Who is that one here?"

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Ekeînos singles something out and holds it at a little distance. The distance may be spatial ("that city"), temporal ("those days"), or simply a way of separating one person from another in the story. Greek has an ordinary word for "he," but ekeînos does more than stand in for a name. It directs the reader's attention: that man, that day, that one and not someone else. The strength of this pointing varies. In some places it marks real contrast or emphasis. In others it simply picks up a person already in view and moves the story forward. English often hides the word inside a plain "he" or "they," so it is worth noticing when the Greek is quietly pointing.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pointing to a particular time ("that day," "those days")

Narrative writers use the word to anchor a story in time: "And it came to pass in those days that a decree went out from Caesar Augustus" (Luke 2:1). The same construction can also point forward to a future day of reckoning: "Many will say to Me in that day, 'Lord, Lord'" (Matthew 7:22). In Matthew 7:22, the phrase does not need further explanation. Jesus' hearers know which day He means, the day when the Lord's verdict is final. Mark 13:32 uses the word the same way when it speaks of "that day and hour" that no one knows.

Standing alone as "that one" ("He," "She," "they")

Used without a noun, the word works like an emphatic personal pronoun. John 1:8 reads, "He was not that Light, but was sent to bear witness of that Light." The "He" here is ekeînos, and it points back to John the Baptist, introduced in verses 6–7. The phrase "that Light" in the same verse translates a different Greek expression. The pointer can also separate two people. In Luke 18:14, Jesus says the tax collector went home justified rather than "the other," meaning the Pharisee (paraphrase).

Marking the very same moment ("the same," "that very")

Attached to a word for time, ekeînos can tie events tightly together. "On the same day, when evening had come," Jesus told the disciples to cross the lake (Mark 4:35). After the Canaanite woman's persistent plea, "her daughter was healed from that very hour" (Matthew 15:28). In that verse, the NKJV phrase "that very hour" carries the word's force. The healing was not a gradual recovery. It is tied to the moment Jesus spoke.

3. Practical Reading Insight

In John's Gospel, this pointer word helps trace a movement away from the messenger toward Christ. In John 1:8 it is used of the Baptist in order to say what he is not. By John 3:28–30 it points to Jesus. The Baptist says he was sent before "Him," and that "He" must increase while he himself decreases (paraphrase). The witness steps back, and the pointer settles on the Son.

The same pattern continues in Jesus' promises about the Holy Spirit. "He will teach you all things" (John 14:26). "When He, the Spirit of truth, has come, He will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13). The word keeps pointing at the Spirit as the One who teaches, testifies (15:26), convicts (16:8), and glorifies Christ (16:14). What John's Gospel shows is a Spirit who acts, hears, speaks, and leads, and whose work directs attention back to Jesus. First John carries the pointer forward to Christ Himself. Believers are to walk just as "He" walked (1 John 2:6), and "He" laid down His life for us (1 John 3:16) (paraphrases).

4. Reading Guidance

"He" does not identify itself. In John 1:8 it means the Baptist, and in John 14:26 it means the Spirit, so the full context, not the nearest noun, decides who is meant. Some have argued that the masculine ekeînos for the Spirit is itself a grammatical proof of His personhood. However, the word can be explained by its link to the masculine noun "Helper." The Spirit's personhood rests more securely on what these chapters say He does.
