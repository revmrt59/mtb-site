UNTIL, TILL, TO, WHILE - (héōs, Strong's G2193)

Summary

Héōs is the Greek word that marks a limit. It tells the reader how far something goes, whether in time ("until," "till"), in space ("to the end of the earth"), or across a stretch of time during which something must happen ("while it is day"). Joined with a question word, it becomes the cry "How long?" Paying attention to héōs helps readers see where a statement begins and ends. It shows how long a command applies, how far a mission reaches, and how much time remains for an opportunity. Again and again it reminds us that the seasons of waiting in Scripture have edges God has already drawn.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Héōs sets a boundary. It can work as a connecting word that introduces a whole clause ("till it came and stood"), or it can come before a noun or phrase to mark a point reached ("to David"). Either way, it measures. Something continues up to a point, extends as far as a place, or lasts through a period.

The word itself does not tell the reader what happens on the far side of the boundary. It marks the line clearly, and the surrounding passage explains whether anything changes once the line is reached. Greek often pairs héōs with a short companion word to form an "until" clause. English usually handles all of this with one word, so the NKJV renders it in several ways: until, till, to, while, and how long.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Time up to an endpoint (until, till)

This is the word's most frequent use. In Matthew 2:9 the star "went before them, till it came and stood over where the young Child was." Héōs marks the moment the guiding stopped, because the star had reached its destination. Paul's instruction to Timothy works the same way: "Till I come, give attention to reading, to exhortation, to doctrine" (1 Timothy 4:13). Paul is not giving temporary advice. He is assigning Timothy his duties for the interval before his arrival.

Extent in space, sequence, or degree (to)

Héōs can also mark the farthest point something reaches. Jesus tells His disciples they will be witnesses in Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, "and to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8). The word means "all the way to," and it leaves no region beyond the reach of the mission. In Matthew 1:17 it measures a history: "from Abraham to David... from David until the captivity in Babylon... and from the captivity in Babylon until the Christ." Each héōs closes off one stage, and the final one arrives at Jesus. The word can also mark degree. In Gethsemane, Jesus describes His soul as sorrowful even to the point of death (Matthew 26:38, paraphrased).

The span during which something happens (while)

Sometimes héōs looks at the whole stretch of time before a limit rather than the limit itself. Jesus says, "I must work the works of Him who sent Me while it is day; the night is coming when no one can work" (John 9:4). The daylight is a window that will close. In Matthew 5:25 the counsel is to settle with an adversary "quickly, while you are on the way with him," before the courtroom ends the chance to make peace.

"How long?" (héōs pote)

Héōs combined with the word for "when" asks where a situation will end. Jesus' exasperation in Matthew 17:17 comes through this phrase: "how long shall I be with you? How long shall I bear with you?" The souls under the altar cry out the same way in Revelation 6:10: "How long, O Lord, holy and true, until You judge and avenge our blood...?"

3. Practical Reading Insight

Because héōs marks limits, it often carries the hope of Scripture. The martyrs' "How long?" in Revelation 6:10 receives an answer in the very next verse. They are told to rest a little while longer, until the number of their fellow servants is complete (Revelation 6:11, paraphrased). Their question used héōs, and God's reply uses it too. Their suffering has an endpoint that God knows.

The same pattern runs through the Psalm 110 promise that the New Testament quotes repeatedly: the Father tells the Son to sit at His right hand "till" His enemies are made His footstool (Matthew 22:44; Acts 2:35; Hebrews 1:13). Hebrews 10:13 pictures Christ, His sacrifice finished, waiting until that day arrives (paraphrased). The church lives inside that "until." The present resistance to Christ's rule is real, but it has a limit, and the One seated at God's right hand is not anxious about how it ends.

John 9:4 adds a sober counterpart. Some windows close, and the time to do the Father's work is now.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume "until" automatically means things change afterward. Jesus promises to be with His disciples "to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20, paraphrased), and His presence plainly does not stop then. In Matthew 1:25, "till" establishes that Joseph had no union with Mary before Jesus' birth, which confirms the virgin conception. Questions about what came afterward must be settled from other passages, not from this word alone. Prophetic sayings such as Matthew 16:28 also call for careful reading of the surrounding context before anyone fixes the boundary the word marks.
