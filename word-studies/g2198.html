LIVE, LIVING, ALIVE - (záō, Strong's G2198)

Summary

The Greek verb záō means "to live" or "to be alive." It can describe ordinary physical life, such as a sick boy who recovers when Jesus says, "Your son lives" (John 4:50). It can also describe how a person conducts that life, and for whom. In the New Testament, though, the word reaches higher than biology. God is "the living God," the One "who lives forever and ever." The risen Jesus is the One who "was dead, and behold, I am alive forevermore." Believers are people who now live by faith, live to God, and live because Christ lives in them.

This one verb links several realities. The life of God stands in contrast to dead idols. The risen Christ lives and keeps interceding for His people. Through His Word and Spirit, that life reaches into believers and changes how they live day to day. Even physical death cannot cancel it. To study záō is to see that the gospel brings people out of death and into the life of God Himself.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Záō is a common Greek verb used throughout the New Testament, from the Gospels to Revelation. It is especially frequent in Romans, John, Revelation, Acts, and Hebrews. Its close relative is the noun zōē, "life." The noun names life itself, while the verb describes someone actually being alive, or the way that life is lived.

The word turns up in very different settings: healing stories, legal discussions about marriage (Rom 7:1–3), sermons to pagans about idols, Paul's teaching on union with Christ, and the worship scenes around God's throne. In NKJV the form "living" often renders the participle used as a description, as in "living God," "living water," or "living hope."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, záō means to be alive rather than dead. Context determines what kind of life is in view.

1. To be physically alive, or to stay alive. This sense covers survival, recovery, and natural lifespan. The nobleman's son "lives" (John 4:50), and Anna "lived" with her husband seven years (Luke 2:36).

2. To possess life in its full, God-given sense. This is life that belongs to God, comes from Him, and outlasts death. In this sense the righteous "live" by faith (Rom 1:17) and the one who believes in Jesus will live even though he dies (John 11:25).

3. To conduct one's life in a certain way or toward a certain end. This is life as conduct and allegiance. The Colossians once "lived" in sinful practices (Col 3:7), and grace teaches believers to live soberly, righteously, and godly (Titus 2:12).

4. Living, as a description of something that has or gives life. The word can describe God, water, bread, a hope, or a stone, such as "the living God" (1 Thess 1:9) or "living water" (John 4:10).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The Gospels and Acts often use záō for physical life restored or proven. Jairus begs Jesus to come so his daughter will live (Mark 5:23). After the resurrection, Jesus "presented Himself alive after His suffering by many infallible proofs" (Acts 1:3). The angels at the tomb ask why the women seek the living among the dead (Luke 24:5).

Paul's letters move the word into the realm of spiritual life and conduct. One construction is worth noticing: living to someone. Christ's life is a life He "lives to God" (Rom 6:10). Believers are "alive to God" (Rom 6:11). "If we live, we live to the Lord" (Rom 14:8). This way of speaking adds direction and ownership to the idea of being alive. It names the Person a life belongs to.

Hebrews and Revelation keep returning to the life of God and the risen Christ. Revelation's repeated title for God, "Him who lives forever and ever" (Rev 4:9–10; 10:6; 15:7), is used in worship, in solemn oaths, and in judgment.

4. Theological Significance

The most basic truth this word reveals is that God is alive. When the Thessalonians "turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God" (1 Thess 1:9), the contrast was sharp. The idols they left could not hear, speak, or act. The God they turned to does all three. Peter's confession that Jesus is "the Christ, the Son of the living God" (Matt 16:16) and Jesus' reminder that God is the God of the living (Matt 22:32) rest on this same reality.

Because God lives, His presence is never neutral. The church is "the temple of the living God," and He promises, "I will dwell in them and walk among them" (2 Cor 6:16). Yet Hebrews warns against "an evil heart of unbelief in departing from the living God" (Heb 3:12) and says plainly, "It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God" (Heb 10:31). The God who lives is near enough to save and real enough to judge. In Revelation, the same title that receives the elders' worship stands over the bowls "full of the wrath of God who lives forever and ever" (Rev 15:7).

God's life also flows into His Word. Hebrews calls the word of God living and powerful, able to pierce and discern the heart (Heb 4:12). Stephen calls the law given at Sinai "living oracles" (Acts 7:38). Peter says believers are born again through the word of God which lives and abides forever (1 Pet 1:23). Scripture carries the vitality of the God who spoke it, and it still gives new life today.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Revelation 1:18 and Hebrews 7:25: the life that cannot end. The risen Jesus tells John, "I am He who lives, and was dead, and behold, I am alive forevermore... And I have the keys of Hades and of Death." His death is a real past event. His life is a permanent present reality. Paul makes the same point when he says Christ died to sin "once for all; but the life that He lives, He lives to God" (Rom 6:10). Hebrews draws out what that unending life means for His people: "He is also able to save to the uttermost those who come to God through Him, since He always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25). Earthly priests died and had to be replaced. This Priest's life will never be interrupted again, so His work on behalf of His people never stops. A believer's standing before God does not depend on how strong that believer feels today. It rests on a living Savior who is speaking for them right now.

Galatians 2:20: the exchanged life. Paul writes that he has been crucified with Christ, and that it is no longer he who lives; Christ lives in him, and the life he now lives in the flesh he lives by faith in the Son of God, who loved him and gave Himself for him. The verb appears again and again in that one verse. The old, self-directed Paul has died with Christ. The life that continues is still lived in an ordinary body, yet its source is the risen Christ dwelling within. Romans 6 asks the same question from the other side: "How shall we who died to sin live any longer in it?" (Rom 6:2). Christian living, then, is more than imitating Jesus from a distance. It is His own life at work in a person who has surrendered.

John 4 and 6: living water and living bread. Jesus offers the Samaritan woman "living water" (John 4:10), and in John 7:38–39 that water is identified with the Spirit flowing from within believers. In John 6 He calls Himself "the living bread which came down from heaven" and says that as He lives because of the living Father, the one who feeds on Him will live because of Him (John 6:51, 57). The chain runs from the Father to the Son and from the Son to the believer. What Jesus gives is Himself, a Person who imparts life.

John 11:25–26: life that death cannot cancel. Standing near Lazarus's tomb, Jesus tells Martha that whoever believes in Him will live, even though he dies, and whoever lives and believes in Him will never die. Physical death is real, and Jesus does not deny it. But it cannot sever the life He gives.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Habakkuk's word that the just shall live by faith (Hab 2:4) is quoted three times in the New Testament, in Romans 1:17, Galatians 3:11, and Hebrews 10:38. In Romans and Galatians, the quotation shows how a person comes into right standing and life before God. In Hebrews, it calls believers to keep enduring until Christ comes. Faith is how life begins, and faith is how it continues.

The whole story turns on Christ's resurrection. He "died and rose and lived again, that He might be Lord of both the dead and the living" (Rom 14:9). Because He lives, Paul can say that whether we live or die, "we are the Lord's" (Rom 14:8). Paul also writes that Christ "lives by the power of God," and "we shall live with Him by the power of God" (2 Cor 13:4). The story ends with martyrs who were killed for their witness, yet "lived and reigned with Christ" (Rev 20:4). The Lamb leads His people to living fountains of waters (Rev 7:17), which completes what Jesus promised at the well in Samaria.

7. Spirit & Life

Paul says Christ "died for all, that those who live should live no longer for themselves, but for Him who died for them and rose again" (2 Cor 5:15). The question this word presses on a believer is less "Am I alive?" and more "Whom am I living to?" "None of us lives to himself" (Rom 14:7). New life comes with a new owner.

That ownership shows up in conduct. If believers live according to the flesh, Paul warns, they will die, but if by the Spirit they put to death the deeds of the body, they will live (Rom 8:13). Grace trains believers to live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age (Titus 2:12). Paul also asks the Colossians why, having died with Christ, they keep living as though they still belong to the world (Col 2:20). The life Christ gives produces a recognizable way of living.

Scripture also exposes a counterfeit. The church at Sardis had a name for being alive while it was dead (Rev 3:1). A woman who lives for pleasure is dead while she lives (1 Tim 5:6). A person can be busy, admired, and outwardly religious, yet have no life from God. The answer is to come back to the living Christ, who "always lives" for those who come to God through Him, and to His Word, which still lives and abides.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 4:4 — Jesus answers the tempter that man shall not live by bread alone but by every word from God's mouth. Real life depends on God speaking.

  • Luke 15:13 — The prodigal wastes his possessions with "prodigal living." The same verb describes a life spent far from the Father.

  • Acts 17:28 — Paul tells the Athenians that in God "we live and move and have our being." Even ordinary human existence depends on Him.

  • 1 Peter 2:4–5 — Christ is the living stone, and believers become living stones built into a spiritual house. His life gives life to His people.

  • 1 John 4:9 — God sent His only begotten Son into the world so that we might live through Him. Life is the stated purpose of His sending.

  • Revelation 13:14 — The beast that was wounded by the sword "lived," a deceptive imitation of resurrection. It stands in contrast with the true Christ "who was dead, and came to life" (Rev 2:8).
