LIFE - (zōḗ, Strong's G2222)

Summary

The Greek word zōḗ means "life": the state of being alive, and sometimes the span of a person's earthly days. The New Testament seldom leaves it at that. Most of its uses describe the life that belongs to God Himself, which He gives to those who belong to His Son. It appears in Jesus' call to "enter into life," in John's frequent phrase "eternal life," and in Paul's contrast between the reign of death and the reign of life. Revelation uses it for the final gifts of God's kingdom: the tree of life, the water of life, and the Book of Life.

The central discovery is that Scripture identifies this life with a Person. "In Him was life" (John 1:4). Jesus calls Himself "the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25), and John concludes, "He who has the Son has life" (1 John 5:12). Eternal life is not something God hands out separately from Himself. It begins now as knowing the Father and the Son, it is nourished by Christ and His words, the Spirit makes it active against sin, and it shows itself even through suffering. It will be complete when mortality is swallowed up and God's people drink freely from the river of life.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Zōḗ is a common Greek noun for life. Greek had other words in this area. Bios usually refers to the course of daily living or one's livelihood. Psuchē often means a person's natural life or soul. Zōḗ can refer simply to physical existence, but the New Testament writers chose it again and again to speak of the life that comes from God.

The word is concentrated in John's writings: his Gospel, his first letter, and Revelation account for nearly half of its New Testament uses. Paul uses it often in Romans 5–8. In Matthew, Mark, and Luke it most often describes the life of the coming kingdom, which a person must "enter."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Physical life or lifetime: a person's existence and the length of their days on earth. Abraham tells the rich man, "in your lifetime you received your good things" (Luke 16:25). James asks what our life is and compares it to a vapor (James 4:14).

2. The life God gives: life as God's own gift, coming from Him and lasting into the age to come. This is the sense in "eternal life" (John 3:16; Romans 6:23) and in the promise of entering life (Matthew 18:8).

The second sense remains the ordinary word "life." What makes it rich is what the surrounding passages reveal about its Source and character.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Synoptic Gospels, zōḗ is something a person enters or inherits. The rich young ruler asks what he must do "that I may have eternal life" (Matthew 19:16; Mark 10:17). Jesus says the gate is narrow and the way difficult "which leads to life" (Matthew 7:14). In Matthew 18:8–9 He says it is better to enter life lame or with one eye than to be thrown whole into everlasting fire. The point of this deliberate exaggeration is the value of what is at stake. Nothing a person keeps is worth losing this life.

Two patterns help a reader. First, zōḗ is constantly paired with "eternal" or "everlasting," a sign that the life described belongs to the age to come. Second, it often appears in phrases built with "of life": the bread of life, the Spirit of life, the crown of life, the Book of Life, the tree of life, the water of life. In each case the noun names what life comes from, or what belongs to the life God gives. Throughout Scripture, life stands directly opposite death: "the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life" (Romans 6:23).

4. Theological Significance

Scripture refuses to treat this life as a commodity God measures out. It identifies life with God Himself and with His Son. The Father has life in Himself and has granted the Son to have life in Himself as well (John 5:26). John opens his Gospel with "In Him was life, and the life was the light of men" (John 1:4). Jesus does not say He brings the way, the truth, and the life. He says, "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). At Lazarus's tomb He tells Martha, "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25), and says this before He calls the dead man out.

That is why John can draw such a clear line: "He who has the Son has life; he who does not have the Son of God does not have life" (1 John 5:12). Paul says the same in his own way, calling Christ "our life" (Colossians 3:4). To receive eternal life is to receive Christ. No one has life apart from Him.

The opposite condition is spelled out just as plainly. Paul describes people outside of Christ as alienated from the life of God because of the blindness of their hearts (Ephesians 4:18). Human beings cannot produce this life or sustain it on their own. It is received as a gift, "in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 6:23).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

John 5:24 and 17:3: life now, and life as knowing God. Jesus says that whoever hears His word and believes "has everlasting life, and shall not come into judgment, but has passed from death into life" (John 5:24). The believer does not merely hope to receive this life someday. The crossing from death to life has already happened. In His prayer Jesus defines what that life is: "this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent" (John 17:3). Endless existence alone does not make eternal life, since even the condemned exist forever. Eternal life is knowing the Father and the Son. Its length matters, but its heart is a relationship.

John 6: life that must be fed. The word appears eleven times in this chapter. After feeding the crowd, Jesus contrasts the manna in the wilderness, which could not keep the fathers from dying, with Himself, the bread of God who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world (John 6:33). He calls Himself the bread of life (6:35, 48). Then He presses further: unless they eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink His blood, they have no life in them (6:53). When many are offended, He explains that it is the Spirit who gives life, and that the words He speaks are spirit and life (6:63). Peter grasps the point. When others walk away, he answers that there is no one else to go to, because Jesus has the words of eternal life (6:68). Life in Christ is not a single transaction that then runs on its own. It is received and kept by continually trusting Him and taking in His words, as a person needs bread every day.

Romans 5–8: two reigns. Paul presents death and life as rival kings. Through Adam's offense "death reigned," but those who receive grace "will reign in life through the One, Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:17). Sin reigned in death, and grace reigns through righteousness to eternal life (5:21). This life is more than a legal status. "The law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has made me free from the law of sin and death" (8:2). The Holy Spirit is the one who makes Christ's life active in the believer, which is why Paul can say, "to be carnally minded is death, but to be spiritually minded is life and peace" (8:6). Even while the body remains subject to death, the Spirit is life because of righteousness (8:10).

2 Corinthians 4:10–12; 5:4: life shown through weakness. Paul describes his ministry as constantly carrying in his body the dying of the Lord Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may be revealed in his mortal body (4:10–11). He then draws a conclusion his readers must have felt keenly: death is at work in him, but life in them (4:12). God did not make His apostle impressive so that the Corinthians could see Christ's life. He displayed that life through Paul's hardships, and the Corinthians received life as a result. Paul's hope is not to escape the body but to be clothed with the new one, so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life (5:4).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Bible's story of life begins in a garden with a tree of life, lost when humanity reached for life apart from God's word. It ends in a city with the same tree restored. The Genesis account is written in Hebrew, but Revelation deliberately picks up its imagery with zōḗ. The risen Christ promises the overcomer in Ephesus the right to eat from the tree of life in the Paradise of God (Revelation 2:7). In the final vision, a pure river of the water of life flows from the throne of God and of the Lamb. The tree of life grows on both sides of it, and its leaves are for the healing of the nations (22:1–2). The way back to the tree, once guarded, is open, and God offers the water of life freely to the thirsty (21:6; 22:17).

Between Eden and the New Jerusalem stands Christ. John writes that "the life was manifested" and that the apostles had seen, heard, and handled "the Word of life" (1 John 1:1–2). Peter accuses Jerusalem of having "killed the Prince of life, whom God raised from the dead" (Acts 3:15). The One who is life entered death and came out the other side. Paul says Christ abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel (2 Timothy 1:10). Hebrews says His priesthood rests on the power of an endless life (Hebrews 7:16).

Revelation also makes the Book of Life the final dividing line. Christ promises not to blot out the overcomer's name (Revelation 3:5). Anyone not found written in it is cast into the lake of fire (20:15), and only those written in the Lamb's Book of Life enter the city (21:27). The story that began with life forfeited ends with life secured by the Lamb.

7. Spirit & Life

The life believers have now and the life they will have forever are the same life. Jesus says the believer already has everlasting life (John 5:24), yet He also speaks of entering life at the end (Matthew 18:8), and Revelation still holds out the river and the tree. Paul holds both together in Colossians: "your life is hidden with Christ in God. When Christ who is our life appears, then you also will appear with Him in glory" (Colossians 3:3–4). The life is real today, but it is hidden. Its full display waits for His appearing. This keeps a believer from despair when present experience feels thin, and from complacency when it feels full.

Jesus also states plainly how this life is gained: "He who loves his life will lose it, and he who hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life" (John 12:25). The first "life" translates psuchē, the natural, self-directed life. Only the last is zōḗ. Jesus is not asking anyone to despise God's gift of existence. He is setting a life organized around self against the life that comes from God, and calling His followers to let go of the first to keep the second. Mark 8:35 makes the same demand using psuchē. The severe images of Matthew 18 belong here too. Whatever must be cut away is small next to entering life.

Because life is a Person, assurance rests on Him. John wrote his letter so "that you may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13), and the ground he gives is the Son, not the strength of our feelings. Because this life is fed by Christ's words (John 6:63), staying near Him through His word is how it stays healthy. Because the Spirit is the Spirit of life, the struggle against sin is fought with the power of life already given, not only with resolve.

Paul's experience in 2 Corinthians 4 changes how a believer can view weakness. Illness, loss, and the hard costs of serving others are not proof that Christ's life is absent. They are often the very places where His life becomes visible and reaches other people.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 12:15 — Jesus warns that a person's life does not consist in the abundance of possessions. Life cannot be measured by what one owns.

  • Acts 17:25 — Paul tells the Athenians that God gives to all life, breath, and all things. Even ordinary physical life is His gift.

  • James 1:12 — "he will receive the crown of life which the Lord has promised to those who love Him." Life is the reward of those who endure trial out of love for God.

  • 1 Peter 3:7 — Husbands and wives are heirs together of the grace of life, which gives a reason to honor one another.

  • Revelation 2:10 — The suffering church in Smyrna is told to be faithful until death, and Christ will give the crown of life. The promise of life is set directly against the threat of death.

  • Revelation 11:11 — After three and a half days, the breath of life from God enters the two slain witnesses and they stand on their feet, a vivid picture of God's power to raise the dead.
