WE - (hēmeîs, Strong's G2249)

Summary

Hēmeîs is the Greek word for "we" when it stands as the subject of a sentence. Greek verbs already show who is acting, so a writer could say "we preach" without adding a separate word for "we." When hēmeîs does appear, the writer has usually chosen to put the group itself in the spotlight. Most often he is setting "us" against someone else, as when the Pharisees tell the healed man, "You are His disciple, but we are Moses' disciples" (John 9:28). It also gathers people into a shared witness, as when Peter says of the risen Jesus, "we are all witnesses" (Acts 2:32), or places the speaker's own group inside a claim. English readers cannot see this choice, because English needs "we" before every verb. Noticing it shows where a writer is drawing lines, who stands behind a testimony, and who is included in a confession.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

In Greek, a verb ending normally tells the reader who is acting. When Paul writes "we preach," the verb alone already says "we." The separate pronoun hēmeîs is therefore not needed for basic meaning, and its presence usually marks focus. It can sharpen a contrast with another party, show who is speaking for a group, or turn attention to the writer's group in a story.

Some care is needed. Not every added pronoun is a trumpet blast. Sometimes it simply makes clear who is acting, or it supports a phrase like "we also." The surrounding sentence shows how much weight it carries. This entry covers only the subject form. The forms that English renders "us" and "our" belong to other Strong's numbers, even though the NKJV occasionally renders hēmeîs itself within "let us" or "we ourselves" (for example, 1 Thessalonians 5:8 and Titus 3:3).

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Drawing a contrast between groups ("we," "but we")

This is the most visible use. In John 9:28 the religious leaders say, "You are His disciple, but we are Moses' disciples." The pronoun draws a line between the healed man's loyalty and their own. Paul uses the same device in 1 Corinthians 1:23: "but we preach Christ crucified, to the Jews a stumbling block and to the Greeks foolishness." The verses just before describe Jews asking for signs and Greeks seeking wisdom, and the pronoun sets the apostles' message apart from both. In 1 Corinthians 4:10 Paul uses the pronoun three times in quick succession. He says, in substance, that the apostles are fools, weak, and dishonored, while the Corinthians think themselves wise, strong, and honored. The repeated "we" against "you" carries his irony.

Standing behind a shared testimony ("we are witnesses," "we have known")

In Acts, the apostles use hēmeîs to show that their message rests on what they themselves saw. Peter says, "This Jesus God has raised up, of which we are all witnesses" (Acts 2:32). Before the council, Peter and John say, in effect, that they cannot stop speaking about what they have seen and heard (Acts 4:20). John writes in the same way: "And we have known and believed the love that God has for us" (1 John 4:16). Here the pronoun points to people who received God's love and believed it, not to a theory about love.

Including oneself in a claim ("we too," "we ourselves")

Sometimes the pronoun's job is to place the speaker inside a statement. In Galatians 2:15–16, Paul reminds Peter that even they, Jews by birth, had to believe in Christ Jesus to be justified. The "we" removes any exception for people with the right background. In Titus 3:3, Paul writes that he and his readers were themselves once foolish and disobedient. The apostle does not stand above the sinners he describes.

Tracking who acts in a narrative

In story material, the pronoun can mark a change of actors. Acts 20:5 says that some companions went ahead to Troas. Verse 6 continues, "But we sailed away from Philippi after the Days of Unleavened Bread, and in five days joined them at Troas, where we stayed seven days." The opening "But we" separates the narrator's group from those who went ahead. It also resumes the first-person travel account that runs through these chapters of Acts.

3. Practical Reading Insight

First John 4 shows how much this pronoun can carry. In verse 10, John says that love consists not in our loving God, but in His loving us and sending His Son as the atoning sacrifice for our sins. The emphatic "we" is set against God's own action, and the contrast puts the starting point of love entirely with Him. Verse 19 states the order plainly: we love because He first loved us. The "we" of verse 16 then reads as the voice of people who have been on the receiving end of that love and have believed it.

First Corinthians 1:23 offers a second payoff. Paul's "but we" does not tell believers to look down on their neighbors. It tells them where they stand. When the world asks for signs or clever arguments, the church's answer is a crucified Christ, and Paul names his group by that message.

4. Reading Guidance

Not every "we" in an English Bible carries special weight, and not every hēmeîs is strongly emphatic. When the pronoun is present, ask whom "we" is being set against or gathered with. Ask also whom "we" includes. In the letters it may mean Paul alone, Paul and his co-senders, or all believers. Context must decide, and in some passages faithful readers disagree.
