BUT, ON THE CONTRARY, NEVERTHELESS, YES - (allá, Strong's G0235)

Summary

Allá is the New Testament's strong "but." It usually sets aside one idea and puts another in its place. Most often it follows a negative: "not this, but that." When Jesus says, "I did not come to destroy but to fulfill" (Matthew 5:17), the word clears away a false idea and names the true one. Allá can also press a statement forward despite an obstacle, which the NKJV often renders "nevertheless" or "yet." Less often it adds a stronger point, translated "yes" or "indeed." Reading it well means finishing the sentence. The negative half clears the ground, and the clause after allá usually carries the writer's real point.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Allá joins two thoughts that cannot stand together on equal terms. The first is denied, limited, or overcome, and the second takes priority. It is sharper than the common Greek connector dé (G1161). Dé often moves a story along gently and can mean "and," "now," or a soft "but." Allá corrects.

This is why it so often pairs with a negative. A speaker rules something out and then tells you what is true instead. Not every use is a correction, though. Sometimes allá holds firm against a difficulty, and sometimes it raises the stakes of what was just said. In each case, the context shows which way the contrast runs.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Correction and replacement ("not … but")

NKJV: but, on the contrary, rather

This is the most common use. Jesus says, "I did not come to destroy but to fulfill" (Matthew 5:17). Some might assume His teaching overturned the Law and the Prophets. Jesus rejects that idea and names His actual purpose. In Romans 3:31, Paul asks, "Do we then make void the law through faith? Certainly not! On the contrary, we establish the law." Here allá stands behind "contrary." Paul does more than deny the charge. He puts the opposite truth in its place.

Holding firm despite a difficulty

NKJV: nevertheless, yet, however

In this use, allá introduces what remains true or what must be done even though something else has just been said. In Gethsemane Jesus prays, "Take this cup away from Me; nevertheless, not what I will, but what You will" (Mark 14:36). The word appears twice in that verse. The first time, it turns from His honest request to His surrender. The second time, it sets the Father's will in place of His own.

Paul uses the same pattern in 1 Corinthians 9:12. He had a real right to financial support from the Corinthians, and he says so: "Nevertheless we have not used this right, but endure all things lest we hinder the gospel of Christ." The right was real, yet he chose not to use it, and his reason was the gospel.

Adding a stronger point

NKJV: yes, indeed, what

Occasionally allá does not oppose what came before. Instead, it climbs higher. Jesus warns, "They will put you out of the synagogues; yes, the time is coming that whoever kills you will think that he offers God service" (John 16:2). Being thrown out of the synagogue is serious, but something worse is coming.

In 2 Corinthians 7:11, Paul uses allá six times in a row to pile up the results of the Corinthians' godly sorrow. The NKJV renders it with the repeated "what": "What diligence it produced in you, what clearing of yourselves, what indignation, what fear, what vehement desire, what zeal, what vindication!" The repetition builds the list toward a climax.

3. Practical Reading Insight

When you meet "not … but," read the second half as the destination. Many readers absorb the denial and miss the replacement. In Matthew 5:17, the denial guards against a misunderstanding, while "to fulfill" tells us what Jesus came to do. In Romans 3:31, Paul is not content to say faith does no harm to the law. He claims that faith establishes it.

Gethsemane shows the word at its most searching. In Mark 14:36, Jesus does not hide what He asks for, and allá does not cancel the request. It shows where His request yields. "Nevertheless" marks the turn, and "not what I will, but what You will" names the replacement. Here obedience does not pretend there is no struggle. It brings the struggle to the Father and gives Him the final word. Paul's choice in 1 Corinthians 9:12 follows the same pattern on a smaller scale. A legitimate right gives way to a greater good, the gospel of Christ.

4. Reading Guidance

English "but" can stand for several Greek words, including the milder dé. You cannot tell from the English alone whether allá is present. Renderings such as "nevertheless" or "on the contrary" do not guarantee it either, because other Greek words share those translations. Let the sentence decide whether allá is correcting, holding firm, or intensifying. A single "but" also cannot carry an entire doctrine. It shows how two statements relate, and the teaching itself comes from what those statements say.
