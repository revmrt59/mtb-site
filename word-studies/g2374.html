DOOR, GATE - (thýra, Strong's G2374)

Summary

Thýra is the ordinary Greek word for a door in the New Testament. It names the entrance of a house, an inner room, a courtyard, a sheepfold, a prison, the temple, or a tomb cut from rock. A door decides who comes in and who stays out, and Scripture uses this everyday object to teach about access to God. At the center of the word stands Jesus' own claim: "I am the door of the sheep" (John 10:7). Jesus Himself is the Door, the entrance God has given into salvation and into the presence of the Father. "If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved, and will go in and out and find pasture" (John 10:9). There is no way in around Him. Those who come through Him find rescue, safety, and daily provision under His care.

The Lord who is the Door also governs every other door that matters. God opens doors for the gospel that opposition cannot close, so Paul asks the church to pray "that God would open to us a door for the word" (Colossians 4:3). The risen Christ sets before a weak church "an open door, and no one can shut it" (Revelation 3:8). He also sets the final boundary. When the bridegroom came, "the door was shut" (Matthew 25:10), and no amount of knocking opened it again. Between the open door and the shut one stands a tender scene: Christ outside His own lukewarm church, saying, "I stand at the door and knock," and offering to come in and share a meal (Revelation 3:20). The word gives believers both security and urgency. Christ is the only way in, He holds the keys, and today the door is still open.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Thýra is a common Greek noun for a door or doorway, and most of its New Testament uses are entirely ordinary. The whole town of Capernaum gathers "at the door" of the house where Jesus is staying (Mark 1:33). A neighbor in bed will not get up because his door is already shut (Luke 11:7). Peter waits outside the door of the high priest's courtyard (John 18:16). Prison doors are locked and guarded in Acts, the temple doors are slammed behind Paul (Acts 21:30), and at Jesus' burial a stone is rolled "against the door of the tomb" (Mark 15:46). The word appears in the Gospels, Acts, Paul's letters, James, and Revelation.

These concrete scenes prepare the reader for the figurative uses. A door is either open or shut, and someone always decides which. That simple fact runs through the whole word. In nearly every passage the question is who stands at the door and who controls it.

Thýra is the door of a building, room, fold, or tomb. Greek uses a different word for the great gates of a city, as in "the gates of Hades" (Matthew 16:18). The two images share the general idea of a boundary. Still, the Old Testament picture of city gates, where elders heard cases and defenders held back armies, should not be read into thýra. The NKJV translates thýra as "gate" only once, for the temple entrance called Beautiful (Acts 3:2).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Thýra means a door or entrance, the opening and the barrier that control passage in or out. In use it carries three closely related senses:

1. A literal door or doorway of a house, room, courtyard, sheepfold, prison, temple, or tomb. Joseph "rolled a large stone against the door of the tomb" (Matthew 27:60).

2. A figurative entrance or opening, meaning access or opportunity granted for something to go forward. Paul speaks of "a door for the word" (Colossians 4:3), and God "had opened the door of faith to the Gentiles" (Acts 14:27). Jesus' words "I am the door" (John 10:9) draw on this sense. He uses the picture of an entrance to describe Himself as the way in.

3. "At the door(s)" as an expression of nearness. Something standing at the door is about to arrive: "know that it is near—at the doors!" (Matthew 24:33).

The word itself simply means "door." What makes it weighty is what Jesus and the apostles teach through it, especially the claim of John 10 that a Person is the entrance into life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of a door changes with the one who opens or shuts it. When people shut doors, they show their fear, reluctance, or hostility. The disciples lock themselves in "for fear of the Jews" (John 20:19). The neighbor at midnight protests that "the door is now shut" (Luke 11:7). The temple guardians shut the doors behind Paul as the crowd drags him out (Acts 21:30). When God or Christ opens or shuts a door, the action shows His authority. His word goes forward, or the matter is settled.

A few patterns of wording bring this out. Paul writes that "a door was opened to me by the Lord" (2 Corinthians 2:12), naming the Lord as the One who opened it. Christ describes Himself as "He who opens and no one shuts, and shuts and no one opens" (Revelation 3:7). John sees "a door standing open in heaven" (Revelation 4:1). It is open before John is ever invited through it, and he did not open it.

The plural "at the doors" is an idiom for something very near, standing right at the entrance (Matthew 24:33; Mark 13:29). James uses the same plural picture for the Judge Himself, though the NKJV renders it in the singular: "Behold, the Judge is standing at the door!" (James 5:9).

4. Theological Significance

God governs access, and He does so through Christ. The risen Lord holds "the key of David" (Revelation 3:7). Paul never claims to have forced a door open. He reports what God opened and asks the churches to pray for what God would open. Throughout this word, entrance into God's presence, into His kingdom, and into fruitful service is God's to grant.

God provides the entrance Himself. The door into salvation is not a set of rules or a rank of religious leaders. It is the Son: "I am the door" (John 10:9). Paul describes the same access with the Father, Son, and Spirit all involved: "For through Him we both have access by one Spirit to the Father" (Ephesians 2:18). The God who is approached also provides the way to approach Him and gives the Spirit by whom His people come. A door exists to be walked through, and God has set one before the world.

Human doors do not hold, and God's final door cannot be forced. The doors people shut against God's purposes give way. Prison doors open at night (Acts 5:19). A sealed tomb is opened (Matthew 28:2). A locked room cannot keep the risen Christ from His frightened disciples (John 20:19). Yet the door the Master shuts at the end does not reopen for any pleading (Luke 13:25). This pattern teaches both the limits of human power and the seriousness of God's patience. His invitation is generous, and it has an end.

The Judge is near. Jesus said that when the signs appear, "it is near—at the doors!" (Mark 13:29). James draws a pointed lesson: "Do not grumble against one another, brethren, lest you be condemned. Behold, the Judge is standing at the door!" (James 5:9). The Judge is close enough to hear what believers say about one another. Readiness for His coming therefore includes how we treat each other today.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

John 10:1–10: The Door of the Sheep. Jesus begins with a sheepfold, and at first the door works as a test. "He who does not enter the sheepfold by the door, but climbs up some other way, the same is a thief and a robber. But he who enters by the door is the shepherd of the sheep" (10:1–2). The true shepherd comes by the proper entrance. "To him the doorkeeper opens, and the sheep hear his voice; and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out" (10:3). Anyone who reaches God's people by going around God's appointed way has stolen his access, whatever title he holds. This warning comes right after John 9, where religious leaders had thrown out a man Jesus healed, so it had real faces attached.

The listeners did not understand (10:6), so Jesus made the picture personal: "Most assuredly, I say to you, I am the door of the sheep" (10:7). The door is now more than the right way for a shepherd to approach the flock. Jesus Himself is the entrance through which the sheep come into life: "I am the door. If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved, and will go in and out and find pasture" (10:9). The promise has three parts. The one who enters "will be saved," rescued from what threatens him. He "will go in and out," which describes the settled freedom of a flock that is secure. Elsewhere Scripture speaks of going out and coming in as the ordinary life of God's people under a faithful leader, so that they are not like sheep without a shepherd (Numbers 27:17). And he will "find pasture," meaning daily nourishment from the One who brought him in.

John 10 also makes the door the place of protection, and it does so in its own words. "The thief does not come except to steal, and to kill, and to destroy. I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly" (10:10). In the next breath Jesus calls Himself the good shepherd who "gives His life for the sheep" (10:11). The passage does not need an outside custom to join entrance and guardianship. Jesus is both the door and the shepherd. The way into the fold is guarded by Someone who lays down His life to keep what is inside.

The rest of John's Gospel and Paul's letters say where this door leads. "I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through Me" (John 14:6). "For through Him we both have access by one Spirit to the Father" (Ephesians 2:18). Entering by Christ is far more than entering a religious community. It means coming home to the Father, brought in by the Son and enabled by the Spirit.

Matthew 25:10 and Luke 13:25: The Shut Door. In the parable of the virgins, "those who were ready went in with him to the wedding; and the door was shut" (Matthew 25:10). The others arrive later: "Lord, Lord, open to us!" The answer comes: "Assuredly, I say to you, I do not know you" (25:11–12). Jesus draws the lesson Himself: "Watch therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour in which the Son of Man is coming" (25:13).

Luke sharpens the scene. "When once the Master of the house has risen up and shut the door, and you begin to stand outside and knock at the door, saying, 'Lord, Lord, open for us,' and He will answer and say to you, 'I do not know you, where you are from'" (Luke 13:25). Those outside protest, "We ate and drank in Your presence, and You taught in our streets" (13:26). Eating near Jesus and hearing His teaching had not made them His. Earlier Jesus had promised, "knock, and it will be opened to you" (Luke 11:9). Here the knocking comes too late. Both statements are true. The invitation is generous now, and it will end. That is why Paul pleads, "Behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Corinthians 6:2).

Acts 14:27; 1 Corinthians 16:9; 2 Corinthians 2:12; Colossians 4:3; Revelation 3:7–8: Doors Opened for the Gospel. When Paul and Barnabas returned to Antioch, they reported "that He had opened the door of faith to the Gentiles" (Acts 14:27). The opening was God's work, and the news was about what He had done. Paul wrote Colossians 4:3 "in chains," asking for prayer "that God would open to us a door for the word" while he himself sat behind a locked one. His plea shows where he believed the real key lay.

An open door did not mean an easy road. From Ephesus Paul wrote, "For a great and effective door has opened to me, and there are many adversaries" (1 Corinthians 16:9). He planned to stay because of that door, and the opposition did not change his mind. An open door was not an automatic command to stay put, either. At Troas "a door was opened to me by the Lord," yet Paul had no rest in his spirit because Titus was not there, and he went on to Macedonia (2 Corinthians 2:12–13). Opportunity, opposition, and the Lord's leading all had to be weighed together.

To the small church at Philadelphia, Christ says, "See, I have set before you an open door, and no one can shut it; for you have a little strength, have kept My word, and have not denied My name" (Revelation 3:8). The church's strength was small, but the One who set the door before it holds the key of David. A church with little strength can still walk through a door Christ has opened.

Revelation 3:20 and 4:1: The Knock, the Meal, and the Open Heaven. Revelation 3:20 reverses the usual pattern. The One who opens and no one shuts now stands outside the door of Laodicea, a church that thought it needed nothing. He comes in love, not contempt: "As many as I love, I rebuke and chasten. Therefore be zealous and repent" (3:19). Then He says, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (3:20).

He does not break the door down. He knocks, speaks, and waits for someone inside to open. He addresses a whole church, yet the promise is to "anyone," so a single listening heart is enough to let Him in. What He offers is a shared meal, the ordinary sign of friendship and restored fellowship. The contrast with Luke 13 is sharp. There, people who had eaten in His presence found the door shut against them. Here, the Lord Himself asks to come in and eat with those who will open to Him. The meal at the door also leads somewhere: "To him who overcomes I will grant to sit with Me on My throne" (3:21). The letter closes, "He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches" (3:22). The voice at the door reaches His people as the Spirit speaks.

Immediately afterward John sees "a door standing open in heaven" and hears, "Come up here, and I will show you things which must take place after this" (4:1). The churches had just heard their weaknesses named. Now God opens heaven so that they can see the throne that rules over all of it. The God who opens doors for the gospel on earth also opens heaven so that His people know who reigns.

Prison Doors, Locked Rooms, and the Tomb. In Acts the authorities keep locking the apostles up. "At night an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors and brought them out" (Acts 5:19). The next morning the guards found the prison shut securely and themselves standing before the doors, with no one inside (5:23). Peter slept chained between soldiers while guards kept the door (12:6). At Philippi an earthquake opened "all the doors" (16:26). There is gentle irony in Acts 12. After the angel led Peter past every prison barrier, he was left knocking at the door of the house where believers were praying for him, and the servant girl Rhoda was so glad that she ran to tell the others instead of opening it (12:13–14). Human locks could not hold God's servant, yet His own praying people were slow to believe their prayer had been answered.

The tomb gives the deepest example. Joseph "rolled a large stone against the door of the tomb" (Matthew 27:60), and the women on their way asked, "Who will roll away the stone from the door of the tomb for us?" (Mark 16:3). Death was the one door no one could open, and they had no answer. An angel rolled the stone back and sat on it (Matthew 28:2). The risen Jesus later stood among His disciples while "the doors were shut" (John 20:19, 26), so the stone was not moved to let Him out. It was moved so that witnesses could go in, see the place where He had lain, and hear that He was risen. John does not explain how Jesus entered the locked room. He directs attention to what Jesus did there: He stood among frightened disciples, said, "Peace be with you," showed His wounds, and sent them out (20:19–21).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of the door runs through Christ from beginning to end. Several earlier passages use different Hebrew words, so they are thematic connections rather than occurrences of thýra. They still help explain why a single, God-given entrance means so much.

In the flood account, those who entered the ark "went in as God had commanded him; and the LORD shut him in" (Genesis 7:16). There was one way in, God Himself closed it, and those inside were safe. At the Passover, the blood of the lamb on the doorposts and lintel marked the line between judgment and protection (Exodus 12). Paul calls Christ "our Passover" (1 Corinthians 5:7). Read alongside that, Jesus' claim to be the door suggests an entrance marked by sacrifice, and His words about the good shepherd who "gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11) point the same way. The New Testament does not link these scenes directly, but they explain one another naturally. God has always provided one way of shelter, and in His Son He provides it fully.

Isaiah 22:22 speaks of the key of the house of David that opens and no one shuts. Revelation 3:7 takes up those words for the risen Christ, so this connection is stated in Scripture itself. The authority to open and shut belongs to David's greater Son. Hebrews describes believers entering God's presence "through the veil, that is, His flesh" (Hebrews 10:20). It uses a different image, but the meaning agrees with John 10. Christ's own body, given in sacrifice, is the way into the presence of God. Those "who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" and now belong to "the household of God" (Ephesians 2:13, 19).

So the movement of the word gathers around one Person. He is the door by which sinners enter life. He passed through the door of death, and the stone was rolled away so His witnesses could see. He comes to disciples behind shut doors with peace and a commission. He opens doors for the gospel among the nations and knocks at the door of His own complacent church. He stands at the door as the coming Judge, and at the last He will shut it. The final occurrence of thýra in Scripture is the door standing open in heaven (Revelation 4:1). Through Christ, God's presence is no longer closed to His people.

7. Spirit & Life

Jesus' first teaching about a door concerns prayer: "But you, when you pray, go into your room, and when you have shut your door, pray to your Father who is in the secret place; and your Father who sees in secret will reward you openly" (Matthew 6:6). The hypocrites in the same passage pray in order "to be seen" (6:5). Shutting the door removes the audience. It is a deliberate refusal of the reward that comes from being noticed and a choice to trust the Father who sees what no one else does. A simple household act becomes a guard against turning prayer into a performance.

The Father behind that closed room is nothing like the neighbor in Luke 11. That man called from his bed, "the door is now shut... I cannot rise and give to you" (Luke 11:7). Jesus told the story to show the difference: "knock, and it will be opened to you" (11:9). When we shut our door to the crowd, we find the Father's door open. We do not come on our own standing. Through Christ we have "access by one Spirit to the Father" (Ephesians 2:18), and that welcome gives confidence to prayer. It also makes us one household with every believer who comes the same way, whatever their background.

The door also teaches a disciple how to serve. We do not have to pry open doors of ministry, and adversaries do not prove that God has closed one. We pray for open doors, as Paul did. We walk through the ones the Lord gives, listen for His leading as Paul did at Troas, and expect opposition on the other side. A church that prays this way will be neither reckless nor timid.

For a fearful believer, John 20 offers something steady. The disciples locked their doors out of fear, and Jesus came, stood among them, and spoke peace. Fear had shut the door, but it could not keep Him out. For a believer who has grown cold, Revelation 3:20 holds the same tenderness. Christ is still knocking, and He is not asking for a performance. He wants to sit at the table. The voice at the door is the voice the sheep of John 10 know, and the Spirit still speaks it to the churches. Hearing and opening are the response He asks for.

Scripture also treats the threshold as a place to keep watch. God warned Cain that "sin lies at the door" and that he "should rule over it" (Genesis 4:7). James places the Judge at the door in the middle of ordinary relationships (James 5:9), so the nearness of Christ bears on what we say about a brother this afternoon. A gentler picture comes from Exodus 21:5–6. There a servant who loved his master and chose to stay was brought to the door and marked for lifelong service. That scene is an illustration, not a prophecy, but it fits the life of one who has come in through Christ and has no wish to leave. The door is open today, and the Judge is standing near it. Both truths call for an answer now.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Mark 2:2 — So many gathered that there was no room, not even near the door. The crowd blocking the doorway explains why the paralyzed man's friends went in through the roof.

Mark 11:4 — The disciples find the colt tied by the door outside on the street, exactly as Jesus had said. His word proves accurate down to the doorway.

John 18:16 — Peter stands outside at the door of the high priest's courtyard until another disciple speaks to the doorkeeper and brings him in. The door he enters leads him to his denial.

Acts 3:2 — A man lame from birth is laid daily at the temple gate called Beautiful. He sat at the threshold of worship until Peter and John, in the name of Jesus, sent him through it walking and leaping.

Acts 5:9 — Peter tells Sapphira that the feet of those who buried her husband are at the door. The same nearness that announces the Judge in James falls here on one who lied to the Holy Spirit.

Acts 21:30 — Paul is dragged out of the temple, and immediately the doors are shut. The temple whose gate had seen a lame man healed now closes its doors against the apostle.
