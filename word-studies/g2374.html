DOOR, GATE - (thýra, Strong's G2374)

Summary

Thýra is the ordinary Greek word for a door: the movable barrier that lets people into a house, an inner room, a prison, a tomb, or the temple, or keeps them out. Because a door can be opened or shut, the New Testament uses it to talk about access, boundaries, and timing. Crowds press around the door of a house in Capernaum, prison doors swing open at night, and a stone is rolled against the door of Jesus' tomb.

The word carries great spiritual weight in several places. Jesus calls Himself "the door of the sheep" and promises that whoever enters by Him "will be saved." His parables warn that a day comes when the Master shuts the door. Paul speaks of doors for the gospel that only God can open. James warns that "the Judge is standing at the door." In Revelation the risen Christ stands outside the door of His own complacent church and knocks. Taken together, these passages show that God governs every threshold that matters, and that Jesus Himself is the way in.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Thýra is a common noun in everyday Greek. It names a real door, usually one that turns on hinges and can be bolted. That makes it different from a bare opening or a passage. Its New Testament settings are concrete: family homes (Mark 1:33; Luke 11:7), a private room (Matt 6:6), courtyards (John 18:16), prisons (Acts 5:19; 16:26), tombs (Mark 15:46), and the temple (Acts 3:2; 21:30). The word appears throughout the Gospels and Acts, in Paul's letters, in James, and in Revelation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

A thýra is a door or entrance that controls who may pass through. The New Testament uses it in two closely connected ways:

1. A literal door or doorway of a house, room, prison, tomb, or temple building. Example: "the whole city was gathered together at the door" (Mark 1:33). Once, in Acts 3:2, the NKJV renders it "gate" for the temple entrance where the lame man was laid.

2. A figure of speech for access or nearness, drawn from the way a door works: an opening for the gospel (1 Cor 16:9), the way into salvation (John 10:9), or the nearness of the coming Lord, "at the doors" (Mark 13:29).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most occurrences are physical, and the setting shows who controls the door. In Luke 11:7 a sleepy neighbor protests, "the door is now shut." In Acts the doors belong to jailers and the state. At the tomb the door is sealed by a stone.

Who opens or shuts the door makes most of the difference in meaning. When people shut doors, they are protecting themselves, as the frightened disciples do (John 20:19), or shutting the gospel out, as when the temple doors are slammed behind Paul (Acts 21:30). When God opens a door, His word goes forward. When the Master shuts one, the matter is settled.

One pattern of wording matters. "At the doors," with the plural, is an idiom for something very near, standing right at the entrance (Matt 24:33; Mark 13:29). James uses the same plural image for the Judge Himself, though the NKJV translates it "at the door" (James 5:9).

Thýra should not be confused with the word for a great city gate used in "the gates of Hades" (Matt 16:18). That is a different Greek word, though the two images overlap.

4. Theological Significance

Across these passages one conviction stands out: God holds the keys. Paul says “a door was opened to me by the Lord” (2 Corinthians 2:12), and he asks the Colossians to pray “that God would open to us a door for the word” (Colossians 4:3). Acts 14:27 tells how Paul and Barnabas reported that God had opened the door of faith to the Gentiles. The risen Christ describes Himself as the One who opens and no one shuts, and He sets an open door before the weak church in Philadelphia (Revelation 3:7–8). God's servants make plans and take action while depending on Him to grant access and make their witness fruitful.

An open door does not mean an easy road. Paul writes, "a great and effective door has opened to me, and there are many adversaries" (1 Cor 16:9). For Paul, opposition was no sign that the door had closed. The two came together. Yet an open door was not an automatic command to stay. At Troas the Lord opened a door for the gospel, but Paul had no rest in his spirit because he did not find Titus there, and he went on to Macedonia (2 Cor 2:12–13). He also wrote Colossians 4:3 "in chains," asking God for an open door while he sat behind a locked one.

God's control of the threshold runs in both directions. The same Lord who opens doors for His word also shuts the door of the kingdom when the time for invitation is over (Section 5). Scripture holds both truths together, and so the believer can be confident about God's mercy and sober about its limits.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

John 10:1–9 — "I am the door." Jesus begins with a sheepfold. The true shepherd "enters by the door," while the one who "climbs up some other way" is "a thief and a robber" (vv. 1–2). The listeners miss the point (v. 6), so Jesus says it plainly: "I am the door of the sheep" (v. 7). Then He adds, "If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved, and will go in and out and find pasture" (v. 9). The image carries two ideas. First, there is one entrance. Anyone who gets in some other way is a thief, so salvation cannot be reached around Christ. Second, the door is a place of safety and supply. The sheep go in to rest in safety and go out to find pasture. A familiar picture of a shepherd sleeping across the entrance of the fold fits the image well, but Jesus' own words make the point clearly enough. He is the way into life, and He is also the guard who keeps the flock and the way out to daily provision.

Matthew 25:10; Luke 13:25 — The shut door. In the parable of the virgins, those who are ready go in with the bridegroom to the wedding, and then the door is shut. Luke sharpens the scene. Once the Master "has risen up and shut the door," people outside knock and plead, "Lord, Lord, open for us," and He answers, "I do not know you, where you are from." Their knocking is not the problem. The problem is that they knock too late. These parables teach that God's invitation has a limit in time, and no amount of remorse afterward can reopen a door He has shut.

Revelation 3:20 — Christ outside His church. This verse is often used in evangelism, but it was first spoken to Laodicea, a church that called itself rich and needing nothing. Christ says He stands at the door and knocks. If anyone hears His voice and opens, He will come in and dine with that person, and that person with Him. The Lord of the church stands outside its door, and He does not break it down. He knocks and calls. What He offers is a shared meal, the ordinary sign of friendship and reconciliation. To a church grown comfortable without Him, He offers Himself.

John 20:19, 26 — Christ among His disciples behind shut doors. Twice John records that “the doors were shut,” the first time “for fear of the Jews.” Jesus came, “stood in the midst,” and said, “Peace be with you.” John leaves the manner of His entrance unexplained and directs attention to the risen Lord present with His frightened disciples. Their locked room could not prevent Him from bringing peace, showing His wounds, and sending them into the world (20:19–21).

Acts 5, 12, 16 — Prison doors. An angel "opened the prison doors" for the apostles (Acts 5:19), and the next morning the guards found the doors still secured and the prison empty (5:23). Peter sleeps chained between soldiers while guards keep the door (12:6). In Philippi an earthquake opens "all the doors" (16:26). There is a gentle irony in Acts 12: after the angel leads Peter past every prison barrier, he is left knocking at the door of the house where the church is praying for him (12:13), because the servant girl is too overjoyed to open it. Human locks could not hold God's servants, yet His own people were slow to believe their prayers had been answered.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Followed through the New Testament, thýra traces the believer's relationship with Christ. He is the door by which we enter life (John 10). He comes among His disciples behind shut doors with peace and their commission (John 20). He opens doors for His gospel to reach the nations (Acts 14:27; Colossians 4:3). He knocks at the door of a complacent church to restore fellowship (Revelation 3:20). He stands at the door as the coming Judge (James 5:9), near enough that His people should live ready.

Revelation 4:1 carries the story upward. Right after the letters to the churches, John sees a door standing open in heaven and is called to come up. The struggles of seven small congregations are now seen from the throne, where God reigns and is worshiped. The God who opens doors on earth also opens heaven so that His people can see who truly rules. The word's final occurrence in Scripture is that open door: through Christ, God's own presence is no longer closed to His people.

7. Spirit & Life

Jesus also tells His disciples to shut a door. "When you pray, go into your room, and when you have shut your door, pray to your Father who is in the secret place" (Matt 6:6, paraphrased in part). The hypocrites in the same chapter pray "to be seen" (6:5). Closing the door shuts out the human audience so that only the Father remains. A simple act becomes a guard against praying for applause.

So the believer lives with doors in both directions. We shut the door of the prayer room, and we open the door to Christ's knock. Neither is an achievement. Both are ways of responding to a Lord who is already present and already calling.

Entering through Christ gives prayer a welcome to rest upon. Through Him, believers have access to the Father by the Spirit (Ephesians 2:18). The closed door of the prayer room can therefore become a place of confident fellowship. We come because Christ has brought us near, and we can receive fellow believers as members of the same household, however different their earthly backgrounds may be.

Paul's prayers show how to wait for doors. He did not treat an open door as permission to stop praying, or adversaries as proof that God had closed the way. He asked others to pray for openings, used them as the Lord led, and expected trouble on the other side. A church that prays this way will be neither reckless nor timid.

James draws a surprising lesson from the Judge at the door. His warning comes in the middle of instructions about everyday relationships: "Do not grumble against one another, brethren, lest you be condemned" (James 5:9). The nearness of Christ bears on more than what we think about the end. It also bears on what we say about a brother this afternoon.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Mark 2:2 — So many gathered that there was no room, not even near the door. The crowd at the doorway explains why the paralytic's friends went through the roof.

  • Mark 11:4 — The disciples find the colt tied by the door. Jesus' word proves exact down to the doorway.

  • John 18:16 — Peter stands outside at the door of the high priest's courtyard until another disciple brings him in. The door through which he enters leads him to his denial.

  • Acts 3:2 — The lame man is laid daily at the temple gate called Beautiful. At that entrance he receives healing through Jesus' name.

  • Acts 21:30 — Paul is dragged out of the temple, and immediately the doors are shut. The religious establishment closes its doors against the gospel's messenger.

  • Matthew 27:60; Mark 15:46 — Joseph rolled a stone against the door of the tomb. The empty tomb later turns that entrance into a witness to Christ's victory over death.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Access to the Father through Christ. Hebrews describes believers entering God's presence “through the veil, that is, His flesh” (Hebrews 10:20). The veil uses a different Greek word, but Hebrews explicitly connects it with Christ's body. Jesus' sacrifice opens the way into God's presence. Paul's teaching gives a related explanation of the access Christ provides: “For through Him we both have access by one Spirit to the Father” (Ephesians 2:18). Those once far off have been brought near by His blood and received as members of God's household (2:13, 19). These are related passages about access, rather than occurrences of thýra. They help us see the fullness of entering through Christ: welcome before the Father in the Spirit, among a people brought near together.

Established biblical imagery. In Exodus 12 the blood of the Passover lamb was applied to the doorposts and lintel. The doorway marked the line between judgment and protection, and the destroyer passed over the blood-marked house. Paul calls Christ "our Passover" (1 Cor 5:7). Read alongside that, Jesus' claim to be the door suggests an entrance marked by sacrifice. The New Testament does not draw this link directly, but the two images explain each other naturally. Earlier still, God warned Cain that sin lies at the door, desiring him, and that he must rule over it (Gen 4:7, using a Hebrew word). In Scripture the threshold is also a place to keep watch.

A Hebrew servant who loved his master and chose to stay had his ear pierced at the door or doorpost (Exodus 21:5–6). That detail offers a fitting picture of willing, lifelong devotion to Christ. The empty tomb gives another illuminating scene. The women asked who would roll the stone away from its door (Mark 16:3), and an angel rolled it back and sat on it (Matthew 28:2). The opened entrance allowed the witnesses to see the place where Jesus had lain and hear that He was risen (28:6). Their feared obstacle became the place where they learned of His victory.
