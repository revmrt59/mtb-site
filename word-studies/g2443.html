IN ORDER THAT, THAT, SO THAT - (hína, Strong's G2443)

Summary

Hína is one of the most frequent connecting words in the New Testament. It usually introduces a goal and answers the question "why?" When John writes that God gave His Son "that whoever believes in Him should not perish" (John 3:16), hína names what God's giving was meant to achieve. The word also has other jobs. After verbs of praying, asking, or commanding, it often supplies the content of the request or command and tells us "what" rather than "why." In a few places it points to an outcome or consequence. The NKJV usually renders it "that" and sometimes uses an infinitive ("to"), so English readers can easily pass over it. Noticing it helps a reader see where a sentence is heading: toward God's purpose, toward the substance of a command, or toward what actually followed.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hína introduces a dependent clause, a statement that hangs on the main verb and completes its thought. Most often that clause gives the aim behind the action, much like the English "in order that." In New Testament Greek, however, the word had broadened beyond strict purpose. It regularly introduces the content of a prayer, wish, or instruction, and it can explain a preceding word such as "this." So the English "that" may carry several different relationships, and the reader has to ask what the clause is doing. Does it give the reason for the action, spell out what was said or asked, or describe what resulted? The surrounding sentence decides.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Purpose: "that," "in order that," "to"

This is the most common use. John 3:16 moves from God's giving to its intended goal: "He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life." Ephesians 2:10 works the same way. Believers are "created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them." Hína ties God's preparation to the life He intends His people to live. When joined with a negative, the purpose can be preventive. The NKJV renders this "lest" in 2 Corinthians 13:10. Matthew's repeated formula, which says that events happened so that the prophet's word might be fulfilled (Matthew 1:22; 2:15, paraphrased), also uses hína. With it, Matthew presents Jesus' life as unfolding toward a goal God had already announced.

Content of a command, prayer, or statement: "that"

Here hína does not explain why something happened. It tells what was commanded or asked. "This is My commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you" (John 15:12). The clause is the commandment itself. In John 17:15 Jesus prays, "I do not pray that You should take them out of the world, but that You should keep them from the evil one." Both clauses state what He is asking. The same pattern explains a word like "this." In John 6:29 (paraphrased), Jesus says the work of God is this: that you believe in the One He sent. The NKJV sometimes uses an infinitive for this use, as in John 4:34, where Jesus' food is to do the will of the One who sent Him.

Result or outcome: "that"

Occasionally hína seems to describe what followed rather than what anyone intended. In John 9:2 the disciples ask, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" They are not asking who sinned in order to cause blindness. They assume the blindness was the consequence of someone's sin. This use is less common, and interpreters sometimes disagree about where purpose ends and result begins.

3. Practical Reading Insight

John 9 sets two uses of hína side by side. The disciples look backward and treat the man's blindness as the outcome of some past sin. Jesus answers, "Neither this man nor his parents sinned, but that the works of God should be revealed in him" (John 9:3). His hína looks forward to what God would do. The disciples wanted someone to blame, and Jesus pointed to a purpose. Soon the man was seeing, and before the chapter ends he was confessing faith in the Son of God and worshiping Jesus (John 9:35–38). Jesus does not offer a theory that explains all suffering. He does refuse the disciples' assumption and turn their attention to God at work in this man.

Romans 5:20 shows why purpose matters in Paul's argument: "Moreover the law entered that the offense might abound. But where sin abounded, grace abounded much more." Read as purpose, Paul is saying that the law was given to expose sin fully, so that God's overflowing grace would stand out against it. Read as result, the verse describes the law's actual effect. Either way, the sentence moves toward grace.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume every English "that" is the same word. In John 3:16, the first "that" ("so loved the world that") translates a different Greek word, hōste, and only the second renders hína. Do not force "in order that" onto every hína either. "This is My commandment, that you love one another" states a command and does not explain one. Let the main verb and the context decide whether the clause gives purpose, content, or result.
