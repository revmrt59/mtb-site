AND, ALSO, EVEN - (kaí, Strong's G2532)

Summary

Kaí is the Greek conjunction usually translated "and." It is the thread that holds New Testament sentences together. Most often it simply joins words and clauses or moves a story forward, which is why NKJV readers meet it as "and," "then," or "now." The same word can also reach forward to a single word and mean "also," include someone in what has just been said, or mean "even" to mark a surprising case. In Paul's comparisons ("as . . . even so") it insists that the second half matches the first. Because one Greek word stands behind so many English renderings, the surrounding sentence has to show what kaí is doing. A reader who notices this will catch moments where Paul is pressing a point, not just adding to a list.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Kaí is one of the most frequent words in the New Testament. Its basic job is addition. It places one thing beside another: two nouns, two actions, two sentences. From that simple role, context draws out several related functions. In narrative it keeps events moving, often at the start of a sentence, much like the Hebrew "and" that runs through Old Testament storytelling. When it stands directly before a single word or phrase, it often stops joining and starts including or emphasizing: "also" or "even." English needs several words to cover this range, so NKJV renders kaí as "and," "then," "also," "even," "but," "so," and others. The Greek word stays the same. The translator chooses the English that best fits the logic of the sentence.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Joining and moving the story forward (and, then, now, so)

This is by far the most common use. In Matthew 4:11 kaí links the scenes after the temptation: "Then the devil left Him, and behold, angels came and ministered to Him." (The opening "Then" renders a different Greek word.) Revelation uses kaí to begin sentence after sentence, and NKJV often renders it "then" to show sequence: "Then I saw a strong angel proclaiming with a loud voice" (Revelation 5:2). The word carries John from one vision to the next without breaking the flow.

Including someone or something else (also, so)

Placed before a word, kaí adds that item to what has already been said. Romans 11:16 shows kaí doing two jobs in one verse: "For if the firstfruit is holy, the lump is also holy; and if the root is holy, so are the branches." The middle "and" joins two parallel sentences. The "also" and the "so" bring the lump and the branches into the holiness first stated of the firstfruit and the root.

Matching a comparison (as . . . even so)

Paul often pairs "as" with "so" and adds kaí to insist that the second half truly corresponds to the first. Romans 5:21 reads: "so that as sin reigned in death, even so grace might reign through righteousness to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord." Galatians 4:29 uses the same pattern for a story repeating itself: "as he who was born according to the flesh then persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit, even so it is now."

Kaí can also mark a surprising or extreme case. In Luke 6:32, for example, Jesus points out (paraphrased) that even sinners love the people who love them back.

Joining where context supplies a contrast (but, yet)

Sometimes kaí links two statements that pull against each other. The Greek word still simply adds, and NKJV supplies "but" because the situation calls for it. After Zacharias doubted Gabriel's message, the angel continued, "But behold, you will be mute and not able to speak" (Luke 1:20). In Mark 2:8 the scribes reason silently, "But immediately" Jesus perceives their thoughts. The contrast comes from the story. Kaí simply sets the two facts side by side.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The comparison pattern repays careful attention. In Romans 5:21 Paul has just described sin reigning over humanity through death. Kaí in "even so" does not soften the comparison. It insists that grace now holds the throne sin once held, reigning "through righteousness to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord." The word makes the parallel exact, and the surrounding verses (5:20, "grace abounded much more") show that grace does more than match sin. It overcomes it.

Galatians 4:29 works the other way. Paul uses kaí to tell believers that the old hostility between Ishmael and Isaac has not ended: "even so it is now." Opposition from those who rely on the flesh is not a strange new trial. It is the same pattern Scripture already recorded.

Romans 11:16 shows a third payoff. "Also" and "so" carry Paul's argument about Israel: what is true of the root holds for the branches. Missing those small words means missing the argument.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume every "and" signals the next event in time, or that every "but" in English renders a Greek word of contrast. NKJV "but," "then," and "so" sometimes translate kaí and sometimes translate other Greek words entirely. Let the passage's logic decide whether kaí is adding, including, comparing, or setting up a contrast. Do not build a doctrine on the conjunction alone.
