COMMON, UNCLEAN, DEFILED - (koinós, Strong's G2839)

Summary

Koinós is a Greek adjective meaning "common." The New Testament uses it in two ways that pull in opposite directions. In one, "common" means shared, something that belongs equally to everyone in a group. The first believers "had all things in common" (Acts 2:44), Paul calls Titus "a true son in our common faith" (Titus 1:4), and Jude writes of "our common salvation" (Jude 3). In the other, "common" means ordinary in the sense of unholy: outside what is set apart to God and, in disputes over purity, ritually unclean or defiled. The NKJV translates this second sense as "defiled" (Mark 7:2), "unclean" (Romans 14:14), or "common" (Acts 10:14; Hebrews 10:29).

The word's story in Scripture follows how God redraws the lines between holy and common through Jesus Christ. Jesus refuses the Pharisees' charge that unwashed hands make a person "defiled." God teaches Peter through a vision that he "should not call any man common or unclean" (Acts 10:28), and the gospel goes to the Gentiles. Paul insists that nothing is unclean of itself, yet he guards the conscience of the believer who still thinks otherwise. Hebrews then gives the sternest warning attached to this word: a person can treat the blood of Christ, the blood that sanctifies, as "a common thing" (Hebrews 10:29). The word teaches us what God has made clean, what He has made shared, and what must never be treated as ordinary.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

In ordinary Greek, koinós described anything public or jointly owned: a shared road, shared property, or a shared concern. That everyday sense carries straight into the New Testament.

Among Jews of the period, the word also took on a religious meaning. The Law taught Israel's priests to tell the holy from the unholy and the clean from the unclean (Leviticus 10:10). Leviticus presents these as two paired distinctions. Something common in the sense of not consecrated was not thereby impure or forbidden. Ordinary bread was common without being unclean. In the purity disputes of Jesus' day, however, koinós could carry the sharper sense of defiled, as with unwashed hands (Mark 7:2) or forbidden food (Acts 10:14). By the time of Jesus, the Pharisees had extended these concerns well beyond the Law through their traditions of washing. The word has only twelve occurrences in the New Testament, and three of them come in the story of Peter and Cornelius (Acts 10:14, 28; 11:8). The related verb koinóō, "to call common," appears in the same story (10:15; 11:9).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Koinós does not mean "sinful." Its basic idea is "not set apart" or "held in common." Context decides whether that is something to celebrate or something to fear.

1. Shared, belonging to all alike. Something held jointly by a community or possessed equally by every member. "They had all things in common" (Acts 4:32).

2. Common in the sense of profane or ritually unclean. In purity disputes, something treated as defiled, not merely as ordinary or unconsecrated. "I have never eaten anything common or unclean" (Acts 10:14).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The shared sense appears in Luke's descriptions of the Jerusalem church (Acts 2:44; 4:32) and in the epistles, where it describes the faith and salvation every believer holds (Titus 1:4; Jude 3). Here the word is warm and unifying.

The ritual sense appears wherever purity is in dispute: hands (Mark 7:2), food (Acts 10:14; 11:8; Romans 14:14), people (Acts 10:28), and, in Hebrews, the blood of Christ (Hebrews 10:29). In Acts 10:14 and 11:8 koinós stands beside another word for "unclean," and the pairing makes Peter's protest emphatic. He means he has never crossed the line in any form.

One pattern matters for interpretation. In several passages the crucial question is who calls or counts something common. God warns Peter not to call common what God has cleansed (Acts 10:15, paraphrased). Paul says a thing is unclean "to him who considers" it so (Romans 14:14). Hebrews condemns the one who "counted" Christ's blood common. The word often names a judgment that people make, and Scripture asks whether that judgment agrees with God's.

4. Theological Significance

Holiness belongs to God, and only He has the authority to declare what is holy and what is common. Israel's purity laws taught that truth through daily life. A person could not wander casually into God's presence, and the line between holy and common was a mercy that guarded people from a holy God they had offended.

In Christ, God redraws that line without lowering His holiness. He cleanses what was common, and He brings near those who were far off. That is why the same word can also describe the church's deepest possessions. The "common faith" (Titus 1:4) and "common salvation" (Jude 3) are not ordinary or second-rate. They are held in common because no believer has a superior grade of salvation. Apostle and new convert, Jew and Gentile, stand on the same grace. Jude writes about this shared salvation even as he warns against intruders who corrupt it. A gospel that belongs to everyone equally leaves no room for spiritual elites.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Mark 7:2 — defiled hands. The Pharisees saw the disciples "eat bread with defiled, that is, with unwashed hands, they found fault." Mark explains the term for his readers: "defiled" here means unwashed according to tradition. Jesus answers by moving defilement from the hands to the heart. Whatever enters a man from outside "cannot defile him, because it does not enter his heart but his stomach," and Mark adds, "thus purifying all foods" (Mark 7:18–19). What truly defiles comes from within: evil thoughts, greed, deceit, and the like (Mark 7:20–23, paraphrased). The Pharisees worried about the dust of the marketplace and missed the corruption of their own hearts.

Acts 10:14, 28; 11:8 — no person common. On a rooftop in Joppa, hungry and praying, Peter sees a sheet full of animals and hears the command to eat. His answer comes by reflex: "Not so, Lord! For I have never eaten anything common or unclean" (Acts 10:14). God replies that Peter must not call common what God has cleansed, and the exchange happens three times (Acts 10:15–16). The vision is about food on the surface, but Peter soon understands its real target. Standing in the house of the Gentile Cornelius, he says: "You know how unlawful it is for a Jewish man to keep company with or go to one of another nation. But God has shown me that I should not call any man common or unclean" (Acts 10:28). Peter moves the word from animals to people himself. When he later defends his actions in Jerusalem, he repeats the same refusal (Acts 11:8). That repetition shows that the change came from God and overruled Peter's own instincts. The Gentiles were not a contaminating class to be kept outside. God had cleansed them, and the Spirit fell on them as He had on Jewish believers (Acts 10:44–47).

Romans 14:14 — nothing unclean of itself. Paul uses koinós three times in one sentence: "I know and am convinced by the Lord Jesus that there is nothing unclean of itself; but to him who considers anything to be unclean, to him it is unclean." Objectively, the matter is settled, and Paul's conviction rests on the Lord Jesus. Yet a believer who still sees a food as common and eats it anyway wounds his own conscience. Paul therefore tells the strong not to destroy with their food the brother for whom Christ died (Romans 14:15, paraphrased). Being right about clean food is not the highest goal. Love for the brother is.

Hebrews 10:29 — the blood counted common. Hebrews asks what punishment awaits the one "who has trampled the Son of God underfoot, counted the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified a common thing, and insulted the Spirit of grace." The verse holds its own contrast. The blood by which the person was sanctified, set apart as holy, is the very thing he now counts common. That is not a lapse in ritual. It is a deliberate verdict that Christ's sacrifice is no different from any other death. Under the Law, treating the holy as common brought judgment. Hebrews argues from the lesser to the greater: if rejecting Moses' law was deadly, despising the Son's blood is far worse (Hebrews 10:28–31).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Followed through the New Testament, koinós traces a reversal that runs through Christ. In Israel's life the word marked boundaries: clean and unclean food, washed and unwashed hands, Israel and the nations. Jesus declares all foods clean (Mark 7:19). The risen Lord then shows Peter that the same cleansing reaches people (Acts 10:28). Paul explains what made this possible. Christ "has broken down the middle wall of separation," abolishing in His flesh the law of commandments contained in ordinances, "so as to create in Himself one new man from the two" (Ephesians 2:14–15). The word that once divided now describes what believers share: goods, faith, and salvation.

Christ's blood stands at the center of that reversal. It turns the common into the clean, and for that reason it is the one thing that must never be counted common (Hebrews 10:29). The cleansing God offers freely is not cheap.

7. Spirit & Life

The early church shows what happens when the Spirit takes hold of a community. Luke says "all who believed were together, and had all things in common" (Acts 2:44). He also shows where that came from: they were "of one heart and one soul; neither did anyone say that any of the things he possessed was his own" (Acts 4:32). No law forced this. Their goods were sold and "divided them among all, as anyone had need" (Acts 2:45). People who share the same Spirit begin to hold their possessions with open hands. Where "mine" quietly becomes "ours," the gospel has reached the wallet.

This word also teaches a believer two things never to call common. The first is any person God has cleansed. Peter's prejudice did not feel like sin to him. It felt like faithfulness, and God still corrected it. Believers need the same correction whenever they write off a person or a group as beyond God's reach or beneath their fellowship. The second is the blood of Jesus. Familiarity with the gospel can slowly make the cross seem ordinary, one more religious idea among others. Hebrews 10:29 calls believers to treasure as holy what God paid so much to give.

Romans 14 brings these together. The strong believer's freedom is real, and Christ purchased it. But the brother with a tender conscience was also purchased by Christ, and his conscience must not be run over for the sake of a meal.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Most occurrences of koinós have been discussed above. Two deserve a further note.

  • Titus 1:4 — "To Titus, a true son in our common faith." Titus was a Greek (Galatians 2:3). A Jewish apostle calls a Gentile coworker his son in a faith they hold equally, which is Acts 10 lived out in a working friendship.

  • Jude 3 — Jude says he was eager to write about "our common salvation," but felt he had to urge his readers to contend earnestly for the faith once delivered to the saints (paraphrased). The salvation all believers share is also a trust all of them must guard.
