VINE, GRAPEVINE - (ámpelos, Strong's G0288)

Summary

Jesus took one of the most familiar plants in Israel and made it a picture of Himself: “I am the true vine, and My Father is the vinedresser” (John 15:1). Ámpelos is the Greek word for that grapevine. In John 15, Christ is the Vine, and those who belong to Him are branches that live from His life. A branch has no fruit of its own. It bears fruit by staying joined to the vine. Jesus calls His disciples into that same living dependence. They are to abide in Him, let His words abide in them, ask the Father in prayer, keep His commands, and remain in His love. The Father tends this vine Himself, pruning fruitful branches so that they bear more, and their fruit brings Him glory.

The picture carries a long history. God once brought Israel out of Egypt and planted it as His vine, then looked for good grapes and found wild ones. When Jesus calls Himself the true vine, He steps into that story as the One who finally gives the Father the fruit He was looking for. The same word appears at the Last Supper, where Jesus calls the cup of His blood “the fruit of the vine” and promises to drink it new with His disciples in His Father's kingdom. James uses an ordinary grapevine to show that what comes out of a life reveals its root. Revelation closes the story with “the vine of the earth,” gathered into the winepress of God's wrath.

Read side by side, these passages leave every reader with a searching question: which vine is my life drawing from? One vine is tended by the Father and gives life to its branches. The other ripens for judgment. The cup Jesus shared with His friends holds the promise that those joined to Him will sit at His table again.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The grapevine was one of the staple plants of the biblical world. Alongside the olive and the fig, it stood for the fruitfulness of the land God gave His people. Every Israelite family knew the work it required. Isaiah describes the owner who “dug it up and cleared out its stones, And planted it with the choicest vine. He built a tower in its midst, And also made a winepress in it” (Isaiah 5:2). Wine belonged to daily meals, to celebrations, and to the Passover table.

Because the vine was so familiar, the prophets made it one of their great pictures of Israel. The Hebrew word for vine, gephen, stands behind passages like Psalm 80:8: “You have brought a vine out of Egypt; You have cast out the nations, and planted it.” Isaiah 5 and Jeremiah 2:21 use the same picture. When Jesus spoke of Himself as the vine, His disciples heard Him with that history already in their minds.

In the New Testament, ámpelos appears in the Last Supper accounts of Matthew, Mark, and Luke, three times in John 15, once in James, and in Revelation's harvest scene. Paul never uses the word. When he describes the believer's living union with Christ, he usually speaks of a body instead. The word names the plant itself and should be kept separate from ampelōn, the vineyard where vines are grown.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ámpelos means a vine, specifically the grapevine, the plant that produces grapes and, from them, wine. What the vine yields is called “the fruit of the vine.” The word has one basic sense, and it keeps that plain meaning in every use, whether the vine is real or pictured. The word does not mean “Jesus” or “judgment.” Its rich New Testament teaching comes from the settings and descriptive words attached to it, such as “the true vine” and “the vine of the earth.” One definition is enough here.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears in four kinds of settings. At the Last Supper it forms part of the phrase “the fruit of the vine,” a dignified way of naming the wine in the Passover cup (Matthew 26:29; Mark 14:25; Luke 22:18). In John 15 it becomes Jesus' own self-description and the center of an extended picture of His relationship with His Father and His disciples (John 15:1, 4, 5). James uses it of an ordinary plant (James 3:12). Revelation uses it in a vision of judgment (Revelation 14:18–19).

Two details of wording change the meaning noticeably. In John 15:1 Jesus attaches the adjective “true” to the vine and frames the statement as one of His “I am” sayings. Together these make a claim about who He is in relation to an older vine that God had planted. In Revelation the phrase “the vine of the earth” defines that vine by where it belongs, which sets it in pointed contrast with the vine whose vinedresser is the Father. The same noun names Christ and names rebellious humanity, so the surrounding words decide what the vine stands for. A vine in Scripture does not automatically point to Jesus.

James shows the plain, everyday use. He asks whether a fig tree can bear olives or a grapevine bear figs (James 3:12). He has just observed that the same mouth blesses God and curses people made in His likeness (James 3:9–10). A plant produces according to its nature, so speech that swings between praise and cursing exposes something wrong at the root of a life. What comes out reveals what is within.

4. Theological Significance

The vine passages show God as a cultivator who plants with care and expects fruit. Isaiah's song pictures Him doing everything a vinedresser could do and then asking, “What more could have been done to My vineyard That I have not done in it?” (Isaiah 5:4). His expectation is not harsh. He has invested everything in what He planted. Isaiah also names the fruit God was looking for: “He looked for justice, but behold, oppression; For righteousness, but behold, a cry for help” (Isaiah 5:7). The grapes God wants are a people whose lives reflect His own righteousness. His grief over wild grapes shows how seriously He takes the purpose for which He made them.

The vine also shows that life flows in one direction. Fruit comes from the vine through the branch, so nothing a disciple produces begins with the disciple. In John 15 the Father's long search for fruit is finally satisfied in His Son and in those who live from Him. The image reveals a God who does not simply demand fruit from a distance. He supplies the life that produces it, and He stays personally involved as the vinedresser.

The passages also bind fruitfulness to love. Jesus says, “As the Father loved Me, I also have loved you; abide in My love” (John 15:9). The vine is a picture of fellowship: the Father loving the Son, the Son loving His disciples, and the disciples living inside that love. Branches do not stand beside the vine as separate admirers. They share its life.

Finally, God's grace and His judgment both work through the harvest. A vine is judged by what it yields. Psalm 80 grieves over a vine that “is burned with fire, it is cut down” (Psalm 80:16). In Isaiah 5 the most frightening words are God's decision to stop tending the vineyard: “It shall not be pruned or dug, But there shall come up briers and thorns” (Isaiah 5:6). God is patient, yet His patience has a limit. Revelation shows the earth's vine allowed to reach full ripeness before the harvest of wrath comes on His timetable.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

John 15:1 — The true vine and the Father's vineyard. “I am the true vine, and My Father is the vinedresser.” The word “true” carries the weight of Israel's history. Psalm 80 remembers how God brought a vine out of Egypt and “caused it to take deep root, And it filled the land” (Psalm 80:9). Then the psalm turns to grief: “Why have You broken down her hedges, So that all who pass by the way pluck her fruit?” (Psalm 80:12). The psalmist pleads, “Return, we beseech You, O God of hosts; Look down from heaven and see, And visit this vine” (Psalm 80:14). In Isaiah 5 God planted the choicest vine, “So He expected it to bring forth good grapes, But it brought forth wild grapes” (Isaiah 5:2). Jeremiah 2:21 mourns that the noble vine God planted had turned degenerate.

Into that history Jesus says, “I am the true vine.” He means the genuine one, the vine that actually is what Israel was planted to be. He is the faithful Israel, the One who gives God the fruit the nation failed to give. The center of God's life on earth is now a Person, and belonging to God's people comes through living union with Him. Jesus names each role plainly. He is the vine, the Father is the vinedresser, and the disciples are the branches (John 15:1, 5). The Father who once looked in vain over Israel's vineyard now tends His Son and everyone joined to Him.

John 15:2–6 — Living connection, pruning, and the branch that withers. Jesus states the connection without softening it: “Abide in Me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in Me” (John 15:4). Then He repeats it with even greater force: “I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing” (John 15:5). The union is mutual, “in Me, and I in you.” It is a living connection, more than a legal status or a religious label. A branch does not manufacture grapes by effort. The life of the vine flows into it, and fruit appears.

The Father tends this relationship. “Every branch in Me that does not bear fruit He takes away; and every branch that bears fruit He prunes, that it may bear more fruit” (John 15:2). Jesus immediately adds, “You are already clean because of the word which I have spoken to you” (John 15:3). The Greek words for “prunes” and “clean” are closely related, so the Father's pruning and the cleansing work of Jesus' word belong together. Set against Isaiah 5:6, where God's judgment on the old vineyard was that it would no longer be pruned, the Father's knife in John 15 is a sign of His continued care.

The image also carries a serious warning: “If anyone does not abide in Me, he is cast out as a branch and is withered; and they gather them and throw them into the fire, and they are burned” (John 15:6). This draws on established biblical imagery. In Ezekiel 15 God asks what vine wood is good for. Unlike forest timber, it cannot even be made into a peg to hang a vessel on, and it is fit only for the fire (Ezekiel 15:2–5). God applied that picture to Jerusalem's persistent unfaithfulness (Ezekiel 15:6–8). A vine exists for its fruit. Cut off from the life that produces fruit, a branch has nothing left to offer.

John 15:7–12 — What abiding looks like. Jesus explains His own picture, and His explanation rules out passive discipleship. Abiding includes His words living in His disciples and shaping their prayers: “If you abide in Me, and My words abide in you, you will ask what you desire, and it shall be done for you” (John 15:7). It produces fruit that glorifies God: “By this My Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit; so you will be My disciples” (John 15:8). It means remaining in His love through obedience, following His own example: “If you keep My commandments, you will abide in My love, just as I have kept My Father's commandments and abide in His love” (John 15:10). Its aim is joy: “that My joy may remain in you, and that your joy may be full” (John 15:11). And its central command is love: “This is My commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you” (John 15:12).

That love would be shown within hours, as Jesus laid down His life for His friends (John 15:13). Fruitfulness therefore reaches into a disciple's desires, prayers, obedience, and relationships. The branch is entirely dependent, yet it is fully alive and responsive. Jesus goes on to speak of fruit that remains, flowing from the calling He has given them (John 15:16). The Father prunes, the Son gives life, and the disciples abide by listening, asking, obeying, and loving.

Matthew 26:26–29 — The cup and the coming kingdom. At the Passover table Jesus took the cup, gave thanks, and said, “Drink from it, all of you. For this is My blood of the new covenant, which is shed for many for the remission of sins” (Matthew 26:27–28). Then He made a vow: “I will not drink of this fruit of the vine from now on until that day when I drink it new with you in My Father's kingdom” (Matthew 26:29). Mark and Luke record the same promise (Mark 14:25; Luke 22:18). The phrase “fruit of the vine” echoes the familiar Jewish blessing over wine, which thanks God as Creator of the fruit of the vine.

Passover remembered deliverance from Egypt. Jesus now ties the cup to a greater deliverance through His blood. His refusal to drink again turns the meal into a pledge. His death would interrupt His table fellowship with the disciples, but the cup will be drunk again, “new,” in the kingdom, and Matthew adds “with you.” Jesus binds Himself to return and sit at table with His people. The cup looks back to His sacrifice and forward to a feast.

That kingdom table has deep roots. Isaiah foresaw the LORD preparing a feast of rich food and fine wines for all peoples (Isaiah 25:6). Micah promised a day when everyone would sit under his own vine and fig tree with no one to make them afraid (Micah 4:4), echoing the peace of Solomon's reign (1 Kings 4:25). The fruit of the vine Jesus set aside at the Supper points toward that secure and joyful feast.

Revelation 14:18–20 — The vine of the earth. An angel cries out, “Thrust in your sharp sickle and gather the clusters of the vine of the earth, for her grapes are fully ripe” (Revelation 14:18). “So the angel thrust his sickle into the earth and gathered the vine of the earth, and threw it into the great winepress of the wrath of God” (Revelation 14:19). This vine has reached maturity, but its ripeness is ripeness for judgment. The scene draws on prophetic language of God treading the winepress in wrath (Isaiah 63:3), and the result is sobering: “the winepress was trampled outside the city, and blood came out of the winepress” (Revelation 14:20).

This is the last use of the word in Scripture, and it stands in stark contrast to John 15. The true vine is heavenly, tended by the Father, and gives life to its branches. The vine of the earth belongs to this world, gathers humanity in rebellion against God, and ends in the winepress. The branches of the true vine are pruned so that they bear more fruit. The vine of the earth is left to ripen and is then cut down whole.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The vine traces the story of God's people from Egypt to the end of the age. God brought Israel out and planted it, looked for justice and righteousness, and found wild grapes. The psalmist cried for God to visit the vine, and in the same prayer asked, “Let Your hand be upon the man of Your right hand, Upon the son of man whom You made strong for Yourself” (Psalm 80:17), just after speaking of “the branch that You made strong for Yourself” (Psalm 80:15). The psalm does not name the Messiah, but its longing finds a fitting answer when the Son of Man stands in the Upper Room and says, “I am the true vine.” God answered the cry for His vineyard by sending His Son, who gives the Father the fruit He always sought.

Those joined to Him by faith become branches, Jew and Gentile alike. Life with God now comes through union with a Person rather than through belonging to a nation. The Father's purpose for His vineyard has not been abandoned. It has been fulfilled in Christ and extended to everyone who abides in Him.

The cup carries the story forward. The prophets spoke of a cup of God's wrath that the rebellious must drink (Isaiah 51:17), and Revelation uses the same picture for final judgment (Revelation 14:10). In Gethsemane Jesus prayed that the cup might pass from Him, and then He accepted it (Matthew 26:39). Because He took the cup of judgment, the fruit of the vine can be offered to His people as a cup of forgiveness and fellowship. Each time the church shares the cup, it looks back to His blood and forward to the day He drinks it new with them.

Some further connections are suggestive rather than stated, yet they fit the shape of the story. Scripture can call wine “the blood of grapes” (Genesis 49:11; Deuteronomy 32:14), and grapes yield wine only when they are crushed. On the night He called Himself the true vine and named the cup His blood, Jesus went out to be crushed so that His life could be poured out for others. Many believers have also found that seasons of pressure are often when fruit forms in their own lives.

The canon closes with two vines and two harvests. Neither John 15 nor Revelation 14 draws the comparison by itself, but read together they present one vine tended by the Father and bearing fruit that remains, and another gathered into the winepress. Every life is finally drawing from one or the other.

7. Spirit & Life

Jesus' words “without Me you can do nothing” (John 15:5) free believers from a weary kind of religion. A branch does not strain to produce grapes. It stays connected, and the life of the vine does the work. Many Christians treat fruitfulness as a performance to achieve by willpower. Jesus describes it as the overflow of remaining in Him. Yet abiding is anything but idle. It means staying near His words, praying from dependence, keeping His commands, and loving His people (John 15:7–12). Dependence gives obedience its strength, and obedience is how a dependent branch stays in His love. For a struggling disciple, the better question is often less “How can I try harder?” and more “Where have I drifted from the Vine?”

Jesus gave this teaching in the same farewell conversation in which He promised the Spirit of truth, who would dwell with His disciples and be in them (John 14:16–17), and who would testify of Him (John 15:26). John does not call the Spirit the sap of the vine, and the picture should not be pressed that far. Still, the setting makes plain that Jesus would not leave His disciples to sustain their union with Him alone. Paul uses a related image, though not this Greek word, when he describes the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, and the rest (Galatians 5:22–23). Read together, these passages help believers recognize fruitfulness as a life increasingly marked by the Spirit and the love of Christ.

The Father as vinedresser also changes how we read hard seasons. He prunes fruitful branches so they bear more fruit (John 15:2). Pruning means cutting, and it hurts. Hebrews 12:11 admits that discipline seems painful at the time but later yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness. The Father often works through His word, exposing a habit, correcting a priority, or removing something that drained strength. When He cuts something away from a fruitful life, He is not discarding a useless branch. He is caring for one He expects to bear more. Remember Isaiah 5:6: the vineyard under judgment was the one God stopped pruning. The knife is a sign of His attention.

There is humility in the image as well. Ezekiel's question about vine wood shows that a vine has little worth apart from its fruit. The comparison that follows is an illustrative resonance rather than the stated point of John 15. Ezekiel 31:3 uses the towering cedar for an imperial power, yet Jesus chose for Himself a plant whose whole worth lies in what it gives to others. He came with no outward splendor that would make people desire Him (Isaiah 53:2), and His purpose was to give life through fruit. Branches joined to that vine share its character. They are valued for the fruit that comes through them, not for their own impressiveness, and that leaves no room for boasting.

James brings all this down to everyday speech. A grapevine cannot bear figs. If bitterness keeps coming out of our mouths, we need to look at the root, not just polish our words. Where the life of the true Vine flows, the tongue will over time begin to bear fruit that matches Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Mark 14:25 — Jesus solemnly declares that He will no longer drink of the fruit of the vine until the day He drinks it new in the kingdom of God. Mark's “Assuredly” gives the vow the force of a solemn pledge.

Luke 22:18 — Jesus says He will not drink of the fruit of the vine until the kingdom of God comes. Luke places the emphasis on the arrival of the kingdom itself as the moment the vow is fulfilled.

The remaining occurrences (Matthew 26:29; John 15:1, 4, 5; James 3:12; Revelation 14:18–19) are discussed above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

G0290 (ampelōn), vineyard — A closely related noun naming the plot where vines grow. It appears in Jesus' vineyard parables (Matthew 20:1; 21:33).

G2814 (klēma), branch — A distinct word paired with ámpelos in John 15, naming the disciples' role: “I am the vine, you are the branches” (John 15:5).

G3025 (lēnos), winepress — A distinct word paired with “the vine of the earth” in Revelation 14:19.

H1612 (gephen), vine — The Hebrew word behind Israel's vine imagery (Psalm 80:8; Ezekiel 15:2; Micah 4:4). This is a cross-language, thematic connection and the canonical background for John 15, not a lexical relative.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The John 15 picture has limits worth respecting. Christ is the vine, the source of life for fruit-bearing branches. The passage does not speak of Him as the root, even though Scripture elsewhere calls Him the Root of David (Revelation 5:5). The image focuses on living union and fruit, so it should not be stretched into a full description of everything Christ is.
