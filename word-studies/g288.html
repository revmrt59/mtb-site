VINE, GRAPEVINE - (ámpelos, Strong's G0288)

Summary

Ámpelos is the ordinary Greek word for a vine, almost always the grapevine that covered the hillsides of Israel and supplied its wine. Jesus uses it at the Last Supper when He calls the cup "the fruit of the vine." He uses it in the Upper Room when He says, "I am the true vine." James uses it to make a point about honest speech, and Revelation uses it for the harvest of God's judgment.

These few uses carry a large story. In the Old Testament, God planted Israel as His vine and looked for good grapes, but the vine disappointed Him. Jesus steps into that picture and calls Himself the true vine, the one in whom God finally gets the fruit He has always sought. Believers live as branches drawing every bit of their life from Him. The fruit of the vine in the cup points to His blood and to a coming feast in His Father's kingdom. Scripture also describes a second vine, "the vine of the earth," which ripens for judgment. Set beside John 15, that second vine makes a sobering comparison and presses the question of which vine a life is drawing from.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The grapevine was one of the staple plants of the ancient Mediterranean world, alongside the olive and the fig. Every Israelite family knew the work of planting, pruning, harvesting, and treading grapes. The Old Testament draws on that familiarity again and again, picturing Israel as a vine God planted in the land (Psalm 80; Isaiah 5; Jeremiah 2:21).

In the New Testament, ámpelos appears in the Last Supper accounts of Matthew, Mark, and Luke, three times in John 15, in James, and in Revelation's harvest scene (14:18–19). Paul never uses it. When he describes the believer's living union with Christ, he speaks of a body instead. The word refers to the plant itself and should be kept separate from ampelōn, the vineyard where vines are grown.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ámpelos means a vine, specifically the grapevine, the plant that produces grapes and, from them, wine. The word has one basic sense. Its rich New Testament meanings come from the settings and descriptive words attached to it, such as "true vine" and "vine of the earth." The word itself keeps its plain meaning in every use.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears in four kinds of settings. At the Last Supper it forms part of the phrase "the fruit of the vine," a dignified way of naming the wine in the Passover cup (Matthew 26:29). In John 15 it becomes Jesus' own self-description and the center of an extended picture of His relationship with His disciples. James uses it literally, as an example from nature (James 3:12). Revelation uses it in a vision of judgment (Revelation 14:19).

The descriptive words matter. The same noun names Christ when it is "the true vine" and names rebellious humanity when it is "the vine of the earth." The context determines what the vine stands for. A vine in Scripture does not automatically point to Jesus.

4. Theological Significance

The passages using this word reveal a God who plants with purpose and expects fruit. Isaiah's song shows the Owner doing everything a vinedresser could do: "What more could have been done to My vineyard That I have not done in it?" (Isaiah 5:4). God's desire for a fruitful people runs through the whole story, and John 15 shows Him finally satisfied in His Son.

These passages also teach that life flows in one direction. Fruit comes from the vine through the branch, so nothing a disciple produces originates with the disciple. God is also patient in judgment. He lets the earth's vine come to full ripeness before the harvest of wrath. His patience with evil has a limit, and the harvest comes on His timetable. James adds a moral law built into creation: what a thing produces reveals what it is.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

John 15:1 — "I am the true vine." Jesus speaks against a long Old Testament background. Psalm 80 remembers how God brought Israel out of Egypt and planted it: "You have brought a vine out of Egypt; You have cast out the nations, and planted it" (Psalm 80:8). The vine once filled the land, but now its hedges are broken down, and the psalmist pleads, "Look down from heaven and see, And visit this vine" (80:14). Isaiah 5 is sharper still. God planted "the choicest vine," built a tower and a winepress, and "expected it to bring forth good grapes, But it brought forth wild grapes" (Isaiah 5:2). Jeremiah 2:21 likewise mourns a noble vine that turned degenerate.

When Jesus calls Himself the true vine, He means the genuine one, the vine that actually is what Israel was planted to be. He succeeds where the nation failed. The center of God's life on earth is now a Person, and belonging to God's people comes through living union with Him. Jesus also names the roles plainly: He is the vine, "My Father is the vinedresser" (John 15:1), and the disciples are the branches (15:5). The Father who once looked in vain over Israel's vineyard now tends His Son and everyone joined to Him.

John 15:2–5 — Abiding and pruning. A branch cannot produce grapes from a life of its own; it bears through its union with the vine. Jesus states the spiritual reality plainly: “without Me you can do nothing” (15:5). He commands His disciples to abide in Him as He abides in them (15:4). The Father tends this living relationship, pruning fruitful branches so they bear more. Jesus then tells the disciples that they are already clean because of His spoken word (15:2–3). The Greek words for “prunes” and “clean” are closely related, connecting the Father's tending with the cleansing work of Jesus' word. The image carries a serious warning as well: a branch that does not abide withers and is burned (15:6). Living union with Christ is indispensable to fruitfulness.

John 15:7–17 — What abiding looks like. Jesus explains His own image through words, prayer, love, obedience, and joy. His words are to abide in His disciples, shaping what they ask of the Father (15:7). They are to remain in His love by keeping His commands, following His own obedience to the Father (15:9–10). His central command is that they love one another as He has loved them, a love He will show by laying down His life (15:12–13). Fruitfulness therefore reaches into their desires, relationships, and service. It glorifies the Father (15:8), shares Christ's joy (15:11), and produces fruit that remains through the calling He has given them (15:16). The branch image describes a responsive, fruitful life in fellowship with Jesus.

Matthew 26:29 — The fruit of the vine. Over the cup He has just called His blood of the new covenant (26:27–28), Jesus makes a vow: "I will not drink of this fruit of the vine from now on until that day when I drink it new with you in My Father's kingdom." The cup looks back to His sacrifice and forward to a feast. His death breaks table fellowship with His disciples, and His promise guarantees that the fellowship will be restored in glory, with Him at the table.

Revelation 14:19 — The vine of the earth. An angel "thrust his sickle into the earth and gathered the vine of the earth, and threw it into the great winepress of the wrath of God." The previous verse gives the reason the harvest happens now: the grapes are fully ripe (14:18). This vine produces rebellion. Its fruit matures until judgment falls. The branches of the true vine are pruned so they bear more fruit, while the vine of the earth is left to ripen and is then cut down whole.

James 3:12 — The grapevine and the tongue. James asks, "Can a fig tree, my brethren, bear olives, or a grapevine bear figs?" He has just observed that the same mouth blesses God and curses people made in His likeness (3:9–10). A plant produces according to its nature, so speech that swings between praise and cursing shows something wrong at the root of a life.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The vine traces the story of God's people. God plants Israel and looks for fruit, but finds wild grapes. In the fullness of time He sends His Son, who stands in the Upper Room and claims the identity Israel never fully lived out. On the same night, the fruit of the vine in the cup becomes the sign of His blood. Within hours, the true vine goes to be crushed so that His life could be poured out for others. The story moves toward a kingdom feast where Jesus will drink the cup new with His people. Read together, John 15 and Revelation 14 present two vines, one tended by the Father and bearing fruit that remains, the other gathered into the winepress. Neither passage draws that comparison by itself, but together they invite every reader to ask which vine his life is drawing from.

7. Spirit & Life

John 15 gives a weary believer a place to draw life and a clear way to walk. Fruit depends on Christ, so self-reliance and boasting have no place. Yet abiding involves attentive response: receiving His words, bringing desires to the Father in prayer, keeping His commands, and loving His people. These are the ways Jesus Himself develops the branch's relationship to the vine (15:7–17). Dependence upon Him gives obedience its strength and purpose.

The pruning image belongs to fruitful branches. When the Father cuts back a believer's life, often through His word, by exposing a habit, correcting a priority, or removing something that drained strength, He is caring for a branch that already bears fruit so that it can bear more. Pruning is a sign of His attention, not His rejection.

Jesus gave this teaching in the same farewell conversation in which He promised the Spirit of truth, who would dwell with and be in His disciples (John 14:16–17). His departure would not leave them to sustain their life with Him alone. Paul's description of the fruit of the Spirit gives a related view of the character God produces: love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control (Galatians 5:22–23). Paul is using a related fruit image rather than this Greek word for vine. Read together, the passages help believers recognize fruitfulness in a life increasingly shaped by the Spirit and the love of Christ.

James brings this down to everyday speech. Words reveal the root. If the life of the true vine flows in a person, the tongue will over time begin to bear the same kind of fruit.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Mark 14:25 — "Assuredly, I say to you, I will no longer drink of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new in the kingdom of God." Mark's "Assuredly" gives the vow the force of a solemn pledge.

  • Luke 22:18 — "I will not drink of the fruit of the vine until the kingdom of God comes." Luke places the emphasis on the coming of the kingdom itself.

  • The remaining occurrences (Matthew 26:29; John 15:1, 4, 5; James 3:12; Revelation 14:18–19) are discussed above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G0290 (ampelōn), vineyard — a related noun for the plot where vines grow; compare Jesus' parable of the vineyard owner (Matthew 21:33).

  • G2814 (klēma), branch — paired with ámpelos in John 15 as the disciples' role (15:5).

  • G3025 (lēnos), winepress — paired with "the vine of the earth" in Revelation 14:19.

  • H1612 (gephen), vine — the Hebrew word behind Israel's vine imagery (Psalm 80:8; Ezekiel 15:2); a thematic, cross-language connection.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The John 15 picture has limits. Christ is the vine, the source of life for fruit-bearing branches. The passage does not speak of Him as the root, even though Scripture elsewhere calls Him "the Root of David" (Revelation 5:5). The image focuses on living union and fruit, so it should not be stretched into a full description of everything Christ is.

Established biblical imagery. In Ezekiel 15, God asks what vine wood is good for. Unlike forest timber, it cannot be used even for a peg to hang a vessel on. It is either fruitful or fit for the fire (Ezekiel 15:2–5). God applies the picture to the inhabitants of Jerusalem, who had persisted in unfaithfulness and would be given to the fire (15:6–8). Jesus' warning about the cut-off branch that is gathered and burned (John 15:6) draws on the same reality.

Exploratory associations. The contrast with the proud cedar, which Ezekiel 31:3 uses for an imperial power, offers an illustrative resonance. The true vine did not come as a towering tree. He came with no beauty that we should desire Him (Isaiah 53:2), and His whole purpose was to give life through fruit. Grapes yield wine only when crushed, and the Old Testament speaks of "the blood of grapes" (Genesis 49:11; Deuteronomy 32:14). That makes the cup's link to Christ's crushing on the cross deeply fitting, and many believers have found that seasons of pressure are often when fruit forms in their lives. The Old Testament picture of peace as each person sitting under his vine and fig tree (1 Kings 4:25; Micah 4:4) also points toward the kingdom feast Jesus promised at the Supper. Solomon's peace was brief, and these associations suggest a fuller rest to come. These connections are suggestive applications and should not be treated as stated meanings of the word.
