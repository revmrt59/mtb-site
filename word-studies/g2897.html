CAROUSING, DISSIPATION - (kraipálē, Strong's G2897)

Summary

Kraipálē is the Greek word behind "carousing" in Luke 21:34, and it appears nowhere else in the New Testament. In ordinary Greek it described heavy drinking and its aftermath: the dizzy, staggering, dull-headed condition we call a hangover. The word covers both the binge and the stupor that follows.

Jesus uses it in His teaching about the end of the age. He warns His disciples, "take heed to yourselves, lest your hearts be weighed down with carousing, drunkenness, and cares of this life, and that Day come on you unexpectedly." The image is of a heart so dulled and heavy that it cannot respond to God. Jesus places this kind of excess beside the ordinary worries of daily life, because both weigh the heart down. Against this He calls His followers to watch and pray, so that when the Son of Man comes they will be found standing.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kraipálē comes from everyday Greek, where it referred to the physical effects of too much wine. Writers used it for the sickness, heaviness, and confusion after a drinking bout as well as for the carousing that caused them. English translations reflect this range. The KJV's "surfeiting" points to excess, "dissipation" to a wasted condition, and the NKJV's "carousing" to the revelry itself.

Its only biblical setting is Luke 21, where Jesus speaks about the destruction of Jerusalem, the signs of the end, and the coming of the Son of Man. A word from the tavern appears in one of the most solemn discourses in the Gospels.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Kraipálē means drunken excess together with its result. The standard Greek lexicon (BDAG) describes it as including both intoxication or carousing and its aftermath, noting the dizziness and staggering of someone whose head no longer works properly. One definition with two connected aspects is enough:

1. Carousing, drunken indulgence, and the hangover that follows: a state of heaviness, dullness, and impaired alertness brought on by excess (Luke 21:34).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Because the word occurs only once, its biblical meaning comes from Luke 21:34. Two details stand out.

First, Jesus does not speak about bodies being affected by carousing. He speaks about hearts. The heart in Scripture is the center of a person's thinking, desires, and choices. Jesus takes a word for physical stupor and applies it to the inner life.

Second, the verb "weighed down" fits the noun closely. A hangover makes a person heavy, slow, and unresponsive. Jesus uses that experience to describe a heart burdened until it can no longer keep watch.

The word also stands in a short list: "carousing, drunkenness, and cares of this life." Carousing and drunkenness belong together naturally, but the third item is unexpected, and Section 5 takes it up.

4. Theological Significance

This word shows that the Lord takes the condition of His people's hearts seriously in light of His coming. The Day Jesus speaks of is certain and will arrive "unexpectedly." What matters is whether the heart is alert when it comes.

The warning also reveals the Lord's kindness. Jesus does not leave His disciples to discover the danger too late. He names it ahead of time, describes how it works, and gives the remedy in the same breath. He wants them clear-eyed enough to recognize Him.

Finally, the passage shows that spiritual dullness is usually something we take in, not something that simply happens to us. A hangover follows indulgence. The heart becomes heavy by consuming what deadens it, which means the condition can be resisted.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Luke 21:34–36: From a heavy heart to standing before the Son of Man. These three verses move in a clear sequence. It begins inside the person: "lest your hearts be weighed down." It then turns to what happens outside: "that Day come on you unexpectedly. For it will come as a snare on all those who dwell on the face of the whole earth" (vv. 34–35). A snare catches what is not paying attention, and a staggering, groggy person cannot see the trap in front of him. The movement ends with Christ Himself: "Watch therefore, and pray always that you may be counted worthy to escape all these things that will come to pass, and to stand before the Son of Man" (v. 36).

The contrast in posture is plain. Kraipálē pictures a person slumped under a weight, unsteady on his feet, pulled downward. Jesus' goal for His disciples is that they stand: upright, awake, facing the Son of Man. Between those two postures He places watching and praying. Prayer keeps the heart awake to God when everything else would dull it.

Luke 21:34 with Mark 4:19 and Matthew 6:25–34: Indulgence and worry side by side. The most searching detail in Luke 21:34 is that "cares of this life" stands in the same list as carousing and drunkenness. Most people would rank a drinking binge as a serious sin and daily worry as an understandable weakness. Jesus names them together because they produce the same result: a heart weighed down and unready for the Day.

Elsewhere Jesus describes worry in similar terms. In the parable of the sower, "the cares of this world, the deceitfulness of riches, and the desires for other things entering in choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful" (Mark 4:19). In the Sermon on the Mount He tells His disciples, "do not worry about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink" (Matt. 6:25), and turns their attention to "your heavenly Father" who feeds the birds and clothes the lilies (vv. 26–30). The cure for anxiety there matches the cure for heaviness in Luke 21: "seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness" (v. 33).

The point cuts both ways. The person who has never been drunk can still have a heart as heavy as a drunkard's, crowded with bills, plans, and fears. The warning reaches the reckless and the respectable alike.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Luke 21 looks toward the return of Jesus as the Son of Man, the One who will come "with power and great glory" (Luke 21:27). The warning about kraipálē belongs to that hope. The danger is not merely a wasted evening but a wasted readiness, being found asleep when the King arrives.

Paul carries the same teaching into the life of the church using different Greek words. He tells the Thessalonians that when people say "Peace and safety!" sudden destruction will come, "and they shall not escape" (1 Thess. 5:3). But believers "are not in darkness, so that this Day should overtake you as a thief" (v. 4). Drunkenness belongs to the night, "those who get drunk are drunk at night" (v. 7), so "let us who are of the day be sober, putting on the breastplate of faith and love, and as a helmet the hope of salvation" (v. 8). In Romans 13:11–13 Paul likewise urges believers to wake from sleep because salvation is nearer than when they first believed, and to walk properly as in the day, putting away revelry and drunkenness.

The pattern is consistent across these passages. The world's ordinary routine, whether that means partying, working, or worrying, can feel like the only reality. Christ's coming is the reality that will wake everyone. His people are called to live awake now, as children of the day that is about to dawn.

7. Spirit & Life

The picture of a hungover heart helps explain why spiritual dullness is so hard to notice from the inside. A person in a stupor does not feel alarmed; he feels heavy and wants to be left alone. Prayer feels like effort, Scripture seems distant, and the thought of Christ's return seems unreal. Jesus' word suggests that such a state often follows what we have been feeding on. Endless entertainment, constant noise, and indulgence of every appetite can numb the heart as surely as wine numbs the head.

Because Jesus links carousing with "cares of this life," believers cannot measure their readiness only by the vices they avoid. A useful question is what keeps the heart from lifting toward God. For one person it is pleasure. For another it is the mortgage, a sick parent, or a career that never lets up. Matthew 6 shows that the remedy for worry is not to try harder at not worrying but to look at the Father who already knows what we need.

Jesus' remedy is to "watch" and "pray always." Prayer brings the heart back into the presence of the One it will someday stand before. Each time a believer turns to God in the middle of distraction or anxiety, he is practicing the posture Jesus wants to find in him at His coming: awake, upright, and facing Him. This warning is a mercy. Christ wants His people clear-headed enough to recognize Him and glad to stand before Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Kraipálē occurs only in Luke 21:34, which is discussed above. Passages such as 1 Thessalonians 5:6–8 and Romans 13:13 develop the same theme of sober watchfulness, but they use different Greek words and are not further occurrences of this term.
