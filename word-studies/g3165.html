ME, I - (mé, Strong's G3165)

Summary

Mé is the ordinary Greek word for "me" when the speaker receives an action. It most often marks the person being loved, denied, followed, or welcomed. It can also mark the person someone comes toward, or the person who is the subject of an infinitive ("to do," "to be"). In English it usually appears as "me," or as "Me" when Jesus or God is speaking. Sometimes Greek grammar requires an English "I" instead, as in Jesus' question about who the crowds say He is or the jailer's question about what he must do. Noticing this word helps readers see who is on the receiving end of a sentence. In the Gospels, and in John above all, that person is very often Jesus Himself, calling people to respond to Him personally.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Greek shows a word's job in a sentence by changing its form. Mé is the "receiving" form of the first-person pronoun. The speaker who would be "I" as the one acting becomes "me" as the one acted upon. Most often it is the direct object of a verb: someone loves me, denies me, sends me, sees me. It also follows certain prepositions, especially when someone comes "to me." A third use surprises English readers. When a Greek clause is built around an infinitive, that infinitive's subject stands in this same form. Translators must then turn "me" into "I" to make natural English. Mé is the light, unstressed form of the pronoun. Greek writers had a separate fuller form, emé, when they wanted strong emphasis.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

The person receiving an action (direct object)

NKJV: me, Me

This is the most frequent use. In Matthew 10:33 Jesus says, "But whoever denies Me before men, him I will also deny before My Father who is in heaven." The pronoun makes Jesus Himself the one being denied. The issue is loyalty to Him, not to an idea. John 14:15 puts Him in the same place: "If you love Me, keep My commandments." Love for Jesus comes first, and obedience flows from it.

The person toward whom someone moves (object of a preposition)

NKJV: to Me, to me

In Matthew 11:28 the weary are not sent to a program or a place: "Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." The pronoun names the destination. The rest Jesus promises is found in coming to Him.

The subject of an infinitive (rendered "I")

NKJV: I

In Luke 9:18 Jesus asks, "Who do the crowds say that I am?" The Greek runs roughly, "Whom do the crowds say me to be?" In Acts 16:30 the jailer cries, "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?" The Greek is closer to "what is it necessary for me to do?" In both verses English needs "I," but the Greek pronoun is the same word. It marks the person the infinitive's action belongs to.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Watch who stands on the receiving end of the verbs. John's Gospel repeatedly records Jesus speaking of people who come to Him, believe in Him, see Him, hear Him, and love Him. He also speaks of those who hate Him or reject the One who sent Him. The Gospel keeps bringing every response back to one Person. In Matthew 25:35–43 Jesus says that feeding, welcoming, clothing, and visiting "the least of these" was done to Him, and so was neglecting them. The same pronoun appears again and again through that passage.

Paul uses the word just as personally. In Galatians 2:20 he speaks of the Son of God, who loved him and gave Himself for him. The apostle places himself as the one Christ loved. Faith becomes personal at exactly this point, when a reader sees himself on the receiving end of Christ's love.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not read emphasis into this word. NKJV capitalizes "Me" when God or Christ speaks as a mark of reverence, not to show Greek stress. Mé is actually the unstressed form; Greek used emé for emphasis. Also remember that English "me" can stand for other Greek forms, such as moi ("to me") or mou ("of me"). When the NKJV shows "I" or "My," as in Matthew 26:46, the English has simply rearranged the sentence.
