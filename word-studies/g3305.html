YET, NEVERTHELESS, HOWEVER - (méntoi, Strong's G3305)

Summary

Méntoi is a Greek word of contrast. It sets what actually happened beside what we would have expected, or sets a firm truth against a troubling situation. The NKJV usually renders it "yet," "however," or "nevertheless." Five of its eight New Testament uses are in John's Gospel. There it often marks a moment of hesitation: disciples who wonder but stay silent, rulers who believe but will not confess, and a disciple who sees the empty tomb but does not go in. In 2 Timothy 2:19 it marks a turn in the argument. After describing false teachers who were overturning people's faith, Paul writes, "Nevertheless the solid foundation of God stands." When readers notice this word, they can see two realities side by side and understand why the writer wants them held together.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

In most of its uses, méntoi joins two statements that pull against each other. The first statement creates an expectation. The second statement, introduced by méntoi, either holds back from that expectation or overrides it. Ordinary contrast words can simply mark a change of subject. Méntoi does more than that, because it signals a contrast the writer wants us to feel: "this is true, and yet that is also true."

The word works mainly in two settings, with one minor affirming use beside them (see below). In narrative it describes how people actually responded. In argument it introduces a truth strong enough to stand against what has just been said. In both settings the reader should ask the same question: what was expected, and what does the writer set against it?

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Contrast between expectation and response (narrative)

NKJV: "yet," "However," "Nevertheless"

John uses méntoi to show people holding back. In John 4:27 the disciples "marveled that He talked with a woman; yet no one said, 'What do You seek?' or, 'Why are You talking with her?'" Their surprise would naturally lead to questions, and the word marks their silence. At the tomb, the beloved disciple "saw the linen cloths lying there; yet he did not go in" (John 20:5). He had come close enough to see but stopped at the entrance. John 7:13 follows the same pattern. After the crowd's murmuring about Jesus, John adds in paraphrase that no one spoke openly of Him for fear of the Jews. In John 21:4 Jesus stood on the shore, yet the disciples did not know that it was Jesus.

John 12:42 is the most sobering example: "Nevertheless even among the rulers many believed in Him, but because of the Pharisees they did not confess Him." Here the NKJV's "Nevertheless" represents méntoi together with a neighboring Greek word. The verse contrasts the unbelief John has just described with real belief among the rulers. A second contrast follows, because that belief stayed hidden.

A firm truth set against a troubling situation (argument)

NKJV: "Nevertheless"

In 2 Timothy 2:18 Paul names men who "overthrow the faith of some." Then he writes: "Nevertheless the solid foundation of God stands, having this seal: 'The Lord knows those who are His'" (2:19). The word turns the reader from the damage false teaching was doing to what false teaching cannot touch.

Contrast within a comparison

NKJV: "Likewise" (with a separate Greek word)

Jude 8 reads, "Likewise also these dreamers defile the flesh, reject authority, and speak evil of dignitaries." The English "Likewise" mainly reflects a different Greek word that stands beside méntoi. Méntoi adds a note of contrast. Jude has just recalled Israel in the wilderness, the fallen angels, and Sodom. These men do the same things anyway, even with those warnings in plain view.

Affirming emphasis (minor use)

NKJV: "really"

Once, méntoi strengthens a statement instead of setting up a contrast. James writes, "If you really fulfill the royal law according to the Scripture, 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself,' you do well" (James 2:8). Here méntoi supplies the "really" or "indeed": genuine love of neighbor is the standard, and James affirms those who truly keep it before he turns to the sin of partiality.

3. Practical Reading Insight

In John's Gospel, méntoi repeatedly marks the distance between what people see or believe and what they are willing to do about it. Disciples wonder but do not ask. A crowd talks but will not speak openly. Rulers believe but will not confess. A disciple sees but does not enter. John is not cataloguing failures for their own sake. He shows how often fear, uncertainty, or concern for reputation holds back a response that ought to follow. John 12:43 gives the reason plainly: they "loved the praise of men more than the praise of God."

Paul uses the same kind of word to point the other way. False teaching was overturning the faith of some, and that loss was real. Nevertheless, God's foundation stands, and the Lord knows those who are His. The word does not deny the crisis. It sets God's secure work beside it.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not treat "yet" or "nevertheless" as a mild pause. When méntoi stands behind the English in a contrast, name both sides of the contrast before moving on; in James 2:8 it simply adds emphasis. Remember also that English renderings can blend méntoi with neighboring Greek words, as in John 12:42 and Jude 8. The NKJV's "Likewise" in Jude 8 does not show that méntoi itself means "likewise."
