WITH, AFTER - (metá, Strong's G3326)

Summary

Metá is one of the Greek New Testament's most frequent prepositions. It does two main jobs. It can place people or things together, "with," or it can place events in order, "after." The form of the following noun usually tells the reader which job it is doing. As "with," it describes companionship, shared experience, the attitude that accompanies an action, and even conflict when the verb is about fighting. As "after," it marks sequence in narratives, prophecies, and visions. Matthew uses it to open his Gospel with Immanuel, "God with us" (Matthew 1:23). Mark uses it to set the timing of the resurrection, "after three days rise again" (Mark 8:31). Noticing metá helps a reader see who stands beside whom, whose side they are on, and how God's works unfold in order.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Metá links one thing to another, either alongside it or following it. Greek nouns change form to show their role in a sentence, and those forms are called "cases." When metá is followed by the genitive form, it usually means "with." When it is followed by the accusative form, it usually means "after." The surrounding verb then adds the flavor. Eating with someone means fellowship. Going with someone means traveling together. Making war with someone means fighting against them.

Revelation 4:1 shows both senses in a single verse: "After these things I looked... a trumpet speaking with me, saying, 'Come up here, and I will show you things which must take place after this.'" One Greek word marks the order of the vision and the presence of the voice.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Association and companionship (with, among). This is metá's most common use. It places people together in presence, fellowship, or shared activity. John 3:22 says, "there He remained with them and baptized." In Mark 3:14, Jesus appoints the Twelve so that they might be with Him before He sends them out (paraphrase). The order matters: being with Jesus comes before going for Him. The same word appears in the angels' question in Luke 24:5, which asks why the women seek the living among the dead (paraphrase).

Temporal sequence (after, afterward, later). Metá orders events in time. Mark 8:31 says the Son of Man must "be killed, and after three days rise again." Matthew 24:29 begins, "Immediately after the tribulation of those days." In John and Revelation, "after these things" often marks a turn in the account, as in John 3:22 and Revelation 4:1.

Manner or accompanying attitude (with, or an English adverb). Metá can pair an action with the spirit in which it is done. In Hebrews 4:16 the Greek reads literally "with boldness," which the NKJV renders "come boldly to the throne of grace." Hebrews 10:34 reads literally "with joy," rendered "joyfully accepted the plundering of your goods." In both verses the English adverb stands for the whole Greek phrase, so no single English word lines up with metá. Philippians 2:12 ("with fear and trembling") and Philippians 4:6 ("with thanksgiving") work the same way.

Opposition with verbs of conflict (against, with). When the verb is about fighting or going to court, being "with" someone means facing them as an opponent. Revelation 11:7 says the beast "will make war against them." First Corinthians 6:6 grieves that "brother goes to law against brother."

3. Practical Reading Insight

Matthew's Gospel begins and ends with metá. It opens with the promise of Immanuel, "God with us" (Matthew 1:23). It closes with the risen Jesus telling His disciples that He is with them always, to the end of the age (Matthew 28:20, paraphrase). Between those two verses, the One who was promised as God-with-us lives, dies, and rises, and then He sends His people out with the same promise. Revelation 21:3 carries the thought to its end, using metá repeatedly to describe God dwelling with His people. The word is ordinary, but Scripture uses it to state one of its central hopes: God does not stay at a distance.

Metá's "after" also carries weight. The resurrection is not an afterthought to the cross. It comes after the cross in the order God appointed.

4. Reading Guidance

"With" does not always mean friendship. Revelation 17:14 shows this clearly. In that one verse, the kings make war with the Lamb, and those who are with Him are called, chosen, and faithful (paraphrase). The verb shows whether metá describes enemies or allies.

Be careful also with "after three days" (Mark 8:31). Ancient counting normally included partial days. Matthew 27:63–64 shows the chief priests quoting Jesus as saying "after three days" and then asking for a guard "until the third day" (paraphrase), so they treated the two phrases as describing the same timing.
