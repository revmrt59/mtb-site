ANYONE, ANY - (mḗtis, Strong's G3387)

Summary

Mḗtis is a Greek question word that tells you how the speaker expects the question to be answered. It combines a negative with "anyone" or "anything," so it asks, in effect, "No one has done this, have they?" English translations usually render it with a plain "anyone," "any," or "Did," which can make the question sound neutral. In all four New Testament uses, the speaker leans toward "No." Sometimes that "No" is honest puzzlement, as when the disciples wonder who could have fed Jesus. Sometimes it is a confident or scornful dismissal, as when the Pharisees insist that none of the rulers believe in Him. Sometimes it is an appeal to what the hearers already know, as when Paul asks whether Titus ever cheated the Corinthians. Hearing that built-in expectation helps the reader catch each speaker's tone, and it also exposes the irony when a confident "No" turns out to be wrong.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Mḗtis introduces a question that leans one way before anyone answers it. Greek can tilt a yes-or-no question toward a particular answer, and questions opened with this negative form expect "No." The word asks about a person ("anyone," "any of") or a thing ("anything," "any"). It can also attach to the verb so that the whole question tilts toward denial.

This matters because the tilt tells you what the speaker assumes. A question like this reveals the speaker's mind, whether that is confidence, doubt, or a rhetorical challenge. Any English word that renders it, whether "anyone," "any," or "Did," cannot by itself show that lean, so context has to supply it.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Asking about a person: "anyone," "any of"

In John 4:33, after Jesus says He has food they do not know about, "the disciples said to one another, 'Has anyone brought Him anything to eat?'" The question means something like, "Surely no one brought Him food, did they?" Their tone is bewildered rather than hostile. They cannot imagine where food would have come from, and they are still thinking of bread while Jesus is speaking of doing His Father's will (John 4:34, paraphrased).

John 7:48 has a sharper edge. When the temple officers come back without arresting Jesus, the Pharisees demand, "Have any of the rulers or the Pharisees believed in Him?" This is not a request for information. It is a dismissal that expects the officers to agree: "None of us believe in Him." The question works as a put-down, because it implies that only the ignorant crowd could be taken in.

Asking about a thing or an action: "any," "Did"

In John 21:5, the risen Jesus calls from the shore, "Children, have you any food?" They answered Him, "No." Here the expected answer and the actual answer match. Jesus frames the question around their empty nets, and the disciples admit it plainly. That honest "No" sets up the miraculous catch that follows.

In 2 Corinthians 12:18, Paul writes, "Did Titus take advantage of you?" He is defending himself and his co-workers against suspicion about money. The question invites the Corinthians to answer from their own experience: "No, he did not." Paul does not argue the point. He lets the readers testify for him. The next two questions in the verse ("Did we not walk in the same spirit? Did we not walk in the same steps?") lean the opposite way and expect "Yes." The verse moves from one denial to two confident affirmations.

3. Practical Reading Insight

This word shows what a speaker expects. It does not prove that the speaker is right. John 7:48 makes the point clearly. The Pharisees frame their question so that no faithful leader could possibly believe in Jesus. Two verses later, Nicodemus speaks up. John describes him as "one of them," a ruler and a Pharisee who had come to Jesus by night (John 7:50, paraphrased; compare John 3:1–2). He asks that Jesus be given a fair hearing. Later John reports that many even among the rulers believed in Him, though they did not confess it openly for fear of the Pharisees (John 12:42, paraphrased). Nicodemus also comes with spices at Jesus' burial (John 19:39). The confident "No" rested on pride, and the story itself answers it.

The other passages reward the same attention to tone. The disciples' puzzled question in John 4 shows how slowly they grasped what Jesus meant by His food. Paul's question in 2 Corinthians 12 shows a pastor appealing to the church's own memory rather than to his authority. Reading these questions with the right tone turns them from flat inquiries into windows on the people asking them.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not read every "any" or "anyone" question as a neutral request for facts, and do not overcorrect by hearing hostility in all of them. The expected "No" can carry wonder, scorn, honest admission, or rhetorical appeal. The surrounding scene decides which. Remember also that the grammar records the speaker's expectation, not the truth of the matter. A question built to expect "No" may be answered in the narrative with a resounding "Yes."
