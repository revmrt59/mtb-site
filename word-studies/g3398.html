SMALL, LITTLE, LEAST - (mikrós, Strong's G3398)

Summary

Mikrós is the ordinary Greek word for "small" or "little." It can describe the size of a seed, the height of a man, a short stretch of time, or a person's low standing. The NKJV usually renders it "little," "least," or "small." It is a plain, everyday word, but Jesus and the apostles use it at some of the most revealing moments in the New Testament.

Jesus calls His followers "little ones" and "little flock," and He speaks of them with fierce tenderness. The Father does not want one of them to perish, and He takes pleasure in giving them the kingdom. Jesus says the one who is least will be great. The risen Christ opens a door that no one can shut for a church with "a little strength." The pairing "small and great" gathers all people before God in judgment and in worship. The word also carries warnings, because a little leaven and the tongue, "a little member," do great damage. And "a little while" marks the time believers must wait before the Lord comes. Again and again, the things the world overlooks turn out to be where God is at work.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mikrós is a common adjective in Greek, the natural opposite of "great." Most of those uses are in the Gospels and Revelation, with a handful in Acts and the letters. The settings vary widely: farm illustrations, crowd scenes, church discipline, prophetic promises, and visions of the final judgment. Because the word is so ordinary, it takes its weight from what it describes. Small seeds, small people, and short waits all carry significance because of who is speaking about them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mikrós means small in size, amount, time, or importance. Context decides which kind of smallness is in view.

1. Small in size or amount: a seed "smaller than all the seeds on earth" (Mark 4:31), Zacchaeus being "of short stature" (Luke 19:3), the tongue as "a little member" (James 3:5).

2. Low in rank or standing: "least" in contrast to "great" (Luke 9:48), including the phrase "small and great," which means everyone (Revelation 20:12).

3. Short in time: "a little while longer" (John 7:33; Revelation 6:11).

Jesus' affectionate phrase "little ones" belongs to the second sense. Its tenderness comes from the way He uses it rather than from the word itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Gospels, Jesus uses mikrós to turn human ideas of importance upside down. The "little ones who believe in Me" (Mark 9:42) are disciples, not only children. They are small because the world counts them small.

Paul and James use the word as a warning about influence. Something slight in size can be large in effect, as with leaven in dough (1 Corinthians 5:6; Galatians 5:9) or the tongue, "a little member" that James compares to a small fire setting a forest ablaze (James 3:5).

When used for time, mikrós frames waiting. Jesus' time on earth was short (John 7:33; 12:35), believers wait only a little while for the Lord's coming (Hebrews 10:37), and the martyrs rest a little while longer (Revelation 6:11). Even Satan's final release lasts only "a little while" (Revelation 20:3). God measures out every season, including evil's last one.

4. Theological Significance

The phrase "small and great" shows how God sees humanity. Societies rank people. The God of Scripture gathers them together. The phrase appears where God's grace reaches His people: "all shall know Me, from the least of them to the greatest of them" (Hebrews 8:11). It appears where His people worship: "Praise our God, all you His servants and those who fear Him, both small and great!" (Revelation 19:5). And it appears where all people answer to Him: "I saw the dead, small and great, standing before God" (Revelation 20:12). No title shortens the line at the throne, and no obscurity removes anyone from it. Revelation 11:18 even promises reward to "those who fear Your name, small and great." God sees the faithfulness of the unknown as clearly as that of the prominent.

The same phrase also shows how easily people are swept along together. In Samaria, everyone listened to Simon the sorcerer "from the least to the greatest," calling him "the great power of God" (Acts 8:10). The beast forces "all, both small and great" to take his mark (Revelation 13:16). Human unity apart from God is a crowd being led astray. The only safe gathering of small and great is one that bows before the true God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Matthew 18:6–14 — The Father's protection of the little ones. The disciples have just asked who is greatest, and Jesus has set a child in front of them (Matthew 18:1–5). He then speaks about "these little ones," and every statement guards them. Whoever causes one of them to stumble would be better off drowned with a millstone around his neck (18:6; Mark 9:42). No one should despise them, because their angels always see the face of the Father in heaven (18:10). Then Jesus tells of the shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to search for one that has strayed (18:12–13). He closes by saying that it is not the Father's will that one of these little ones should perish (18:14). The lost sheep parable sits between two statements about little ones. The straying sheep is one of them. People may overlook a weak believer, but heaven does not. Even a cup of cold water given to "one of these little ones" receives its reward (Matthew 10:42).

Luke 9:48 with 7:28 — The least will be great. In Luke 9:46, the disciples argue over rank. Jesus answers with a child: "Whoever receives this little child in My name receives Me... For he who is least among you all will be great." Receiving the small one means receiving Christ and the Father who sent Him. Earlier, Jesus called John the Baptist the greatest of prophets, yet "he who is least in the kingdom of God is greater than he" (Luke 7:28). This does not lower John's standing. It shows how great it is to belong to the kingdom Jesus brings. Greatness there comes from receiving, not from climbing.

Luke 12:32 — The little flock and the Father's pleasure. "Do not fear, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom." Jesus has just told His disciples not to worry about food and clothing but to seek God's kingdom (12:22–31). The flock is small and defenseless, and the gift is the kingdom itself. The reason for the gift lies in the Father's delight, not in the flock's strength. Because the Father gives so freely, the next verse can tell them to sell their goods and give alms (12:33). People who have been given a kingdom can hold their possessions loosely.

Revelation 3:8 — An open door for little strength. The risen Christ, who holds "the key of David" (3:7), tells the church in Philadelphia that He has set before them an open door that no one can shut. He gives the reason: they have a little strength, yet they have kept His word and not denied His name. He does not criticize their weakness or tell them to become impressive first. What He values is faithfulness: holding to His word and confessing His name. The door stays open because of who holds the key.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The mustard seed, "smaller than all the seeds on earth" (Mark 4:31), describes how the kingdom begins: one Galilean teacher, a few disciples, and a cross. It grows until it gives shelter to others. Christ Himself came in lowliness, and His kingdom follows that same pattern.

Hebrews 8:11 quotes Jeremiah's promise of a new covenant (Jeremiah 31:34). Under that covenant, knowing the Lord would no longer belong to an elite few but to everyone "from the least of them to the greatest." Christ, the Mediator of that covenant, fulfills the promise. The least believer knows God personally.

The phrase "a little while" stretches across the story of redemption. Jesus' earthly presence was a short window of light (John 12:35). His people now wait, encouraged by Hebrews 10:37, which echoes Habakkuk's call to wait for the vision: "For yet a little while, And He who is coming will come and will not tarry." The martyrs wait a little longer (Revelation 6:11). Seen from God's side, where "one day is as a thousand years" (2 Peter 3:8), the whole age of waiting is short. The story ends with small and great standing before His throne.

7. Spirit & Life

These passages free believers from the pressure to become impressive before they can be useful or loved. The Father protects the little ones, gives the kingdom to the little flock, and opens a door for a church with little strength. In each case, the smallness is real, and God's response is personal and generous. A believer who feels weak, overlooked, or unimportant is exactly the kind of person Jesus described with this word. His call is to faithfulness, to keep His word and not deny His name, rather than to gain influence.

The same word also warns believers. Paul tells the boasting Corinthians that "a little leaven leavens the whole lump" (1 Corinthians 5:6). He is confronting tolerated sin in the church. James says "the tongue is a little member and boasts great things. See how great a forest a little fire kindles!" (James 3:5). In that verse mikrós describes the tongue; the smallness of the fire is expressed with a different Greek word. Small compromises and careless words spread far. So believers must take small sins seriously and small people seriously. Christ's warnings in Matthew 18 apply to both, since the one who causes a little one to stumble often does it through something that seemed minor.

The phrase "a little while" also helps believers endure. Suffering feels long while it lasts. Scripture measures it against God's timetable and calls it brief, because He who is coming will come.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 19:3 — Zacchaeus "was of short stature" and could not see over the crowd. Jesus stopped and called the small man by name.

  • Mark 15:40 — Among the women at the cross is Mary the mother of "James the Less." The title probably separated him from another James, whether by height or age. His mother is remembered among the faithful women at Calvary.

  • Acts 26:22 — Paul testifies before Agrippa that he has spoken "both to small and great," saying only what the prophets and Moses foretold. The gospel did not change for the audience.

  • 2 Corinthians 11:1, 16 — Paul asks the Corinthians to bear with "a little" of his folly as he reluctantly boasts. He is shaming those who prize self-promotion.

  • Revelation 19:18 — The flesh of "small and great" is given to the birds after the final battle. Those who oppose God share one end, whatever their rank.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G3173 (megas), "great" — a canonical pairing. Mikrós and megas are paired in the phrase "small and great," which means everyone (Hebrews 8:11; Revelation 20:12). They are also contrasted in Jesus' teaching on greatness (Luke 9:48).
