REMEMBER, BE MINDFUL - (mimnḗskō, Strong's G3403)

Summary

Mimnḗskō means to remember, to call something to mind, or to be mindful of someone. It appears only four times in the New Testament, and in each one memory does something. A worshiper remembers a wronged brother and leaves his gift at the altar. God is mindful of frail humanity, and Hebrews moves directly to Jesus tasting death for everyone. Believers are told to remember prisoners as if chained with them, and to remember the apostles' words when false teachers creep into the church.

Together these verses show that remembering in Scripture is attention that leads to action. When God remembers people, He acts for them. Hebrews shows the fullest form of that care in the Son who became one of us. When God's people remember, they make peace, stand with those who suffer, and hold to the truth already given. A healthy memory, in the Bible's sense, keeps God's reality in front of us and calls for a response.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mimnḗskō is a Greek verb from the family of words built around remembering. It is closely related to mnaomai (G3415), which also means "to remember." In the New Testament it appears in four settings: worship at the altar in Matthew, a psalm about God's care for humanity, a command about prisoners in Hebrews, and a warning against false teachers in Jude.

Other related words carry much of the New Testament's language about remembering. This verb opens a small window onto that larger theme, but through it we see four important parts of life with God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to bring someone or something to mind. Two closely related senses appear:

1. To recall or call to mind something known before, such as a brother's grievance suddenly recalled at the altar (Matthew 5:23) or the apostles' earlier warnings (Jude 17).

2. To be mindful of a person: to keep someone in mind with concern, as God is mindful of man (Hebrews 2:6) and believers are to remember prisoners (Hebrews 13:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What changes most from verse to verse is who is remembering. When God is the subject in Hebrews 2:6, the second line of the verse supplies the meaning: "that You take care of him." For God, to be mindful is to care.

With human subjects, the word works in two ways. In Matthew 5:23 the memory comes unbidden in the middle of an act of worship. In Hebrews 13:3 and Jude 17, "remember" is a command. God holds His people responsible for what they keep in mind.

Hebrews 13 places two commands side by side: "Do not forget to entertain strangers" (v. 2) and "Remember the prisoners" (v. 3). Forgetting leads to neglect. Remembering leads to care.

4. Theological Significance

When God "remembers," it never means He had forgotten. It means He turns toward people to bless, sustain, and deliver them. In Psalm 8:4 the parallel line reads "that You visit him," and in Hebrews "that You take care of him." Either way, God's attention leads to God's action. The Creator of the heavens thinks about small, mortal people, and His thinking about them is never idle.

The human uses show that memory is a moral matter. What we bring to mind before God, in our relationships, toward suffering believers, and in the face of false teaching, is not neutral. In all four passages, remembering brings past truth or present reality to bear on the moment and asks for a response.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Hebrews 2:6–9 — God's mindfulness takes flesh. Hebrews quotes Psalm 8: "What is man that You are mindful of him, Or the son of man that You take care of him?" The psalm marvels that God crowned man "with glory and honor" and set all things "in subjection under his feet." Then the writer of Hebrews admits what everyone can see: "But now we do not yet see all things put under him." Humanity has not reached that glory. It lives under sin and death.

Then the turn: "But we see Jesus, who was made a little lower than the angels, for the suffering of death crowned with glory and honor, that He, by the grace of God, might taste death for everyone" (v. 9). The psalm's question finds its answer in a Person. God's mindfulness of humanity did not stay in heaven as kindly thought. It sent the Son into our condition.

The chapter goes on to say that because the children share flesh and blood, He shared the same. He did this to destroy the one who held the power of death, and He was made like His brethren so He could be a merciful and faithful High Priest (Hebrews 2:14–17, paraphrased). God's remembering us went as far as the cross.

Matthew 5:23–24 — Remembering at the altar. Jesus has just traced murder back to anger and contempt (vv. 21–22). Then He pictures a worshiper standing at the altar who "there remember[s] that your brother has something against you." Notice who holds the grievance. It is the brother who has something against you. You are the one who caused the offense.

Jesus' instruction is startling: "leave your gift there before the altar, and go your way. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift." The offering is delayed, not cancelled. God wants the gift, but He wants the broken relationship mended first.

It fits the passage that this memory comes precisely while the worshiper is drawing near to God. His presence tends to bring to light what we have been content to leave buried. John makes the same point plainly: whoever does not love the brother he has seen cannot love the God he has not seen (1 John 4:20).

Hebrews 13:3 — Remembering as solidarity. "Remember the prisoners as if chained with them—those who are mistreated—since you yourselves are in the body also." The phrase "as if chained with them" asks believers to imagine themselves in the cell and to let that move them. These readers knew what this meant. Earlier they had shown compassion to prisoners and accepted the seizure of their own goods (Hebrews 10:34, paraphrased).

The reason given is "since you yourselves are in the body also." Most naturally this means that you, too, live in a body that can be chained and beaten. Some take it as a reference to the body of Christ. Either reading leads to the truth Paul states: "if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it" (1 Corinthians 12:26).

Jude 17 — Remembering against deception. Certain men had "crept in unnoticed," turning "the grace of our God into lewdness" (Jude 4). Jude's answer is not a new strategy: "But you, beloved, remember the words which were spoken before by the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ." The apostles had already warned "that there would be mockers in the last time" (v. 18). The deception was real, but it was not a surprise. Jude then calls believers to keep building themselves up "on your most holy faith, praying in the Holy Spirit" (v. 20), and to keep themselves in God's love (v. 21). A church that knows what the apostles said is not easily carried off by what intruders say.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The whole biblical story can be told as God remembering His people. He remembers His promises, hears their cries, and comes to save. Hebrews 2 shows where that story leads: the God who is mindful of man becomes a man. Psalm 8's wonder at human dignity is fulfilled in Jesus, the true Son of Man, who was crowned with glory after suffering death for us.

The other uses fit within this story. Jude's readers are to remember the words of the apostles "of our Lord Jesus Christ," so the church's memory is anchored in Christ's own teaching. Hebrews 13:3 also echoes chapter 2. The Son did not stay at a distance from those in bondage; He shared our flesh and blood. When believers remember prisoners "as if chained with them," they take up the same kind of love Christ showed in coming near.

7. Spirit & Life

These four verses overturn the idea that memory is mostly nostalgia. In Scripture, holy remembering interrupts us. It stops us at the altar when a wronged brother comes to mind. It disturbs our comfort by placing us, in our imagination and our prayers, beside believers in chains. It cuts through confusion by bringing back what the apostles already said.

The order matters. All of it rests on Hebrews 2:6. Before we are told to remember anyone, God has remembered us, and His remembering cost Him His Son. A believer who knows he has been remembered like that has reason to let God direct his own memory.

In practice, this means letting God's presence bring a broken relationship to mind and then going to make it right. It means praying for persecuted believers by name and supporting them as though their chains were ours. It means knowing the apostles' writings well enough that they come to mind when error sounds convincing.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This word occurs only four times in the New Testament: Matthew 5:23, Hebrews 2:6, Hebrews 13:3, and Jude 17. All four are developed in Section 5, so no further occurrences remain to survey.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

Lexical relatives:

  • mnaomai (G3415): "to remember," closely related to mimnḗskō. It is used in the thief's request to Jesus (Luke 23:42).

  • mnēmoneuō (G3421): "to remember, keep in mind," as in Paul's call to remember Jesus Christ (2 Timothy 2:8).

  • anamimnēskō (G363): "to remind, call back to mind" (2 Timothy 1:6).

  • hypomimnēskō (G5279): "to remind, bring to remembrance," used of the Spirit's work in John 14:26.

Canonical pairing:

  • zakar (H2142): the Hebrew "remember" in Psalm 8:4, the verse Hebrews 2:6 quotes. It links this word to the Old Testament's rich language of God remembering His people.
