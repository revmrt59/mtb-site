REMEMBER, BE MINDFUL - (mnáomai, Strong's G3415)

Summary

Mnáomai means "to remember," "to recall," or "to be mindful of." In the New Testament it describes people bringing a past word or event back to mind. Peter remembers Jesus' warning after his denials. The disciples remember His words after the resurrection. A dying thief asks Jesus to remember him. Again and again, remembering in these passages leads somewhere: to tears, to faith, to steadfastness, or to regret.

The word also describes God remembering. God never forgets anything, so when Scripture says He remembers, it means He acts on what He has promised or on what He has seen. Mary and Zacharias praise God for remembering His mercy and His holy covenant by sending the Messiah. Hebrews then announces the other side of that mercy: God says of His people's sins, "I will remember no more." The God whose memory sets rescue in motion freely chooses not to hold forgiven sin against those who belong to Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mnáomai belongs to a common Greek family of words about memory and mindfulness. It appears nineteen times in the New Testament, mostly in the Gospels, with further uses in Acts, several letters, and Revelation. The settings vary widely. The word appears in songs of praise at Jesus' birth, at the trial and the cross, at the empty tomb, in an angel's message to a Roman centurion, in Paul's affectionate letters, and in a vision of final judgment. The New Testament writers inherited a way of speaking from the Old Testament, where God "remembered" Noah (Genesis 8:1) and "remembered His covenant" with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Exodus 2:24). In both passages, rescue followed immediately.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, mnáomai means to call something to mind. Context determines the nuance:

1. To recall a past word or event, bringing it back to conscious thought, as when the women at the tomb "remembered His words" (Luke 24:8).

2. To keep someone or something in mind with care and regard, as when Paul tells Timothy he is mindful of his tears (2 Timothy 1:4).

3. Of God, to take note of someone or something and act toward them, whether in mercy or judgment, as when Cornelius hears, "your alms are remembered in the sight of God" (Acts 10:31).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Human remembering in the New Testament is rarely idle. Recall often comes as a turning point. A sound, a command, or a fulfilled event brings back a word, and the person suddenly sees what they could not see before.

Some uses are commands or requests, and these carry real weight. The angels say, "Remember how He spoke to you" (Luke 24:6). Abraham tells the rich man, "Son, remember" (Luke 16:25). The thief prays, "Lord, remember me" (Luke 23:42). Each one calls for memory to do its work, whether that work is faith, accountability, or mercy.

When God is the subject, the word moves from thought to action. To be "remembered before God" can bring blessing or wrath, depending on what He remembers.

4. Theological Significance

God's remembering is never the recovery of something lost. It is His faithfulness going into action. What He has promised, He performs. What He has seen, He answers.

Two verses show this with sobering balance. To Cornelius, a Gentile who prayed and gave generously, the angel says, "your alms are remembered in the sight of God" (Acts 10:31). That remembrance did not end in a pleasant feeling. It sent Peter to his house with the gospel. In Revelation, the same language turns toward judgment: "great Babylon was remembered before God, to give her the cup of the wine of the fierceness of His wrath" (Revelation 16:19). Nothing slips past Him. A seeker's quiet prayers are not lost, and the long record of a proud, unrepentant world is not lost either. God's memory guarantees that righteousness will be answered and evil will be judged with perfect fairness.

This makes the promise in Hebrews all the more astonishing. The God who misses nothing chooses, for His people, to remember their sins no more.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Luke 1:54, 72 and Hebrews 8:12; 10:17 — God remembers mercy and forgets sin. Mary sings that God "has helped His servant Israel, in remembrance of His mercy" (Luke 1:54). Zacharias praises Him "to perform the mercy promised to our fathers and to remember His holy covenant" (Luke 1:72). Notice how Zacharias pairs "perform" with "remember." For God, the two belong together. The birth of John and the coming of Jesus are what God's remembering looks like. Centuries of promise did not fade. Then Hebrews, quoting Jeremiah's promise of a new covenant, records God saying, "their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more" (Hebrews 8:12). The writer repeats it in Hebrews 10:17 to close his argument that Christ's one sacrifice has fully dealt with sin. The all-knowing God does not suffer amnesia here. He makes a binding decision never again to bring forgiven sin against His people. He remembers His mercy, and so He refuses to remember their guilt.

Matthew 26:75 — memory that breaks a proud heart. After Peter's third denial the rooster crows. He remembers the exact word Jesus had spoken: before the rooster crowed, he would deny Him three times. He goes out and weeps bitterly. Only hours earlier Peter had insisted he would never fall away. The recalled word of Christ cut through that confidence. Peter's weeping was the beginning of repentance, and Jesus would later restore him.

Luke 23:42 and Luke 16:25 — remembered by the King, or tormented by one's own memory. The dying thief brings nothing to Jesus but a request: "Lord, remember me when You come into Your kingdom." To be remembered by a king meant to receive his favor. The thief stakes everything on Christ's grace, and Jesus promises him paradise that very day. Set this beside the rich man in Hades, to whom Abraham says, "Son, remember that in your lifetime you received your good things" (Luke 16:25). The thief asks to be remembered and receives life. The rich man is told to remember, and his own memory becomes part of his torment.

John 2:17, 22; 12:16; Luke 24:6, 8; Acts 11:16 — remembering after the resurrection. John is honest about how little the disciples understood at first. When Jesus cleansed the temple, "His disciples remembered that it was written, 'Zeal for Your house has eaten Me up'" (John 2:17). His saying about raising the temple made sense only later: "when He had risen from the dead, His disciples remembered that He had said this to them; and they believed the Scripture and the word which Jesus had said" (John 2:22). At the triumphal entry, "His disciples did not understand these things at first; but when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered" (John 12:16). At the empty tomb the angels' command, "Remember how He spoke to you," brought the women to the moment when "they remembered His words" (Luke 24:6, 8). Even after Pentecost the pattern continues. When the Spirit fell on Cornelius's household, Peter says, "Then I remembered the word of the Lord" (Acts 11:16). Jesus had promised that the Holy Spirit would "bring to your remembrance all things that I said to you" (John 14:26). What looked like confusion at the time was seed already sown. The resurrection and the Spirit's work turned remembered words into faith.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

God's remembering runs through the whole story of redemption. He remembered Noah in the flood and Israel in Egypt, and rescue followed. Luke opens his Gospel by announcing that God has remembered once more, this time in the coming of the Messiah. At the cross, the remembering that people longed for becomes personal, as a condemned man asks Jesus Himself to remember him. Jesus, the King, now holds the power of remembering. In the risen Christ the disciples finally understand the Scriptures they had heard, because the Spirit helps them remember. Hebrews shows the result: in Christ's sacrifice, God's mercy reaches its goal in a promise to remember sins no more. Revelation closes the picture with the certainty that unrepentant evil will one day be remembered before God for judgment. In every stage, Christ stands at the center. His coming fulfills what God remembered, His cross brings forgiveness that God will not take back, and His return brings the final reckoning.

7. Spirit & Life

Peter, who once wept over a remembered word, later wrote to strengthen the church through remembering. He urged believers to be mindful of the words spoken before by the holy prophets and of the commandment of the apostles (2 Peter 3:2). Then he warned that scoffers would come in the last days, mocking the promise of Christ's return (2 Peter 3:3–4). His answer to their cynicism was not a new argument. He called the church to hold fast to the word God had already given. A believer who keeps Scripture in mind has an anchor when the culture laughs at the idea that Jesus is coming again.

The disciples' experience also gives hope to anyone who reads Scripture and does not yet understand it. Words heard today may come back, opened by the Spirit, in a season of need or crisis. Nothing Christ has spoken to His people is wasted.

Most of all, the believer's security does not rest on how well we remember God. We forget Him often. It rests on His choice to remember us, as He remembered the thief, while He refuses to remember our forgiven sins. We can pray the thief's prayer with the same confidence, and we can stop rehearsing guilt that God Himself has promised to remember no more.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 27:63 — The chief priests and Pharisees tell Pilate they remember Jesus' prediction that He would rise after three days, and they use that memory to seal the tomb. They recalled the word perfectly but refused to believe it.

  • 1 Corinthians 11:2 — "Now I praise you, brethren, that you remember me in all things and keep the traditions just as I delivered them to you." Remembering the apostle shows up in faithfully keeping what he taught.

  • 2 Timothy 1:4 — Paul longs to see Timothy, being mindful of his tears. Here the word expresses loving remembrance between a spiritual father and his son in the faith.
