REMEMBER, CALL TO MIND, MAKE MENTION - (mnēmoneúō, Strong's G3421)

Summary

Mnēmoneúō is a common New Testament verb meaning "to remember." Scripture rarely uses it for idle recollection. Most often it describes calling something to mind so that it changes what a person believes, feels, or does right now. Jesus asks His anxious disciples, "Do you not remember?" about the loaves (Mark 8:18). He tells them to remember His warnings so that persecution will not catch them off guard (John 16:4). Paul asks the churches to remember the poor, his chains, and the gospel he preached. The risen Christ tells failing churches to remember where they came from and repent (Revelation 2:5).

Memory, then, is a spiritual battlefield. Forgetting what God has done opens the door to fear, drift, and deception. Remembering rightly strengthens faith, starts repentance, and steadies believers in suffering. Remembering wrongly is also possible: longing for the life God called us out of ("Remember Lot's wife," Luke 17:32). At the far end of the story, God Himself "has remembered" Babylon's iniquities (Revelation 18:5) and acts in judgment, while He promises His forgiven people that their sins He "will remember no more" (Hebrews 8:12).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mnēmoneúō belongs to a family of Greek words connected with memory and memorials. It appears across the Gospels, Acts, Paul's letters, Hebrews, and Revelation. It shows up most often in moments of pressure: disciples worried about bread, elders facing coming false teachers, churches drifting from their first love, believers enduring persecution.

The biblical way of speaking about memory lies behind these uses. In the Old Testament, when God remembers Noah or His promises, He acts. When Israel is told to remember the Lord, the point is faithful living. New Testament remembering carries the same practical weight.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to call to mind, to remember, or to keep someone or something in mind. Context brings out three distinct senses:

1. To recall something past so that it affects the present. Jesus asks, "Do you not yet understand, or remember the five loaves of the five thousand and how many baskets you took up?" (Matthew 16:9).

2. To keep a person or group in mind with concern. This is the sense in "Remember my chains" (Colossians 4:18) and the request that Paul remember the poor (Galatians 2:10).

3. To mention, to speak of something remembered. Hebrews 11:22 says Joseph, when dying, "made mention of the departure of the children of Israel."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Two patterns stand out. The first is the command, "Remember," which Jesus, Paul, and the writer of Hebrews use when people are tempted to lose their footing. The second is the reproachful question, "Do you not remember?" Jesus uses it with the disciples (Mark 8:18), and Paul uses it with the Thessalonians after they were unsettled about the Lord's coming (2 Thessalonians 2:5). Both patterns treat forgetting as a failure the hearer is responsible for. The disciples and the Thessalonians had been given what they needed. They had let it slip.

The object of remembering makes the difference. Remembering God's works and words builds faith. Calling to mind "that country from which they had come out" (Hebrews 11:15) pulls the heart backward. When God is the one who remembers (Revelation 18:5), the word moves from human recollection to divine action.

4. Theological Significance

When God remembers, He acts. "For her sins have reached to heaven, and God has remembered her iniquities" (Revelation 18:5). God has not suddenly recalled something He had forgotten. Babylon's sins have piled up until His patience gives way to judgment. Set beside this is the promise of the new covenant, which uses a different Greek verb for remembering: "their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more" (Hebrews 8:12). God is not forgetful. He chooses not to bring His people's sins forward for judgment, because those sins have been dealt with. The same God who remembers unrepented sin and judges it also promises to remember forgiven sin no more.

When people remember, repentance can begin. The risen Christ gives two failing churches the same first step. To Ephesus He says, "Remember therefore from where you have fallen; repent and do the first works" (Revelation 2:5). To Sardis He says, "Remember therefore how you have received and heard; hold fast and repent" (Revelation 3:3). Spiritual decline rarely comes through one dramatic denial. It comes through gradual forgetting, and Christ begins restoration by sending His people back to what they once knew. Paul uses remembrance in a related way when he tells Gentile believers to "remember that you, once Gentiles in the flesh" were far from God (Ephesians 2:11). Remembering what grace saved them from keeps that grace from becoming ordinary to them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Matthew 16:9 and Mark 8:18 — forgetting fuels fear. The disciples were in a boat arguing because they had brought only one loaf. Jesus had just fed five thousand and then four thousand, with baskets left over each time. His rebuke traces their anxiety to a failure of memory: "Having eyes, do you not see? And having ears, do you not hear? And do you not remember?" They treated a small shortage as a new emergency, as if they had never seen what Jesus could do. The evidence was fresh. They simply did not bring it to bear on the moment. In Jesus' teaching, faith in a crisis depends on remembering what He has already shown Himself to be.

John 15:20; 16:4; 2 Timothy 2:8 — memory as an anchor in persecution. On the night before the cross, Jesus told His disciples to remember His words. "Remember the word that I said to you, 'A servant is not greater than his master.' If they persecuted Me, they will also persecute you" (John 15:20). He explains why He is telling them now: "that when the time comes, you may remember that I told you of them" (John 16:4). Suffering foretold by the Lord does not mean He has lost control. When hostility came, the disciples could recall that Jesus had named it in advance, and their trust in Him would hold.

Paul carries this forward from prison. Writing to Timothy as he faces hardship, he gives one thing to hold on to: "Remember that Jesus Christ, of the seed of David, was raised from the dead according to my gospel" (2 Timothy 2:8). Pain tends to fill the whole view. Paul directs Timothy's mind to the risen Christ, the promised Son of David who went through death and came out alive. Jesus' forewarnings show that suffering is no surprise to Him, and His resurrection shows that suffering does not have the last word. Together they give a persecuted believer firm footing.

Luke 17:32 and Hebrews 11:15 — the wrong kind of remembering. Some remembering is dangerous. Speaking of the day the Son of Man is revealed, Jesus says simply, "Remember Lot's wife." She left Sodom with her feet, but her heart looked back, and she perished. Hebrews 11:15 describes the opposite in the patriarchs: "if they had called to mind that country from which they had come out, they would have had opportunity to return." Their faith showed in what they refused to dwell on. One kind of remembering keeps a pilgrim moving toward God's promise. The other kind is longing for the old life, and it slowly turns the pilgrim around.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Remembering in Scripture keeps pointing God's people to His saving acts, and in the New Testament those acts center on Jesus. The disciples were to remember the loaves He multiplied. They were to remember His words before the cross. Timothy was to remember Him "raised from the dead." Paul's phrase "of the seed of David" ties Christ's resurrection to God's long-standing promise to David's line. When Timothy remembers Jesus, he remembers the whole story reaching its fulfillment.

Hebrews 11:22 shows this forward-looking memory in the Old Testament. The dying Joseph "made mention of the departure of the children of Israel." Remembering God's promise to Abraham, he spoke of an exodus that had not yet happened and left instructions for his bones. His memory looked forward. Believers live the same way, remembering the cross and the empty tomb while looking for Christ's return.

The story ends with both sides of divine remembrance. God remembers Babylon's iniquities and judges her (Revelation 18:5). Through Christ, God remembers His people's sins no more (Hebrews 8:12). What separates the two is whether those sins have been answered for at the cross.

7. Spirit & Life

To remember is to act, and in the church that often means sharing someone's burden. The Jerusalem leaders asked Paul to remember the poor, and he says this was the very thing he was eager to do (Galatians 2:10). That eagerness led to collections and journeys. "Remember my chains" (Colossians 4:18), written in Paul's own hand, asked for prayer and loyalty to a prisoner. "Remember those who rule over you, who have spoken the word of God to you, whose faith follow" (Hebrews 13:7) links remembering to imitation. Remembering a fellow believer means doing something for them.

Remembering sound teaching guards the church. Paul told the Ephesian elders that savage wolves would come, then said, "Therefore watch, and remember that for three years I did not cease to warn everyone night and day with tears" (Acts 20:31). Truth heard once and set aside offers little protection. Truth kept in mind exposes error when it arrives.

Joseph's example shows that remembering can be spoken. He did not keep God's promise to himself. He put it into words for the generation after him. Families and churches remember well when they say aloud what God has done and promised.

Jesus also gives a picture of how present grief will one day give way to joy. A woman in labor has sorrow, "but as soon as she has given birth to the child, she no longer remembers the anguish, for joy" (John 16:21). Jesus told His grieving disciples that their sorrow would turn to joy. The pain is real, and Jesus does not deny it. Yet the joy of seeing Him will so fill the heart that the memory of the anguish loses its hold.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Thessalonians 1:3 — Paul gives thanks, "remembering without ceasing your work of faith, labor of love, and patience of hope." His prayers dwell on the evidence of God's work in the church.

  • 1 Thessalonians 2:9 — "For you remember, brethren, our labor and toil." Paul appeals to what the church saw with its own eyes: he worked night and day so that he would not be a burden to them.

  • 2 Thessalonians 2:5 — "Do you not remember that when I was still with you I told you these things?" Paul steadies a confused church by sending them back to teaching they had already received.

  • Acts 20:35 — Paul tells the Ephesian elders to remember the words of the Lord Jesus that it is more blessed to give than to receive. He ties his own example of supporting the weak to Christ's teaching.
