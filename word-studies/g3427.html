TO ME, FOR ME, ME - (moí, Strong's G3427)

Summary

When the Greek New Testament says "to me" or "for me" without special stress, it usually uses moí. The word is the ordinary first-person pronoun in the form Greek uses for the person who receives something, benefits from something, or is affected by it. The NKJV usually renders it "me" or "to me." It also appears where English needs "I" or "my," because Greek often expresses ownership as "something is to me." Seeing how moí works helps readers follow who gives, who receives, who is followed, and who is affected in a sentence. It also explains why one Greek form can appear in English as "me," "My," or "I." In the Gospels, many of these occurrences come from Jesus Himself. He speaks of what the Father has given "to Me," and He calls disciples to "Follow Me."

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Moí is a personal pronoun. It points back to the speaker, but it does more than say "me." Its form tells the reader how the speaker relates to the action. Usually the speaker receives something, such as a gift, a word, or authority. Sometimes the speaker is the person the action concerns or the one it benefits. With certain verbs, moí names the person being followed or worshiped. In a few settings it marks the owner of something.

It is also a light, unstressed form. It rides along in the sentence without drawing attention to itself. Greek has a separate, stressed form for when a speaker wants to say "me, and no one else."

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Receiving something given or spoken ("to me," "me")

This is the most frequent pattern. Jesus says, "All things have been delivered to Me by My Father" (Matthew 11:27). Here moí names Jesus as the One who receives what the Father hands over. Speech works the same way. In "Not everyone who says to Me, 'Lord, Lord,' shall enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 7:21), moí marks the person being addressed.

The person followed or worshiped ("me")

Some Greek verbs take their object in this receiving form, even where English treats the word as a plain direct object. When Jesus saw Matthew at the tax office, He said, "Follow Me" (Matthew 9:9). The same call appears to Levi in Mark 2:14 and to Peter in John 21:19, 22. In the wilderness, Satan offered Jesus the kingdoms of the world "if You will fall down and worship me" (Matthew 4:9). Both following and worship are directed toward a person, and moí names that person.

Possession ("I have," "my")

Greek often says "X is to me" where English says "I have X" or "my X." Peter's reply to the lame man reads, "Silver and gold I do not have" (Acts 3:6). Read woodenly, it says silver and gold do not belong to me. When Jesus asked the man in the tombs his name, the reply was, "My name is Legion" (Mark 5:9), which reads literally as "Legion is the name to me." The NKJV correctly turns both expressions into natural English.

The person concerned or affected ("to me," "for me")

Sometimes moí marks the person an event concerns. Mary answered the angel, "Let it be to me according to your word" (Luke 1:38). She names herself as the one in whom God's promised work will take place.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Tracking this pronoun shows how often the Gospels portray Jesus as the One who receives from the Father. Matthew 11:27 says all things are delivered to Him. In His prayer in John 17, Jesus repeatedly speaks of the people and the words the Father has given Him. After the resurrection, He announces that all authority has been given to Him (Matthew 28:18).

Set that beside Matthew 4:9. Satan offered the world's kingdoms on the condition that Jesus bow and worship "me." Jesus refused the shortcut. He then received from the Father the authority the tempter falsely offered. The grammar itself is ordinary. The contrast comes from Matthew's story, but following the "to me" in each scene brings that story into focus.

Mary's words in Luke 1:38 carry a quieter lesson. "Let it be to me" is the language of someone willing to have God's word fulfilled in her own life.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not read special emphasis into moí. It is the unstressed form. When the New Testament stresses "me," it typically uses emoí (G1698), even though English renders both as "me." Also, an English "I" or "my" may stand for this Greek form, as in Acts 3:6 and Mark 5:9. Translation does not always match the original word for word.
