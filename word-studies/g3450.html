MY, MINE, OF ME - (moû, Strong's G3450)

Summary

Moû is the Greek pronoun most often behind the "my" in the New Testament. It names what belongs to the speaker or is bound to the speaker: "My beloved Son," "these sayings of Mine," "My brethren." Greek grammar also calls for this same form after certain prepositions and verbs, and in comparisons, so English sometimes renders it "Me" or "I" instead. Noticing it helps readers hear who is claiming whom. When the Father says "My beloved Son," or the risen Jesus says "My Father and your Father," a single word of belonging carries the weight of the relationship.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Moû is the ordinary, unstressed "of me" form of the Greek first-person pronoun. Most of the time it attaches to a noun and marks that noun as the speaker's own. That may be a possession, a body part, a family member, a friend, or words the speaker has spoken. Greek also uses this same "of me" form where English would simply say "me." It appears after prepositions such as "after," after verbs like "touch," and in comparisons such as "greater than I." In every case, it points back to the person speaking. English translators then choose "my," "mine," "me," or "I" according to what sounds natural in English.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Belonging and relationship ("My," "my," "Mine")

This is the dominant use. At Jesus' baptism the Father speaks: "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:17). The word does more than label ownership. It declares a relationship, and the Father owns the Son publicly before His ministry begins. The same function ties words to the speaker who stands behind them: "whoever hears these sayings of Mine, and does them" (Matthew 7:24). These are Jesus' own sayings, and what the hearer does with them is a response to Him.

After prepositions ("Me," "me")

Some Greek prepositions require this form. Jesus' call to the fishermen is literally "come after me," which the NKJV renders "Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men" (Matthew 4:19). The pronoun marks Jesus as the One to follow.

With verbs that take this form ("Me")

Certain verbs, including "touch" and "hear," take their object in this form. When the woman reaches for His garment, Jesus says, "Somebody touched Me" (Luke 8:46). The second "Me" in that verse, "power going out from Me," represents a different Greek form, so only the first belongs here. The same verb lies behind His words to Mary Magdalene: "Do not cling to Me" (John 20:17). Before the ascension, Jesus reminds the disciples of the Promise of the Father, "which," He said, "you have heard from Me" (Acts 1:4). Here the Greek verb for hearing takes moû directly; English supplies "from." The pronoun identifies Jesus as the source from whom they heard the promise.

In comparisons ("than I")

Greek can express "greater than" by placing the person compared in this form. John the Baptist says that the One coming after him is mightier than he is (Matthew 3:11, paraphrased). Jesus says that His Father is greater than He is (John 14:28, paraphrased). Here the pronoun is the standard of measure in the comparison.

3. Practical Reading Insight

John 20:17 shows how much this word can carry. On resurrection morning Jesus tells Mary, "go to My brethren and say to them, 'I am ascending to My Father and your Father, and to My God and your God.'" The disciples had scattered and failed Him only days before. Yet the risen Lord calls them "My brethren," which claims them as family.

He also chooses His words carefully. He does not say "our Father." He says "My Father and your Father," which places the disciples within His family relationship while keeping His own relationship to the Father distinct. The grammar alone does not settle the full doctrine of the Son. Still, the passage plainly presents the Father as truly theirs because He is first and uniquely Jesus' Father. Reading the "My" with care lets this grace come through without blurring the difference between the Son and those He brings home.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume every English "my" or "me" represents moû. In Luke 8:46 two English "Me"s stand for different Greek forms. In John 15:11, "My joy" uses the related possessive adjective emē, rather than moû. Moû is the ordinary, unemphatic form, so readers should not build special emphasis on it alone. Let the sentence and context show the weight.
