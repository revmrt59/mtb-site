YES, EVEN SO, SURELY - (naí, Strong's G3483)

Summary

Naí is the New Testament's word for "yes." Most often it answers a question, as when Peter tells the risen Jesus, "Yes, Lord; You know that I love You." It also does more. A speaker can use it to drive home a point, to answer his own question in an argument, or to give solemn agreement to what God has said or done. In the NKJV the same word appears as "Yes," "Even so," and "Surely." In Jesus' teaching and in Paul's letters, "yes" also becomes a test of truthfulness, a word that should mean exactly what it says. Paul takes that one step further and declares that in Christ all God's promises are "Yes." Noticing this word lets readers hear when a person is giving a real answer, when a writer is pressing a point, and when God's people are saying their wholehearted "yes" to Him.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Naí affirms. It confirms that what was asked or said is true, and it puts the speaker on record in agreement. In conversation it stands at the head of a reply, often with "Lord." In teaching and argument it reinforces what comes next, much as English speakers say "Yes, indeed." In worship and prophecy it becomes a solemn endorsement of God's word or judgment.

Because the word is so plain, it can stand for honesty itself. Jesus and James treat a person's "yes" as something that should carry its full weight with no oath attached. Paul treats God's "yes" in Christ as the guarantee that every promise will stand.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Answering a question (NKJV "Yes")

This is the everyday use. When Jesus asks the two blind men whether they believe He can heal them, they answer "Yes, Lord" (Matthew 9:28). At the lakeshore, Peter replies, "Yes, Lord; You know that I love You" (John 21:15). Peter's earlier boasts have failed, and here his answer rests on what Jesus knows rather than on his own claims. Martha likewise answers Jesus with a "yes" that becomes a confession of Him as the Christ (John 11:27, paraphrased).

Reinforcing a statement or answering one's own question (NKJV "Yes")

Jesus uses naí to press a point home. John the Baptist is a prophet, and Jesus adds a "yes" before saying he is more than a prophet (Matthew 11:9; Luke 7:26). In Luke 12:5 a "yes" doubles the warning to fear God. Paul uses it to answer his own rhetorical question: God is the God of the Gentiles too, "yes," of the Gentiles also (Romans 3:29, paraphrased). In Philemon 20 the word turns a request into a warm, personal appeal.

Solemn agreement with God (NKJV "Even so," "Yes," "Surely")

Here the word answers God rather than a question. Jesus thanks the Father for hiding these things from the wise and revealing them to babes, then affirms the Father's choice: "Even so, Father," for it pleased Him (Matthew 11:26; Luke 10:21, paraphrased). In Revelation, a voice from the altar agrees that God's judgments are true and righteous (16:7), and the Spirit confirms the blessing on those who die in the Lord (14:13). After the announcement of Christ's coming, the prophet responds: "Even so, Amen" (Revelation 1:7). In Revelation 22:20 the word sounds twice: "Surely I am coming quickly." Amen. Even so, come, Lord Jesus!

"Yes" as a word of truthfulness (NKJV "Yes," used as a noun)

Jesus says, "But let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No'" (Matthew 5:37), and James repeats the command against oaths (James 5:12). The word itself becomes the thing promised. A disciple's plain yes should be as binding as any oath.

3. Practical Reading Insight

In 2 Corinthians 1:17–20, Paul faces the charge that he changed his travel plans because he was fickle. He asks whether he plans "according to the flesh, that with me there should be Yes, Yes, and No, No." In other words, was he saying both at once? His answer reaches past his calendar to God: "as God is faithful, our word to you was not Yes and No." Paul's message about Jesus could not be wavering, because Jesus Himself "was not Yes and No, but in Him was Yes." Then comes the great statement: "all the promises of God in Him are Yes, and in Him Amen, to the glory of God through us."

God says "Yes" to His promises in Christ, and the church answers "Amen." Revelation closes with the same pattern. Christ says, "Surely I am coming quickly," and His people answer, "Even so, come, Lord Jesus!" When believers keep their own plain "yes," they reflect a God who has never wavered on His word.

4. Reading Guidance

"Even so" and "Surely" can hide the fact that the Greek word is an ordinary "yes." That connection is worth noticing, but not every "yes" carries theological weight. Context decides whether it is a simple reply or a solemn endorsement. The doubled "Yes, Yes" also works differently in each setting. In Matthew 5:37 it means a yes that holds. In 2 Corinthians 1:17 it describes saying yes and no at the same time. "Amen" is a separate word that often stands beside naí.
