THE, WHO, WHICH - (ho, Strong's G3588)

Summary

The Greek word ho is the definite article, normally translated "the." In many New Testament verses, though, it does more than point to a noun that is already there. It creates one. When the article is placed before a verb form, an adjective, a phrase, or a name, that expression becomes a person, a group, or a thing the reader can talk about. English has no single equivalent for this, so the NKJV renders these uses as "who," "which," "those," "the things," and sometimes even "son" or "wife." Seeing the article behind these words shows how often the New Testament identifies people by what they do or are, as in "those who hunger" and "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world." It also shows how a single short phrase can gather a whole area of life together, as in "the things of the Spirit."

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Most of the time ho simply marks a noun as definite, much like English "the." Greek writers could also use the article on its own, without a noun, to turn almost anything into a noun phrase. They could place it before a participle (a verb form that works like an adjective), an adjective, a phrase beginning with a preposition, or a name in the possessive form. The result names someone or something by a description. Greek can say "the hungering ones," where English needs "those who hunger." The verses in this study are mostly of that kind. In the NKJV, the article in these verses usually disappears into words like "who," "which," or "things," even though no separate relative pronoun stands in the Greek.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Naming people by what they do or are (NKJV: who, those, whom)

The article often identifies people by an action or quality. The article joins a participle or adjective, and the action or trait becomes the person's identity. "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness" (Matthew 5:6). Jesus is not describing a passing appetite. He names a kind of person, the hungering ones, and pronounces them blessed. The same construction can identify a single person. "Behold! The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Here John the Baptist uses the article and participle to make "the one taking away sin" a title that tells who Jesus is.

Gathering things into a category (NKJV: things, which, what)

When the article is neuter, it can bundle together whatever belongs to a person or realm without naming any particular object. Romans 8:5 contrasts minds set "on the things of the flesh" with minds set on "the things of the Spirit." Paul supplies no list. The article alone points to everything that belongs to each sphere. In the NKJV of Matthew 6:34, "tomorrow will worry about its own things" works the same way. The phrase covers whatever concerns tomorrow will bring. Jesus' answer about the coin uses a similar construction for what belongs to Caesar and what belongs to God (Matthew 22:21, paraphrased).

Implying a family relationship (NKJV: son, wife, mother)

Before a name in the possessive form, the article can signal a well-known relationship that Greek leaves unstated. In Mark 3:17, "James the son of Zebedee" is literally "James the [one] of Zebedee." English needs the word "son," and the translators supply it. Matthew 1:6 is more striking. Solomon was born "by her who had been the wife of Uriah." Matthew writes only "by the [woman] of Uriah." He never names Bathsheba, and that phrasing keeps Uriah, the man David wronged, in plain view within the genealogy of the Messiah.

3. Practical Reading Insight

This construction repeatedly identifies people by what defines them. When John calls Jesus "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world," the article turns that work into a name. Taking away sin is not one thing Jesus happens to do. It is who He is. Revelation pushes this pattern further. God is "the One who is and who was and who is to come" (Revelation 1:8, paraphrased), and John even places the article before an ordinary past-tense verb, "the was," so that God's eternal existence becomes a title.

The same article raises a searching question in Romans 8:5. "The things of the flesh" and "the things of the Spirit" are not two short lists of activities. They are two whole directions of the mind, two sets of concerns a person keeps returning to. Paul's question goes deeper than which behaviors to avoid. He is asking what we are habitually oriented toward.

4. Reading Guidance

The NKJV's "who" or "which" can represent two different Greek constructions. Sometimes it is a true relative pronoun (hos). Often, as in this study, it is the article with a participle. Be careful not to over-press that participle, though. Its tense alone does not prove continuous action. Context must also decide whether "those who" means a specific group or anyone who fits the description. In family phrases, the relationship word is supplied by translators from context.
