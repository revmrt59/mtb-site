BUILD, EDIFY - (oikodoméō, Strong's G3618)

Summary

Oikodoméō is the ordinary Greek verb for building: putting up a house, a barn, a tower, a tomb, or a temple. In the Gospels it usually keeps that literal sense, and Jesus uses it to tell some of His most searching parables. Two men build houses, one on rock and one on sand. A rich fool tears down his barns to build bigger ones. A man begins a tower he cannot finish. In the letters of Paul and Peter, the same verb describes people being built up. English translations usually render this "edify," though the building picture remains.

The word gathers around one great promise. Jesus said He would build His church, and the gates of Hades would not prevail against it. Around that promise stand the religious "builders" who rejected God's chosen stone, the believers who are themselves being built into a spiritual house, and the church members who build one another up through love. The word also carries a warning. People can build barns, tombs, and false confidence, and they can even "build up" a weaker believer toward sin. The question is always who is building, and on what foundation.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Oikodoméō is a Greek verb built from the familiar word for "house." In practice it means to build anything at all. The New Testament uses it for houses, a synagogue, a city set on a hill, a vineyard tower, tombs, and Herod's temple. Most of the literal uses come in the Gospels, especially in Matthew and Luke. Acts uses the word both ways: Solomon built a house, and the churches were edified. In Paul's letters and in 1 Peter, the word nearly always describes building people and communities.

The English word "edify" comes from Latin for "build," but in modern speech it has softened to mean something like "instruct" or "encourage." The Greek verb is sturdier than that. When Paul says love edifies, he is still talking about building.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to build or construct. Context decides what is being built and how.

1. To build a physical structure. This means constructing a house, barn, tower, tomb, or temple: "a man building a house, who dug deep and laid the foundation on the rock" (Luke 6:48).

2. To build or establish a community as a dwelling. The verb describes forming God's people as a house, whether Christ building His church (Matthew 16:18) or believers being built up as a spiritual house (1 Peter 2:5).

3. To build up a person or group inwardly. This means to strengthen, establish, or edify: "he who prophesies edifies the church" (1 Corinthians 14:4). The building can also go the wrong way. In 1 Corinthians 8:10 the NKJV renders the verb "emboldened," describing a weak conscience being built up toward eating idol food.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What changes most in the word's use is the builder. When human beings build in the Gospels, Jesus usually examines what they are building and why. The rich fool builds bigger barns for himself (Luke 12:18). The lawyers and Pharisees build tombs for the prophets their fathers killed (Luke 11:47; Matthew 23:29). The Jews in John 2:20 point to forty-six years of temple construction.

One recurring phrase deserves notice. "The builders" in Psalm 118:22 becomes a title for Israel's leaders. It is quoted in Matthew 21:42, Mark 12:10, Luke 20:17, Acts 4:11, and 1 Peter 2:7. In the letters, the object of building moves from structures to people. Paul uses the word to judge whether knowledge, liberty, and spiritual gifts actually strengthen others (1 Corinthians 8:1; 10:23; 14:4, 17).

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these passages teach that God is the true builder and that every other building is tested by its foundation. Human construction in Scripture often looks impressive and proves empty. There are barns that cannot secure a soul, tombs that honor dead prophets while the living One is rejected, and a temple whose builders boasted of decades of labor. Against all of it stands the One who says I will build.

The word also shows that God's building is relational. The house He builds is made of people, and the mortar is love. Acts 9:31 shows what this looked like in the early church: "the churches throughout all Judea, Galilee, and Samaria had peace and were edified. And walking in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit, they were multiplied." Luke ties the strengthening of the church to reverence for God and the Spirit's comfort before he mentions numbers. Growth in size followed growth in strength.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The two builders (Matthew 7:24–27; Luke 6:47–49). Jesus ends the Sermon on the Mount with two men who both build. Nothing in the story suggests one house looked better than the other. The difference lay underground. In Matthew, the wise builder is the one who hears Jesus' sayings and does them. The foolish builder hears the same words and does not act on them. Luke adds that the wise man "dug deep and laid the foundation on the rock," and when the flood came the stream "could not shake it" (Luke 6:48). Hearing alone is building on sand. Both men face the same storm, and the storm does not create the difference between them. It reveals it.

Christ the builder (Matthew 16:18). After Peter confesses Jesus as the Christ, the Son of the living God, Jesus promises that He will build His church and that the gates of Hades will not prevail against it. Every element of the sentence matters. The builder is Jesus Himself. The church belongs to Him; He calls it "My church." The promise looks forward, so the building is still in progress. Its security rests on His power, since the most fearsome gates imaginable cannot hold out against it. Believers will later build one another up, but they work inside a project that is His from foundation to completion. A church that forgets this begins to think it must hold itself together. A church that remembers it can labor without despair.

The stone the builders rejected (Psalm 118:22; Matthew 21:42; Acts 4:11). Jesus applies this psalm to Himself at the end of the parable of the wicked vinedressers. "Builders" is a professional word. A builder does not stumble over a stone by accident. He inspects it, judges it unfit, and sets it aside. Israel's leaders were entrusted with God's house, and they examined God's chosen stone and threw Him out. God overruled their judgment and made that very stone "the chief cornerstone." Peter later stands before the same council that condemned Jesus and says it to their faces: "This is the 'stone which was rejected by you builders, which has become the chief cornerstone'" (Acts 4:11). Their authority to appraise had become the measure of their guilt.

Love builds, knowledge inflates (1 Corinthians 8:1, 10; 10:23; 14:4). Paul writes, "Knowledge puffs up, but love edifies" (8:1). Inflated things look large but carry no weight. Love builds something that bears weight in another person. Paul makes this his test for Christian liberty: "all things are lawful for me, but not all things edify" (10:23). He applies the same test to worship. Tongues without interpretation build up the speaker, while prophecy builds up the church (14:4).

Then, in 8:10, Paul uses the same verb in a sobering way. A believer with knowledge eats in an idol's temple, and the weak brother's conscience is "emboldened," literally built up, to do the same. The verb that describes love's work here describes construction toward ruin. We are always building something in one another. Liberty used without love can build a structure of excuses in a weaker believer that ends in his fall.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word traces a story of God's house. Stephen recalls that "Solomon built Him a house" (Acts 7:47), and then he quotes the Lord's own question: "What house will you build for Me?" (Acts 7:49). The God whose throne is heaven was never confined to stone.

The Gospels bring that question to Jesus. John's only use of the word comes from His opponents: "It has taken forty-six years to build this temple, and will You raise it up in three days?" (John 2:20). Decades of human building are set beside three days of God raising His Son, for "He was speaking of the temple of His body." At His trial, false witnesses twisted His words into a claim that He would destroy the temple and build it in three days. Mark records the contrast between a temple made with hands and another made without hands (Mark 14:58). Mockers at the cross repeated the charge (Matthew 27:40; Mark 15:29). They meant it as ridicule, yet they were describing what God was about to do through the death and resurrection of Jesus.

On the far side of the resurrection, the building continues in people. Peter writes that believers, coming to Christ the living stone, are themselves being built up as a spiritual house and a holy priesthood (1 Peter 2:5). Two verses later he quotes the rejected stone once more (2:7). Paul's picture in Ephesians 2:20–22 uses related compound words rather than this exact verb, but it runs parallel: believers "are being built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit." The house Solomon built pointed forward to the dwelling God was always after, which is His people gathered around His Son.

7. Spirit & Life

Jesus' woe about tombs exposes a temptation that never goes away. The Pharisees honored dead prophets with fine monuments while their hearts resisted the living word God was speaking. "You build the tombs of the prophets, and your fathers killed them" (Luke 11:47). Religion can still prefer revelation safely sealed in the past to a Lord who speaks now and expects obedience. Peter's living stones are the opposite of those tombs.

Other buildings in Scripture fail in their own ways. The rich fool's bigger barns provided security for everything except his soul (Luke 12:18). The man who begins a tower without counting the cost becomes a public ruin (Luke 14:28–30), and Jesus uses him to warn against beginning discipleship on enthusiasm alone. In Galatians 2:18, Paul says that rebuilding the system of works he had torn down would make him a transgressor. Some things are meant to stay demolished.

The constructive side is just as concrete. Paul's aim was to preach "not where Christ was named, lest I should build on another man's foundation" (Romans 15:20). He laid foundations and trusted others to build on them. Ordinary believers share in this work: "comfort each other and edify one another, just as you also are doing" (1 Thessalonians 5:11). Before speaking, acting, or exercising a freedom, a believer can ask whether this will bear weight in someone else's faith. Acts 9:31 shows the conditions under which that building flourishes: the fear of the Lord and the comfort of the Holy Spirit.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 7:5 – Jewish elders commend a Roman centurion who loved their nation and built them a synagogue, a rare case of a Gentile building for Israel's worship.

  • Luke 4:29 – The people of Nazareth drive Jesus to the brow of the hill on which their city was built, intending to throw Him down.

  • Luke 17:28 – In the days of Lot, people bought, sold, planted, and built, absorbed in ordinary life until judgment fell. Jesus warns that His coming will find people the same way.

  • Matthew 21:33; Mark 12:1 – The landowner plants a vineyard and builds a tower, showing the care God lavished on His people before the vinedressers rejected His Son.

  • 1 Corinthians 14:17 – A person may give thanks well in a tongue, yet the other person is not edified, because building up the church requires understanding.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Symbolic Use

Scripture makes one building symbol explicit. Peter identifies "you builders" of Psalm 118:22 as the council that rejected Jesus (Acts 4:11). The identification belongs to the Psalm 118 quotations and does not mean every builder in Scripture represents a false leader.

Some building images are well established across Scripture. A foundation stands for what a life rests on (Luke 6:48). Building tombs pictures religion that honors dead revelation (Luke 11:47). A house of living stones represents God's dwelling among His people (1 Peter 2:5).

Other associations are exploratory and should be held more loosely. Setting forty-six years of temple building beside three days of resurrection (John 2:20) fits John's irony well. Some readers hear an echo of Babel in Luke 14's unfinished tower, but Jesus does not draw that connection. The tower may simply be a farm watchtower, though the warning against self-reliant beginnings stands either way.
