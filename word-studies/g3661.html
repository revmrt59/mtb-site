WITH ONE ACCORD, WITH ONE MIND - (homothymadón, Strong's G3661)

Summary

Homothymadón describes a group of people acting together with one mind, one purpose, or one shared impulse. The NKJV usually translates it "with one accord," and once "with one mind." It describes more than people standing in the same room or agreeing on paper. It pictures a company of people moved by the same inner drive and acting as one.

Luke uses this word throughout Acts. It marks the praying disciples before Pentecost, the gathered church when the Spirit came, the believers' daily life together, their prayer under threat, and the hard-won agreement of the Jerusalem council. Luke also uses the same word for mobs that rushed at Stephen and seized Paul's companions, so the word does not make unity good by itself. Everything depends on what moves the crowd. Paul's single use in Romans 15:6 shows the purpose behind the church's unity. He prays that believers who have learned to bear with one another may "with one mind and one mouth glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Homothymadón is a Greek adverb. It describes the manner in which people act. It does not name a thing. In the New Testament it belongs almost entirely to Luke's account in Acts. Romans 15:6 is the only use outside that book. Greek writers used it for crowds, assemblies, and groups that moved or spoke together. The Greek Old Testament uses it for Israel's corporate response at Sinai, where "all the people answered together and said, 'All that the LORD has spoken we will do'" (Exodus 19:8). Behind the New Testament word, then, stands the picture of God's people responding to Him as one body.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means acting with a shared mind, purpose, or impulse. It stresses inner agreement expressed in common action, and it is morally neutral. The word tells us that a group is united. The context tells us whether that unity comes from God or from human anger.

1. With one mind or purpose: a shared inner agreement that leads to common action, as when the Jerusalem leaders reached a decision "being assembled with one accord" (Acts 15:25).

2. All together, as one body: a group moving or acting at the same moment with a common impulse, as when Stephen's hearers "ran at him with one accord" (Acts 7:57).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Luke's uses run along two opposing tracks. Along the first, the believers are "with one accord" in prayer, at Pentecost, in daily fellowship, in public gatherings, and in decision-making. Along the second, the word describes crowds united in hostility against Stephen, against Paul in Corinth, and against Paul's companions in Ephesus. A few uses are neutral, describing a crowd listening to Philip or a delegation seeking peace with Herod.

The subject of the verb changes the meaning of the scene. When the subject is the church at prayer, the word describes Spirit-given harmony. When the subject is an angry crowd, it describes collective rage. Luke reports both with the same word and lets the reader compare them.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God forms a people, not merely a collection of saved individuals. In Acts the Spirit does not only fill people one by one. He produces a community that prays, worships, decides, and endures together. The unity in Acts is a fruit of His work. It grows from shared devotion to Christ, the apostles' teaching, and prayer (compare Acts 2:42–46). Organization and enforced sameness did not produce it.

The same pages also show a darker unity. People can be gripped by a common impulse that opposes God. Stephen's hearers, the Jews in Corinth, and the Ephesian crowd all acted "with one accord." Fallen human nature can produce powerful agreement around wounded pride, threatened income, or rejected truth. That kind of agreement destroys. The unity the Spirit gives builds up, prays, and glorifies God. Because the word stays the same, the reader is forced to look past mere agreement and ask who holds a group together.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Acts 1:14 and 2:1 — From rivalry to one accord. On the night Jesus was betrayed, the disciples were still arguing: "there was also a dispute among them, as to which of them should be considered the greatest" (Luke 22:24). A few weeks later, "These all continued with one accord in prayer and supplication, with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and with His brothers" (Acts 1:14). The company includes Jesus' brothers, who had not believed in Him during His ministry (John 7:5). The cross and the resurrection had humbled the disciples and drawn these men into one company. Jesus had told them to wait for the Promise of the Father, and they waited together in prayer. When Pentecost came, "they were all with one accord in one place" (Acts 2:1). Afterward the same harmony marked ordinary life: "continuing daily with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house, they ate their food with gladness and simplicity of heart" (Acts 2:46). It also marked their public witness, as they gathered "with one accord in Solomon's Porch" while signs and wonders were done among the people (Acts 5:12). Their unity began in prayer and then carried them through daily life and public witness.

Acts 4:24 — One voice under threat. After the council threatened Peter and John, the believers "raised their voice to God with one accord and said: 'Lord, You are God, who made heaven and earth and the sea, and all that is in them.'" Fear might have scattered them, or they might have quarreled over strategy. Instead, pressure drove them together toward God. They began with His sovereignty as Creator. They read their situation through Psalm 2, where the nations rage against the Lord and His Anointed. Then they asked for boldness to keep speaking His word rather than for safety (Acts 4:25–29). Their shared convictions about God gave their prayer its single voice.

Acts 15:25 — Agreement that came through dispute. The Jerusalem council met over a question that could have split the church: what is required of Gentile believers? Luke honestly records much debate (15:7). The leaders did not reach agreement by silencing the conflict. Peter told how God gave the Spirit to Gentiles. Paul and Barnabas reported what God had done among them. James showed that the prophets agreed with this. Only then could the letter say, "it seemed good to us, being assembled with one accord." A few verses later they credit the Holy Spirit as well (15:28). Unity in the Spirit does not mean believers never disagree. They work through disagreement by submitting together to God's Word and to what He is plainly doing.

Romans 15:6 — Unity for the sake of worship. Paul's only use of the word stands at the end of a long section about believers who disagree over food and holy days (Romans 14:1–15:5). He tells the strong not to despise the weak and the weak not to judge the strong, "for God has received him" (14:3). He reminds them that "none of us lives to himself" (14:7). He appeals to Christ, who did not please Himself (15:3). Then he prays that God would grant them to be like-minded toward one another "that you may with one mind and one mouth glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (15:6). The patient bearing of one another's scruples serves worship. One mind inside the church makes possible one mouth praising God.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

At Sinai Israel answered "together" that they would do all the LORD had spoken (Exodus 19:8), yet their history shows how quickly that shared resolve broke apart. In Acts a new kind of unity appears, and it comes from the risen Christ pouring out the Spirit He promised. The disciples who once competed for greatness become one praying company because Jesus has died, risen, and sent the Spirit.

Romans 15 roots this unity in Christ Himself. Believers receive one another as Christ received them (15:7), and their agreement is "according to Christ Jesus" (15:5). The goal reaches past the church's peace to the glory of "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ." A people from different backgrounds, praising the Father with one voice through the Son, shows in advance the gathered worship toward which God's whole plan is moving.

7. Spirit & Life

Luke's two kinds of "one accord" give believers a searching question about their own fellowship: what is actually holding us together? Shared grievance, a common enemy, or group pressure can make a congregation look united while it acts like the crowds in Acts. The church's one accord began on its knees, with people who had stopped asking who was greatest.

This unity is also tougher than it looks. It held when the authorities threatened, and it held when leaders disagreed sharply about the gospel. In both cases the believers turned toward God rather than toward self-protection or winning the argument. The way to "one mind" still leads through prayer, through Scripture, and through honest testimony of what God is doing.

Romans 14–15 brings this into everyday church life. Bearing with a believer whose convictions differ from yours, refusing to despise or judge, and laying aside your own preferences for another person's good are not small social courtesies. Paul treats them as preparation for worship. When such patience produces one mind, the church can glorify God with one mouth.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 8:6 — As Philip preached in Samaria, the crowds with one accord paid close attention to his words while hearing and seeing the miracles he did. A whole city was drawn together to hear the gospel.

  • Acts 12:20 — The people of Tyre and Sidon came to Herod with one accord to seek peace. Here the word simply describes a united delegation, showing its ordinary use.

  • Acts 18:12 — "the Jews with one accord rose up against Paul and brought him to the judgment seat." Hostility to the gospel produced its own kind of unanimity.

  • Acts 19:29 — "the whole city was filled with confusion, and rushed into the theater with one accord." The Ephesian riot, stirred up by silversmiths whose trade was threatened, shows a crowd moving as one while most of its members did not even know why they had come (19:32).
