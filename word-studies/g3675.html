OF ONE MIND, LIKE-MINDED - (homóphrōn, Strong's G3675)

Summary

Homóphrōn means "of one mind" or "like-minded." It describes people who share the same basic outlook, purpose, and inner direction. The word appears only once in the New Testament, in 1 Peter 3:8: "Finally, all of you be of one mind, having compassion for one another; love as brothers, be tenderhearted, be courteous." Because the word appears only there, that verse and its setting define what Peter means by it.

Peter's "one mind" is a shared heart that shows itself in compassion, brotherly love, and tenderness, and it does not require identical opinions. He places the command at the close of a long section on yielding to others for the Lord's sake, and just before he tells a pressured church not to repay insult with insult. A church that shares the mind of Christ can stand together under hostility and answer its enemies with blessing.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Homóphrōn joins homos ("same") with a word family built on phrēn, which concerns the mind and the way a person thinks and is inclined. Paul expresses the same idea often, but he uses a phrase built on the verb phroneō, meaning "think the same thing." Peter uses this single adjective, and it occurs nowhere else in the New Testament.

Peter wrote to believers scattered as "exiles" across Asia Minor, people slandered and pressured by their neighbors. His letter repeatedly asks how a holy people should live among those who misunderstand them. Homóphrōn belongs to that practical, pastoral setting.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means sharing the same mind: being agreed in outlook, harmonious in purpose, united in inner direction. It describes people who are pulling in the same direction. It does not describe people who have lost their individuality or settled every question alike. A single sense covers its one use, and context supplies the rest of its color.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In 1 Peter 3:8, homóphrōn heads a string of five short descriptions addressed to "all of you." The NKJV renders them as commands: be of one mind, have compassion, love as brothers, be tenderhearted, be courteous. The words that follow fill out the meaning of "one mind." Shared thinking in this verse travels together with shared feeling and kindness.

The last word in the list varies among Greek manuscripts. The text behind the NKJV reads "courteous" (literally "friendly-minded"), while many others read "humble-minded." Either way, the verse opens and closes with a "-minded" word, so that the inner life Peter begins with is the inner life he ends with.

4. Theological Significance

The God who calls His people to one mind does not unite them by forcing everyone into the same opinions. Peter defines unity by compassion and brotherly love, and that reveals how God intends His family to hold together: through hearts turned toward one another's good. Paul says the same thing in Romans 12. He places "Be of the same mind toward one another" (Romans 12:16) right after "Rejoice with those who rejoice, and weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15), and right before warnings against pride: "associate with the humble. Do not be wise in your own opinion." In both apostles, a shared mind means sharing one another's joys and griefs. It is the opposite of an arrogant mind that keeps its distance.

Two errors stand exposed here. A church can agree on every doctrine while its members remain cold toward one another. Another church can be warm and affectionate while lacking any shared understanding of the truth. Peter's word joins mind and heart, and biblical unity requires both.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Peter 3:8 as the conclusion of 2:13–3:7. "Finally" signals that Peter is gathering up what came before. Beginning at 2:13 he has told believers to "submit yourselves to every ordinance of man for the Lord's sake," then addressed servants under masters "not only to the good and gentle, but also to the harsh" (2:18), and then wives and husbands (3:1–7). Each group has been asked to set aside its rights for the Lord's sake. Then Peter turns to "all of you" and names the result he has been aiming at: one mind.

So the earlier instructions were never about rank for its own sake. They were about selflessness, and when every member lays down the demand to have their own way, harmony follows. A choir sings different parts but follows one director, and the church reaches one mind through each person's humility before God and others. Peter places Christ's own example at the center of this section (2:21–25), so that the pattern for this yielding is Jesus Himself.

1 Peter 3:8–9 and the example of Christ. Verse 9 follows immediately: believers are not to repay evil with evil or reviling with reviling, but to bless instead. That language deliberately echoes what Peter has just said of Jesus, "who, when He was reviled, did not revile in return; when He suffered, He did not threaten, but committed Himself to Him who judges righteously" (2:23). Believers who think alike and care for one another can refuse to retaliate, because they are following the same Lord in the same steps.

The order matters. A church that is divided, competitive, or indifferent to its own wounded members will not have the strength to bless its persecutors. A people of one mind become a refuge for one another, and from that refuge they can give the gentle, respectful answer Peter calls for later in the chapter (3:15–16). For exiles under pressure, like-mindedness was a necessity for survival and for witness.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never treats unity of mind as a negotiated middle ground. In Romans 15:5 Paul prays that God would grant believers to be like-minded toward one another "according to Christ Jesus" (paraphrased). In Philippians 2:5 he calls them to let the mind of Christ Jesus be in them, the mind that humbled itself to the cross. Believers come to share one mind with each other because each has come to share the mind of their Lord. Like-mindedness is a gift that flows from Christ.

This reaches back to Jesus' prayer in John 17, where He asked the Father that those who would believe through the apostles' word might be one, as the Father and Son are one, so that the world would believe the Father sent Him. In John 13:35 Jesus said that all people would know His disciples by their love for one another. Peter's scattered churches, holding together in compassion while being slandered, were living answers to that prayer. Their one mind pointed beyond themselves to the Son who had made them one family.

7. Spirit & Life

Peter's word confronts the habit of treating faith as a private matter of personal convictions, personal worship preferences, and personal boundaries. He brings his teaching on daily conduct to a climax with a corporate command. Growth in Christ shows itself in how well we think and feel together with His people.

The surrounding verses also show that how believers treat one another affects their life with God. Peter has just warned husbands to honor their wives so that their prayers are not hindered (3:7). After the call to one mind, he tells the church it was called to inherit a blessing (3:9), and he quotes Psalm 34 to say that the Lord's eyes are on the righteous and His ears open to their prayers (3:12). Disunity is not a minor social problem. It touches the believer's access to God.

The practical path to a shared mind is the one Peter traces. We look to Christ, who did not revile in return, and we let His way of thinking govern ours. Then we take up the specific work he names: compassion that feels another's trouble, love that treats fellow believers as family, tenderness, and a humble, friendly spirit. Like-mindedness grows in people who do these things, and those same people are able to bless when they are cursed.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Homóphrōn occurs only in 1 Peter 3:8, which is discussed above. The following verses express the same idea in different Greek wording and help show how widely the New Testament values a shared mind:

  • Philippians 2:2 — "fulfill my joy by being like-minded, having the same love, being of one accord, of one mind." Paul stacks phrases about unity and ties them to "the same love," which follows his appeal to the comfort of love and fellowship of the Spirit (2:1).

  • 2 Corinthians 13:11 — "Be of good comfort, be of one mind, live in peace; and the God of love and peace will be with you." To a church troubled by division, Paul joins one mind with peace and promises the presence of the God of peace.
