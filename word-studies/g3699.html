WHERE, WHEREVER - (hópou, Strong's G3699)

Summary

Hópou is the Greek word behind most of the "where" and "wherever" statements in the New Testament. It points to the place, situation, or setting in which something happens. Sometimes that place is a real spot on a map, such as the Jordan crossing where John baptized. Sometimes it is an open-ended "wherever," such as the gospel going out to any place it is preached. At other times it marks a condition rather than a location, as when Paul speaks of envy and strife among the Corinthians. Paying attention to hópou helps readers ask a useful question: what does this "where" actually describe? In John's Gospel the answer becomes deeply personal. Jesus speaks again and again of "where I am," and His promise is that His people will one day be with Him there.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hópou introduces a clause that tells us where something is or happens. It does not ask a question. It answers one, by attaching a description of a place or setting to the main statement. In Matthew 6:19, Jesus warns against storing up "treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal." The main command forbids hoarding treasure. Hópou then describes the kind of place earth is for treasure: a place where things decay and get stolen. The word can point to a definite location, to any location at all ("wherever"), or to a set of circumstances. Context decides which.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pointing to a definite place: "where"

This is the most common use. Hópou ties an event to a known location. John 1:28 reads, "These things were done in Bethabara beyond the Jordan, where John was baptizing." The word anchors the testimony of John the Baptist in real geography. The same use appears at the empty tomb, where the angel invites the women to see the place where the Lord lay (Matthew 28:6; Mark 16:6, paraphrased). The resurrection is announced at an actual, identifiable spot.

Pointing to any place: "wherever"

Often combined with a small Greek particle that makes it open-ended, hópou describes an unspecified or unlimited location. Jesus says of the woman who anointed Him, "wherever this gospel is preached in the whole world, what this woman has done will also be told as a memorial to her" (Mark 14:9). No place is excluded, and the reach of that promise is as wide as the gospel itself. The same open "wherever" appears in a proverb: "For wherever the carcass is, there the eagles will be gathered together" (Matthew 24:28). It also appears on the lips of a would-be disciple who offers to follow Jesus wherever He goes (Luke 9:57, paraphrased).

Pointing to a situation or condition: "where," "whereas"

Here "where" describes circumstances rather than places. Paul writes, "For where there are envy, strife, and divisions among you, are you not carnal and behaving like mere men?" (1 Corinthians 3:3). The "place" is the condition of the church. James uses the word the same way when he says that confusion and every evil thing are present where envy and self-seeking exist (James 3:16, paraphrased). In 2 Peter 2:11 the NKJV renders it "whereas": "whereas angels, who are greater in power and might, do not bring a reviling accusation against them before the Lord." The word sets the restraint of angels against the reckless slander of false teachers.

Adapted in English: "which"

Occasionally English needs a different shape. Revelation 17:9 says, "The seven heads are seven mountains on which the woman sits." The Greek says something closer to "where the woman sits on them," and the NKJV smooths it into natural English.

3. Practical Reading Insight

John's Gospel uses hópou more than any other book, and it traces a clear line through Jesus' words about "where I am." To His opponents He says that where He is going they cannot come (John 7:34; 8:21, paraphrased). He tells His disciples the same thing at first (John 13:33). When Peter presses Him, the answer changes: Peter cannot follow now, but he will follow afterward (John 13:36). Then comes the promise: "I will come again and receive you to Myself; that where I am, there you may be also" (John 14:3). Jesus' prayer carries the same desire, that those the Father gave Him would be with Him where He is (John 17:24).

The "where" in these sayings is real. Jesus speaks of His Father's house and of preparing a place. Yet the place is described by who is there. Heaven's chief glory is that Christ is in it. A believer's hope is not a vague better location but being with the Lord Himself. In the same way, the one who serves Jesus must follow Him, and where He is, His servant will also be (John 12:26).

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume every "where" refers to geography. In 1 Corinthians 3:3 and James 3:16 it describes a situation. Also avoid treating "wherever" as an automatic promise. It simply leaves the location open, and the surrounding words supply the meaning. In Mark 9:18 it describes a spirit that seized a boy wherever it pleased (paraphrased). Finally, the question "Where?" usually renders a different Greek word, pou. In John 13:36, Peter's question ("Where are You going?") uses that question word, and Jesus' answer uses hópou.
