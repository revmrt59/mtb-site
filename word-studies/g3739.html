WHO, WHICH, THAT - (hós, Strong's G3739)

Summary

Hós is the Greek word behind many of the New Testament's "who," "whom," "whose," "which," and "that." It is a relative pronoun. It points back to a person or thing just mentioned and opens a clause that tells us more about that person or thing. The NKJV says of Jesus, "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." The word ties the Father's delight directly to the Son.

Hós can also stand on its own as "whoever" or "whatever," as in Jesus' call that "whoever loses his life for My sake and the gospel's will save it." Combined with certain small words, it produces phrases of time and place such as "till," "while," and "where." Because its form matches the person or thing it points back to, it often settles who is being described. Matthew's genealogy is a striking case: the wording makes plain that Jesus was born of Mary, not fathered by Joseph.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

A relative pronoun joins two thoughts by letting the second one describe something named in the first. In English we say "the star which they had seen" and hardly notice the joint. Greek handles this with more precision. Hós matches the gender and number of the word it points back to, which grammarians call its antecedent. Its case, however, comes from the job it does inside its own clause. So the form of hós does two things at once. It identifies the person or thing being described, and it shows that person's role in the new statement. Sometimes no antecedent is written at all. In that case hós carries the idea itself: "the one who" or "the thing that."

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Describing someone or something already named (who, whom, whose, which, that)

This is the most common use. In Matthew 2:9, "the star which they had seen in the East went before them." The clause singles out that particular star, the one the wise men had already seen and followed. In Matthew 3:17 the Father says, "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." Here hós points back to "Son" and becomes the object of "in" within its own clause. The Father's pleasure rests on a specific Person.

Standing on its own (whatever, what, whoever, the one who)

Here hós contains its own antecedent and works as a noun. Jesus tells His disciples, "Whatever I tell you in the dark, speak in the light; and what you hear in the ear, preach on the housetops" (Matthew 10:27). The pronoun supplies the very content they must proclaim: the things Jesus Himself has said. When it is paired with a particle of indefiniteness, hós opens the door to anyone at all: "For whoever desires to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for My sake and the gospel's will save it" (Mark 8:35). In that verse, "whoever" renders hós together with that particle. Jesus' warning and promise therefore reach every listener, with no exceptions.

Fixed phrases of time and place (till, until, while, where)

Joined with certain prepositions, hós forms set expressions in which a word like "time" or "place" is understood. Matthew 1:25 says Joseph "did not know her till she had brought forth her firstborn Son." The English "till" represents a short Greek phrase meaning roughly "until the time when." It marks how long Joseph's restraint lasted. Other phrases built this way are rendered "while," "since," or "because," depending on context.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Matthew's genealogy repeats one pattern again and again: this man "begot" that man. Then, at the climax, the pattern breaks: "And Jacob begot Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus who is called Christ" (Matthew 1:16). The English "whom" could point to either Joseph or Mary. In Greek, the form of hós is feminine singular, so it points to Mary alone. Matthew does not say Joseph begot Jesus. He traces the legal line through Joseph and then states, with grammatical care, that Jesus was born of Mary. The account of the virgin birth that follows (1:18–25) does not come out of nowhere. The groundwork is already laid in a single pronoun.

This is the kind of help hós gives throughout the New Testament. In long sentences, especially in Paul's letters, several nouns may stand close together. The form of the pronoun often tells the careful reader which one is meant, and it keeps us from attaching a description to the wrong person or idea.

4. Reading Guidance

When "what" or "where" renders hós, it is not asking a question. It means "that which" or "the place where." Likewise, "until," "while," or "since" may stand for hós combined with a preposition. Gender and number usually identify the person or thing described, but not always. When two nearby nouns share the same form, context must decide, and nearness alone does not settle the matter.
