WHEN, WHENEVER - (hótan, Strong's G3752)

Summary

Hótan is the Greek word behind many of the New Testament's "when" and "whenever" clauses. It introduces a time frame that is real but left open. Sometimes it points to a future event that is sure to happen but has no date attached, such as the return of Christ or the coming of the Holy Spirit. At other times it describes a situation that keeps coming up, like prayer, giving, or trials, and tells us what to do each time it arrives. Reading hótan well helps us see what Jesus and the apostles took for granted. They assumed God's people would pray, give, and suffer. They spoke of Christ's coming as certain while leaving its timing with God.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hótan joins a time clause to a main statement. It answers the question "at what point?" without naming a calendar moment. Lexicons describe it as a combination of hote ("when") and the particle an, which adds an open or "whatever the case may be" quality. The result is close to "at whatever time" or "whenever."

In the New Testament the word usually takes a verb in the subjunctive, the Greek mood used for what is expected or contingent rather than simply reported. That openness belongs to the timing or the circumstances. It does not cast doubt on whether the event will happen. When Jesus says, "When the Son of Man comes," the coming is certain. Only the moment is unspecified.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Expected future event with open timing

NKJV: "when"

Hótan often introduces something God has promised and will surely do, while leaving the date unannounced. Jesus describes the judgment this way: "When the Son of Man comes in His glory, and all the holy angels with Him, then He will sit on the throne of His glory" (Matthew 25:31). The clause sets the scene, and the main statement ("then He will sit") tells what will follow.

The same pattern carries His promise in the Upper Room: "However, when He, the Spirit of truth, has come, He will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13). The disciples are not given a day. They are given a certainty, together with what the Spirit will do once He arrives.

Paul uses hótan in the same way for the believer's hope. When Christ, who is our life, appears, His people will appear with Him in glory (Colossians 3:4, paraphrased). When the perishable puts on the imperishable, the saying "Death is swallowed up in victory" will come true (1 Corinthians 15:54, paraphrased).

Recurring or general circumstance

NKJV: "when," "whenever"

Hótan can also describe a situation that happens repeatedly, and the main clause then gives the instruction for each occasion. "And whenever you stand praying, if you have anything against anyone, forgive him" (Mark 11:25). Jesus is not pointing to one particular prayer. Every time a disciple prays, the same question about unforgiveness comes up.

James uses the word to treat hardship as a regular part of Christian experience: "My brethren, count it all joy when you fall into various trials" (James 1:2). He does not say "if." The trials are expected, and his instruction concerns how to meet them.

Habitual action in narrative

NKJV: "whenever"

Occasionally hótan describes something that happened again and again in the past. Mark reports that whenever the unclean spirits saw Jesus, they fell down before Him and cried out that He was the Son of God (Mark 3:11, paraphrased). The word shows that this was a repeated pattern, not a single incident. Revelation 4:9 uses "whenever" in a similar way for the ongoing worship around the throne.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The clearest payoff shows up in the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus says, "Therefore, when you do a charitable deed, do not sound a trumpet before you as the hypocrites do" (Matthew 6:2). He speaks the same way about prayer and fasting in Matthew 6:5 and 6:16. The hótan clauses take the practice for granted, and the commands that follow deal with motive and manner. Jesus does not stop to argue that His followers should give, pray, and fast. He assumes they will, and He cares deeply about how they do it and for whose eyes. A disciple reading these verses should not ask, "Do I have to?" The better question is, "When I do, whom am I trying to please?"

The future uses of hótan offer a different kind of help. They let believers hold both certainty and patience. Christ will come, the Spirit did come, and death will be swallowed up. God has fixed what will happen and kept the timing in His own hands. That is a call to readiness, and John makes the same point when he urges believers to abide in Christ so that when He appears they may have confidence (1 John 2:28, paraphrased).

4. Reading Guidance

English often uses "when" for both hótan and the related word hote. Hote usually points to a definite occasion, while hótan generally leaves the moment open. Do not read calendar precision into a hótan clause, and do not treat its subjunctive verb as a sign that the event is doubtful. Context shows whether the word looks toward a single promised event or a recurring situation. The NKJV sometimes renders it "till" (Mark 9:9) or "as" (John 9:5), so the English word alone will not always reveal it.
