WHEN, WHILE - (hóte, Strong's G3753)

Summary

Hóte is the Greek word usually translated "when." It introduces a clause that fixes the time of the main action, as in "when Jesus had ended these sayings," "when the Lamb opened one of the seals," or "when we were in the flesh." Most often it points to something that actually happened or a condition that actually held. That lets the biblical writers move a story into its next scene, mark the steps of a vision, and set a believer's former life against what God has since done. It can also name a definite time still to come. Jesus speaks of an hour that is coming when true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth (John 4:23, paraphrased). Noticing hóte helps readers see where a passage turns and which period of time a statement describes.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hóte joins two thoughts by time. The "when" clause answers the question "at what time?" and the main clause tells what happened, or will happen, at that time. Usually it is paired with ordinary statements of fact rather than with uncertain or repeated situations. Greek has a related word for those, hotan, which is built from hóte plus a particle of indefiniteness and generally carries the sense of "whenever" or "when it happens." Hóte tends to point to a particular moment or a definite stretch of time. The NKJV almost always renders it "when," and occasionally "while" (John 17:12; Hebrews 9:17) or "as soon as" (Luke 15:30).

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Setting the scene in narrative (when)

In the Gospels and Acts, hóte often closes one action and opens the next. "Now when they came up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord caught Philip away" (Acts 8:39). The clause ties Philip's sudden departure to the moment the baptism ended.

Matthew turns this into a fixed formula. "And so it was, when Jesus had ended these sayings, that the people were astonished at His teaching" (Matthew 7:28). Similar wording follows each of Jesus' major blocks of teaching in Matthew (11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1). In each case, hóte marks the point where Matthew moves from discourse back to narrative.

Marking the steps of a vision (when)

In Revelation, hóte gives order to what John sees. "Now I saw when the Lamb opened one of the seals" (Revelation 6:1). "When He opened the second seal, I heard the second living creature saying, 'Come and see'" (Revelation 6:3). The same pattern carries through the remaining seals (6:5, 7, 9, 12) to the seventh in 8:1. The repetition shows that every development in the vision follows the Lamb's action.

Defining a former condition (when)

Paul uses hóte to describe a period in the believer's past. "For when we were in the flesh, the sinful passions which were aroused by the law were at work in our members to bear fruit to death" (Romans 7:5). "For when you were slaves of sin, you were free in regard to righteousness" (Romans 6:20). The clause names the time, and the surrounding argument shows that the time has ended.

The contrast is plain in 1 Corinthians 13:11: "When I was a child, I spoke as a child... but when I became a man, I put away childish things." Here two hóte clauses set two periods of life side by side.

Naming an appointed coming time (when)

Hóte can also describe a definite future time, often after words like "hour," "day," or "time." Jesus says the hour is coming when people will worship the Father neither on that mountain nor in Jerusalem (John 4:21, paraphrased), and when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God (John 5:25, paraphrased). Paul warns that a time will come when people will not endure sound doctrine (2 Timothy 4:3, paraphrased). He also speaks of the day when God will judge people's secrets by Jesus Christ (Romans 2:16, paraphrased). In these verses the future is not vague. It is a set time.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Galatians 4 places two of these "when" clauses back to back. "Even so we, when we were children, were in bondage under the elements of the world" (Galatians 4:3). The next verse answers with another hóte: when the fullness of the time had come, God sent forth His Son (Galatians 4:4, paraphrased). Paul sets the time of bondage beside the time of God's action. The first "when" describes what we were. The second describes what God did at the moment He chose.

Romans follows the same pattern. Romans 7:5 describes the time "when we were in the flesh," and Paul goes on in 7:6 to say that we have now been delivered from the law (paraphrased). The word hóte only marks the period. The gospel, spoken in the clauses that follow, is what declares that period finished. Read this way, Paul's "when" clauses are a reminder of where believers once stood, written so they can see clearly what Christ has done.

4. Reading Guidance

English "when" can mean "at that time," "whenever," or even "if." The NKJV uses "when" for hóte and also for hotan and other constructions, so the English word alone cannot tell you which Greek word stands behind it. Hóte marks time. A cause may be implied by the story, as in John 4:45, where the Galileans welcomed Jesus after seeing His works in Jerusalem. Even so, the word itself states time, not reason.
