THAT, BECAUSE, FOR, SINCE - (hóti, Strong's G3754)

Summary

Hóti is one of the most frequent connecting words in the Greek New Testament. It does two main jobs. First, it introduces the content of what someone says, knows, sees, or believes, much like English "that." Second, it gives a reason or explanation, usually translated "for" or "because." It can also open a direct quotation, working much like quotation marks. Because one Greek word carries both "that" and "because," the reader has to watch what the clause is attached to. A hóti clause after "we know" may tell us what we know, or it may tell us how we know it. When the reader keeps those two uses apart, passages such as 1 John 3:14 and Luke 7:47 become clearer, and readers are spared confusion about how love relates to forgiveness and new life.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hóti introduces a clause that does work for another part of the sentence. In one use, the clause becomes the object of a verb. It states what is said, believed, heard, or known. In another use, the clause supports a statement by giving its ground, explanation, or evidence. A third use introduces a speaker's actual words. English needs several different words to cover these jobs, so the NKJV renders hóti most often as "that," "because," and "for," and sometimes leaves it untranslated. In each passage the reader has to decide which job the word is doing and which statement the clause supports.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Content of speech, knowledge, or belief ("that")

This is the most common use. After verbs of saying, knowing, seeing, or believing, hóti introduces what is actually being said or known. Jesus tells Peter, "And I also say to you that you are Peter, and on this rock I will build My church" (Matthew 16:18). Here the clause is the declaration itself. Hóti can also explain a word that comes just before it. In Luke 10:20 Jesus says, "do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you." The hóti clause tells the disciples exactly what "this" means.

Reason, ground, or explanation ("because," "for," "since")

Here hóti gives the reason behind a statement. "Blessed are those who mourn, For they shall be comforted" (Matthew 5:4). The blessing is not found in the mourning itself. It rests on the comfort God promises. In Luke 10:20, the same verse that used "that" goes on: "but rather rejoice because your names are written in heaven." In a single sentence Jesus moves the disciples' joy from what they could do to what God has done for them. In 1 John 4:19 the reason is the source of the action: "We love Him because He first loved us." God's love comes first, and our love answers it.

Introducing direct speech

Hóti sometimes introduces a speaker's exact words. In that case it works as a spoken quotation mark rather than as "that" leading into reported speech. English translations usually show this with punctuation and leave the word untranslated. At times the NKJV supplies "saying." One example is Peter's denial in Matthew 26:74, where his own words follow directly (reference only).

3. Practical Reading Insight

The "because" use does not always name the cause of an event. Sometimes it names the evidence that the event has happened. First John 3:14 reads, "We know that we have passed from death to life, because we love the brethren." The "because" clause attaches to "we know." Love for fellow believers is how John's readers can recognize that new life is already theirs. It is not what moved them from death to life. Love is the proof of that life, not its price. Placed beside 1 John 4:19, the order is plain. God's love comes first and produces our love, and our love then gives evidence of the life He has given.

The same question comes up in Luke 7:47: "her sins, which are many, are forgiven, for she loved much." Hóti by itself could be read either way: her love as the reason for forgiveness, or her love as the evidence of it. The context settles the matter. Jesus has just told the parable of two debtors, in which the one forgiven more loves more (Luke 7:41–43). He then ends the verse with "But to whom little is forgiven, the same loves little." The woman's lavish love shows how much she had been forgiven.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume that every "because" or "for" names an originating cause. Ask which statement the clause supports. It may explain why something happened, or it may explain how we can know that it happened. Also remember that English "for" often translates a different Greek word, gar. A single conjunction cannot settle a doctrine. The surrounding passage has to do that work.
