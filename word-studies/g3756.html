NOT, NO - (ou, Strong's G3756)

Summary

Ou is the New Testament's ordinary word for "not" and "no." It says plainly that something is not so: an event did not happen, a claim is not true, a person is not who others suppose. The NKJV usually renders it "not" or "no," and sometimes folds it into words like "cannot," "nothing," or "never." Because it denies what is actually the case, ou does a great deal of quiet work in the Gospels and letters. It sets limits on what we may say about Jesus, His mission, and His people. It turns questions into confident appeals that expect "Yes." It also gives the bare, honest answer "No." Reading these denials carefully helps us see what Scripture rules out as well as what it affirms.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Ou negates. Most often it stands before a verb or a statement and denies its reality: "this did not happen," "this is not true." Greek had a second common negative, mē, which generally belongs with commands, wishes, conditions, and purposes. Ou, by contrast, usually belongs with plain statements about what is or was. That distinction is a tendency rather than an ironclad rule, but it holds often enough to help readers. When ou appears, the writer is usually describing how things stand.

Ou can also open a question. When it does, the speaker is not asking for information. He expects the listener to agree. Ou can also stand alone as a one-word answer: "No."

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Denying a fact or event ("not," "no")

This is the most common use. Matthew says Joseph "did not know her till she had brought forth her firstborn Son" (Matthew 1:25). The negative states plainly what did not happen, and it protects Matthew's account of the virgin birth. John writes that "the darkness did not comprehend it" (John 1:5). Here ou denies the darkness any success against the light. The verse describes what happened when the Word came into the world. It is not a hope or a possibility.

Questions expecting "Yes" ("not")

After pointing to the birds that "neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns," Jesus asks, "Are you not of more value than they?" (Matthew 6:26). He is not inviting debate. The question is built so that every listener answers, "Yes, we are," and that answer becomes the ground for trusting the Father's care. Paul uses the same form in a run of questions: "Am I not an apostle? Am I not free? Have I not seen Jesus Christ our Lord? Are you not my work in the Lord?" (1 Corinthians 9:1). Each question assumes the Corinthians already know the answer.

Direct answer or refusal ("No")

Asked whether he is Elijah, John the Baptist said, "I am not." Asked whether he is the Prophet, he answered, "No" (John 1:21). The bluntness fits a man whose whole role was to point away from himself. Jesus also uses the word as the plain answer of an honest person: "But let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No'" (Matthew 5:37). No oath needs to stand behind it.

Clearing away a wrong idea before stating the right one ("not… but")

Ou often denies a mistaken expectation so that the true statement can stand out. In Mark 10:45 (paraphrase), Jesus says the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve and give His life as a ransom. In John 3:17 (paraphrase), God sent His Son into the world not to condemn the world but so that it might be saved through Him. In each case the denial is half of the message.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The "not… but" pattern rewards slow reading. When Scripture says what Jesus did not come to do, it is correcting an assumption the hearers likely held, and often one we still hold. Many expected a Messiah who would be served. Jesus says plainly that He came to serve and to give His life. Many assume God's first instinct toward a sinful world is condemnation. John 3:17 says the Son was not sent for that. When you meet ou in a sentence like these, ask what idea is being ruled out. The answer usually shows you the misunderstanding the passage was written to correct.

The questions built with ou carry a pastoral warmth that is easy to miss. In Matthew 6:26, Jesus does not lecture anxious people. He asks them something they already know is true and lets their own "Yes" quiet their fears.

4. Reading Guidance

English "not" does not always represent ou. It may stand for mē, for the stronger form ouchi, or for related words such as oude ("neither," "nor"). For that reason, avoid building conclusions on the English word alone. Also remember that Greek can combine ou with mē for an especially strong denial. In this ou mē construction, the two negatives strengthen the denial rather than canceling it. Even so, the difference between ou and mē is a general tendency. It should not be used by itself to prove a doctrine.
