WOE - (ouaí, Strong's G3759)

Summary

Ouaí is the New Testament's cry of "Woe!" or "Alas!" It is heavy speech, never casual. On the lips of Jesus it usually announces that people stand under God's judgment: unrepentant towns, hypocritical religious leaders, the comfortable rich, and the man who would betray Him. The same word can also express pity for those caught in coming disaster, grief from those watching their world collapse, or Paul's sense of what would become of him if he refused his calling. To hear it rightly, a reader has to ask who is speaking and why. Its most striking feature is who receives it. In the Gospels, Jesus speaks His sharpest woes to people who were confident they were right with God, and He speaks them while there is still time to turn.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Ouaí declares that someone is headed for ruin, or already in it. When Jesus says "Woe to you," He is not hurling an insult. He is announcing someone's standing before God, much as Israel's prophets did, and the words that follow usually explain why. The same cry can also express sorrow. In the speech of grieving onlookers it becomes the wail of a funeral, translated "Alas" in the NKJV. Because the Greek word is identical in both kinds of passage, the speaker and the situation decide whether it sounds like a verdict, a lament, or both. In Revelation it can even be used as a noun, naming a specific wave of judgment.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Prophetic verdict. Most uses belong here. In Matthew 11:21 Jesus cries, "Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida!" These are towns that had seen His mighty works and stayed unmoved. In Matthew 23 the word sounds eight times against the scribes and Pharisees, the densest cluster in the New Testament. Each woe names a specific failure: "For you shut up the kingdom of heaven against men" (23:13), or "you pay tithe of mint and anise and cummin, and have neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faith" (23:23). Luke 6:24–26 sets woes against the rich, the full, the laughing, and the well-spoken-of, right after the Beatitudes. Jude 11 turns the same cry on false teachers who have gone the way of Cain.

Warning to the betrayer. At the Last Supper, Jesus says that the Son of Man goes as it is written, but woe to the man by whom He is betrayed (Matthew 26:24; Mark 14:21; Luke 22:22). Judas is sitting at the table when he hears it. The woe is both a sentence and a final warning, spoken to his face. Jesus speaks in a similar way about whoever causes others to stumble (Matthew 18:7; Luke 17:1).

Pity for sufferers. Speaking of the coming distress on Jerusalem, Jesus says woe to those who are pregnant and nursing in those days (Matthew 24:19; Mark 13:17; Luke 21:23). These women have done nothing wrong. Here the word grieves over how hard the suffering will be for them.

Lament and judgment in Revelation. An angel flying through the midst of heaven cries "Woe, woe, woe" over the earth's inhabitants (8:13). The word then becomes a noun: one woe is past, and more are coming (9:12; 11:14). In chapter 18, kings, merchants, and sailors stand at a distance and wail "Alas, alas" over fallen Babylon (18:10, 16, 19). They mourn what they profited from, not what grieved God.

Personal anguish. In 1 Corinthians 9:16 Paul turns the word on himself and says woe to him if he does not preach the gospel. He is not describing an optional career. Refusing God's charge would be his own ruin.

3. Biblical Connections & Distinctions

In the Greek Old Testament, ouaí commonly renders the Hebrew prophetic cries hôy and 'ôy. These cries ring through Isaiah 5:8–22 and Habakkuk 2:6–19 in a series of woes against injustice, greed, and drunkenness. When Jesus speaks His chain of woes in Matthew 23, He is speaking in that prophetic voice. The prophets usually framed their woes as the word of the LORD. Jesus pronounces His in His own name, and the Gospels present Him as more than a prophet.

Paul's woe also has a prophetic background. Isaiah cried woe over himself when he saw the Lord, then received his commission (Isaiah 6:5–8). That passage uses a Hebrew word, not this Greek one, so it is a thematic parallel rather than another occurrence. Isaiah's woe came before his calling, and Paul's woe would come from abandoning his.

Luke places the woes directly after the blessings. "Blessed" (makarios) is a different word, an adjective rather than a cry, but the pairing is deliberate. "Woe to you who are full, For you shall hunger" (Luke 6:25) reverses the blessing on the hungry.

4. Spirit & Life

The Gospel woes reveal where Jesus saw the greatest danger. Tax collectors and sinful women do not hear woe from Him. The people who do are towns that saw miracles and shrugged, teachers who loaded others with burdens, and leaders whose outward religion hid what Jesus described as "dead men's bones and all uncleanness" (Matthew 23:27). Matthew 11:21 adds a sobering measure: Tyre and Sidon would have repented with less. Greater light brings greater accountability.

These woes are not cold. Right after the eight woes of Matthew 23, Jesus laments over Jerusalem, recalling how often He longed to gather her children as a hen gathers her chicks (23:37). The one who pronounced the verdict also grieved over it. The woe to Judas follows the same pattern. Jesus warns him plainly while he still sits at the table. A woe from Christ is a sentence, but it is spoken before the end, and so it carries mercy. It tells people the truth while they can still turn.

5. Reading Guidance

Before deciding what an ouaí means, ask who is speaking and to whom. Jesus confronting hypocrites, Jesus pitying nursing mothers, merchants mourning Babylon, and Paul weighing his calling all use the same Greek word. Remember too that "Alas" in Revelation 18 is this same cry, so the English change does not signal a different original word.
