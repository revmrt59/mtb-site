NOR, NEITHER, NOT EVEN - (oudé, Strong's G3761)

Summary

Oudé is the New Testament's way of saying "and not." Most often it adds another item to a denial already underway, and English readers meet it as "nor" or "neither." It can also push a negative to its limit, meaning "not even." In that use it names the most surprising case that is still excluded, such as Solomon in all his glory or a whole nation privileged to know God. A third use carries a negative from one situation over to another ("neither will I," "neither can you"). Watching for these uses helps readers see when Jesus or an apostle is simply listing what is missing, when he is building to a climax, and when he is drawing a sober comparison.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Oudé joins the ordinary Greek word for "not" (ou) with a short connecting word, and it behaves like both. As a connector, it attaches a new denial to an earlier one, so a speaker can stack up several things that are not true or not done. As an emphasizer, it reaches past the expected cases to an extreme one: "not even this." The difference matters because the second use carries an argument. If the greatest case fails, everything lesser fails with it. The word also links a denial about one person or situation to a matching denial about another. Context decides which of these is at work, and English translations do not always make the choice visible.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Adding another denial ("nor," "neither," "or")

This is the most common use. In Matthew 6:26 Jesus says the birds "neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns." The first negative is the ordinary "not." Oudé supplies the next two, and each one removes another stage of the farmer's year. Galatians 3:28 uses the same pattern in a weightier setting: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free." Each "there is neither" opens a fresh denial, and oudé joins the paired opposite to it. In the third pair the Greek wording shifts to "male and female," so the NKJV's final "nor" does not represent this word.

Pressing to the extreme case ("not even")

Here oudé names the case no one would expect to be excluded. In Matthew 6:29 Jesus says "even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these." In this verse the NKJV divides the single Greek idea of "not even" between "even" and "not." In Matthew 8:10 Jesus marvels at a Gentile centurion: "I have not found such great faith, not even in Israel!" (Luke 7:9 records the same remark.) Israel was the people who should have shown such faith, and that is exactly the point. In John 1:3, oudé joins the word for "one" to form "not even one thing," which the NKJV renders "nothing": not even one thing that was made came into being without the Word.

Carrying a negative across ("neither will I," "neither can you")

Sometimes oudé means "also not," matching one denial with another. In John 15:4 Jesus says a branch cannot bear fruit by itself, and in the same way the disciples cannot either unless they abide in Him. In Matthew 6:15 the word links an unforgiving person with the Father's response: if you do not forgive others, your Father will not forgive you either. In Matthew 21:27 the religious leaders refuse to answer Jesus, and He answers in kind that He will not tell them either.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Matthew 6:26–29 shows the word doing two different jobs within four verses. In verse 26 it simply adds to a list. The birds do none of the work of sowing, reaping, or storing, yet the Father feeds them. By verse 29 the same word carries the weight of the argument. Solomon was the richest and most splendid king Israel ever had, and not even he was dressed like a wildflower. Jesus is not belittling human effort. He is showing that the Father's provision exceeds anything human glory can produce, and that is why worry has no place in His children (6:27–28).

The "neither can you" pattern in John 15:4 deserves the same attention. Jesus draws the comparison as tightly as the language allows. A detached branch and a disciple living apart from Christ are in the same condition, which is fruitless. The word does not soften the comparison, and readers should not soften it either.

4. Reading Guidance

The NKJV often renders "not even" as a plain "not" or "nor," so the climax can slip past unnoticed. When a list of negatives ends with someone great or privileged, check whether the sense is "not even." Remember also that English "nor" can represent other Greek words, such as oute in paired "neither…nor" lists, or mēde with commands. A connecting negative organizes a sentence. It cannot settle a larger doctrinal debate by itself.
