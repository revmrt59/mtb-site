NO ONE, NOTHING, NONE - (oudeís, Strong's G3762)

Summary

Oudeís is the New Testament's word for a complete "no." It means "no one" when it points to people, "nothing" when it points to things, and "no" or "not any" when it stands beside a noun, as in "no fault." It closes off a whole group without exception. That makes it the word behind some of Scripture's most sweeping statements. "No one has seen God at any time." "Without Me you can do nothing." "I find no fault in this Man." Reading it well means asking two questions. What group is being shut out? And what does the passage set in place of that empty space? Often the answer is Christ Himself: the Son who makes the unseen God known, the Vine without whom the branches bear nothing, and the innocent Man whom Pilate could not fault.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Oudeís negates by naming an empty set. A simple "not" denies an action. Oudeís denies that anyone or anything fills a particular slot in the sentence. It can stand alone as the subject or object of a verb, as in "no one" or "nothing." It can also modify a noun directly, as in "no accusation."

Its force is complete within its setting. When Jesus says "no one can serve two masters," He does not mean that most people find divided loyalty difficult. He means it cannot be done. The context decides how wide the circle is: all people, all disciples, or everyone present at a scene. Inside that circle, oudeís leaves no exceptions.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

"No one," referring to persons (renderings: no one, none, no)

This is the word's most frequent use. In Matthew 6:24 Jesus says, "No one can serve two masters... You cannot serve God and mammon." The word turns a piece of household wisdom into a firm rule with no exceptions, and the final sentence applies it directly to God and money. John 1:18 works the same way: "No one has seen God at any time." The statement covers all of humanity. It also prepares for the very next words: "The only begotten Son... He has declared Him."

"Nothing," referring to things (renderings: nothing, anything)

In the neuter form the word excludes things, actions, or results. Jesus tells His disciples, "for without Me you can do nothing" (John 15:5). The sentence follows the picture of the vine and branches, so the "nothing" is the fruit that a branch cut off from the vine cannot produce. In Matthew 17:20 the same word points the other way. After describing faith as small as a mustard seed, Jesus says, "nothing will be impossible for you." Here oudeís rules out every obstacle instead of every achievement.

"No [noun]," modifying a noun (renderings: no, any)

Placed beside a noun, oudeís denies that any example of that thing exists. Pilate declares, "I find no fault in this Man" (Luke 23:4). He does not say that the charges are weak. He says that he has found none at all. Festus says something similar about Paul: "they brought no accusation against him of such things as I supposed" (Acts 25:18). The charges Festus expected simply never appeared.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Watch what fills the space that oudeís empties. John 1:18 shuts every human being out of seeing God, then immediately names the Son as the one who has made Him known. The "no one" is the background against which Christ's unique role stands out. John 15:5 has the same pattern. "Nothing" describes the branch apart from the Vine, while "much fruit" describes the branch that abides. Jesus is not exaggerating to make a point. Within the picture He has given, the life of the branch comes from the vine, so a branch on its own produces no fruit at all.

Luke's account of the trial makes the word's force cumulative. Pilate's "no fault" in Luke 23:4 is repeated in Luke 23:14 and 23:22, and in Luke 23:15 he states that Jesus has done nothing deserving death. John records the same verdict more than once (John 18:38; 19:4). Each time, the record of Jesus' innocence grows firmer, and the condemnation that follows looks all the more unjust. The One who had no fault went to the cross for those who had plenty.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume "no one" always means "no one in the universe." Context sets the boundary. In Mark 5:4, "neither could anyone tame him" covers the people who had tried to restrain the man. Jesus then does what they could not. English renderings also vary widely. They include "anyone," "not," "none," and even "despised" (Acts 19:27), so an English concordance will not catch every occurrence. A related word, mēdeis (Strong's G3367), also means "no one," but it usually appears in commands and conditions, such as "let no one...," and not in plain statements of fact.
