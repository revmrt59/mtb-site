NO LONGER, NOT ANY MORE - (oukéti, Strong's G3765)

Summary

Oukéti is the New Testament's way of saying "no longer." It denies something while pointing back to a time when that thing was true. Jesus had walked openly among the Jews, but after the plot against Him He "no longer" did. The Galatians had lived as slaves, but now they are "no longer" slaves but sons. In Paul's arguments, the word can also close a door of logic: if something rests on grace, it cannot rest on works. Readers who notice oukéti will see the before-and-after the writer wants them to see. It may mark a change in events, a change in a person's standing before God, or a conclusion that rules out an alternative.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Oukéti is a negative adverb that carries time inside it. An ordinary "not" denies a statement. Oukéti denies it as a change from what came before. Something was happening, or someone held a certain status, and now that has ended. In these temporal uses, the word points back to a "before," even when the writer does not spell it out. Context supplies the cause of the change, such as a plot, a death, a redemption, or a completed sacrifice.

The word does not by itself say the change is permanent. Whether the change lasts depends on what the passage says caused it. The NKJV most often renders the word "no longer," and sometimes "anymore" or "no more."

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Cessation in narrative ("no longer," "anymore")

In the Gospels and Revelation, oukéti reports that an activity has stopped.

  • John 11:54: "Therefore Jesus no longer walked openly among the Jews." The word marks a turning point. After the council resolved to kill Him (11:53), Jesus withdrew to Ephraim.

  • Revelation 18:11: "for no one buys their merchandise anymore." Here "no one" comes from a separate Greek word. Oukéti adds that the busy trade of Babylon, once constant, has ended.

Luke 15:19 and 21 work the same way. The returning son says he is no longer worthy to be called his father's son. He is measuring himself against the place he once had.

Change of status or identity ("no longer… but")

In Paul's letters, oukéti often stands in a "no longer X, but Y" pattern. The pattern contrasts an old standing with a new one.

  • Galatians 4:7: "Therefore you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ."

  • Ephesians 2:19: "Now, therefore, you are no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God."

In both verses, the word points back to what the readers were, and the "but" clause names what they now are. Philemon 16 applies the same pattern to a real household. Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus no longer as a slave, but as a beloved brother.

Logical exclusion ("no longer")

In some arguments, oukéti shows that one established fact rules out another.

  • Romans 11:6: "And if by grace, then it is no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace." Paul is not tracing time here. He is showing that grace and works cannot both serve as the basis of God's choosing, because adding works would empty grace of its meaning.

  • Hebrews 10:18: "Now where there is remission of these, there is no longer an offering for sin." Once forgiveness is complete, any further offering has no purpose.

Galatians 3:18 argues the same way. If the inheritance came by the law, it would no longer come by promise.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The before-and-after built into oukéti lets Scripture tell the story of redemption in a single word. When Paul tells the Galatians they are "no longer" slaves, he does not ask them to feel like sons while they keep living as slaves. He tells them their old standing has ended, because God sent His Son and the Spirit of His Son (Galatians 4:4–6). The same is true of Gentiles in Ephesians 2. Their old status as outsiders is described in the past tense, because Christ brought them near by His blood (2:13).

The word also appears at the center of the gospel. In Romans 6:9 Paul uses it twice. Christ, raised from the dead, dies no more, and death no longer has dominion over Him. His resurrection is the grounding change, and the believer's "no longer" status rests on His. Hebrews uses the same word for a sober warning. In 10:18 there is no longer any offering for sin, because Christ's sacrifice is complete. Yet 10:26 warns that for someone who goes on sinning willfully after receiving the truth, no sacrifice for sins remains. Read together, these verses teach that the finished work of Christ is the only remedy.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not load a theology of permanence into the word itself. Oukéti marks that something has stopped, and the passage tells you why and for how long.

Also watch for passages where time and logic overlap. In Romans 7:17 ("But now, it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells in me"), interpreters debate two readings. Paul may be speaking of a change since conversion, or he may be drawing a conclusion from his divided will. Both elements may be present, so read the whole chapter before settling the point.
