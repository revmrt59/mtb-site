THEREFORE, THEN, SO - (oûn, Strong's G3767)

Summary

Oûn is the New Testament's most common way of saying "this follows from that." It links a new sentence to what came before, and the NKJV usually renders it "therefore," "then," or "so." In the letters it often marks a conclusion or a command that rests on truth already taught, as when Paul moves from God's mercies to "I beseech you therefore" (Romans 12:1). In the Gospels and Acts it more often moves the story forward, carrying the reader from one scene to the next. It can also bring a writer back to his main subject after a side comment. Knowing which job oûn is doing in a passage helps a reader follow the argument without overloading every "then" with theological weight. It also keeps the reader from skipping past the reasoning that a real "therefore" rests on.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Oûn joins a sentence to the thought or event before it and tells the reader that the new statement grows out of what was just said. In Greek it normally sits second in its clause. English translations move it to the front, which is why "Therefore" so often opens a verse in the NKJV.

The strength of the link varies. Sometimes it is tight logic: these facts are true, so this conclusion or duty follows. In narrative it is usually looser: given the situation just described, here is what happened next. In both cases the word looks backward before it moves forward, so the reader should keep the preceding context in view.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Drawing a conclusion or grounding a command ("therefore," "then")

This use dominates the letters. Romans 5:1 gathers up Paul's argument about Abraham and faith and draws out its result: "Therefore, having been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ." Peace with God does not arrive as a new topic. It follows from the justification Paul has just explained.

The same word often turns teaching into a command. John the Baptist sees religious leaders coming to his baptism and tells them, "Therefore bear fruits worthy of repentance" (Matthew 3:8). If they are truly fleeing the coming wrath, their lives must show it. Paul's recurring question in Romans, which the NKJV renders with "then" (Romans 6:1; 8:31), works in the same way. It asks what conclusion should be drawn from what has just been said.

Moving the story forward ("then," "so," "now")

In narrative, oûn usually signals the next step in a sequence of events. It is especially common in John's Gospel. Having said that Jesus needed to go through Samaria, John continues: "So He came to a city of Samaria which is called Sychar" (John 4:5). Later, after explaining that Judas knew the garden where Jesus often met with His disciples, John writes, "Then Judas, having received a detachment of troops ... came there with lanterns, torches, and weapons" (John 18:3). The word shows that one event leads to the next. It does not build a formal argument.

Resuming the main subject ("therefore")

Sometimes oûn brings a writer back to his topic after a digression. In 1 Corinthians 8:1 Paul raises the question of food offered to idols and then pauses to warn that knowledge puffs up while love builds up. In verse 4 he returns to the question: "Therefore concerning the eating of things offered to idols, we know that an idol is nothing in the world." Here "therefore" works much like "So, to return to the question." It picks up the thread more than it draws a fresh deduction.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The old advice "When you see a 'therefore,' ask what it's there for" is sound, and Romans 12:1 shows why. "I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that you present your bodies a living sacrifice." Paul has spent eleven chapters describing God's mercy to sinners, Jew and Gentile alike. Now he calls them to present their bodies as a living sacrifice. A believer's offering of himself to God rests on that mercy and responds to it. It is never the price of earning it. If you read chapter 12 as a fresh list of rules, you lose its foundation. If you read it as the outflow of chapters 1–11, you see why surrender is called "your reasonable service."

The same habit helps in the Gospels, though the question changes. In John, ask what led to this moment rather than what doctrine is being proven. The chain of "then" and "so" lets you watch one response provoke the next, until the story arrives at the cross.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume every "therefore" introduces a weighty theological conclusion. In the Gospels and Acts, oûn often marks only the next event. Ask whether the passage is making an argument or telling a story. Keep in mind that English "then" may translate other Greek words, and that the NKJV sometimes renders oûn as "now," "but," or "indeed." The English word alone cannot tell you which Greek word stands behind it.
