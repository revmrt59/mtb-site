THEREFORE, THEN, SO - (oûn, Strong's G3767)

Summary

Oûn is a Greek connecting word that ties what comes next to what has just been said or done. In the letters of the New Testament it usually draws a conclusion: because this is true, here is what follows. That is why the NKJV so often renders it "therefore," as in Romans 5:1, where peace with God follows from having been justified by faith. It also introduces questions that test a conclusion, and it marks the turn from teaching to living, as in Ephesians 4:1. In the Gospels and Acts, especially John, it more often moves a story forward and is rendered "so," "then," or "now." Readers who notice oûn can follow an argument link by link and see why a command rests on a truth. They should also avoid reading a heavy logical claim into every "so" or "then" in a story.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Oûn joins a new sentence or clause to what came before. It signals that the next statement grows out of the previous one, whether as a logical result, a natural next step, or a return to the main line of thought. In Greek it normally stands second in its clause, so English translations move it to the front as "Therefore," "So," or "Then."

The strength of the connection varies. In an argument, oûn can carry real weight: what follows depends on what has been established. In a narrative, the link is often lighter, closer to "and so" or "next." The word stays the same, but the setting determines how much logical force it carries.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Drawing a conclusion: "therefore," "then"

Here oûn presents a result that follows from earlier teaching or argument. Jesus closes the Sermon on the Mount with "Therefore whoever hears these sayings of Mine, and does them, I will liken him to a wise man" (Matthew 7:24). The "therefore" reaches back over everything He has just taught and presses it toward obedience. Paul does the same after showing that Abraham was counted righteous by faith: "Therefore, having been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). Peace with God is presented as the outcome of justification, not the means of getting it.

Turning from teaching to living, or resuming the main line: "therefore"

Oûn often marks a major turn in a letter. After three chapters on what God has done in Christ, Paul writes, "I, therefore, the prisoner of the Lord, beseech you to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called" (Ephesians 4:1). Paul had already begun to speak of himself as a prisoner in chapter 3 before turning aside into prayer and praise, so the "therefore" also picks up a thought he had set down earlier. Romans 12:1 makes a similar turn. There Paul appeals to his readers to present their bodies to God on the basis of the mercies he has just described (paraphrase).

Asking what follows: "then"

Oûn frequently introduces a question that tests a possible conclusion. In Romans 6:1, Paul asks what should be concluded from the abundance of grace, and whether believers should keep sinning so that grace may increase (paraphrase). He raises the same kind of question in Romans 6:15, 9:14, and 11:1, and in each case he rejects the wrong inference. In the Gospels, questioners use oûn the same way. In John 1:21, priests and Levites press John the Baptist with a follow-up question, asking whether he is Elijah (paraphrase).

Moving a story forward: "so," "then," "now"

In narrative, oûn often carries the account to its next scene. "So He came to a city of Samaria which is called Sychar" (John 4:5) follows the note in verse 4 that Jesus had to pass through Samaria. "Then Judas, having received a detachment of troops... came there with lanterns, torches, and weapons" (John 18:3) moves the story into the arrest. Sometimes the link is so light that the NKJV uses "Now": "Now there were seven brothers" (Luke 20:29).

3. Practical Reading Insight

The old advice to ask what a "therefore" is there for works well in the letters. Romans 5:1 and Ephesians 4:1 both show the order of the gospel. In each passage the conclusion or command comes after the truth it rests on. Paul does not tell the Ephesians to walk worthy so that God will call them; he urges them to walk worthy of a calling they have already received. The word itself only marks the link. The passages supply the content, and that content shows obedience flowing from what God has already done.

The same habit needs adjusting in narrative. John uses oûn far more than any other book, mostly to keep his story moving. When a scene in John opens with "So" or "Then," read it as the next step in the account, not as a hidden theological argument.

4. Reading Guidance

Not every English "then" or "therefore" translates oûn. Matthew's frequent "Then Jesus..." usually translates a different Greek word, tote, which marks time. Other Greek connectors are also rendered "therefore." Let context decide whether oûn signals a firm conclusion or simple sequence. A single "therefore" shows how a passage is connected; it cannot prove a doctrine on its own.
