THIS, THESE, HE, SHE - (hoûtos, Strong's G3778)

Summary

Hoûtos is the New Testament's ordinary word for "this" and "these." Its job is to point. It singles out a person, a thing, or an idea and holds it up so the listener cannot miss it. Sometimes it points back to someone just mentioned. Sometimes it points ahead to a statement about to be made. When it stands alone, English translations often render it simply as "he," "she," or "they."

The same word carries very different moments in Scripture. The Father's voice uses it to identify Jesus at His baptism. John uses it to announce the heart of his message. Jesus' critics use it to talk about Him without saying His name. The practical habit it teaches is simple: whenever you meet "this," ask what it is pointing to. That question will often show you where a sentence places its weight and what tone a conversation carries.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hoûtos works like a pointing finger. It does not name anything itself. It directs attention to something already present in the conversation or about to be supplied.

It can stand beside a noun, as in "this man" or "this cup." It can also stand alone as "this one." When it stands alone and refers to a person, English usually smooths it into "he," "she," or "they," and some of its pointing force can fade in translation. The word also changes form for masculine, feminine, neuter, and plural. Those forms help the original reader track what is being pointed to, much as English "this" and "these" signal one or many. Context, not the word itself, determines whether the pointing carries honor, plain identification, or contempt.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pointing back to someone just mentioned (often "he," "she," "they")

"He was in the beginning with God" (John 1:2).

John has just written that the Word was with God and was God (John 1:1). The Greek of verse 2 begins, in effect, "This one." The word keeps the Word firmly in view while John restates and sharpens what he has said: this same Word was with God from the very beginning. The NKJV's "He" is accurate, but the original points more deliberately.

Identifying a person present ("This is…")

"This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:17).

Here the word points to someone standing in front of the witnesses. At the Jordan, the Father identifies the One who has just been baptized. No one is left guessing whom heaven means. The same words of identification sound again at the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:5).

Pointing forward to what follows

"This is the message which we have heard from Him and declare to you, that God is light and in Him is no darkness at all" (1 John 1:5).

"And this is the testimony: that God has given us eternal life, and this life is in His Son" (1 John 5:11).

In these verses "this" announces content that is still coming. It tells the reader to pay attention because the main point follows. First John 5:11 shows the word working in both directions within one sentence. The first "this" points ahead to the testimony itself. The second points back to the eternal life just named and declares where that life is found.

Singling someone out in a charged setting

"This Man receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2).

"This Man is not from God, because He does not keep the Sabbath" (John 9:16).

Both remarks come from Pharisees speaking about Jesus. They point at Him without naming Him, and the surrounding complaint gives the words a cold, dismissive edge. The word itself is not an insult. The same word carries the Father's delight in Matthew 3:17. The speaker and the situation supply the tone.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Set Matthew 3:17 beside John 9:16 and something striking appears. Heaven points at Jesus and says, "This is My beloved Son." His critics point at the same Man and say, "This Man is not from God." The same pointing word sets two verdicts side by side, and the Gospels leave no room for neutrality about which one is true.

Luke 15:2 goes further. The Pharisees meant their words as an accusation: "This Man receives sinners and eats with them." Jesus answers with the parables of the lost sheep, the lost coin, and the lost son (Luke 15:3–32). In those stories the charge turns out to describe exactly what God Himself does. A sneer, carefully read, becomes one of the clearest summaries of the gospel in the New Testament. Jesus does receive sinners, and the whole chapter shows heaven rejoicing over it.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume "this" always points to the nearest noun. First John 5:20 ends, "This is the true God and eternal life." Many read "This" as pointing to "His Son Jesus Christ," the nearest name. Others take it to mean "Him who is true," the Father. Closeness in the sentence does not settle the question by itself, and the verse should be read in light of John's whole letter. Christ's deity does not rest on this one pronoun (see John 1:1; 20:28).

Remember also that not every English "he" or "this" translates hoûtos. Even within John 9:16, the "such signs" later in the verse comes from a different, related Greek word.
