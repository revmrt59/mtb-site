ALWAYS - (pántote, Strong's G3842)

Summary

The Greek word pántote means "always," "at all times," or "on every occasion." It is a plain, everyday adverb that removes exceptions from whatever it describes. In the New Testament its weight comes from who is acting. When Jesus says, "I always do those things that please Him" (John 8:29), and "I know that You always hear Me" (John 11:42), the word describes the unbroken fellowship between the Son and the Father. When Hebrews says the risen Christ "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25), it describes a priestly ministry that death can never interrupt.

The same word reaches into ordinary Christian life. Jesus teaches that "men always ought to pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1), and Paul calls believers to rejoice, give thanks, and do good "always." Paul's own prayers for the churches are marked by this word again and again. Its final use points forward: when Christ returns, His people will be with the Lord always (1 Thessalonians 4:17). The believer's "always" of prayer and joy rests on Christ's "always" of obedience and intercession, and it moves toward the day when the believer's whole life will be spent in His presence.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pántote is formed from pas ("all, every") and a short word for time, so it means something close to "at every time." It is a common word in Greek and carries no special religious meaning of its own. Most of the New Testament uses come from Paul's letters and John's Gospel. In Paul the word appears most often in his prayers and his instructions to the churches. In John it gathers around Jesus' relationship with the Father.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Pántote describes something that holds true on every occasion, without exception. Context shows whether the idea is regular habit or unending permanence.

1. On every occasion, regularly, as a settled practice. Paul thanks God "always" for the Corinthians (1 Corinthians 1:4), meaning they were a constant part of his prayers.

2. Permanently, without interruption or end. Christ "always lives to make intercession" (Hebrews 7:25), and believers will be with the Lord always after His return (1 Thessalonians 4:17).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word is used in three main ways. First, it describes Jesus' fellowship with the Father and His present work as High Priest. Second, it appears in commands and calls to the believer: to pray (Luke 18:1), to rejoice (Philippians 4:4; 1 Thessalonians 5:16), to give thanks (Ephesians 5:20), to pursue what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:15), and to abound in the Lord's work (1 Corinthians 15:58). Third, it describes the steady habits of Paul and his co-workers, especially their prayer and thanksgiving for the churches.

Paul sometimes sets pántote beside similar phrases to strengthen the point: "without ceasing I make mention of you always in my prayers" (Romans 1:9), and "always in every prayer of mine" (Philippians 1:4). This pairing shows how fully he meant it.

The word can also describe something sad. Jesus told the disciples they would have the poor with them always, but would not always have Him (Matthew 26:11; Mark 14:7; John 12:8). In that setting "always" marks what is ordinary and lasting in this world, set against a brief and precious opportunity.

4. Theological Significance

The New Testament never lets the believer's "always" stand by itself. Before the church is told to rejoice and pray always, Scripture shows a God who acts "always" toward His people. Paul thanks God, who always leads believers in triumph in Christ (2 Corinthians 2:14). He wrote that line out of a ministry full of distress, and he still described Christ's victory as constant. In 2 Corinthians 9:8 he says God is able to make all grace abound, so that believers, always having all sufficiency in all things, may abound in every good work. God supplies grace for every occasion before He asks for faithfulness on every occasion.

This order matters. A command to rejoice always would be crushing if it depended on mood or circumstances. It becomes possible because the God who gives it does not change, and because His triumph and His provision come first.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

John 8:29 and 11:42: the Son and the Father. In John 8:29 Jesus says, "the Father has not left Me alone, for I always do those things that please Him." Earlier He had said that "the Son can do nothing of Himself, but what He sees the Father do" (John 5:19). His obedience had no gaps and no reluctant moments. At Lazarus's tomb He turns the word toward the Father: "I know that You always hear Me" (John 11:42). He adds that He prayed aloud "because of the people who are standing by," so they might believe the Father sent Him. The Son always pleases the Father, and the Father always hears the Son. Every word and miracle in Jesus' ministry came out of that unbroken fellowship. Our salvation rests on a Savior whose obedience never faltered.

Hebrews 7:25: the intercession that never stops. "He is also able to save to the uttermost those who come to God through Him, since He always lives to make intercession for them." The verse just before explains the contrast (Hebrews 7:23–24): earlier priests were many because death stopped each one, but Christ continues forever. The reason He can save "to the uttermost" is that He "always lives" to intercede. Paul gives the same picture of the risen Christ at God's right hand, "who also makes intercession for us" (Romans 8:34). John calls Him "an Advocate with the Father" (1 John 2:1). There is no hour when a believer is left without an advocate.

2 Corinthians 4:10 and 5:6: dying and living at the same time. Paul says he is always carrying in his body the dying of the Lord Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be shown in his body (2 Corinthians 4:10, paraphrased). Weakness, danger, and the wearing down of mortal life were constant for him, and through that very weakness Christ's resurrection life kept showing. A few verses later he can say that "we are always confident" (2 Corinthians 5:6). The constant pressure did not cancel his constant confidence. Each "always" explains the other.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Followed through the New Testament, pántote traces the course of redemption. It begins with the incarnate Son, who always did what pleased the Father. It reaches the short window of His earthly presence, when Mary's costly anointing was right because the disciples would not always have Him (Matthew 26:11). It continues into the present age, where the risen Christ always lives to intercede for His people. It ends at His return. After describing the resurrection of the dead and the gathering of living believers, Paul concludes that so we shall always be with the Lord (1 Thessalonians 4:17).

That last "always" gathers up every earlier one. The disciples lost Jesus' physical presence for a time. The church now prays, rejoices, and endures through the dying of Jesus in this age. At His coming the separation ends for good, and the believer's hope is complete: permanent life in the company of Christ Himself.

7. Spirit & Life

The "always" commands are real commands, and they rest on something deeper than willpower. Jesus' parable in Luke 18:1 was told so that people "always ought to pray and not lose heart." Paul writes, "Rejoice in the Lord always" (Philippians 4:4), and the phrase "in the Lord" carries the reason. Joy that depended on circumstances would come and go. Joy rooted in the Lord can stay because He stays. "Giving thanks always for all things" (Ephesians 5:20) is offered "in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ," through the One who always intercedes.

Paul's own letters show what this looks like in ministry. He thanks God always for the Romans, the Corinthians, the Philippians, the Colossians, the Thessalonians, and Philemon. Epaphras was "always laboring fervently for you in prayers, that you may stand perfect and complete in all the will of God" (Colossians 4:12). For these men, prayer for God's people was a steady habit that ran beneath everything else they did. That kind of shepherding cannot be kept up by human energy alone. The Spirit sustains it, and it reflects in small measure Christ's own unceasing intercession.

Luke 15:31 adds a warning. To the resentful older brother, the father says that the son is always with him and all he has is the son's (paraphrased). He had lived every day in his father's house and in reach of everything the father owned, yet he saw only years of duty. A believer can live near God's presence and provision and still miss its joy. The father's answer is an invitation to see what was always there.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 6:34 — The crowd asks Jesus to give them the bread from heaven always. They wanted a constant supply but had not yet understood that He Himself is the bread.

  • John 18:20 — Before the high priest, Jesus says He always taught openly in synagogue and temple. His ministry had been public, regular, and open to examination.

  • Philippians 1:20 — Paul hopes that Christ will be magnified in his body, as always, whether by life or by death.

  • Colossians 4:6 — Believers' speech should always be gracious and seasoned with salt, fit for answering each person.

  • 2 Timothy 3:7 — Paul describes people always learning yet never able to come to the knowledge of the truth. Constant activity is no virtue when it never reaches the truth.

  • Galatians 4:18 — Paul says it is good to be zealous in a good thing always, not only when he is present. Real devotion does not depend on who is watching.
