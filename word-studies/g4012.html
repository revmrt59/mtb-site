ABOUT, CONCERNING, AROUND, FOR - (perí, Strong's G4012)

Summary

The Greek preposition perí usually points to what something is about. It ties speaking, hearing, knowing, worrying, praying, and testifying to their subject. That is why the NKJV most often renders it "concerning," "about," or "of." Less often it describes what surrounds something, such as a belt around a waist, or gives an approximate time, as in "about the third hour." In prayer and sacrifice it names the people or the sins that the action is directed toward. Jesus' blood is "shed for many," and Christ is "the propitiation for our sins." Recognizing perí helps readers see where a sentence is aimed. When Jesus speaks "concerning John," or when John's Gospel speaks of witness borne "of" Jesus, the word identifies the subject the whole conversation is about.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Perí is a preposition, a word that links a noun to the rest of the sentence and shows how the two relate. In New Testament Greek the ending of the noun after perí helps decide its sense. With one ending it marks a subject: what is spoken of, thought about, prayed over, or offered for. With another it marks surroundings, either physical ("around") or approximate time ("about"). The subject-matter use dominates. John's Gospel often uses perí in the language of witness. Most of the time perí answers a simple question: what or whom is this about?

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Subject of speech, thought, or testimony — "concerning," "about," "of"

Matthew 11:7 reads: "Jesus began to say to the multitudes concerning John." John the Baptist becomes the subject of Jesus' teaching, and the questions that follow ("What did you go out into the wilderness to see?") are all about him. In John 5:31 Jesus says, "If I bear witness of Myself, My witness is not true." Here "of" means "about," and it introduces John's extended theme of testimony concerning Jesus. Worry and care also take perí, as when Jesus asks why His hearers worry about clothing (Matthew 6:28, paraphrased).

Surroundings and approximate time — "around," "about"

Matthew 3:4 describes John with "a leather belt around his waist," so the belt encircles him. In Matthew 20:3 the landowner "went out about the third hour," which gives an approximate time and does not claim a precise one.

The people or sins an action is directed toward — "for"

In prayer, perí names the people prayed over: "Brethren, pray for us" (1 Thessalonians 5:25). In sacrifice, it names those or that which the offering concerns. Jesus speaks of His blood "which is shed for many" (Matthew 26:28). Hebrews uses the same pattern for "sacrifices for sin" (Hebrews 10:6, 8, referenced), echoing the Old Testament language of the sin offering.

Fixed expressions with "the" — "affairs," "state," "companions"

Paired with the Greek article, perí forms compact idioms. "The things concerning us" becomes "that you may know our affairs" (Ephesians 6:22). "Those around Paul" becomes "we who were Paul's companions" (Acts 21:8). In these idioms the English rendering absorbs the preposition entirely.

3. Practical Reading Insight

First John 2:2 uses perí three times in one sentence: "And He Himself is the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the whole world." The word ties Christ's atoning work directly to sins. His sacrifice is aimed at the actual problem that separates people from God, in the same way the old offerings were made "for sin." The repetition also widens the scope on purpose. John will not let his readers think Christ's sacrifice concerns only their own circle, so he extends it to "the whole world."

John's Gospel shows a second pattern. The Baptist, the Father, the works, the Scriptures, and the Spirit all bear witness "of" Jesus (John 1:7; 5:32–39; 15:26, referenced). Reading those "of"s as "about" shows how much of the Gospel is a gathering of testimony concerning one Person.

4. Reading Guidance

An "of" representing perí is not possessive. "Witness of Myself" means witness about Himself, and the Spirit convicting the world "of sin" (John 16:8, referenced) means concerning sin. Likewise, "for" may represent perí or the Greek hyper ("on behalf of"). Their meanings overlap in New Testament Greek, so the choice of one preposition alone cannot settle debates about the atonement. The wider passage must carry that weight.
