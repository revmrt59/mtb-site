PETER - (Pétros, Strong's G4074)

Summary

Peter was a Galilean fisherman named Simon, son of Jonah, whom Jesus called to follow Him and renamed. The name Peter (Greek Pétros) means "a stone" or "a rock." It is the Greek form of the Aramaic Cephas, and John's Gospel spells this out: "You shall be called Cephas" (which is translated, A Stone) (John 1:42). Peter belonged to the inner circle of the Twelve. He spoke the great confession that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God, and he also contradicted his Lord, denied Him three times, and wept bitterly. After the resurrection Jesus restored him by the Sea of Galilee. Filled with the Holy Spirit, Peter preached at Pentecost, led the Jerusalem church through its first persecutions, and opened the door of the gospel to the Gentile household of Cornelius. He later wrote two letters of Scripture as an elder who expected to die for Christ.

Peter's story does not show a man who lived up to his name by sheer strength. It shows the Lord making a rock out of unstable material. Jesus prayed for Peter before he fell, sought him out after he failed, and entrusted His sheep to him. The fisherman who once tried to talk Jesus out of the cross later taught suffering churches to rejoice in sharing Christ's sufferings, and he described Christ as the living Stone on whom every believer is built. His life gives the church one of its clearest pictures of how grace takes hold of a weak disciple and does not let go.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The Peter of Strong's G4074 is one man: Simon Peter, apostle of Jesus Christ. His birth name was Simon. Jesus first met him through his brother Andrew, looked at him, and said, "You are Simon the son of Jonah. You shall be called Cephas" (which is translated, A Stone) (John 1:42). Matthew 16:17 calls him "Bar-Jonah," the Aramaic way of saying "son of Jonah."

Meaning of the name. Pétros is a Greek masculine noun meaning "a stone" or "a rock." The meaning is not a later guess. John himself translates the Aramaic Cephas as "A Stone," so the meaning is stated in Scripture. Peter is therefore a name Jesus gave rather than a name inherited from family. Jesus gave it at their first meeting (John 1:42) and explained it at Caesarea Philippi, where He told Simon that he was Peter and that upon this rock He would build His church (Matthew 16:18). That saying is a deliberate wordplay on the name. Interpreters differ on what "this rock" refers to: Peter himself as the confessing apostle, the confession he had just made, or Christ whom he confessed. The text clearly ties the name to Peter's God-given confession of who Jesus is. The name does not mean Peter was naturally steady. The Gospels show the opposite, so the name reads best as a statement of what Christ would make of him.

Related and separately indexed names. Peter's full biblical portrait also draws on names that Strong's lists under other entries. These are not occurrences of G4074, but the same man is meant:

  • Simon (G4613), his birth name, used alone or in the combination "Simon Peter" (Matthew 4:18; John 1:42).

  • Cephas (G2786), the Aramaic name, which Paul uses often (for example 1 Corinthians 1:12; 9:5; 15:5).

  • Simeon (G4826), a Hebrew-style form of Simon. It appears in the Greek of Acts 15:14, where James speaks of him (the NKJV renders it "Simon"), and in 2 Peter 1:1 in Stephanus' 1550 Received Text and many critical Greek texts (where the NKJV again prints "Simon"). Some Greek editions, including Scrivener's 1894 Received Text, read Simon instead.

He must not be confused with the other Simons of the New Testament: Simon the Zealot, Simon of Cyrene, Simon the sorcerer of Samaria, and Simon the tanner of Joppa, in whose house Peter stayed.

Family, home, and setting. Peter and his brother Andrew were fishermen on the Sea of Galilee (Matthew 4:18). John notes that Bethsaida was "the city of Andrew and Peter" (John 1:44), and Mark places their house in Capernaum (Mark 1:21, 29). Peter was married. Jesus healed his mother-in-law (Matthew 8:14), and Paul mentions that Cephas took a believing wife along in his ministry (1 Corinthians 9:5). He lived in first-century Galilee under Roman rule and later served in Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, the coastal towns of Lydda and Joppa, Caesarea, and Antioch. His first letter sends greetings from "Babylon" (1 Peter 5:13). Many take this as a coded name for Rome, which fits early church tradition that he died there, but Scripture itself does not name the city of his death.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

The call of a fisherman

Peter met Jesus through Andrew (John 1:40–42). The decisive call came beside the lake. Jesus borrowed Simon's boat as a pulpit, then told him, "Launch out into the deep and let down your nets for a catch." Simon's reply shows both weariness and willingness: "Master, we have toiled all night and caught nothing; nevertheless at Your word I will let down the net" (Luke 5:4–5). The catch was so large the nets began to break. Peter fell at Jesus' knees and begged Him to leave, confessing that he was a sinful man (Luke 5:8). Jesus did not leave. He told him that from then on he would catch men, and Peter, James, and John left everything to follow Him (Luke 5:10–11; compare Matthew 4:18–20).

Among the inner three

Every list of the apostles puts Peter first (Matthew 10:2; Mark 3:16; Luke 6:14; Acts 1:13). With James and John he saw what the others did not. He watched Jesus raise Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:37; Luke 8:51) and saw Him transfigured in glory on the mountain. There Peter, not knowing what to say, offered to build three tabernacles (Matthew 17:1–4; Mark 9:2–5; Luke 9:28–33). He was also taken farther into Gethsemane than the others, and he slept while his Lord prayed in agony (Matthew 26:37, 40; Mark 14:33, 37).

Peter is often the disciple who speaks first. He asked Jesus to explain a parable (Matthew 15:15). He asked how many times he must forgive his brother (Matthew 18:21). He reminded Jesus that the disciples had left everything (Matthew 19:27; Mark 10:28). He pointed out the withered fig tree (Mark 11:21) and asked whether a parable was meant for the disciples or for everyone (Luke 12:41). When Jesus asked who had touched Him, Peter answered for the group (Luke 8:45). Jesus even used a coin from a fish's mouth to pay the temple tax "for Me and you" (Matthew 17:24–27).

The same boldness put him on the water. At night, in a storm, Peter asked Jesus to command him to come, and he walked on the sea toward Him. When he saw the wind, he became afraid and began to sink, and Jesus caught him and asked why he doubted (Matthew 14:28–31). The scene previews Peter's whole story: real faith, real fear, and a Savior close enough to grab his hand.

The great confession and the sharp rebuke

When many disciples turned away after the bread of life teaching, Jesus asked the Twelve if they would leave too. Peter answered that there was no one else to go to, because Jesus had the words of eternal life (John 6:66–69). At Caesarea Philippi he went further and confessed Jesus as the Christ, the Son of the living God. Jesus called him blessed, because flesh and blood had not revealed this to him but the Father in heaven. Jesus then declared that He would build His church, promised that the gates of Hades would not prevail against it, and gave Peter the keys of the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 16:16–19; compare Mark 8:29; Luke 9:20).

Within moments Jesus began to speak of His coming suffering, death, and resurrection, and Peter took Him aside and rebuked Him. Jesus answered by calling him Satan and a stumbling block, because his mind was on the things of men rather than the things of God (Matthew 16:21–23; Mark 8:32–33). The man who had just received revelation from the Father was now speaking for the enemy. Matthew sets the two scenes side by side and does not soften either one.

The upper room and the denial

At the Last Supper Peter refused to let Jesus wash his feet. Jesus told him that unless He washed him, Peter had no part with Him, and Peter swung to the opposite extreme and asked to be washed head to toe (John 13:6–9). Peter then insisted he would follow Jesus anywhere, even to death (John 13:36–37). Jesus warned him that Satan had asked to sift him like wheat. He also told him He had prayed that Peter's faith would not fail, and that once Peter had turned back he should strengthen his brothers (Luke 22:31–32). Peter vowed that even if all stumbled, he would not (Matthew 26:33–35; Mark 14:29). Jesus told him that before the rooster crowed he would deny Him three times (Luke 22:34).

In Gethsemane Peter drew a sword and cut off the ear of Malchus, the high priest's servant. Jesus told him to put the sword away, because He would drink the cup the Father had given Him (John 18:10–11). Peter then followed at a distance into the high priest's courtyard (Matthew 26:58; John 18:15–16). Warming himself by a fire, he denied knowing Jesus to a servant girl, then to others, and finally with oaths when his Galilean accent gave him away (Matthew 26:69–74; Mark 14:66–71; John 18:17–27). The rooster crowed. Luke adds a detail found nowhere else: the Lord turned and looked at Peter. Peter remembered His word, went out, and wept bitterly (Luke 22:60–62).

Resurrection and restoration

On resurrection morning the angel told the women to tell the disciples "and Peter" that Jesus was going ahead of them to Galilee (Mark 16:7). Hearing the women's report, Peter ran to the tomb (Luke 24:12; John 20:2–6). The risen Lord appeared to Simon personally (Luke 24:34), and Paul lists this appearance to Cephas first among the resurrection witnesses he names (1 Corinthians 15:5).

The full restoration came beside the Sea of Tiberias. Peter had gone fishing again, and once more Jesus gave a miraculous catch. When John said, "It is the Lord," Peter threw himself into the sea to reach Him (John 21:3–11). By a charcoal fire, recalling the fire where he had denied his Lord, Jesus asked him three times whether he loved Him. Each time Peter answered, Jesus gave him a charge to feed and tend His lambs and sheep. Peter was grieved at the third question, but it matched his three denials (John 21:15–17). Jesus then told him that when he was old another would bind him and lead him where he did not wish to go, signifying by what death he would glorify God, and He renewed the call: "Follow Me" (John 21:18–19). When Peter asked about John's future, Jesus turned him back to his own path of following (John 21:20–22).

Pentecost and the Jerusalem church

After the ascension Peter led the believers in replacing Judas (Acts 1:15). At Pentecost he stood with the Eleven and explained the outpouring of the Spirit as the fulfillment of Joel's prophecy. He preached the crucified and risen Jesus as Lord and Christ, and when the hearers were cut to the heart, he called them to repent and be baptized in Jesus' name for the forgiveness of sins and the gift of the Holy Spirit. About three thousand were added (Acts 2:14–41).

With John he healed a man lame from birth at the temple gate in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, and he made it plain that the power was not their own (Acts 3:1–16). Arrested and questioned by the rulers who had condemned Jesus, Peter, filled with the Spirit, declared that salvation is found in no other name (Acts 4:8–12). The council marveled at the boldness of these uneducated men and realized they had been with Jesus (Acts 4:13). Ordered to stop, Peter and John answered that the rulers must judge whether it was right to listen to them rather than to God (Acts 4:19), and later the apostles declared that they must obey God rather than men (Acts 5:29).

Peter also confronted sin within the church. He exposed the lie of Ananias and Sapphira as lying to the Holy Spirit, and both fell dead (Acts 5:1–11). People carried the sick into the streets hoping his shadow might fall on them (Acts 5:15).

Samaria, the coast, and Cornelius

When Samaria received the word through Philip, the apostles sent Peter and John, who prayed for the new believers to receive the Holy Spirit (Acts 8:14–17). There Peter sternly rebuked Simon the sorcerer for thinking God's gift could be bought (Acts 8:18–24). Traveling through the region, Peter healed the paralyzed Aeneas at Lydda and raised Dorcas (Tabitha) from death at Joppa (Acts 9:32–42).

In Joppa, praying on a housetop, Peter saw a sheet full of animals and heard a voice tell him to kill and eat. When he protested that he had never eaten anything common or unclean, the voice answered that he must not call common what God had cleansed (Acts 10:9–16). At the same time messengers arrived from Cornelius, a God-fearing Roman centurion in Caesarea. Peter went with them and drew the lesson himself: God had shown him not to call any person common or unclean (Acts 10:28). He confessed that God shows no partiality, and while he was still preaching Christ, the Holy Spirit fell on the Gentile listeners, to the astonishment of the Jewish believers with him (Acts 10:34–46). Challenged in Jerusalem, Peter told the story in order and concluded that he could not withstand God (Acts 11:1–18).

Prison and deliverance

Herod Agrippa killed James the brother of John and then arrested Peter, planning to execute him after Passover. The church prayed without ceasing. On the night before his trial, chained between two soldiers, Peter was sleeping when an angel woke him, his chains fell off, and he was led out through the gates. At the house of Mary, John Mark's mother, the servant girl Rhoda was so overjoyed at his voice that she forgot to open the door (Acts 12:1–17).

The council, Antioch, and Paul

At the Jerusalem Council Peter reminded the apostles and elders that God had chosen him to bring the gospel to the Gentiles, had given them the Holy Spirit, and had purified their hearts by faith. He argued that both Jews and Gentiles are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus (Acts 15:7–11). James then summarized his testimony: "Simon has declared how God at the first visited the Gentiles to take out of them a people for His name" (Acts 15:14).

Paul's letter to the Galatians fills in more. Three years after his conversion, Paul went to Jerusalem to see Peter and stayed with him fifteen days (Galatians 1:18). Later the Jerusalem leaders recognized that the gospel to the circumcised had been entrusted to Peter, as the gospel to the uncircumcised had been entrusted to Paul, and that the same God worked through both (Galatians 2:7–9). In Antioch, however, Peter had been eating freely with Gentile believers. When men came from James, he withdrew for fear of the circumcision party, and others followed him, even Barnabas. Paul opposed him to his face, because his conduct was not straightforward about the truth of the gospel (Galatians 2:11–14). In Corinth some believers rallied around his name, "I am of Cephas," a division Paul firmly rejected (1 Corinthians 1:12; 3:21–23).

The letters and the end of the road

Peter's two letters are his final word in Scripture. He writes to scattered believers as an apostle (1 Peter 1:1) and as "a bondservant and apostle of Jesus Christ" (2 Peter 1:1). He calls himself a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ (1 Peter 5:1), and he urges other elders to shepherd God's flock willingly until the Chief Shepherd appears (1 Peter 5:2–4). The charge Jesus gave him by the lake has become the charge he passes on. In his second letter he recalls standing on the holy mountain and hearing the Father's voice (2 Peter 1:16–18). He also knows his death is near, as the Lord had shown him (2 Peter 1:14). He closes by commending "our beloved brother Paul" and placing Paul's letters alongside "the rest of the Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:15–16), a striking word from the man Paul had once corrected in public.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

A foundational apostle and witness. Scripture states plainly that Peter was chosen, named, and sent by Jesus, and that he was a primary witness of Christ's glory, sufferings, and resurrection (2 Peter 1:16–18; 1 Peter 5:1; Luke 24:34; 1 Corinthians 15:5). Paul describes the church as built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Himself the chief cornerstone (Ephesians 2:20). Peter's foundational role is real and is shared with the other apostles. His prominence comes from his witness to Christ and his place in the church's beginnings. Scripture does not present it as an office handed down to successors. Later debates over papal primacy lie beyond what these texts address directly.

The keys in use. Jesus gave Peter the keys of the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 16:19). Acts never uses the word "keys," but Peter is present at each new opening of the gospel: to the Jews at Pentecost (Acts 2), to the Samaritans when the Spirit is given through the prayers of Peter and John (Acts 8:14–17), and to the Gentiles at Cornelius's house (Acts 10). Reading these events as the keys in action is a careful conclusion drawn from the shape of Luke's account, not a statement made by the text. Peter himself saw his role this way. He told the council that God had chosen his mouth to bring the word of the gospel to the Gentiles (Acts 15:7), and James agreed that God had visited the Gentiles "to take out of them a people for His name" (Acts 15:14).

The wall comes down. God used Peter, a man who had never eaten anything unclean, to welcome Gentiles into His people without requiring them to become Jews. Peter's own explanation of the vision was about people: God had shown him not to call any person common or unclean (Acts 10:28). The way the vision speaks of food also signals that the old boundaries of clean and unclean no longer separate God's people from the nations (compare Mark 7:19). The promise to bless all nations through Abraham was taking visible form in a Roman officer's house in Caesarea.

Grace that keeps a failing servant. The New Testament records Peter's failures in unusual detail: the rebuke at Caesarea Philippi, the denial, and the hypocrisy at Antioch. Scripture states the facts. A reasonable conclusion follows from them: no apostle's authority stands above the gospel itself, and God builds His church through people who must keep returning to grace. Peter's later letters show that he learned this.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

Peter is not a type of Christ. His significance lies in how Christ named him, prayed for him, restored him, and spoke through him. Several connections to the larger biblical story are well supported.

The rock and the living Stone. Jesus named Simon "A Stone" (John 1:42), yet when Peter himself wrote about stones, he pointed entirely to Christ. He preached that Jesus was the stone the builders rejected, now made the chief cornerstone (Acts 4:11, drawing on Psalm 118:22). In his first letter he calls Christ the living Stone, chosen by God and precious, and calls believers living stones being built into a spiritual house and a holy priesthood (1 Peter 2:4–8). The New Testament never explicitly links this passage to Peter's name, so this is a thematic connection rather than a stated interpretation. Still, it is hard to miss that the man named "Stone" spends his letter directing every reader to the true Cornerstone and teaching them that they too are stones in God's house. Whatever "rock" Peter was, Christ is the foundation.

The Shepherd shares His flock. Jesus is the Good Shepherd (John 10:11). By the lake He entrusted His sheep to Peter (John 21:15–17), and Peter in turn calls elders to shepherd the flock under "the Chief Shepherd" (1 Peter 5:2–4). The flock always belongs to Christ. Peter only tends it.

Israel's calling given to a mixed people. Peter applies Israel's calling from Exodus 19:6, a royal priesthood and holy nation, to the churches of Jews and Gentiles. He also uses Hosea's words about those who were "not a people" becoming God's people (1 Peter 2:9–10; Hosea 1:10; 2:23). This is explicit New Testament interpretation. The man who opened the door at Cornelius's house now explains from the Old Testament what God has built.

Prophecy fulfilled through his preaching. At Pentecost Peter interprets the Spirit's outpouring as the fulfillment of Joel (Acts 2:16–21) and Jesus' resurrection as the fulfillment of David's words in Psalm 16 (Acts 2:25–31). Here he stands as a witness to what the Scriptures promised about Christ.

A death that glorified God. Jesus foretold that Peter would one day be bound and led away, signifying the death by which he would glorify God (John 21:18–19). The disciple who once tried to keep Jesus from the cross would at last follow Him in suffering. Peter's own letter recalls the reply he had once refused to accept: Christ suffered for us, leaving us an example to follow in His steps (1 Peter 2:21).

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Jesus prays before we fall. Before Peter ever denied Him, Jesus told him that He had prayed for him, that his faith would not fail, and that after turning back he should strengthen his brothers (Luke 22:31–32). The prayer did not stop the sifting, and it did not stop the denial. It did make sure the failure would not be final, and it gave the failure a future use. Notice what Jesus said: "when you have returned," not "if." Every believer who has failed badly can find hope here. The Lord who knew all about Peter's coming collapse had already prayed for him and had already planned his restoration.

Grace is personal. Two small details carry much of the weight. The Lord turned and looked at Peter just as the rooster crowed (Luke 22:61), and the angel's message named Peter alone among the disciples (Mark 16:7). The look broke him. The message called him back. Jesus did not restore Peter with a general announcement to the group. He went after the man who had failed and spoke to him by name, three questions for three denials, beside another charcoal fire. God's forgiveness deals honestly with sin, and it is never vague.

We must be washed by Him. Peter's refusal at the foot-washing looked like humility, but it was really a refusal to receive. Jesus' answer, that unless He washed him Peter had no part with Him (John 13:8), applies well beyond that evening. The first step of discipleship is letting Christ do for us what we cannot do for ourselves. Peter had to learn the same thing at the lake when he begged Jesus to leave him because he was sinful (Luke 5:8). Jesus' answer was to keep him close.

Zeal is no substitute for the Spirit. On his own strength Peter promised to die for Jesus and then could not stand up to a servant girl. He swung a sword in the garden and then denied his Lord in the courtyard. After Pentecost the same man stood before the council that had condemned Jesus and said they must obey God rather than men (Acts 5:29). The council could only explain it by saying these men had been with Jesus (Acts 4:13). Courage of this kind comes from the presence of Christ by His Spirit, and fresh resolve cannot produce it.

Knowing grace does not make us immune to fear. Peter had seen the vision, watched the Spirit fall on Gentiles, and defended it in Jerusalem. Even so, at Antioch he withdrew from Gentile believers because he feared what certain people would think (Galatians 2:12). Sound theology did not protect him from the pull of approval. This should keep every leader humble and teachable, and it is a reason to welcome a brother who speaks the truth to our face. Peter's later warm words about "our beloved brother Paul" (2 Peter 3:15) suggest he received the correction well.

Failure can become a school for suffering. The man who rebuked Jesus for speaking of the cross, and who denied Him to avoid sharing it, later wrote to persecuted Christians that they should rejoice to share in Christ's sufferings (1 Peter 4:12–13), and that suffering for doing good is commendable before God (1 Peter 2:19–21). He was not writing as a man who had never flinched. He wrote as one who had learned through his own collapse that Christ is worth suffering for and that God's grace holds when our courage does not.

Keep your eyes on your own calling. When Peter looked at John and asked about him, Jesus answered that John's future was not Peter's concern, and said, "You follow Me" (John 21:21–22). Comparing ourselves with others is one of the oldest distractions from obedience. Christ gives each servant his own path and asks each one to follow Him on it.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • John 1:40–44 — Andrew brings Simon to Jesus, who renames him Cephas, "A Stone"; his hometown is Bethsaida.

  • Luke 5:1–11 — The miraculous catch, Peter's confession of sinfulness, and his call to catch men.

  • Matthew 14:28–31 — Peter walks on the water, sinks in fear, and is rescued by Jesus.

  • Matthew 16:13–23 — The great confession, the wordplay on his name, the keys of the kingdom, and the sharp rebuke.

  • Matthew 17:1–4; 2 Peter 1:16–18 — The Transfiguration and Peter's later testimony as an eyewitness of Christ's majesty.

  • John 13:6–9, 36–38 — The foot-washing and Peter's overconfident vow.

  • Luke 22:31–34, 54–62 — Christ's prayer for Peter, the prediction and the denial, and the Lord's look.

  • Mark 16:7; Luke 24:34; 1 Corinthians 15:5 — The risen Lord's personal message and appearance to Peter.

  • John 21:15–22 — Threefold restoration, commission to shepherd, prophecy of his death, and the call to follow.

  • Acts 2:14–41 — Peter's Pentecost sermon and the birth of the church.

  • Acts 3:1–4:20; 5:29 — Healing in Jesus' name and bold witness before the council.

  • Acts 8:14–24 — Peter and John in Samaria; the rebuke of Simon the sorcerer.

  • Acts 10:1–11:18 — The vision, Cornelius, and the gospel opened to the Gentiles.

  • Acts 12:1–17 — Imprisonment, the church's prayer, and angelic rescue.

  • Acts 15:7–14 — Peter's testimony at the Jerusalem Council and James's summary.

  • Galatians 1:18; 2:7–14 — Paul's visit, the shared mission, and the confrontation at Antioch.

  • 1 Peter 2:4–10 — Christ the living Stone; believers as living stones and a royal priesthood.

  • 1 Peter 4:12–13; 5:1–4 — Rejoicing in suffering; Peter as fellow elder under the Chief Shepherd.

  • 2 Peter 1:14; 3:15–16 — His approaching death and his affirmation of Paul's letters as Scripture.
