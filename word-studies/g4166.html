SHEPHERD, PASTOR - (poimḗn, Strong's G4166)

Summary

The Greek word poimḗn means a shepherd: someone who tends a flock, feeds it, leads it to pasture, and protects it from harm. In the New Testament it describes the working shepherds near Bethlehem who first heard of Christ's birth. Once, it refers to leaders Christ gives to His church, where the NKJV translates it "pastors." Its most frequent and weighty use is for Jesus Himself. He looks on the crowds with compassion because they have no shepherd. He calls Himself the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep. On the night of His arrest He names Himself as the Shepherd God would strike.

The word carries the Old Testament's long hope that God would one day shepherd His people Himself after their human leaders had failed them. In Jesus that hope takes flesh. He feeds the weary and dies for the flock. He is raised as the great Shepherd who keeps working in His people, and He will separate sheep from goats at the end of history. Every human pastor serves under Him and borrows the title from Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Poimḗn is an ordinary Greek word for a herdsman, and the New Testament uses it in that plain sense. Shepherding was rough, outdoor work that demanded constant watchfulness. Long before the New Testament, Scripture had already given the word a second life. Israel's kings and leaders were called shepherds, and the prophets judged them by that standard. Ezekiel 34 is the sharpest example. God condemns shepherds who fed themselves instead of the flock and then promises to search for His sheep Himself.

Fifteen of the noun's eighteen New Testament uses are in the Gospels. Paul uses it only in Ephesians 4:11. Revelation never uses the noun, but it does use the related verb "to shepherd." That background explains why a simple occupational word could carry so much weight when Jesus applied it to Himself.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is "one who tends sheep." Context determines whether the word is literal or figurative.

1. A literal herdsman who keeps and guards a flock, as in "shepherds living out in the fields, keeping watch over their flock by night" (Luke 2:8).

2. Figuratively, one who leads, feeds, protects, and cares for people, used above all of Jesus (John 10:11; Hebrews 13:20).

3. A leader given to the church to care for and teach God's people, translated "pastors" in Ephesians 4:11.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Luke 2 the word is purely literal, and all four uses there describe the Bethlehem shepherds. In Matthew 9:36 and Mark 6:34 it appears in the phrase "sheep not having a shepherd," which pictures a people left without guidance or care. In John 10 Jesus fills the word with His own identity. He calls Himself "the shepherd of the sheep" who enters by the door (John 10:2) and "the good shepherd" (John 10:11). He sets this figure against the hired hand who runs when the wolf comes.

The letters use the word as a title for the risen Christ. Hebrews calls Him "that great Shepherd of the sheep" (Hebrews 13:20), and Peter calls Him the Shepherd and Overseer of souls (1 Peter 2:25). In Matthew 25:32 the image changes from care to judgment. Christ separates the nations the way a shepherd separates sheep from goats.

4. Theological Significance

The word gathers around a single conviction: God takes personal responsibility for His people. Ezekiel's promise that the Lord would seek the lost, bind up the broken, and feed the flock (Ezekiel 34) is fulfilled in Jesus. When He looks at crowds and sees sheep without a shepherd, He is passing judgment on the leaders who should have cared for them, and He is also stepping into the work God said He would do Himself.

That same shepherding includes judgment. Ezekiel had warned, "I shall judge between sheep and sheep, between rams and goats" (Ezekiel 34:17). In Matthew 25:32 the returning Son of Man does exactly this. Sheep and goats may graze together in the same field, but the Shepherd knows which animals belong to Him, and no one can mislead Him about who is truly His flock. The tenderness of the Good Shepherd and the authority of the Judge belong to one Person.

The flock belongs to Him because He paid for it. Paul tells the Ephesian elders "to shepherd the church of God which He purchased with His own blood" (Acts 20:28). Human shepherds serve a flock they do not own.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Mark 6:34 — Compassion that feeds. Mark writes that Jesus "was moved with compassion for them, because they were like sheep not having a shepherd. So He began to teach them many things." His first act of shepherding is teaching. The scene then moves to the feeding of the five thousand. Jesus has the people sit down on the green grass (Mark 6:39) and feeds them in a deserted place. Moses had prayed that the Lord's people would not be like sheep without a shepherd (Numbers 27:17). Psalm 23 sang of a Shepherd who makes His sheep lie down in green pastures. Mark places these images side by side, and Jesus answers both the prayer and the psalm. Matthew's parallel ends with Jesus urging His disciples to pray for laborers for the harvest (Matthew 9:36–38), so His compassion reaches outward through others.

John 10 — The Shepherd who dies. Kings in the ancient world were called shepherds because they protected their subjects. Jesus names what sets Him apart: He voluntarily gives His life for His sheep. "The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The hired hand has no stake in the flock and runs from the wolf (v. 12). The Good Shepherd stays, and He lays down His life of His own will (vv. 15, 18). His relationship with the sheep is personal. He knows His own and they know Him, in the same way the Father and the Son know one another (vv. 14–15). He also has other sheep outside this fold who will hear His voice, so that there will be one flock under one shepherd (v. 16). This echoes Ezekiel 37:24, where one shepherd from David's line rules over a kingdom that had been split in two. Jesus heals a deeper division by bringing Jew and Gentile into a single flock.

Mark 14:27 — The Shepherd God strikes. On the night of His betrayal Jesus quotes Zechariah: "I will strike the Shepherd, And the sheep will be scattered." In Zechariah 13:7 the Lord of hosts calls for a sword against "My Shepherd, Against the Man who is My Companion." Jesus gives the line in God's own voice, "I will strike." His arrest is the Father's purpose being carried out, even though it looks like the plan has collapsed. The scattered sheep in that moment are the Eleven, who would desert Him within hours. The very next words promise regathering: after He is raised, He will go before them to Galilee (Mark 14:28). The flock scatters when the Shepherd falls, and it gathers again when He rises.

Ephesians 4:11 — Shepherds given by Christ. The ascended Christ "Himself gave some to be apostles, some prophets, some evangelists, and some pastors and teachers." Pastors are His gifts to the church, and Paul joins them closely with teachers, because shepherds feed the flock with God's Word. Their purpose is to equip the saints for the work of ministry (v. 12). A pastor does not do all the ministry; a pastor prepares God's people to do it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Read together, the occurrences trace the whole course of Christ's work. He comes as the compassionate Shepherd who fills the gap left by failed leaders. In John 10 He dies as the Shepherd who substitutes His life for the flock. He is the stricken Shepherd of Zechariah, struck by the Father's own purpose.

Hebrews then speaks of "the God of peace who brought up our Lord Jesus from the dead, that great Shepherd of the sheep, through the blood of the everlasting covenant" (Hebrews 13:20). The blood that sealed the new covenant also secures His continuing work. The next verse prays that God would make believers complete in every good work, working in them what pleases Him (v. 21). The risen Shepherd keeps working in His people after saving them.

Peter adds the story of the wandering sheep. Isaiah confessed, "All we like sheep have gone astray" (Isaiah 53:6). Peter tells believers they were like straying sheep but have now returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of their souls (1 Peter 2:25). That return rests on the verse before it, where Christ bore our sins in His own body on the tree (2:24). The Shepherd brings the sheep home by carrying their sin.

The story ends where Matthew 25:32 points, with the Shepherd-King dividing the nations. Revelation adds a final picture with the verb instead of the noun: "the Lamb who is in the midst of the throne will shepherd them and lead them to living fountains of waters" (Revelation 7:17).

7. Spirit & Life

The Shepherd in Scripture saves His sheep by becoming one. Human shepherds defend a flock with a staff or sling. Jesus defends His flock by taking the wolf's attack on Himself, as the Lamb led to the slaughter. That is why Revelation can say the Lamb shepherds. Scripture also shows God rescuing His people with mighty power and judgment, but the rescue highlighted here does not come through a show of force. It comes through a Shepherd who gave His own life.

John 10 also changes what belonging to God means. The sheep know their Shepherd's voice. Christian life comes down to listening to Him through His Word and following where He leads. A believer can be sure of being known by name, with the same closeness that exists between the Father and the Son.

Peter's phrase "your souls" reaches the inner life. Returning to the Shepherd means giving Him charge over the hidden places, including fears, desires, and wandering thoughts, so that He can watch over them and heal them.

For those who lead, every pastor is an under-shepherd. Jesus told Peter, "Tend My sheep" (John 21:16), and He called them My sheep. A pastor's faithfulness is measured by how well he feeds Christ's flock with Christ's words and reflects His willingness to give himself for others.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 2:8 — The first people to hear of the Savior's birth were shepherds keeping watch by night near Bethlehem, the town where David once kept his father's sheep.

  • Luke 2:15–20 — These shepherds hear, hurry to find the Child, tell what they have seen, and return glorifying and praising God. They show what a right response to God's word looks like.

  • John 10:2 — The true shepherd enters by the door. The way a leader comes to the flock shows whether his leadership is legitimate.

  • Matthew 26:31 — Matthew's version of the Zechariah quotation explicitly names "the sheep of the flock," and in that hour the scattered sheep are the disciples themselves.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • (a) Lexical relatives: G4165 (poimainō), "to shepherd, tend" (John 21:16; Acts 20:28; Revelation 7:17). G750 (archipoimēn), "Chief Shepherd" (1 Peter 5:4).

  • (b) Canonical pairing: G1985 (episkopos), "Overseer," which is joined to Shepherd in 1 Peter 2:25.

  • (c) Thematic connections: H7462 (ra'ah), the Hebrew verb for shepherding behind Ezekiel 34 and Psalm 23. G721 (arnion), the "Lamb" who shepherds in Revelation 7:17.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Symbolic Use

Scripture itself defines some of the shepherd imagery. In Mark 14:27 and Matthew 26:31 Jesus applies "the sheep" of Zechariah 13:7 directly to His apostles. Other uses are established imagery drawn from the Old Testament: the one shepherd of Ezekiel 37:24 behind John 10:16, and the green pastures of Psalm 23 behind Mark 6:39.

One association is exploratory and should be held loosely. Later rabbinic writings suggest that flocks kept near Bethlehem were raised for temple sacrifice, and Micah 4:8 speaks of the "tower of the flock" in that region. If those were temple flocks, the men who guarded lambs meant for sacrifice were the first to see the Lamb of God. Because those sources come from well after the New Testament, this is a fitting illustration rather than a certain fact.
