WHAT, WHICH, WHAT KIND OF - (poîos, Strong's G4169)

Summary

Poîos is a Greek question word. It asks what or which, and often carries the sharper sense of what kind of. It appears in direct questions such as "Which is the great commandment?" and in indirect ones such as "signifying by what death He would die." Its sharpest moments come in confrontations over authority. When the chief priests ask Jesus "By what authority?" and the council asks the apostles "By what power or by what name?", they are not asking for a bare label. They want to know what sort of authority is at work: from God, from men, or from somewhere darker. Paying attention to this word helps readers hear the real question being asked and then notice how Scripture answers it.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Poîos introduces a question about a person, thing, or idea, usually by modifying a noun: what authority, which commandment, what body, what death. In older Greek it leaned toward quality ("of what sort?"). In New Testament usage it often simply means "which?" or "what?" It still frequently keeps that qualitative edge, so context decides how much weight "kind" carries.

The word works in two settings. In a direct question, someone asks it outright. In an indirect question, a question is folded into a larger sentence, as when the narrator explains what Jesus was signifying or when Jesus warns that His disciples do not know the hour of His coming.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Asking about kind or nature (NKJV "what," "what manner of")

In 1 Corinthians 15:35 the skeptic asks, "How are the dead raised up? And with what body do they come?" The objection is about the nature of a resurrected body, and Paul answers it on those terms with seeds, differing bodies, and a body "sown" and "raised" in different conditions (vv. 36–44, paraphrased). In 1 Peter 1:11 the prophets were "searching what, or what manner of time, the Spirit of Christ who was in them was indicating." They wanted to know what that time would be like, not only when it would come.

Singling out one item (NKJV "which," "which ones")

The lawyer asks, "Teacher, which is the great commandment in the law?" (Matthew 22:36), pressing Jesus to choose. When Jesus tells the rich young man to keep the commandments, the man replies, "Which ones?" (Matthew 19:18), and Jesus begins to list them. In other places Jesus asks for which of His good works His opponents mean to stone Him (John 10:32, paraphrased).

Probing the source of authority (NKJV "by what")

The temple leaders confront Jesus: "By what authority are You doing these things? And who gave You this authority?" (Matthew 21:23). The first question asks what kind of authority is at work, and the second asks who granted it. The same challenge appears in Mark 11:28 and Luke 20:2. After the healing of the lame man, the council asks Peter and John, "By what power or by what name have you done this?" (Acts 4:7).

Indirect questions about what cannot yet be seen (NKJV "what")

John uses a recurring expression for a death foretold. Jesus spoke of being lifted up, "signifying by what death He would die" (John 12:33; compare 18:32). Of Peter, "This He spoke, signifying by what death he would glorify God" (John 21:19). The same kind of embedded question warns that disciples do not know what hour their Lord is coming (Matthew 24:42, paraphrased; compare Revelation 3:3).

3. Practical Reading Insight

The authority questions in Matthew 21 and Acts 4 show how much rides on this word. The leaders were asking for a classification, and Jesus answers in exactly that form. He asks whether John's baptism was from heaven or from men (Matthew 21:25, paraphrased). In effect He says, "You want to know what kind of authority I have. First tell Me what kind John had." Peter, filled with the Spirit, answers the council directly: the man stands healed by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom they crucified and God raised (Acts 4:10, paraphrased). The question meant to corner the apostles becomes the opening for their clearest witness.

John's "by what death" carries its own weight. Jesus was not swept unaware toward the cross; He named its manner beforehand. When He described Peter's death, He immediately added, "Follow Me." The death Peter would die would glorify God, and Jesus called him to walk toward it in His steps.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not force "what kind of" into every use. In Matthew 19:18 and 22:36 the simple sense "which?" fits naturally. English "what" also translates more than one Greek word. In 1 Peter 1:11, "what manner of" represents poîos, while the first "what" stands for a separate question in the Greek. Let the setting show whether a question asks for selection or for nature.
