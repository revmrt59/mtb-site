TO, TOWARD, WITH, AGAINST - (prós, Strong's G4314)

Summary

Prós is one of the most common prepositions in the Greek New Testament. Its basic idea is direction: something moves, speaks, or is turned toward someone or something. That one idea appears in several settings. People come to Jesus, and angels and apostles speak to their hearers. A conversation can circulate among a group. A struggle can be aimed against an enemy. Scripture can be written for a purpose. Most memorably, John says that "the Word was with God" (John 1:1). There prós describes the Word in God's presence, distinct from the Father and turned toward Him, even as John goes on to say that "the Word was God." Noticing prós helps readers ask a useful question in each passage: toward whom or what is this action, speech, or relationship directed, and is that direction friendly, hostile, or purposeful?

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Prós links an action or state to the person, place, or goal it is directed toward. In the New Testament it nearly always governs a noun in the accusative, the Greek form that commonly marks a direction or destination. The surrounding verb and context determine what kind of direction is meant. With verbs of going and coming, prós marks a destination. With verbs of speaking, it identifies who is being addressed. With a verb of being, it can describe presence with someone. In contexts of conflict, the same directedness becomes opposition. With abstract nouns, it points to a goal or purpose. English needs several words for this range, which is why the NKJV renders prós as "to," "with," "among," "against," "toward," "for," and others.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Direction toward a person or place / "to," "toward"

This is by far the most frequent use. At the Jordan, John protests, "I need to be baptized by You, and are You coming to me?" (Matthew 3:14). Prós marks Jesus as the one approaching and John as the one approached, which is exactly what troubles John. The same use runs through Jesus' invitations to come to Him (for example, Matthew 11:28; John 6:37).

Address in speech / "to"

After verbs of speaking, prós identifies the hearer: "But the angel said to him, 'Do not be afraid, Zacharias...'" (Luke 1:13). Luke and Acts use this pattern constantly. It keeps the reader clear about who is being addressed in a scene with several speakers.

Presence, relationship, and interaction / "with," "among"

With a verb of being, prós can describe someone in another's presence: "the Word was with God" (John 1:1). John uses the same expression of "that eternal life which was with the Father and was manifested to us" (1 John 1:2). With a reflexive pronoun, prós describes interaction within a group: after Jesus drove out an unclean spirit, "they questioned among themselves" (Mark 1:27). The crowd's questions were directed back and forth among its members.

Opposition / "against"

When the setting is conflict, prós marks the opponent. Paul writes, "For we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this age, against spiritual hosts of wickedness in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 6:12). The repetition names the true opponents one after another. To Saul, the risen Lord says, "It is hard for you to kick against the goads" (Acts 9:5). Saul's resistance was aimed at Jesus Himself.

Purpose or goal / "for"

With abstract nouns, prós points to what something is meant to accomplish. The Old Testament accounts "were written for our admonition" (1 Corinthians 10:11). Paul also uses prós four times in a row when he describes Scripture as profitable for doctrine, reproof, correction, and instruction in righteousness (2 Timothy 3:16, paraphrased).

3. Practical Reading Insight

John's opening sentence holds two truths together, and prós carries one of them. "The Word was God" identifies the Word fully with God. "The Word was with God" keeps the Word from being simply another name for the Father. Before creation there was already Someone in God's presence, turned toward Him in personal relationship. John later describes the Son as being "in the bosom of the Father" (John 1:18, paraphrased), and his first letter speaks of "that eternal life which was with the Father and was manifested to us" (1 John 1:2). The One who came near to us in the flesh had been with the Father from the beginning. When Jesus makes the Father known, He speaks from that relationship, not as an outsider describing God from a distance.

The same word also helps believers see where their efforts are aimed. In Ephesians 6:12, prós redirects the conflict. Christians are tempted to treat other people as the enemy, but Paul names spiritual powers as the true opponents. That truth makes it possible to stand firm against evil while still loving the people caught up in it.

4. Reading Guidance

Prós shows direction, but the context decides whether that direction is friendly, hostile, or purposeful. Some teachers press "face-to-face" into every use, especially John 1:1. The preposition does express presence and orientation, but the richness of the relationship comes from John's whole presentation, not from prós alone. Likewise, the close-combat picture in Ephesians 6:12 comes from "wrestle," not from prós. English "with" can also translate other Greek words, such as meta or syn, so the English word alone does not reveal which one stands behind it.
