CLOSED, ROLLED UP - (ptýssō, Strong's G4428)

Summary

Ptýssō is an ordinary Greek verb meaning "to fold" or "to roll up." In the New Testament it appears only once, in Luke 4:20, where Jesus rolls up the scroll of Isaiah in the Nazareth synagogue. The NKJV translates it "closed": "Then He closed the book, and gave it back to the attendant and sat down."

The word carries no spiritual meaning of its own. Its weight comes from who rolls up the scroll, which scroll it is, and where He stops. Jesus read Isaiah 61, and Luke's quotation ends at "the acceptable year of the LORD," before the prophet's next clause, "the day of vengeance of our God." Then He sat down to teach, every eye in the room turned to Him, and He announced, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing." Read with the rest of the New Testament, that stopping point fits what His first coming was for, and the quiet motion of closing the scroll moves attention from the written promise to the Person who fulfills it.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Ptýssō belongs to everyday Greek. It describes folding cloth or rolling up a written document. In the first century, books of Scripture were long scrolls of parchment or papyrus. A reader unrolled the scroll to the passage, read it, and then rolled it back up for safekeeping.

Luke frames the scene with two matching actions. In verse 17 Jesus "opened the book," and in verse 20 He "closed the book." In the Greek text behind the NKJV, the verb in verse 17 is a related compound meaning "to unroll," so the two verbs form a natural pair. Some modern critical editions read a different, more general verb for "open" in verse 17. Either way, the physical picture stays the same: an open scroll, a reading, a closed scroll.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ptýssō means to roll up or fold together. Here it refers specifically to rolling a scroll closed after reading, which is why the NKJV renders it "closed the book." One sense covers its single biblical use. Everything significant about Luke 4:20 comes from the setting in which the word occurs. The verb itself adds nothing to that significance.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

With only one occurrence, there is no range of usage to trace across authors or books. What matters is the sequence Luke records around the word. In verse 16, Jesus "stood up to read," following His custom on the Sabbath. In verse 20 come three quick actions: He closed the book, gave it back to the attendant, and sat down.

Luke's careful pacing is part of the meaning. Standing to read showed reverence for the Scriptures. Sitting was the recognized posture of a teacher; Jesus sat to teach the crowds elsewhere as well (compare Matthew 5:1). Closing the scroll marks the turn from reading God's Word to explaining it.

4. Theological Significance

This moment shows how Jesus relates to Scripture. He receives the scroll, finds the place, reads it reverently, and closes it with full authority. He does not add a long survey of earlier teachers' opinions. He says the prophecy is fulfilled "today," in Him. The One who ends the reading where He does speaks as One who knows what the prophecy means and when each part of it comes true.

The passage also shows the order of God's purposes. Isaiah spoke of favor and vengeance in a single breath. Luke's quotation stops with the favor, and the wider New Testament shows that through Christ God offers mercy first and announces judgment as something still to come. This reveals God's heart: He does not delight in vengeance, and He opens the door of grace before the day of reckoning arrives.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Luke 4:16–21: Where Jesus closed the scroll. Compare Isaiah 61:2 with Luke 4:19. Isaiah's sentence runs: "To proclaim the acceptable year of the LORD, And the day of vengeance of our God; To comfort all who mourn." Luke's quotation ends: "To proclaim the acceptable year of the LORD." Luke ends the quotation before Isaiah's next clause.

Jesus found the passage Himself (v. 17). By ending the reading there and declaring, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (v. 21), He claimed what He had read for His present ministry: good news to the poor, healing for the brokenhearted, liberty for captives, sight for the blind. The next clause, about the day of vengeance, was not part of what He read that day. Read in the light of the rest of the New Testament, Isaiah's single sentence covers two comings of the Messiah. His first coming brings "the acceptable year," the time of the Lord's favor. The "day of vengeance" belongs to His return, when He will judge (compare 2 Thessalonians 1:7–8). Luke does not explain an interval between the two comings himself; that understanding comes from the wider witness of Scripture.

Luke 4:20: From reading to teaching. The three actions in this verse set up what follows. Jesus closes the book, hands it to the attendant, and sits down. Each step tells the congregation the reading is over and the teaching is about to begin. Luke then gives the result: "the eyes of all who were in the synagogue were fixed on Him." The unhurried pace builds expectation, so when He speaks, the whole room is waiting for His words.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

While the scroll lay open, attention rested on the words of a prophet written centuries earlier. Once Jesus rolled it up and gave it away, every eye moved to Him. The prophecy about One anointed by the Spirit was now seated in front of them.

The rest of the New Testament explains this. Jesus told His opponents that the Scriptures "are they which testify of Me" (John 5:39). Hebrews opens by saying that God "spoke in time past to the fathers by the prophets" but "has in these last days spoken to us by His Son" (Hebrews 1:1–2). In Nazareth that change happened in a single room. Closing the scroll does not set Scripture aside, since Jesus has just honored it by reading it and declaring it fulfilled. It shows where the written promise was leading all along.

Read with the rest of Scripture, the unread line in Isaiah also places us within God's larger story. We live in the "acceptable year." The day of vengeance is real and will come, but God has held it back for a long time. Peter explains why: the Lord is "longsuffering," not wanting anyone to perish but all to come to repentance (2 Peter 3:9).

7. Spirit & Life

The closed scroll speaks to anyone who fears that God's first word to them is judgment. Jesus had that line of Isaiah in His hands and did not read it that day. He came first to bring good news to the poor and freedom to the oppressed. If you come to Him now, you come during the Lord's favor, and the invitation still stands. That is reason for hope and also for urgency. The unread clause has not disappeared from Isaiah, and the time of favor will not last forever.

The scene also guides how we read the Bible. The people of Nazareth heard Scripture every Sabbath, yet on this day the point was to look up from the scroll to the One it described. Bible reading reaches its purpose when it brings us to fix our eyes on Christ. Even this small verb, describing nothing more than rolling up a scroll, points to Him.

Finally, Jesus' quiet authority is worth noticing. He did not argue or build a case. He read, closed the book, sat down, and spoke. The same Lord still speaks through His Word with authority, and He deserves the same undivided attention.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Ptýssō occurs only in Luke 4:20, which is discussed above. Its companion action, opening the scroll in Luke 4:17, appears there as well.
