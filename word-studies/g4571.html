YOU - (sé, Strong's G4571)

Summary

Sé is the Greek word for "you" when one person receives an action or stands at the end of a preposition. It is the singular object form: someone sees you, knows you, sends something to you. English uses the same "you" for one person or many, and for the one acting or the one acted upon, so readers often miss what the Greek makes plain. When sé appears, a single addressee is in view. In the Gospels this often brings Jesus' words down to one person: He saw Nathanael under the fig tree before Philip ever called him. Noticing the singular helps readers hear how personally Scripture often speaks.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Sé marks the person spoken to as the object of the sentence. It usually stands after a verb, naming the one who is seen, known, called, healed, or sent. It can also follow a preposition, naming the person toward whom something moves or with whom something happens. Greek has other forms for "you." Some are singular and some plural, and different forms serve as subject, possessor, or indirect object. Sé is the singular form that receives the action. That is why it so often lands on one individual: a disciple, a petitioner, a governor, or God Himself addressed in prayer.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Direct object of a verb ("you," "You")

This is the word's most familiar work. In John 1:48 Nathanael asks how Jesus knows him, and Jesus answers, "Before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you." In the Greek, sé appears twice here: Philip called you, and I saw you. The words "you were" use a different construction. Jesus' knowledge is aimed at one man.

The same pattern appears in Mark 1:24, where the unclean spirit says, "I know who You are—the Holy One of God!" Here the singular object pronoun singles out Jesus alone as the One the demon recognizes. NKJV smooths the Greek into "who You are," so the pronoun is absorbed into the English clause.

Object of a preposition ("to you," "at your house")

Sé also follows prepositions. In Matthew 26:18 Jesus sends word that "I will keep the Passover at your house with My disciples." "At your house" is NKJV's natural English for a Greek phrase meaning roughly "with you" or "at your place." The man is being told that Jesus is choosing him as host. In Acts 23:30 the Roman commander writes to Governor Felix that "I sent him immediately to you." Sé names Felix as the one official now responsible for Paul's case.

A singular "you" addressed to a city or church

Sometimes sé addresses a group that is spoken of as one. In Luke 19:43–44 Jesus weeps over Jerusalem and speaks to the city as "you" when He warns that enemies will surround her. In Revelation 3:16 the risen Christ tells the church in Laodicea that He will spit "you" out of His mouth (paraphrased). The grammar is singular, but the one addressed is a whole city or congregation, treated as a single body that answers for itself.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Sé keeps reminding us that God's word often comes to a person, not to a crowd. Jesus did not tell Nathanael that He sees people in general. He said, "I saw you." Nathanael's response in the next verse is a confession of faith, and the singular pronoun is part of what moved him. Being individually known by Christ disarmed his skepticism.

The same singular "you" runs through some of Scripture's most personal moments. In John 21:15–17 Peter tells Jesus three times that he loves Him, and each time the pronoun is singular. In Hebrews 13:5 God promises, as a paraphrase puts it, that He will never leave or forsake you, and both "you"s are sé. The promise is made to the whole church, yet it is spoken so that each believer can receive it personally. In John 17:3 the direction reverses. Jesus prays that eternal life is to know You, the only true God. Sé names the Father as the One to be known.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not overpress the singular. Sé means one addressee, but that addressee may be a whole city or church spoken of as one (Luke 19:43–44; Revelation 3:16). A singular "you" in a letter to Timothy or Titus does not mean the instruction is irrelevant to anyone else. Also remember that many English "you"s render other Greek forms. Not every "you" in a verse is sé, as Acts 23:30 shows.
