YOURSELF - (seautoû, Strong's G4572)

Summary

Seautoû is the Greek word for "yourself" when it is spoken to one person. It points an action, a statement, or a measure back to the person being addressed: show yourself, take heed to yourself, love your neighbor as yourself. The word appears in healing instructions, apostolic charges to Timothy and Titus, the great command to love one's neighbor, and the taunts thrown at Jesus on the cross. Readers who notice it will see where Scripture turns a command toward the conscience of the hearer. They will also see how often Jesus was urged to use His power for Himself and refused.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Seautoû is a reflexive pronoun. It shows that the person addressed as "you" is also the one receiving the action or the one being spoken about. Because it always reflects back to someone already in view, Greek gives it no subject form. It appears as the object of a verb ("show yourself"), after a preposition ("about yourself"), or as a point of comparison ("as yourself").

The word is singular. Each time it is used, a speaker is addressing one person: a leper, a jailer, Timothy, Jesus, or the individual hearer of God's law. English "yourself" can hide this, because English uses "you" for one person or many. In Greek, seautoû is always aimed at a single person.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Reflexive object of an action (NKJV: "yourself," occasionally "you" or "your")

This is the most frequent use. The person addressed is told to do something to or for himself. After cleansing a leper, Jesus says, "go your way, show yourself to the priest" (Matthew 8:4). The man himself is the evidence of healing. Paul charges Timothy, "Take heed to yourself and to the doctrine… for in doing this you will save both yourself and those who hear you" (1 Timothy 4:16). Paul lists Timothy's own life before his teaching and before his hearers. Similar charges include exercising yourself toward godliness (1 Timothy 4:7), keeping yourself pure (1 Timothy 5:22), and presenting yourself approved to God (2 Timothy 2:15). The jailer in Philippi is urged not to harm himself (Acts 16:28).

Standard of comparison (NKJV: "as yourself")

Joined with "as," the word becomes the measure for treating others: "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Matthew 22:39). James calls this "the royal law" (James 2:8). The pronoun does not add a new command. It assumes the care a person already gives his own needs and turns that care outward toward the neighbor.

Speaking about or relating to oneself (NKJV: "about yourself," "of Yourself")

After a preposition, the word marks the person as the subject of his own testimony or claim. Leaders ask John the Baptist, "What do you say about yourself?" (John 1:22). The Pharisees object to Jesus, "You bear witness of Yourself" (John 8:13). In John 17:5, Jesus prays for the Father to glorify Him alongside the Father Himself, with the glory He had before the world existed. There the word is addressed to the Father.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Tracing this word through the Gospels reveals a striking pattern. At the temptation, the devil urged Jesus to throw Himself down from the temple (Matthew 4:6; Luke 4:9). At the cross, passers-by, soldiers, and a dying criminal repeated one demand: save Yourself (Matthew 27:40; Mark 15:30; Luke 23:37, 39). Opponents in John accused Him of making Himself something He was not (John 8:53; 10:33). Again and again, people pressed Jesus to turn His power and identity back toward Himself. He would not do it.

He did not seek His own glory. He asked the Father for it (John 17:5). The word itself proves no doctrine. Its repeated use, though, shows clearly that the One who would not save Himself was saving others.

The same word applies the same truth to us. Paul tells the spiritual person who restores a fallen brother to do so "considering yourself lest you also be tempted" (Galatians 6:1). Correction of others begins with honest watchfulness over one's own heart. Romans 2:1, 2:21 makes the same point sharply: the one who condemns others condemns himself, and the one who teaches others is asked whether he teaches himself.

4. Reading Guidance

"Love your neighbor as yourself" is sometimes read as a command to learn to love yourself first. The pronoun sets the measure for loving the neighbor; it does not introduce a separate command. Also, not every English "yourself" represents this word.
