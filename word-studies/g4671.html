YOU, TO YOU, FOR YOU, YOUR - (soí, Strong's G4671)

Summary

Soí is the Greek word for "you" when the "you" is one person and is receiving something, being spoken to, or being affected by what happens. When Jesus says to Peter, "I say to you," or the angel tells Zacharias that Elizabeth "will bear you a son," this word points the statement at one individual. It can also show belonging, as in "What is your name?", which in Greek reads roughly "What name is to you?" It also follows small connecting words such as "with" and "in." English "you" does not show whether one person or many are meant, so knowing this word is singular helps readers see when Scripture is addressing one person directly. That knowledge helps most with personal promises, rebukes, and prayers.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Soí is the singular second-person pronoun in a particular grammatical form, the dative case. In Greek, the dative usually marks the person "to" or "for" whom something happens. The word does not tell the reader what is happening. It tells the reader whom the action reaches: the one who receives a gift, hears a solemn word, benefits from an event, owns something, or stands alongside someone.

Modern English hides one important feature. Older English kept "thee" for one person and "you" for a group, but most readers today see only "you." In Greek, soí always means one person. A different word is used when a group is addressed.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Receiving something given (indirect object)

Common renderings: "you," "to you"

Jesus tells Peter, "And I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 16:19). Peter is the one who receives the keys. Note that the later "you bind" and "you loose" in the same verse come from verb endings, not from this word.

Being spoken to (addressee)

Common renderings: "to you"

"Assuredly, I say to you that this night, before the rooster crows, you will deny Me three times" (Matthew 26:34; compare Mark 14:30). Jesus has just heard Peter promise that he will never be made to stumble (Matthew 26:33); the other disciples join the promise only afterward (26:35). With this word He turns the warning on Peter alone. The same pattern appears when Jesus raises Jairus's daughter and speaks to her alone (Mark 5:41).

For someone's benefit or harm

Common renderings: "you," "for you"

The angel tells Zacharias, "your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son" (Luke 1:13). John's birth is presented as God's answer given for this aging priest who had prayed. The line between "to you" and "for you" is not always sharp, and both ideas can be present.

Belonging or relationship

Common renderings: "your"

Jesus asks the man with the unclean spirit, "What is your name?" (Mark 5:9). Peter asks Ananias about the land, "While it remained, was it not your own?" (Acts 5:4). In Greek, the land "remained to you." Peter's point is that Ananias was under no compulsion. The sin was the lie, not the keeping.

After words like "with" and "in"

Common renderings: "with you," "in you"

"Your money perish with you" (Acts 8:20). Here "Your" and "you" render two different Greek forms. Only the second is soí, and it binds Simon and his money to the same fate.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Because soí is singular, it lets readers hear when a word is pointed at one person. Matthew 26:34 is sharpened by it. Jesus does not issue a general caution to the group. He looks at the most confident disciple and tells him what he will do that very night. Jesus' later restoration of Peter in John 21 is just as personal, so the singular address runs both ways, in warning and in grace.

The same word turns up in prayer. When Jesus says "I thank You, Father" (Matthew 11:25; Luke 10:21; John 11:41), He addresses the Father as one Person, directly and personally. When the Lord answers Paul's pleading by saying His grace is enough for him (2 Corinthians 12:9, paraphrased), the promise is addressed to one weary apostle. Believers rightly take comfort from that verse. The singular simply shows that God's grace first reached Paul as one person in his particular weakness.

4. Reading Guidance

Singular does not automatically mean exclusive. Peter alone receives the keys in Matthew 16:19, yet in Matthew 18:18 Jesus gives the authority to bind and loose to the disciples using plural forms. Compare passages before drawing conclusions from one pronoun. Also, not every English "you" or "your" in a verse represents soí. Verb endings and other pronoun forms are also translated "you."
