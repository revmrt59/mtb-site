YOUR, YOURS, YOU - (soû, Strong's G4675)

Summary

Soû is the Greek word for "your" or "yours" when a speaker addresses one person. It is the singular "you" in the form Greek uses for belonging and relationship. Most often it attaches to a noun to show whose something is: "your prayer," "your faith," "Your name." It also appears after certain prepositions, where English needs a plain "you," as in "out of you" or "prayed for you." Occasionally it stands alone to declare ownership: "Yours is the kingdom."

Modern English uses "you" and "your" for one person or a whole crowd, so the singular force of soû usually disappears in translation. Noticing it can bring a promise or a warning down to a single person. When Jesus warns that Satan wants to sift the disciples, He then turns to Peter alone and says, "I have prayed for you."

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Soû points to one person who is being spoken to and connects something to that person. That something might be a possession, a family member, a body part, a prayer, a faith, or a destiny. The connection is usually belonging or relationship, but Greek also uses this form after certain prepositions and verbs. In those places, English simply says "you."

The word is always singular. Older English kept that distinction with "thy" and "thine." Modern translations like the NKJV use "your" and "yours" for both singular and plural, so the reader has to rely on context to sense that one person is in view. When people speak to God in prayer, they use this same singular form. The Father is addressed directly and personally.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Belonging and relationship (your, Your, own)

This is by far the most common use. In Luke 1:13 the angel tells one aging priest, "Do not be afraid, Zacharias, for your prayer is heard; and your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son." The pronoun ties the answered prayer and the promised child to Zacharias himself. The same form appears in worship: "Hallowed be Your name" (Matthew 6:9). There it marks the name that belongs to the Father who is being addressed.

The NKJV sometimes renders it "own" for emphasis. In Matthew 7:3–5 Jesus contrasts the speck in a brother's eye with the plank in "your own eye." The point lands on the individual listener, who must deal with his own plank first.

After a preposition or verb (you)

Some Greek prepositions take this form of the pronoun, and English translates it as "you." Micah's prophecy, quoted in Matthew 2:6, addresses Bethlehem as a single town: "For out of you shall come a Ruler." In Luke 22:32 Jesus says to Peter, "I have prayed for you." The word still points to one person, but here it names the one acted upon or spoken about, not the one who owns something.

Declaring ownership (Yours)

Sometimes soû stands as the predicate and announces to whom something belongs. The prayer in Matthew 6:13 closes, "For Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever." In Luke 4:7 the devil uses the same word in his temptation. He promises Jesus that if He will worship him, all the kingdoms will be His. The prayer Jesus taught His followers answers that false offer by declaring that the kingdom already belongs to the Father.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Luke 22:31–32 shows why this word is worth noticing. Jesus says, "Simon, Simon! Indeed, Satan has asked for you, that he may sift you as wheat." Those first "you"s use a different, plural Greek word that covers the whole band of disciples. Then Jesus turns to Peter alone: "But I have prayed for you, that your faith should not fail; and when you have returned to Me, strengthen your brethren."

The danger threatened all of them, but Jesus' intercession is spoken to Peter by name. That includes the man who would deny Him that same night. Jesus already speaks of Peter's return and gives him work to do afterward. The singular pronoun lets us hear the Lord's prayer for one failing disciple.

A similar shift happens in Matthew 6:2–6. Jesus moves to singular address when He speaks of giving and praying. He describes "your Father who sees in secret" as the Father of each individual disciple. That private relationship is exactly what hypocrisy tries to replace with public applause.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not turn "singular" into "exclusive." Commands such as "love your neighbor as yourself" are addressed to each person individually, but they bind every reader. The singular presses the word home to the individual. It does not limit who receives it.

Also be aware that the same English "yours" can render a different Greek word. In Luke 15:31, for example, "all that I have is yours" uses a related possessive adjective meaning "yours," rather than sou. The plural "your" is another Greek word entirely, so context and the original form, not the English, settle who is being addressed.
