SOW, SOWER, SOWN - (speírō, Strong's G4687)

Summary

Speírō is the ordinary Greek word for sowing, the farmer's work of scattering seed by hand over the ground and expecting a harvest. Jesus and the apostles took this everyday word and used it for some of the deepest realities of the kingdom of God. Jesus describes Himself and His messengers as sowers of God's word, and He describes Himself as the Son of Man sowing His people into the world. Paul uses the word for the moral law that what we sow we reap, for generous giving, and most strikingly for the burial of a believer's body, which he calls a sowing that will be raised in glory.

What ties these uses together is expectation. No one sows for the sake of sowing; seed goes into the ground because a harvest is coming. The word teaches us to read God's work, our own choices, and even the grave in light of the harvest still ahead. God scatters His word generously, places His people deliberately, and treats the bodies of His children as seed He will bring back to life.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

In first-century Galilee and Judea, grain was sown by broadcasting. The farmer walked the field with a bag of seed and flung it in wide arcs over the ground. The listeners in Jesus' parables knew this work firsthand, and they understood its cost. Seed grain was food that could have been ground into flour. Throwing it into the dirt meant giving up bread now in hope of more bread later.

The word appears in the Gospels, especially in the kingdom parables of Matthew 13, Mark 4, and Luke 8, as well as in John 4, in several of Paul's letters, and once in James. It is closely related to the Greek nouns for seed, but the verb puts its focus on the act itself: scattering, planting, and entrusting seed to the soil.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Speírō means to sow, to scatter or plant seed so that it will grow. The New Testament uses this single meaning in two ways:

1. Literal sowing of seed in a field. Jesus points out that the birds of the air do not sow, yet the Father feeds them (Matthew 6:26).

2. Figurative sowing, in which some action is treated as seed that will produce a later result. Examples include proclaiming the word (Mark 4:14), placing people in the world (Matthew 13:37–38), moral and financial choices (Galatians 6:7–8; 2 Corinthians 9:6), and the burial of the body (1 Corinthians 15:42–44).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word changes with what is being sown. In the Parable of the Sower, the seed is the word of the kingdom. In the Parable of the Wheat and Tares, the seed is people: the sons of the kingdom sown by the Son of Man, and the sons of the wicked one sown by the enemy (Matthew 13:37–39). Paul makes believers themselves the sowers, so that their conduct and their giving become the seed. In 1 Corinthians 15 the body itself is what is sown.

One grammatical detail is worth noticing. In Jesus' explanation of the Sower, the phrase NKJV renders as "he who received seed" (Matthew 13:19) uses this same verb. More literally, it means "the one sown." Jesus describes the hearer as the one sown, so closely is the person joined to the word he has heard. What happens to the word shows what is happening in the person.

4. Theological Significance

Sowing shows us how God usually works. He starts with something small and hidden and brings it, over time, to a harvest no one could have predicted. In the Parable of the Mustard Seed, a man sows the smallest of seeds in his field, and it grows until the birds nest in its branches (Matthew 13:31–32; Mark 4:31–32). The kingdom began with a carpenter from Nazareth and a handful of fishermen. Its beginnings looked unimpressive, but the life was in the seed.

Sowing also reveals a moral order that God Himself upholds. Paul warns that God is not mocked: what a person sows, he will also reap (Galatians 6:7). The principle is neutral in itself. An enemy can sow tares as surely as a farmer sows wheat (Matthew 13:25), and the harvest always matches the seed. The God who generously sows the gospel is also the God who guarantees that sin's seed will not grow into anything but corruption.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The Parable of the Sower (Matthew 13:3–23; Mark 4:3–20; Luke 8:5–15). "Behold, a sower went out to sow" (Matthew 13:3). The sower does not inspect the ground first. Seed falls on the wayside, on stony places, among thorns, and on good ground. Jesus interprets the parable plainly: the sower sows the word (Mark 4:14). The seed is the same everywhere, and so is the sower's hand. The difference in harvest comes from the soil. This is grace scattered widely, offered to hard, shallow, and crowded hearts alike. It also places a serious responsibility on every hearer. The birds that devoured the wayside seed represent the moment when "the wicked one comes and snatches away what was sown in his heart" (Matthew 13:19). Wherever the word is sown, opposition appears quickly. A hardened heart does more than ignore the word; it leaves the word lying on the surface where it can be taken away.

The Wheat and the Tares (Matthew 13:24–30, 36–43). Here the picture shifts. The Son of Man sows good seed, and the good seed is the sons of the kingdom. Christ's people are not in the world by accident. He planted them there. At night an enemy sows tares among the wheat, and the servants want to pull them up at once. The master refuses, so that none of the wheat is uprooted with them, and he waits for the harvest at the end of the age. This explains why evil still grows next to good, even close to God's people. It reflects God's patience, and His concern for His wheat, rather than any weakness on His part.

The Body Sown in Hope (1 Corinthians 15:36–44). Paul answers the question of how the dead are raised by pointing to a field. What you sow does not come to life unless it dies, and you sow only a bare grain, not the plant that will grow. God then gives it a body as He pleases (vv. 36–38). Then comes a fourfold contrast built on this verb: the body is sown in corruption and raised in incorruption, sown in dishonor and raised in glory, sown in weakness and raised in power, sown a natural body and raised a spiritual body (vv. 42–44). Paul could have said "buried." He chose "sown." A farmer does not grieve over seed in the furrow as if it were lost, because putting it in the ground is the first step toward a harvest. A Christian burial is a planting. The weakness and dishonor of death are real, and Paul names them honestly. But they describe the seed. They do not describe the harvest, and the God who gives each seed its own body will give His people bodies of glory.

Sowing and Reaping (Galatians 6:7–9; 2 Corinthians 9:6). Paul applies the farmer's certainty to everyday life. Whoever sows to his flesh will reap corruption, and whoever sows to the Spirit will reap everlasting life (Galatians 6:8). Whoever sows sparingly will reap sparingly, and whoever sows bountifully will reap bountifully (2 Corinthians 9:6). Choices, habits, and gifts are not isolated events that disappear. They are seeds. Because sowing cost real grain, Paul adds the encouragement not to grow weary in doing good, since in due season we will reap if we do not lose heart (Galatians 6:9). This echoes Psalm 126:5–6, where those who sow in tears reap with joy.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament prophets saw Israel scattered in judgment, and they also promised a scattering of a different kind. Through Hosea, God speaks of sowing His people for Himself in the earth (Hosea 2:23), and the Greek Old Testament uses this same verb there. Jeremiah promises that God will sow the house of Israel with the seed of man (Jeremiah 31:27). When Jesus says the Son of Man sows the sons of the kingdom in the field of the world (Matthew 13:37–38), He takes up that hope. The spread of God's people across the earth is a deliberate planting aimed at a worldwide harvest.

The Sower then becomes the seed. Facing the cross, Jesus said that a grain of wheat which falls into the ground and dies produces much grain, while one that does not die remains alone (John 12:24). That verse uses different vocabulary, but it shows the same pattern Paul describes. Christ was sown in weakness and dishonor and raised in power and glory, the firstfruits of all who sleep (1 Corinthians 15:20). Because the first seed has already come up from the ground, the burial of everyone who belongs to Him is a sowing with a certain outcome. The story ends at the harvest at the end of the age, when the wheat is gathered into the barn (Matthew 13:30).

7. Spirit & Life

Sowing teaches us where our responsibility stops. In another parable, a man scatters seed, sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed sprouts and grows, "he himself does not know how" (Mark 4:26–27). Once the seed leaves the hand, the sower cannot make it sprout. This frees the parent praying over a child, the teacher with a restless class, and the believer who has shared Christ with a friend who walked away. Our part is to sow faithfully. The growth belongs to God, who works in soil we cannot see.

It also ends competition among those who serve. In Samaria the disciples gathered a harvest they had not planted, and Jesus said the purpose was "that both he who sows and he who reaps may rejoice together" (John 4:36). He added, "One sows and another reaps" (John 4:37). The grandmother who prayed for decades and the evangelist who sees the conversion share the same joy. Neither one works alone, and neither one owns the harvest.

James shows what kind of soil righteousness needs: "the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace" (James 3:18). He has just described bitter envy and self-seeking (vv. 14–16). Righteousness does not grow in a church that is at war with itself. Peacemakers do more than stop fights. They prepare the ground where God's people can grow.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 6:26; Luke 12:24 — The birds and ravens neither sow nor reap, yet God feeds them. The ordinary work of farming becomes a lesson in trusting the Father.

  • Matthew 25:24–26; Luke 19:21–22 — The unfaithful servant accuses his master of reaping where he did not sow. The servant uses his harsh picture of the master as an excuse for doing nothing.

  • 1 Corinthians 9:11 — Paul reasons that those who have sown spiritual things among a church may rightly reap material support from it.

  • 2 Corinthians 9:10 — God is the One who supplies seed to the sower. Even our generosity starts with what He first gives us.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • (a) Lexical relatives: G4690 (sperma, seed, offspring) and G4703 (sporos, seed sown). These nouns describe the seed, while speírō describes the act of sowing it (2 Corinthians 9:10).

  • (b) Canonical pairing: Sowing is regularly paired with reaping (John 4:36–37; Galatians 6:7–8). The two belong together in Scripture's thinking.

  • (c) Thematic background: H2232 (zāraʿ, to sow) supplies the Old Testament imagery behind the New Testament usage (Psalm 126:5–6; Hosea 2:23).

10. Other Relevant Insights

Symbolic Use

Explicit symbolism: Jesus Himself interprets two sowing parables. The sower sows the word (Mark 4:14). The one who sows good seed is the Son of Man, the good seed is the sons of the kingdom, and the enemy who sows tares is the devil (Matthew 13:37–39). These identifications belong to those parables and should not be pressed onto every use of the word. In Galatians 6, for example, the sower is the individual believer.

Established imagery: Scripture treats sowing as costly, hopeful investment that is followed by a waiting period before the harvest (Psalm 126:5–6; Galatians 6:9). It also treats sowing as God's purposeful planting of His people (Hosea 2:23; Matthew 13:38).

Exploratory association: Releasing seed from the hand illustrates handing over results to God. Mark 4:26–29 supports this application, though it uses different wording for the scattering.
