YOU, YOUR, YOURS - (sý, Strong's G4771)

Summary

This Greek pronoun is the Bible's way of turning speech toward a person and saying "you." It names the one being spoken to as the subject of a sentence, as the one who receives an action, or as the owner of something: "your name," "your prayer," "your cheek." Greek verbs already show who is acting, so when a speaker adds the pronoun as a subject, it often carries weight: you, as opposed to someone else. Peter's confession, "You are the Christ," is the clearest example. English uses one word, "you," for both one person and many, so careful readers watch the context to see who is being addressed, whether an individual, a group, or God Himself. Noticing this small shift in address often shows where a passage stops speaking about people in general and starts speaking to someone in particular.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

This pronoun points directly at the hearer. Its forms let it serve as the subject of a clause, the object of a verb or preposition, or a possessive attached to a noun. In ordinary Greek a verb could stand alone, because its ending already says "you." An explicit pronoun subject is therefore often a deliberate choice that draws attention to the person addressed, frequently by contrast with others in the scene. In its other uses the pronoun is less emphatic but just as important. It marks who is affected by an action and whose something is, and it keeps prayer, command, and promise personal.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Emphatic subject: "You are..."

At Caesarea Philippi, after the disciples report the crowds' opinions, Peter answers: "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" (Matthew 16:16; compare Mark 8:29). The stated pronoun puts Jesus at the center of the sentence. Others have said He is John the Baptist or one of the prophets. Peter says, in effect, that You are the Christ.

Possession and relationship: "your"

The pronoun often follows a noun to show belonging. In the Lord's Prayer, "Hallowed be Your name" (Matthew 6:9), it ties the name to the Father being addressed. To Zacharias the angel says, "your prayer is heard; and your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son" (Luke 1:13). These repeated pronouns make the announcement specific. This is Zacharias's own prayer, his own wife, and a son given to him.

Receiver of an action, including fixed expressions

The pronoun also marks the person on the receiving end: "whoever slaps you on your right cheek" (Matthew 5:39). It appears as well in a common idiom of separation. The demon's cry, "What have we to do with You, Jesus of Nazareth?" (Mark 1:24), uses a set expression that means roughly "What do we have in common with you?" It is a recoil from Jesus, not a request for information.

3. Practical Reading Insight

In Matthew 16:15–16 the question and the answer are both pointed. Jesus has asked about the crowds and now turns the question on His disciples as a group: "But who do you say that I am?" Peter replies by addressing Jesus directly: "You are the Christ." Opinion about Jesus is being gathered, and Jesus will not let His followers hide inside it. He asks for their own verdict, and Peter gives it face to face.

Matthew 5:39 shows a similar narrowing. Jesus begins, "But I tell you," speaking to His listeners together. Then He speaks to each of them as one person facing one insult: "whoever slaps you on your right cheek." The teaching on nonretaliation is addressed to a crowd, but it is meant to be obeyed by each person, one cheek at a time. Watching who "you" is helps the reader hear where Scripture presses its word home to the individual heart.

4. Reading Guidance

English "you" cannot show whether one person or many are addressed. Greek has distinct singular and plural forms, so do not assume a command is individual or corporate without checking the context. The NKJV's capitalized "You/Your" for God reflects the translators' judgment. It is not a feature of the Greek text. Finally, not every "you" is emphatic. Emphasis depends on an explicit pronoun subject and its setting, not on the English word alone.
