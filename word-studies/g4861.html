OF ONE ACCORD, HARMONIOUS - (sýmpsychos, Strong's G4861)

Summary

Sýmpsychos describes people who are united in heart and inner life, sharing one soul together. The New Testament uses it only once, in Philippians 2:2, where Paul pleads with a church he loves: "fulfill my joy by being like-minded, having the same love, being of one accord, of one mind." The NKJV renders it "of one accord." Its single appearance gives it a sharp focus. Paul is describing the unity of believers who share one life in Christ, a unity deeper than agreeing on the same policies or tolerating one another politely.

That unity has a source and a pattern. Paul grounds it in what the Philippians have already received: consolation in Christ, the comfort of love, and the fellowship of the Spirit (Philippians 2:1). He then names its enemy, selfish ambition, and holds up its model, Jesus Christ, who "humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death" (Philippians 2:8). A church becomes "of one accord" as its members take on the mind of the Servant. From a prison cell, Paul says nothing would complete his joy more than seeing this happen.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Sýmpsychos is a compound adjective. It joins syn ("with, together") and psychē, the common word for soul, life, or the inner self. The pieces suggest something like "together in soul." Here the meaning matches the parts, and Paul's use confirms it.

The word appears only in Philippians, a warm letter Paul wrote while in chains (Philippians 1:13). The church there faced pressure from outside, and Paul also had to address strain inside the fellowship. He writes as a spiritual father who longs for the church to stand together.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Sýmpsychos means united in spirit, harmonious, sharing the same heart and inner life. It describes a relationship among several people, so in Philippians 2:2 it applies to the congregation as a whole. One sense covers its use. The NKJV's "of one accord" captures the harmony. The Greek word reaches further inward, to the place where desires, loves, and loyalties live.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Paul places sýmpsychos in a row of four overlapping phrases in Philippians 2:2: "like-minded, having the same love, being of one accord, of one mind." The phrases on either side are built on the verb for thinking, so they speak of a shared outlook. "The same love" names a shared affection. Sýmpsychos sits among them and describes souls bound together. Paul presses the point from several directions so the Philippians cannot reduce unity to one narrow thing.

The word belongs to a thread of "soul" language that runs through these chapters. In Philippians 1:27, Paul urges the church to "stand fast in one spirit, with one mind striving together for the faith of the gospel." There "with one mind" translates "one soul," and the setting is opposition from outside. In 2:2 the same concern turns inward, toward how believers treat one another. Later, in 2:20, Paul uses another rare compound of psychē for Timothy: "I have no one like-minded." The English "like-minded" appears in both 2:2 and 2:20, but it translates different Greek words.

4. Theological Significance

The unity Paul asks for is a gift before it is a duty. The "therefore" of Philippians 2:1 links the command to realities already present: "if there is any consolation in Christ, if any comfort of love, if any fellowship of the Spirit, if any affection and mercy." Each "if" assumes a yes. Because believers share one Christ and one Spirit, they can share one soul. Similar backgrounds or personalities cannot produce this. The fellowship of the Spirit creates it.

The passage also shows the Trinity at work in the life of a church. The Spirit's fellowship comes first (2:1). The mind of Christ supplies the pattern (2:5). The whole movement ends "to the glory of God the Father" (2:11). A congregation that is truly sýmpsychos displays the life of God among His people, and that is why the matter is so serious.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Philippians 2:1–8: Harmony and the mind of Christ. Paul's call to be "of one accord" leads straight into the great passage about Christ's humility. Right after the command, he names what destroys unity: "Let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit, but in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself" (2:3). He adds, "Let each of you look out not only for his own interests, but also for the interests of others" (2:4). Then comes the ground for all of it: "Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus" (2:5). The Son, "being in the form of God," "made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant," and went down to "the death of the cross" (2:6–8).

The order matters. Paul does not give a technique for church harmony. He points to the cross. Souls bind together only as each person lets go of the claim to come first, and Jesus gave up far more than any of us will ever be asked to surrender. Shared humility is what keeps a church "of one accord." Pride, in the words of 2:3, keeps it from happening.

Philippians 2:2 with 1:13: Paul's joy made full. Paul writes as a prisoner whose "chains are in Christ" (1:13). He prays for the Philippians "with joy" (1:4). In 2:2 he asks them to "fulfill" his joy, to fill it up to completion, and he names how: by being like-minded, loving alike, and being "of one accord." He does not tie his fullest joy to release, comfort, or a favorable verdict. He ties it to a church living as one. This shows what Paul believed the gospel is meant to produce: people saved one by one and then joined together in a shared life.

Philippians 2:2 with 2:20: The whole church and one coworker. Paul calls Timothy "like-minded," literally "equal-souled," and adds that he has no one else who "will sincerely care for your state" (2:20). That word describes a rare kinship between two men who shared one pastoral heart. Sýmpsychos is addressed to the whole congregation. Not every believer will carry Timothy's calling or match the depth of his partnership with Paul. Every believer is called to share in the common soul of the church, bound to the others in love and humility.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Christ stands at the center of this word's only use. The unity of Philippians 2:2 flows from "consolation in Christ" and takes its form from the One who emptied Himself. The story then rises past the cross to the day when "every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father" (2:11). A church that shares one soul around a humble, exalted Lord gives the world an early look at that day.

The theme reaches across the New Testament. Jesus prayed that His followers would be one (John 17). The first believers in Jerusalem were described as being of one heart and soul (Acts 4:32). Those passages use different wording, but they point toward the same reality. God's purpose has always been a people, gathered from scattered individuals into one family by His Son and His Spirit.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers treat the soul as private ground and the church as a voluntary gathering of separate people. Sýmpsychos challenges that view. Paul expects believers' inner lives to be shared. They are to care about what others care about and carry the interests of others alongside their own (2:4). That is uncomfortable for anyone who prefers to keep a safe distance.

The path into this kind of unity is not effort to like everyone. It begins by receiving what Christ has already given and then choosing the lower place. When conflict arises, Philippians 2 does not first ask who is right. It asks whose mind is being followed. The person who obeys "let each esteem others better than himself" (Philippians 2:3) is not pretending to be worthless. He is following a Lord who gave up His rights out of love.

Paul's joy also shows what to value in the church. A pastor's heart, like Paul's, is gladdened most by people who love one another well. Programs and growth can encourage, but the deepest sign of grace at work is a fellowship where souls have been knit together by the Spirit.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Sýmpsychos occurs only in Philippians 2:2, which is discussed above. The nearby "soul" language in Philippians 1:27 ("with one mind") and 2:20 ("like-minded") helps explain the word, but those verses use different Greek terms.
