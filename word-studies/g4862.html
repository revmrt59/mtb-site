WITH, TOGETHER WITH, BESIDES - (sýn, Strong's G4862)

Summary

Sýn is the Greek preposition usually translated "with." It places people or things side by side: travelers on the road, disciples gathered in prayer, a gift arriving together with a trial. Luke and Acts use it constantly to show who was present and who belonged to the company. Paul uses the same word for something deeper. Believers have died with Christ, are raised with Him, have their life hidden with Him, and will one day be with the Lord forever. Noticing sýn helps a reader see that the gospel is more than Christ doing something at a distance on our behalf. Scripture also describes believers as joined to Him in His death, His life, and His future.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Sýn joins one person or thing to another and presents them as belonging together in the same action or condition. In a sentence it answers the question "Who else?" or "What else?" Someone goes, prays, suffers, or rises, and sýn names who shares that experience. In the Gospels and Acts the setting is usually ordinary: companions, crowds, family members, and fellow workers. In the letters the companion is often Christ Himself. The verbs around Him then carry great weight: died, made alive, raised, hidden, appear in glory. The preposition does not change its basic job. What changes is how much is being shared.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Accompaniment and company (NKJV: "with," "accompanied," "fellow")

This is the most frequent use, especially in Luke and Acts. After the Ascension the believers waited in Jerusalem: "These all continued with one accord in prayer and supplication, with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and with His brothers" (Acts 1:14). Here sýn draws a circle around the gathering. The women and Jesus' own brothers, who had once doubted Him, are counted inside the praying company.

The same word carries longing in Luke 8:38. The man freed from demons "begged Him that he might be with Him." He wanted to stay in Jesus' company. Jesus instead sent him home as a witness.

Sharing Christ's death, life, and future (NKJV: "with")

Paul repeatedly places believers with Christ in the decisive events of salvation. "Now if we died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with Him" (Romans 6:8). "For you died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:3). The pattern runs through Paul's letters. In paraphrase, believers were made alive together with Him (Colossians 2:13) and will live together with Him (1 Thessalonians 5:10). Colossians 3:4 and 1 Thessalonians 4:17 look ahead: believers will appear with Him in glory and always be with the Lord. In these passages sýn ties the believer's past, present, and future to what happened to Jesus.

Along with, in addition to (NKJV: "with," "besides")

Sometimes sýn adds one item to another. On the Emmaus road the downcast disciple said, "Indeed, besides all this, today is the third day since these things happened" (Luke 24:21). That is literally "with all these things," one more disappointment stacked on the rest. Paul uses the same "along with" sense for comfort: God "with the temptation will also make the way of escape" (1 Corinthians 10:13). The way out does not come only after the trial is over. God supplies it together with the trial. In a similar way, Paul pictures the sinful flesh crucified along with its passions (Galatians 5:24, paraphrased), and bitterness put away along with all malice (Ephesians 4:31, paraphrased).

3. Practical Reading Insight

Romans 6 and Colossians 3 rest on this small word. Paul does not only say that Christ died for us, though that is gloriously true. He says we died with Him. This is why he can call believers to live differently. Their old life ended at the cross because they were counted as being there with Christ. Their new life is secure because it is "hidden with Christ in God," kept where no accusation or failure can reach it. The hope of 1 Thessalonians 4:17 completes the picture. The story that began with dying with Christ ends with being with the Lord forever.

The narrative uses deserve attention too. Luke's steady "with" shows that following Jesus was never solitary. People gathered, traveled, prayed, and suffered together, and the same word that places believers with Christ also places them with one another.

4. Reading Guidance

Sýn does not carry the doctrine of union with Christ by itself. In Luke and Acts it often means simple company. The weight in Romans 6 or Colossians 3 comes from the verbs and the argument around it. Greek also has another common word for "with" (metá), and the two overlap heavily in narrative. Readers should not assume that every NKJV "with" represents sýn, or that the choice between the two always signals a theological distinction.
