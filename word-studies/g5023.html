THESE, THESE THINGS - (taûta, Strong's G5023)

Summary

Taûta is the Greek word behind most of the New Testament's "these" and "these things." It points. Usually it points back and gathers up words, events, or teaching that have just been given, so the writer can say something about them as a whole. With the preposition meta ("after"), it forms the familiar phrase "after these things," which moves a story from one scene to the next. Because the word is neuter (grammatically neither masculine nor feminine), it normally points to deeds, words, and situations rather than to particular people. The NKJV often adds "things" or "words" to make the English read smoothly. When a reader meets "these things," the right response is to look back and ask what has just been said. In verses such as John 20:31 and 1 John 5:13, that question leads straight to the reason the Gospel and the letter were written.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Taûta is the neuter plural form of the Greek word for "this." It works like a finger pointing at something already in view. Most often that is material the reader has just heard: a miracle, a block of teaching, a warning, or a string of events. Sometimes it points to something physically present, such as the temple buildings the disciples were admiring.

The word carries no content of its own. Everything it means comes from what it points to. That makes it easy to read past and important to notice. A single "these things" can gather a whole chapter into one phrase and then say something decisive about it: why it was written, what it proves, or what the hearer must do with it.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Gathering up what has just been said or done (NKJV: "these," "these things," "these words")

This is the word's most common work. John closes his account of Jesus' signs by pointing back to them: "but these are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God" (John 20:31). The previous verse mentions many other signs that were not recorded. The "these" therefore marks off the signs John did choose to write down and gives the purpose behind his choosing. Speech can be gathered up the same way: "As He spoke these words, many believed in Him" (John 8:30). In this verse, "words" is the translator's addition, and taûta alone points back to Jesus' teaching.

The neuter can even describe people by what they did. In 1 Corinthians 6:11, after listing serious sins, Paul tells the believers that some of them used to be exactly that (NKJV, "such"). The Greek says, in effect, "these things." Paul then announces that they were washed, sanctified, and justified.

Pointing to what is present (NKJV: "these things")

The word can also point at something in front of the speaker. When people were admiring the temple's stones, Jesus answered, "These things which you see—the days will come in which not one stone shall be left upon another that shall not be thrown down" (Luke 21:6). Here the pointing is almost physical.

Marking the next scene: "after these things" (NKJV: "after these things," "afterward")

Combined with meta, taûta forms a fixed phrase that closes one episode and opens another. Revelation uses it to advance John's visions: "After these things I looked, and behold, a great multitude which no one could number" (Revelation 7:9). The Gospel of John uses the same phrase to begin new episodes (see John 5:1; 6:1; 7:1; 21:1). In John 5:14, the NKJV compresses the whole phrase into a single word: "Afterward Jesus found him in the temple."

3. Practical Reading Insight

Some of the most important "why" statements in the New Testament depend on this word. John writes, "These things I have written to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, that you may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13). Many believers want that kind of assurance, and John says it comes through what he has written. "These things" sends the reader back into the letter. The phrase points at least to the testimony just given that God has given us eternal life in His Son (5:9–12), and probably to the letter's teaching as a whole. Either way, assurance is anchored in God's testimony about Jesus and in the evidence John has laid out, not in a feeling detached from the text.

John 20:31 works the same way. The signs are not a collection of wonders for their own sake. "These" bundles them into a single witness meant to lead to faith and life in Christ's name. Following the pointer shows the reader what the author wants the whole passage to accomplish.

4. Reading Guidance

The word tells you that something is being pointed to, but not how far back the pointing reaches. In Matthew 24:3, the disciples ask, "when will these things be?" right after Jesus predicts the temple's destruction. Readers debate whether their question concerns only that event or a wider set of end-time events. Let the surrounding discourse decide the scope. The pronoun alone cannot settle it.
