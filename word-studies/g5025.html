THESE, THOSE - (taútais, Strong's G5025)

Summary

Taútais means "these." It is a plural form of the common Greek word for "this," and it points to feminine nouns such as "days," "promises," "plagues," and "commandments." Most often it marks time, as in "in those days" or "in these days." That use ties an event to a particular stretch of the story. It also points back to something the writer has just described, so readers know exactly which promises or which plagues are meant. The practical payoff is simple. When you meet "these" or "those," look back. Paul's call to holiness in 2 Corinthians 7:1 rests on specific promises he has just quoted. Jesus' words in Acts 1:5 count forward from the very days the disciples were living through.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Taútais is a pointing word. It does not add new content of its own. Instead, it directs the reader to something already in view, either a period of time in the story or things the writer has just named. Greek uses different endings to match the noun being pointed to. This form agrees with feminine plural nouns, and in Greek "days" is one of them. That is why so many of its occurrences sit inside time phrases.

Because the word points rather than describes, its meaning always depends on what surrounds it. "These promises" means nothing until you know which promises came before. "In those days" could cover a week or several years, depending on the narrative. The surrounding passage supplies the content, and the word ties the reader firmly to it.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Marking a period of time (NKJV: "those," "these," "now")

Luke, especially in Luke and Acts, uses this word to anchor events in time. "Now Mary arose in those days and went into the hill country with haste" (Luke 1:39). The phrase links Mary's visit to Elizabeth with the angel's announcement that comes just before it. Likewise, "Now it came to pass in those days that He went out to the mountain to pray" (Luke 6:12). Here it places Jesus' night of prayer within the season of growing conflict and just before He chose the Twelve. Luke can also count from such a period, as in "after those days" (Luke 1:24; Acts 21:15).

One idiom deserves special notice. In Acts 1:5 Jesus promises, "you shall be baptized with the Holy Spirit not many days from now." The Greek says literally "not after many of these days." The NKJV's "from now" and the older English "hence" both express that sense. Older renderings like that one stand behind the gloss "hence."

Pointing back to what was just described (NKJV: "these")

The word also gathers up things the writer has just named. In 2 Corinthians 7:1, "Therefore, having these promises, beloved, let us cleanse ourselves," Paul points straight back to the promises he quoted at the end of chapter 6. In Revelation 9:20, "the rest of mankind, who were not killed by these plagues," the word reaches back to the judgments of the sixth trumpet just described. Jesus uses it the same way in Matthew 22:40. In paraphrase, He says that all the Law and the Prophets hang on these two commandments, meaning the love of God and of neighbor He has just quoted.

Standing alone as a pronoun (NKJV: "them," "these")

Sometimes the word appears without a noun and simply stands for one already mentioned. In Luke 13:14 the synagogue ruler speaks of six days for work and then tells the people, in paraphrase, to come in them and be healed, not on the Sabbath. In John 5:3 it refers to the porches at the pool of Bethesda, where a great multitude of sick people lay.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Second Corinthians 7:1 shows why this pointing word repays attention. Paul does not issue a general command to be holy. He says "having these promises," and the reader has to look back to 6:16–18 to find them. There God says He will dwell among His people and walk among them, that He will receive them, and that He will be a Father to them, with them as His sons and daughters. The call to "cleanse ourselves" follows from what God has already pledged. Holiness here is the fitting response of people God has claimed as His own, and the small word "these" holds the command and the promise together.

Acts 1:5 carries a second insight. Jesus does not speak of the Spirit's coming as a distant hope. He counts from "these days," the very days the disciples were spending with the risen Lord. The promise was fixed to their present. Revelation 9:20 offers a sober counterpart: "these plagues," specific and undeniable, still did not lead the survivors to repent.

4. Reading Guidance

English translations shift between "these" and "those" to suit English style. Both can render this same Greek word, so the choice itself does not carry meaning. More important, the word never defines its own scope. Context does. "These days" in Acts 3:24 refers to the days of fulfillment Peter was announcing, not to the reader's own time. Whether "these days" in Acts 11:27 covers a short or a longer period is likewise settled by Luke's narrative, not by the word itself.
