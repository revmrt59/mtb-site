AND, BOTH - (té, Strong's G5037)

Summary

The Greek word té is a joining word, usually translated "and" or "both." Its most noticeable job is to pair things: placed with kai ("and"), it forms the "both ... and" construction that holds two people, groups, or ideas together as one complete whole. When Paul says he is a debtor "both to Greeks and to barbarians," té does that binding work. Té also carries a story forward. Luke uses it often in Acts, where it frequently appears in English as "Then," "So," or "And," linking one event closely to the next. Noticing té helps readers see when Scripture is drawing a complete circle around a group, and when a narrative is flowing without a break.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Té is a coordinating conjunction, a word that joins words, phrases, or clauses of equal standing. It never stands first in its clause. It comes right after the word it attaches to, which is why it can quietly mark the first half of a pair before the second half arrives with kai. On its own, té joins a new element closely to what came before. It adds without contrasting and continues without starting a new topic. Greek has other common joining words, especially kai ("and") and de ("and, but, now"). Té tends to suggest that the joined items belong together. Most of its New Testament uses are in Acts. That makes it one of the habits of Luke's storytelling.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pairing in a "both ... and" construction (NKJV: "both," sometimes "whether")

Paired with kai, té sets two items side by side as a complete set. In Romans 1:14, Paul writes, "I am a debtor both to Greeks and to barbarians, both to wise and to unwise." The first "both" in each pair is té. It groups opposite kinds of people so that no one falls outside Paul's obligation. Luke uses the same pattern in Acts 8:12: "both men and women were baptized." The construction makes the point that the gospel's welcome in Samaria reached across that line too.

Carrying a narrative forward (NKJV: "Then," "So," "Now," "And")

Used without a matching kai, té often links one step of a story to the next. Acts 12:8 opens, "Then the angel said to him, 'Gird yourself and tie on your sandals.'" The angel's instructions to Peter follow one another quickly, and té keeps the scene moving. In Acts 2:46, "So continuing daily with one accord in the temple" connects the believers' daily life to the awe and sharing described just before it. The joining is close and natural. It is not a sharp conclusion drawn from an argument.

Adding a closely joined element (NKJV: "and," "even")

Té can also attach a parallel description or a further example. Hebrews 1:3 describes the Son as "the brightness of His glory and the express image of His person, and upholding all things by the word of His power." Here té binds who the Son is to what He is doing, so that His sustaining of creation belongs with His reflecting of God's glory. In Romans 1:26, "For even their women exchanged the natural use" adds a specific case that supports Paul's broader statement.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The "both ... and" pairing deserves attention, because it often carries the passage's main point. At Pentecost, Luke lists nations from Parthia to Rome and then adds "both Jews and proselytes" (Acts 2:10). The crowd that heard God's works in their own languages included people born into Israel and people who had joined Israel from the nations. Paul's "both to Greeks and to barbarians, both to wise and to unwise" makes a similar point. The gospel he carried was owed to every kind of person, whether cultured or uncultured, educated or not. When you see "both" in these passages, read the two items as a deliberate whole. The writer is drawing a circle and leaving no one outside it.

In Acts, té also helps you feel the pace of the story. Luke's frequent "Then" and "So" often show events following closely one after another. The church's growth, Peter's rescue, and Paul's journeys unfold as one connected account of God at work, not as a string of separate episodes.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not read too much into "Then" or "So" when they translate té. They usually mean "and next," not a long passage of time or a firm logical "therefore." Also, not every English "both" or "and" represents té, since kai and de are often translated the same way. The "both ... and" pairing shows that two items are joined. The wider passage, not the particle alone, explains why that inclusion matters.
