SOMEONE, ANYONE, SOMETHING, A CERTAIN - (tìs, Strong's G5100)

Summary

Tìs is the Greek word the New Testament uses to speak of a person or thing without naming who or what it is. Depending on the sentence, the NKJV renders it "someone," "something," "some," "a certain," "anyone," or "anything." In stories it brings new people into view, such as "a certain Samaritan" or "a certain rich man." In promises, conditions, and warnings it widens the circle to include whoever fits the description, as in "If anyone hears My voice." When readers notice this word, they can tell whether Scripture is introducing a real but unnamed individual or stating something that applies to any person who meets the condition. That distinction affects how we read parables, arguments, invitations, and warnings alike.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Tìs is an indefinite word. It points to someone or something without saying exactly who or what. It can stand alone as a pronoun ("someone," "something"), or it can sit beside a noun and introduce it ("a certain disciple"). Its neuter form, ti, usually means "something" or "anything."

The surrounding sentence decides how far the word reaches. In a plain statement, it normally points to one actual person or thing whose identity is left open. After "if," in a question, or alongside a negative, it stretches to cover any possible person or thing. With a negative, the result is "no one" or "nothing." The word does not hide information for effect. It leaves identity open because, for the purpose at hand, the name is not the point.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Standing alone for an unnamed person or thing (someone, somebody, something, some)

When the woman with the flow of blood touched His garment, Jesus said, "Somebody touched Me, for I perceived power going out from Me" (Luke 8:46). The person was real and present, but not yet identified. The next verse shows her coming forward trembling to tell her story. Paul uses the same word to give voice to an imagined objector: "But someone will say, 'How are the dead raised up? And with what body do they come?'" (1 Corinthians 15:35). The unnamed "someone" lets Paul state the question his readers were likely thinking and then answer it.

The word also appears in the expression "to be something," meaning to be someone important. Paul warns that a person who thinks he is something when he is nothing deceives himself (Galatians 6:3, paraphrased). Gamaliel recalls that Theudas claimed to be "somebody" (Acts 5:36, paraphrased).

Introducing a new person or thing (a certain, a, some)

Luke and Acts often use tìs to bring a character into the story. "But a certain Samaritan, as he journeyed, came where he was" (Luke 10:33). The word introduces him, and the noun "Samaritan" carries the weight of the parable. In Acts, the word introduces someone who is then named: "At Joppa there was a certain disciple named Tabitha" (Acts 9:36). In the plural, it introduces groups, such as the "certain men" who came down from Judea teaching that circumcision was required for salvation (Acts 15:1, paraphrased).

Opening a statement to anyone who fits (anyone, anything, any)

In conditions, tìs widens the scope to include every possible case. "If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20). "If you ask anything in My name, I will do it" (John 14:14). With a negative, the same breadth becomes a total exclusion. Jesus says that no one will snatch His sheep out of His hand (John 10:28, paraphrased). The word rules out every possible thief.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Jesus begins one parable, "There was a certain rich man who was clothed in purple and fine linen and fared sumptuously every day" (Luke 16:19). The next verse uses the same word to introduce a certain beggar, and this time Jesus gives him a name: Lazarus (Luke 16:20, paraphrased). The word itself only introduces each man. The choice to name one and not the other belongs to the parable. The man the world would have noticed goes unnamed, while the man lying ignored at the gate is called by name. That reversal anticipates everything that follows in the story.

The "anyone" passages carry a different weight. Revelation 3:20 is spoken to the church at Laodicea, a congregation Christ calls lukewarm. Even so, His offer is not limited to a faithful majority. It reaches any single person within that church who hears His voice and opens the door. A church may have gone cold, yet the Lord still knocks for the individual.

4. Reading Guidance

"A certain" does not mean the writer has a famous or traceable person in mind. Often it works much like the English "a." Readers do better to focus on the noun it introduces than to search for hidden identities. Also, "anyone" broadens a statement without removing its conditions: John 14:14 still says "in My name." Finally, a closely related Greek word (G5101) is spelled with the same letters but asks "who?" or "what?" The earliest manuscripts usually lacked accent marks, so translators rely on context to tell a statement from a question.
