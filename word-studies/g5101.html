WHO, WHAT, WHICH, WHY - (tís, Strong's G5101)

Summary

Tís is the Greek New Testament's question word. It asks who someone is, what something is, which of several options is meant, and, in its neuter form, why something is happening. The NKJV usually renders it "who," "whom," "whose," "what," "which," or "why." Many of the Gospels' turning points depend on this word. Jesus asks His disciples, "Who do men say that I, the Son of Man, am?" Saul, struck down on the Damascus road, cries, "Who are You, Lord?" Just as often, tís forms questions that expect no answer at all. When Paul asks, "If God is for us, who can be against us?" he is declaring that no opponent can overturn God's saving purpose. Readers who learn to tell a real question from a rhetorical one will follow the Bible's arguments and conversations much more clearly.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Tís asks for missing information. It can stand alone as a pronoun ("Who?" "What?") or modify a noun ("what reward," "what king" (Luke 14:31)). Its neuter form, ti, can also work like an adverb asking for a reason, which English renders "why." The word may introduce a direct question spoken aloud. It may also introduce an indirect question folded into a statement, such as "he did not know what to say."

What tís asks for and what it accomplishes are not always the same thing. Sometimes the speaker truly wants an answer. At other times the question rebukes, challenges, or confidently asserts a truth, and the reader has to decide which from the context.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Asking for a person's identity (who, whom, whose)

At Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks, "Who do men say that I, the Son of Man, am?" (Matthew 16:13). The question brings the disciples' understanding of Him into the open and leads to Peter's confession. In Acts 9:5, Saul responds to the light and the voice with "Who are You, Lord?" The answer, "I am Jesus," overturns everything he believed. In both passages, tís asks the question on which faith depends: who Jesus is.

Asking what something is, or which one (what, which)

Jesus answers a lawyer's question with another: "What is written in the law?" (Luke 10:26). The question sends the man back to Scripture he already knew. In Matthew 5:46 tís modifies a noun: "For if you love those who love you, what reward have you?" Here the question exposes how empty love is when it only returns favors. When there is a set of options, "which" asks the hearer to choose, as when Pilate asks the crowd which of two prisoners he should release (Matthew 27:21, paraphrased).

Asking for a reason (why)

In the storm Jesus asks, "Why are you fearful, O you of little faith?" (Matthew 8:26). The question is a gentle rebuke more than a request for an explanation. On the Damascus road the risen Lord asks Saul, "why are you persecuting Me?" (Acts 9:4). Saul must face the reason behind his violence, and he learns that attacking the church means attacking Christ Himself.

Introducing an indirect question (what, who)

Tís can sit inside a statement rather than end in a question mark. At the transfiguration, Mark notes that Peter did not know what to say (Mark 9:6, paraphrased). Each of the letters to the churches in Revelation closes by urging the one who has an ear to hear what the Spirit says to the churches (Revelation 2:7, paraphrased). The word still points to something to be found out, but it does so within the sentence.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Romans 8:31–35 shows tís at its most powerful. Paul builds a run of questions: "What then shall we say to these things? If God is for us, who can be against us?" Then come three more: "Who shall bring a charge against God's elect?" "Who is he who condemns?" "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" Paul is not inviting suggestions. Each question invites the reader to search for an accuser, a judge, or a power strong enough to break Christ's love, and to come back empty-handed. Paul then supplies the answers himself: "It is God who justifies." "It is Christ who died, and furthermore is also risen." The grammar of the questions creates space for the gospel to fill.

Acts 9:4–5 offers a second insight. Two questions sit side by side. The Lord asks Saul "why," and Saul asks the Lord "who." Saul's answer to the first question collapses once he learns the answer to the second. Many of the questions in Scripture work this way. They are asked to bring the hearer face to face with the truth, not to give the speaker information He lacks.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume every question mark signals a request for information. Context decides whether tís asks sincerely, rebukes, expresses amazement, or makes an assertion. Also, the neuter ti can mean "what" or "why," and only the sentence shows which. Finally, do not confuse tís with the similar-looking indefinite word tis (G5100), meaning "someone" or "a certain one." Simplified reference tools sometimes blur the two.
