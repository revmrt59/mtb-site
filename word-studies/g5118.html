SO GREAT, SO MUCH, SO MANY, SO LONG - (tosoûtos, Strong's G5118)

Summary

Tosoûtos measures something and points to that measure at the same time. It means "so great," "so much," "so many," or "so long," and it usually directs the reader to an amount the passage has already shown. That amount might be the size of a person's faith, the number of fish in a net, the years Jesus spent with His disciples, or the distance between Christ and the angels. Often the measure matters because something follows from it. Jesus had done "so many" signs, yet people did not believe. He had been with Philip "so long," yet Philip still did not know Him. In Hebrews the word helps build arguments of proportion: the Son is "so much better" than the angels as His name is more excellent than theirs. When you meet this word, look back. The text has just shown you how great, how many, or how long, and it expects that measure to carry weight.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Tosoûtos is a pointing word of quantity. Some words point to a person or thing ("this," "that"). This one points to an amount, degree, or length of time, as if the writer were holding up what the reader can already see and saying, "this much." It can describe a noun ("so great a cloud"), stand on its own ("so many"), or mark the degree in a comparison ("by so much"). The English "so" carries real meaning here. The NKJV phrase does more than say "very." It ties the size of something to the setting where that size has been observed or established. A closely related word, toioûtos, points instead to the kind or quality of something ("such a person").

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Amount or degree: "so great," "so much"

When Jesus heard the centurion, He said, "I have not found such great faith, not even in Israel!" (Matthew 8:10). The word measures faith the centurion had just shown when he asked Jesus only to speak the word. Hebrews 12:1 uses it of the crowd of faithful people in chapter 11: "since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses." In Acts 5:8, Peter asks Sapphira whether the land was sold "for so much," naming a specific price. She agrees to the figure, and the lie stands exposed (paraphrase).

Number: "so many"

"But although He had done so many signs before them, they did not believe in Him" (John 12:37). Here the number of signs is set against the unbelief, and the contrast is the point. John 21:11 uses the same pattern after giving an exact count of one hundred fifty-three fish: "and although there were so many, the net was not broken." Andrew's question in John 6:9 also turns on number. He asks what five loaves and two fish are among so many people (paraphrase).

Length of time: "so long"

"Have I been with you so long, and yet you have not known Me, Philip?" (John 14:9). The years of shared life make Philip's request to see the Father all the more striking. In Hebrews 4:7, God speaks "Today" through David "after such a long time," centuries after Joshua. That span shows that God's offer of rest was still open.

Proportion in comparisons: "by so much... as"

Hebrews pairs the word with a matching "as" to show that one measure corresponds to another. Christ has become "so much better than the angels, as He has by inheritance obtained a more excellent name than they" (Hebrews 1:4). After arguing that Jesus' priesthood was established with God's oath (7:20–21), the writer concludes, "by so much more Jesus has become a surety of a better covenant" (7:22). Revelation 18:7 uses a similar pattern for judgment. In the same measure that Babylon glorified herself, she is to receive torment (paraphrase).

3. Practical Reading Insight

Hebrews uses this word more than any other New Testament book, and it does so to reason with its readers. The writer does not simply announce that Jesus is greater. He ties the size of Christ's superiority to something already established: the name He inherited, or the oath God swore. The comparison has a standard behind it. In 10:25 the same logic is applied to daily life. Believers are to encourage one another all the more as they see the Day approaching (paraphrase). The nearer that Day comes, the greater the urgency.

John uses the word to expose the human heart. Signs in abundance did not produce belief, and years beside Jesus did not guarantee understanding. These measures warn that seeing and hearing much is not the same as knowing Him. They also show how patient Jesus was. He kept teaching Philip who He was, even after so long a time.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not read "so" as an empty intensifier. Ask what measure the context has just supplied. At the same time, the word marks proportion, not precise arithmetic, so do not press its comparisons into exact formulas. In Galatians 3:4, "Have you suffered so many things in vain," the word gives only the amount. Whether Paul means hardships endured or blessings experienced depends on how the surrounding context is read.
