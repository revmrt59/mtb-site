THIS, THAT, THEREFORE - (toûto, Strong's G5124)

Summary

Toûto is the Greek word for "this" when it points to a thing, an event, or a whole idea rather than to a particular person. New Testament writers use it to look back and gather up what has just been said or done, as in "So all this was done." They also use it to look ahead and announce what is coming, as in "For this is the will of God, your sanctification." When it is joined with a preposition, it becomes one of the most common hinges of argument in the New Testament, usually rendered "Therefore" or "For this purpose." Paying attention to toûto teaches the reader to ask a simple and fruitful question: what exactly is "this"? The answer often reveals how a command rests on a truth, or how an event fits into God's purposes.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Toûto is a pointing word. Its form is neuter, meaning grammatically "it-like" rather than masculine or feminine. Because of that, it often refers to a whole statement, action, or situation rather than to a single noun. A speaker can wrap up an entire scene with one toûto, or hold the listener's attention with it before giving the main point.

Toûto does not supply content of its own. It borrows its meaning from the surrounding words. For that reason, the reader's task is always to trace the pointer, whether backward, forward, or to the reason that underlies a conclusion. In English it usually appears as "this" or "that." Within fixed phrases, it can disappear into words like "therefore" or "partly."

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pointing back to what has happened or been said ("this," "that")

Matthew 1:22 reads: "So all this was done that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet." Here toûto gathers up the whole account before it: Mary's pregnancy, Joseph's distress, and the angel's message. Matthew is not pointing to one detail. He presents the entire sequence as the fulfillment of God's word.

Pointing forward to what is about to be stated ("this")

In 1 Thessalonians 4:3 Paul writes: "For this is the will of God, your sanctification: that you should abstain from sexual immorality." The word raises the question "What is God's will?" and the rest of the sentence answers it. A similar forward-pointing use appears in 1 John 3:8: "For this purpose the Son of God was manifested, that He might destroy the works of the devil." The "this" waits for the "that" clause to explain it.

Joined with a preposition to state a reason or conclusion ("Therefore," "for this reason," "to this end")

The phrase "because of this" commonly becomes "Therefore" in English. In Matthew 6:25 Jesus says: "Therefore I say to you, do not worry about your life." The command not to worry rests on what He has just taught about serving God rather than money. Romans 5:12 uses the same kind of link: "Therefore, just as through one man sin entered the world..." Here toûto lies inside the word "Therefore," so no single English word can be bolded as its equivalent.

A paired idiom expressing "partly ... partly" ("partly")

Hebrews 10:33 uses toûto twice in a balanced construction: "partly while you were made a spectacle both by reproaches and tribulations, and partly while you became companions of those who were so treated." The writer divides the believers' past suffering into two kinds. Some endured public shame, and some stood beside those who did. Both counted as faithfulness.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Many of the New Testament's "therefores" carry a pronoun inside them. "Therefore" in these places means "because of this." It asks the reader to identify the "this." When Jesus says, "Therefore I say to you, do not worry," He does not hand out a freestanding rule for calm living. He grounds the command in what He has just said: we cannot serve two masters, and the Father who is served can be trusted. Freedom from anxiety comes from belonging to God, not from willpower.

Romans 5:12 works the same way on a larger scale. Paul's "Therefore" ties his account of Adam and Christ to what he has already said about peace with God and reconciliation through Christ's death. If readers skip that link, the passage can become an abstract theory about sin. If they follow it, they find a declaration of how fully Christ has undone what Adam began. Following toûto keeps commands connected to the grace that makes obedience possible.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume that "this" or "that" points to the nearest noun. In Ephesians 2:8, "and that not of yourselves," toûto is neuter, while "grace" and "faith" are both feminine in Greek. For this reason, many readers take "that" to mean the whole saving work: salvation by grace through faith. The grammar fits that reading well, though some still apply it to faith alone. Either way, the verse clearly states that salvation is God's gift. Also note that English "that" here is a pronoun, not the connecting word "that."
