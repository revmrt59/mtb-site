THIS, THIS ONE, HIM - (toûton, Strong's G5126)

Summary

Toûton is a Greek pointing word. It marks one particular man or one particular thing as the object of an action, and the NKJV renders it "this," "him," "this Man," or "this one." Sometimes it stands alone and points to a person: "Him they compelled to bear His cross." Other times it sits beside a noun and singles that noun out: "this bread" or "this saying." Noticing the word helps readers see who is being pointed at, and why the speaker wants no confusion about it. Peter's Pentecost sermon is the clearest example. He keeps pointing at the same crucified man, and that man is the one God raised and made Lord and Christ.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Toûton is a form of the common Greek word for "this." It is masculine and singular, and it usually marks the thing receiving the action of the verb. It works in two ways. It can stand on its own in place of a person, much like English "this one" or "him." It can also accompany a masculine noun and specify which one is meant: "this saying," not some other saying.

English "him" can hide what the word is doing. A plain pronoun simply refers back to someone. A demonstrative points at him. When Greek writers place toûton at the front of a clause, the pointing becomes especially noticeable, as though the speaker were directing every eye to a single figure.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pointing to a person (renderings: "him," "Him," "this Man," "this one")

Standing alone, toûton identifies a specific man who is already in view. Matthew introduces a stranger and then fixes attention on him: "Now as they came out, they found a man of Cyrene, Simon by name. Him they compelled to bear His cross" (Matthew 27:32). The word ties the soldiers' order to the man just named, and the NKJV keeps the Greek word order so the emphasis is visible. Peter does the same with Jesus: "Him, being delivered by the determined purpose and foreknowledge of God, you have taken by lawless hands, have crucified, and put to death" (Acts 2:23). Placing "Him" first makes Jesus the unmistakable center of the accusation.

Specifying a thing or person alongside a noun (rendering: "this")

Attached to a noun, toûton narrows attention to one particular item. After His teaching on marriage, Jesus says, "All cannot accept this saying, but only those to whom it has been given" (Matthew 19:11). "This saying" reaches back to the hard words just spoken, so the reader knows exactly which teaching is in question. Paul writes, "For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord's death till He comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26). Here the word points to the bread of the Lord's Supper Paul has just described. The second "this," before "cup," translates a different form of the word.

Pointing with the speaker's attitude attached (renderings: "this," "this fellow")

The word does not carry a tone of its own, but speakers bring their tone to it. The Pharisees say of Jesus, "We know that God spoke to Moses; as for this fellow, we do not know where He is from" (John 9:29). The scorn comes from the speakers and the setting, not from the pronoun. The NKJV supplies "fellow" to convey that dismissive edge.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Peter's sermon in Acts 2 shows the word at its most forceful. He begins with "Him... you have... crucified" (2:23). He continues, "This Jesus God has raised up, of which we are all witnesses" (2:32). Then he closes: "God has made this Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ" (2:36). Each time he points at the same person. The man executed outside Jerusalem weeks earlier is the man God raised and enthroned. Peter leaves no room to turn the resurrection into a vague spiritual idea or to imagine some other Messiah. His hearers are confronted with the identity of Jesus, and that confrontation leads them to ask, "What shall we do?"

Stephen preaches the same way about Moses in Acts 7:35. In paraphrase, he says that this very Moses, whom Israel rejected, is the one God sent as ruler and deliverer. The pattern points toward Jesus: God exalts the very one His people turned away.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not read "him" in these passages as a colorless pronoun. Toûton points. When the NKJV says "this Man" and the Greek has only the pronoun, the word "man" has been supplied from the masculine form. Remember too that the word adds no warmth or contempt of its own. Whether "this one" sounds reverent or dismissive depends on who is speaking.
