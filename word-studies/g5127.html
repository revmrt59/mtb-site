THIS, OF THIS - (toútou, Strong's G5127)

Summary

Toútou is a form of the Greek word for "this." It points to someone or something close at hand: a person just mentioned, an idea just stated, or the present world in which the speaker stands. Most often the NKJV simply renders it "this," as in Jesus' words, "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36). It can also stand alone for a person ("He hears him," John 9:31). It can join a small preposition to make connecting phrases such as "For this reason" (Ephesians 3:1). Because the form is the same whether it means "of this man" or "of this thing," the surrounding sentence must tell the reader which is meant. Paying attention to what toútou points to helps readers follow the speaker's line of thought. It also shows how carefully the New Testament marks off the present age from what God is bringing.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Toútou is a demonstrative, a word that points. In English we point with "this" and "that." Greek uses this word for the nearer "this." The form toútou is genitive, the Greek case that often carries the sense of "of," "belonging to," or "from," and that certain prepositions require. It works in three ways. It can sit beside a noun and narrow it ("this world," "this age"). It can stand alone as "this one" or "this thing." It can follow a preposition to form a phrase that ties one statement to another. In every case, its job is to direct the reader's attention to something specific that the context has already placed in view.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pointing out a specific noun: "of this world," "of this age"

Common NKJV rendering: this

Many occurrences limit a noun to the present setting. Jesus told Pilate, "My kingdom is not of this world. If My kingdom were of this world, My servants would fight" (John 18:36). Paul writes that "the god of this age has blinded" the minds of unbelievers (2 Corinthians 4:4). The word does not describe the world as evil in itself. It fixes the world or age under discussion as the present one, the one Jesus' kingdom does not come from. The same pattern appears in "the ruler of this world" (John 12:31), "the wisdom of this world" (1 Corinthians 3:19), and the closing references to "this book" in Revelation 22:7, 10, and 18.

Standing alone for a person or thing

Common NKJV renderings: him, this man's, this Man, one's, thing

Without a noun, toútou means "this one." The healed blind man argues, "if anyone is a worshiper of God and does His will, He hears him" (John 9:31). "Him" points back to that worshiper. In Paul's sermon at Antioch, "From this man's seed, according to the promise, God raised up for Israel a Savior—Jesus" (Acts 13:23). Here the word reaches back to David in the previous verse. Shortly after, Paul uses it again for Jesus, declaring that through this Man forgiveness of sins is preached (Acts 13:38, paraphrased).

Joined to a preposition: "for this reason," "from that time"

Common NKJV renderings: For this reason, about what, from that time, from then on

Paired with a preposition, toútou links one thought to another. Paul opens, "For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus for you Gentiles—" (Ephesians 3:1). In Ephesians 5:31, the whole phrase "For this reason" renders a different preposition with toútou, introducing the words of Genesis 2:24. With another preposition, it marks a turning point in time or cause. After the Bread of Life discourse, many disciples turned back, and the NKJV renders the phrase "from that time" (John 6:66, paraphrased).

3. Practical Reading Insight

Ephesians 3:1 shows why these phrases deserve a second look. Paul writes "For this reason," then breaks off mid-sentence and spends twelve verses on the mystery entrusted to him. In 3:14 he returns with the same phrase and finally kneels in prayer. Both times, "this" points back to chapter 2. There God has made Jew and Gentile one new people, reconciled to Him through the cross. Paul's prayer for the Ephesians rests on what God has already done.

Acts 13:23 offers another reward. "This man" is David, and Paul's sentence moves directly from David's family line to "a Savior—Jesus." The small pointer carries the reader along the line of God's promise. The demonstrative itself adds nothing new. Paul's argument, however, depends on our knowing exactly whom it points to.

4. Reading Guidance

The same Greek form can mean "of this man" or "of this thing," and context alone decides. Phrases like the one in John 6:66 can sometimes be read as either "from that time" or "for this reason." Readers should also avoid building doctrine on "this" alone. In "this world," the demonstrative identifies the present age. The surrounding teaching explains what that age is like.
