THESE, THOSE - (toútous, Strong's G5128)

Summary

Toútous is a Greek form of "these," used for a group of people or things that receive an action. It points the reader to someone or something definite: the sayings Jesus has just spoken, the twelve men just named, or the disciples standing beside Him in the garden. The NKJV usually renders it "these," and sometimes "those," "them," or "such." Noticing this pointing word keeps the reader from losing track of who is meant. It also brings out one of Paul's most comforting sentences: in Romans 8:30 the same "these" carries God's people from His calling all the way to glory.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Toútous is the accusative masculine plural form of the Greek demonstrative "this/these." That grammar matters for a practical reason. The word normally marks people or things that receive the action of a verb or follow a preposition. It does two kinds of work. Sometimes it stands beside a noun, as in "these sayings." Other times it stands alone and represents a group already in view.

Either way, it points. It tells the reader to look back at what was just said, or to look at who is present in the scene. Sometimes it looks ahead to a description that is about to come. The word supplies no new information of its own. Its value is that it fixes attention on one particular group.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pointing back with a noun ("these sayings," "these twelve")

When joined to a noun, toútous ties that noun to what came before. Jesus closes the Sermon on the Mount with "whoever hears these sayings of Mine, and does them" (Matthew 7:24). The warning is not about religious teaching in general. It applies to the words His hearers have just received in chapters 5–7.

Matthew uses the same pointing language at the turning points of his Gospel. He notes that Jesus had finished "these sayings" when He ended major teaching sections (Matthew 7:28; 19:1; 26:1, paraphrased). The phrase closes one block of teaching and moves the story forward.

In Matthew 10:5, "These twelve Jesus sent out," the word gathers up the list of apostles named in verses 2–4. The mission that follows belongs to those specific men.

Standing alone for people already in view ("these," "them," "those")

Without a noun, toútous works as a pronoun for a group already identified. In the garden, Jesus says, "if you seek Me, let these go their way" (John 18:8). The word points to the disciples beside Him and sets their fate apart from His own. John immediately connects this to Jesus' promise that He would lose none of those the Father gave Him (John 18:9, paraphrased).

In Acts 21:24, "Take them," the NKJV uses a plain "them," but the Greek still points to the four men under a vow just mentioned. In Luke 20:16, "destroy those vinedressers," it identifies the wicked tenants of Jesus' parable.

Picking up a description ("these… whom," "such")

Toútous often resumes a description. Paul writes "whom He predestined, these He also called" (Romans 8:30). Here the "whom" clause describes a group, and "these" picks that group up again as the object of the next verb.

The word can also look ahead to a description. Hebrews 2:15 speaks of Christ releasing those who lived in bondage through fear of death (paraphrased).

When the pointing word follows a portrait of a certain kind of person, the NKJV can render it "such." After describing people "having a form of godliness but denying its power," Paul commands, "from such people turn away!" (2 Timothy 3:5).

3. Practical Reading Insight

Romans 8:30 shows how much weight a pointing word can carry. Paul names four acts of God: predestining, calling, justifying, and glorifying. Three times he uses "these" to pick up the people from the previous step: "whom He predestined, these He also called; whom He called, these He also justified; and whom He justified, these He also glorified."

The sentence never introduces a new or smaller group along the way. The people God called are the people He justified, and they are the people He glorified. Paul even puts "glorified" in the past tense, as though the end were already settled.

The word alone does not build a whole doctrine. The surrounding passage does that, as Paul goes on to ask who can separate us from the love of Christ (8:35–39). What the repeated "these" contributes is clarity. It keeps the same people in view from beginning to end, so the chain reads as one unbroken work of God.

John 18:8 offers a quieter picture of the same care. As Jesus is arrested, He points to His disciples with "these" and asks that they go free. Then He hands Himself over.

4. Reading Guidance

English sometimes softens this word into "them," "those," or "such," which can make it sound like a passing pronoun. Its pointing force remains. Ask each time who or what "these" refers to, and check the whole context rather than the nearest noun. In 2 Timothy 3:5, readers differ on whether "such" points mainly to particular troublemakers in Timothy's day or to anyone who fits Paul's description.
