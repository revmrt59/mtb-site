THIS, TO THIS, BY THIS, THIS MAN, HIM - (toútōi, Strong's G5129)

Summary

Toútōi is a form of the Greek word for "this." It points to someone or something the speaker has in view, and its form usually marks that person or thing as the one receiving an action, the one something belongs to, or the means or setting of what happens. The NKJV most often renders it simply "this," but also "this Man," "him," or "one." John uses it often in the phrase "by this" or "in this," as in "By this we know love" (1 John 3:16). Those phrases tie a spiritual claim to something concrete that can be seen and tested. The same word lets the crowds at Nazareth ask about Jesus, "Where did this Man get this wisdom?" When readers ask what "this" is pointing to, they often find the center of the sentence.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Toútōi is a pointing word. It singles out a nearby or just-mentioned person, object, or idea. Its grammatical form most often expresses meanings English carries with "to," "for," "by," or "in." Sometimes it stands alone as a pronoun ("him," "this one"). Sometimes it is joined to a noun ("this mountain," "this age"). It often follows a preposition, especially in John's "by this" and "in this." The word does not tell the reader what it points to. The surrounding sentence does that. Sometimes it looks back to what was just said, and sometimes it looks ahead to an explanation that follows.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Pointing to the evidence or basis: "by this," "in this"

This use is most frequent in John's writings. Jesus says, "By this all will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another" (John 13:35). The "this" waits for the closing clause: mutual love is the visible mark. In 1 John 3:16 the word again points forward: "By this we know love, because He laid down His life for us." John does not define love in the abstract. He points to the cross. In 1 John 2:5 the word points back instead: "But whoever keeps His word, truly the love of God is perfected in him. By this we know that we are in Him." The first occurrence, translated "in him," refers to the person who keeps God's word. The second, "By this," refers back to keeping His word as the evidence of being in Him.

Pointing to a person: "this Man," "him"

The word can single out a person, sometimes with feeling. In Nazareth the astonished townspeople ask, "Where did this Man get this wisdom and these mighty works?" (Matthew 13:54). Their tone carries both amazement and doubt about the carpenter's son. In the parable of the minas, the master answers the second servant: "Likewise he said to him, 'You also be over five cities'" (Luke 19:19). In Acts the apostles often use this form when preaching about Jesus. For example, Acts 10:43 says in substance (paraphrase) that all the prophets bear witness to Him. Acts 13:39 says (paraphrase) that by Him everyone who believes is justified.

Directing speech or action toward someone or something present

Jesus tells His disciples, "you will say to this mountain, 'Move from here to there,' and it will move" (Matthew 17:20). The word aims the command at the mountain in front of them. The centurion uses it to pick out one soldier among many: "And I say to one, 'Go,' and he goes; and to another, 'Come,' and he comes" (Luke 7:8). Paired with "another," the word works like the English "to one… to another." The centurion is describing authority that reaches any soldier he chooses.

Joined to a noun: "this age," "this world"

The word often marks the present order of things, as in "this age" (Matthew 12:32; Ephesians 1:21) or "this time" (Mark 10:30). Romans 12:2 warns believers not to be conformed to this world (paraphrase). In these passages "this" places the reader in the present age, set against the age to come.

3. Practical Reading Insight

John's "by this" statements reward slow reading. Each time, he takes something believers long to be sure of, such as knowing God, being in Him, or knowing what love is. Then he fastens it to something concrete: keeping His word (1 John 2:5), loving one another (John 13:35), and above all Christ laying down His life (1 John 3:16). The little pointer refuses to let assurance float free of reality. Love is defined by what Jesus did at Calvary, and that same verse draws the conclusion: "we also ought to lay down our lives for the brethren."

The personal uses carry their own weight. The Nazareth crowd says "this Man" with suspicion. The apostles in Acts point to the same Jesus as the One through whom forgiveness and justification come. Following who "this" refers to helps the reader hear how differently people spoke about Christ.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume "by this" always points backward. In John 13:35 and 1 John 3:16 the explanation follows, and in 1 John 2:5 it comes before. Let the flow of the sentence decide. The same Greek form also serves for both "this man" and "this thing," so context alone tells the reader whether a person or an idea is meant. English "this" often renders other forms of the same Greek word as well.
