OF THESE, THESE, THAN THESE - (toútōn, Strong's G5130)

Summary

Toútōn is the Greek word for "these," in the form that means "of these" or "than these." It points to a specific group the reader has just met, such as commandments, disciples, events, or works, and connects that group to something else in the sentence. It can single out one member of a group ("one of the least of these commandments"). It can set a standard of comparison ("do you love Me more than these?"). It can also point back to things just described ("all these things which had happened"). The form is the same whether the group is men, women, or things, so the reader has to look back into the passage to see what "these" means. When Jesus speaks of "the least of these My brethren," or asks Peter about loving Him "more than these," the meaning depends on noticing who or what is being pointed to.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Toútōn is a pointing word. It does not name anything itself. It directs the reader to people or things already in view, usually something just mentioned, and treats them as a definite, recognizable group. Its form adds a relationship, which English usually expresses with "of" or "than."

Greek uses this single form for masculine, feminine, and neuter. The grammar alone does not tell us whether "these" means men, things, or events. When the pronoun stands without a noun, English translations often add a word, as in "these men" or "these things," to make the meaning clear. Those added words are the translator's reading of the context, and they are usually well grounded. Still, they come from the surrounding passage, not from the pronoun.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Picking one out of a defined group ("one of these," "which of these")

Jesus warns, "Whoever therefore breaks one of the least of these commandments" (Matthew 5:19). The word ties His warning to the commandments of the Law He has just upheld, and "the least" picks out the smallest of them. In Acts 1:21–22 Peter speaks of the men who accompanied them and says that "one of these must become a witness with us of His resurrection." This narrows the choice of Judas's replacement to a specific circle of eyewitnesses who had been with Jesus from the beginning. When Jesus asks which "of these three" proved a neighbor (Luke 10:36), He makes the lawyer choose among the priest, the Levite, and the Samaritan.

Setting the standard of comparison ("than these")

After a comparative word such as "more" or "greater," toútōn names what something is measured against. Jesus asks, "Simon, son of Jonah, do you love Me more than these?" (John 21:15). Elsewhere Jesus promises Nathanael greater things than these (John 1:50) and says no commandment is greater than these two (Mark 12:31). In 3 John 1:4 the Greek literally says "greater than these," and what follows explains what "these" means. The NKJV renders it smoothly: "I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth." No single English word stands for toútōn in that translation, so the comparison is carried by the whole phrase.

Pointing back to what has just been told

The two disciples on the Emmaus road "talked together of all these things which had happened" (Luke 24:14). Here "these" gathers up the crucifixion and the reports of the empty tomb that Luke has just narrated. The word can also describe a time or a matter directly, as in "these last days" (Hebrews 1:2). It can sum up a whole argument, as when Hebrews speaks of the forgiveness of "these" sins and lawless deeds (Hebrews 10:18).

3. Practical Reading Insight

Every "these" asks a question: these what? In Matthew 25:40 the King says, "inasmuch as you did it to one of the least of these My brethren, you did it to Me." The word does not point to people in general. It points to a group the King presents as His brethren. Christians have long discussed whether this means Jesus' disciples, believers generally, or all who suffer. Whichever view a reader holds, it has to begin with the King's own words, "these My brethren." The verse is still a sober call to compassion. Its weight comes from Jesus counting what is done to His brethren as done to Him.

John 21:15 rewards the same attention. Peter had once claimed he would stand firm even if all the others fell away. Now Jesus asks whether he loves Him "more than these," at a breakfast beside the boats and nets, with the other disciples present. Holding those surroundings in mind lets the question search Peter, and the reader, without forcing a single answer the text does not settle.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume "these" refers to the nearest noun, and do not treat words such as "men" or "things" as part of the Greek pronoun. In John 21:15 the grammar allows "more than these disciples love Me," "more than you love these men," or "more than these things." Context must decide, and readers should hold their view with humility. English "these" also translates other Greek forms with separate Strong's entries.
