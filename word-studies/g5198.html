HEALTHY, SOUND, WELL - (hygiaínō, Strong's G5198)

Summary

The Greek verb hygiaínō means to be healthy, well, or safe and sound. Luke uses it for ordinary bodily health: the sick servant found well, the prodigal son received home "safe and sound." Jesus uses it when He says, "Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick" (Luke 5:31). John uses it in his prayer that his friend Gaius would "be in health" (3 John 1:2).

In his final letters to Timothy and Titus, Paul takes this everyday word for health and applies it to the truth of the gospel. The NKJV usually translates his uses as "sound doctrine," "sound words," or "sound in the faith." Paul means healthy teaching, truth that keeps believers spiritually alive and strong. He sets it against false teaching he describes as sickness and spreading rot. Healthy doctrine is supposed to produce healthy believers, people sound "in faith, in love, in patience" (Titus 2:2). The word invites a searching question: is what we believe making us spiritually well?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hygiaínō belongs to the ordinary Greek vocabulary of health, and it gives English the word "hygiene." In the New Testament it appears in two clusters. Luke and John use it for physical well-being. Paul uses it only in the Pastoral Epistles (1 and 2 Timothy, Titus), letters written near the end of his ministry, when false teachers were troubling the churches in Ephesus and Crete.

Speaking of "healthy" words or teaching was a familiar figure of speech in the Greek world, so Paul did not invent the image. It may also be significant that Luke, "the beloved physician," traveled with Paul and is the other main user of the word. That connection is suggestive rather than provable. What is clear is that Paul chose a word for bodily health to describe the truth that keeps a church alive.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, hygiaínō describes a state of wholeness and vitality: being well, in good working order, free from disease or harm. Context determines whether the health is physical or spiritual.

1. To be physically healthy, well, or safe and unharmed. The centurion's servant was found well (Luke 7:10), and the prodigal returned "safe and sound" (Luke 15:27).

2. Figuratively, to be sound or wholesome, describing teaching, words, or a person's faith that is spiritually healthy and uncorrupted. Paul speaks of "sound doctrine" (Titus 1:9; 2:1) and of believers being "sound in the faith" (Titus 1:13).

When applied to doctrine, the word does include truthfulness, since diseased teaching is false teaching. Its own emphasis, however, falls on health and life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Gospels the word stays literal, even when Jesus builds a lesson on it. In the Pastorals, Paul applies it in two directions. Most often it describes teaching and words: "sound doctrine" (1 Timothy 1:10; 2 Timothy 4:3; Titus 1:9; 2:1), "sound words" (2 Timothy 1:13), and "wholesome words" (1 Timothy 6:3). Twice it describes people: believers who are "sound in the faith" (Titus 1:13) and older men who are "sound in faith, in love, in patience" (Titus 2:2). The movement from healthy teaching to healthy people is deliberate. The two belong together.

Paul also sets the word against vocabulary of disease. The man who rejects wholesome words is "obsessed with disputes" (1 Timothy 6:4), translating a Greek verb that literally means to be sick. False teaching spreads like gangrene (2 Timothy 2:17). People who will not endure sound doctrine have "itching ears" (2 Timothy 4:3). With these images, Paul places health on one side and disease on the other.

4. Theological Significance

The way Paul uses this word shows how God regards truth. Truth is not inert information to be filed away. It nourishes the people of God. Doctrine is "sound" when it keeps believers spiritually alive, and it agrees with "the glorious gospel of the blessed God" (1 Timothy 1:11). Error is sick, and it spreads. It produces quarrels, pride, envy, strife, reviling, and evil suspicions (1 Timothy 6:4).

The word also exposes the human condition. Jesus' saying about the healthy and the sick assumes that everyone needs a physician, though not everyone will admit it. God's purpose throughout Scripture is restoration. He diagnoses sin as sickness in order to heal it, and He gives His word as the means of keeping His people well.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Luke 5:30–32: the Physician and the self-diagnosed healthy. When the scribes and Pharisees complained, "Why do You eat and drink with tax collectors and sinners?" (5:30), Jesus answered with a saying from ordinary life: "Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick" (5:31). Then He explained it: "I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance" (5:32). The word itself means physically healthy. Jesus turns the image on His critics. The Pharisees counted themselves well and saw no need of Him. The tax collectors knew they were sick, and they were the ones at the table with the Physician. Grace reaches those who stop pretending they are healthy.

1 Timothy 6:3–4: health flows from the words of Christ. Paul warns against anyone who does not consent to wholesome words, which he identifies as the words of our Lord Jesus Christ and the teaching that accords with godliness. In his phrasing, healthy words are Christ's words. The person who turns from them "is proud, knowing nothing, but is obsessed with disputes and arguments over words" (6:4), and the verb behind "obsessed" literally means to be sick. Departing from Jesus' teaching produces a feverish appetite for controversy, which then infects the whole fellowship.

Titus 1:9–2:2: rebuke for the sake of health, doctrine for the sake of character. An elder must hold fast the faithful word "that he may be able, by sound doctrine, both to exhort and convict those who contradict" (1:9). The danger in Crete was real. Deceivers were "teaching things which they ought not, for the sake of dishonest gain" and subverting whole households (1:11). Paul's instruction is pointed: "rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith" (1:13). Even a sharp rebuke has healing as its goal. It works like a surgeon's cut, painful but aimed at recovery. Paul then tells Titus to "speak the things which are proper for sound doctrine" (2:1), and he immediately describes what health looks like in a person: older men "sound in faith, in love, in patience" (2:2). Healthy teaching produces trust toward God, love toward people, and endurance in hardship. A person who can defend correct doctrine but lacks love or patience is not yet well.

Luke 15:27: "safe and sound" or "alive again." When the older brother asks what the celebration means, the servant explains that the father has killed the fatted calf "because he has received him safe and sound." That answer is accurate as far as it goes. The son survived the famine and came home in one piece. The father describes the same event in different terms: "your brother was dead and is alive again, and was lost and is found" (15:32; compare 15:24). The servant sees physical survival. The father sees resurrection. The servant is not speaking in a spiritual metaphor. The contrast lies between his account and the father's. It is possible to be "safe and sound" by every outward measure and still be dead toward God, and the Father's eyes see past the first condition to the second.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

From early in Scripture, sin appears as sickness. Isaiah described rebellious Israel this way: the whole head sick, the whole heart faint (Isaiah 1:5–6). God promised healing, and Psalm 107:20 says He sent His word and healed them. In the Gospels, Jesus enters this story as the Physician. He literally heals bodies, and He names sinners as the sick He came to call (Luke 5:31–32).

After His resurrection and ascension, the Physician's healing work continues through His words. Paul calls Christ's words wholesome (1 Timothy 6:3), and he tells Timothy to hold fast the pattern of sound words in faith and love in Christ Jesus (2 Timothy 1:13). The church stays well by staying close to Jesus' teaching. The prodigal's homecoming points to the end of the story. The Father's purpose is to bring the dead back to life, which goes far beyond keeping people physically safe.

7. Spirit & Life

This word gives believers a practical test for what they hear and believe: is it making me well? Healthy teaching leaves people trusting God more, loving others more, and enduring trials more steadily (Titus 2:2). Teaching that leaves people restless for novelty, proud, or quick to argue shows the symptoms Paul describes in 1 Timothy 6:4, however clever it sounds. Paul warns of a time when people would not endure sound doctrine and would gather teachers to suit their itching ears (2 Timothy 4:3). An itch wants to be relieved. Health sometimes requires hearing what we would rather not hear.

The same word teaches us how to receive correction. When God's word or a faithful pastor rebukes us, the goal is that we may be "sound in the faith" (Titus 1:13). Correction that stings is often God's mercy at work.

Jesus' saying also keeps believers honest. We never outgrow our need of the Physician, and the moment we think ourselves well enough to manage without Him, we have joined the Pharisees at the edge of the room.

John's prayer adds needed balance: "I pray that you may prosper in all things and be in health, just as your soul prospers" (3 John 1:2). God cares about bodies. Praying for one another's physical health is fully biblical, and John held it alongside his joy over Gaius's thriving soul.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 7:10 — The centurion's messengers return to find the sick servant well. Jesus healed him at a distance by a word, on the strength of the centurion's faith.

  • 1 Timothy 1:10 — Paul lists sins that are contrary to sound doctrine. Healthy teaching has moral content, and it agrees with the gospel (1:11).

  • 2 Timothy 1:13 — Timothy is to hold fast the pattern of sound words he heard from Paul, in faith and love in Christ Jesus. The truth is to be kept whole and passed on unaltered.

  • 2 Timothy 4:3 — Paul foresees a time when people will not endure sound doctrine. The verb "endure" suggests that healthy truth can feel burdensome to those who prefer to be soothed.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's application of health language to doctrine appears only in his last letters, when the greatest threat to the churches came from teachers within them. A church can be harmed by attack from outside, and it can also be weakened by sickness from inside. Paul's answer to the inside threat was healthy teaching, faithfully held, and correction offered with recovery in view.

In the Pastorals, Paul builds a consistent picture with medical language. Truth is health (hygiaínō). Error is sickness (1 Timothy 6:4), gangrene (2 Timothy 2:17), and an itch that demands scratching (2 Timothy 4:3). This is Paul's own imagery. It applies to doctrine and conduct, and it does not mean false teachers will suffer physical disease.

Scripture elsewhere presents God's word as the source of health. Proverbs 4:22 calls wisdom's words life to those who find them and health to all their flesh, and Psalm 107:20 says God sent His word and healed. Paul's "sound doctrine" stands in this line. The apostolic teaching is God's medicine for His people.

Some have compared sound doctrine to a body's immune system, which keeps a living body well from within, and have compared sharp rebuke to surgery that restores health. Scripture does not state this comparison. It is a meditation that grows from Paul's imagery. Used with that understanding, it can help a church see why guarding the truth is an act of care for living people.
