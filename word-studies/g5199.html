WHOLE, WELL, SOUND - (hygiḗs, Strong's G5199)

Summary

The Greek word hygiḗs means healthy, sound, or whole. It describes something in the condition it ought to be in, free from disease, weakness, or damage. In the New Testament it almost always describes the result when Jesus or His apostles heal someone. A withered hand is "restored as whole as the other." A man paralyzed for thirty-eight years is "made well." A beggar lame from birth "stands here before you whole." The word focuses less on the process of healing and more on the finished result: a body working again as God made it to work.

Scripture uses this simple word to say a great deal. When Jesus made a man well on the Sabbath, He showed what God's day of rest was meant to serve. When Peter pointed to a healed man before the council, that man's sound body was public evidence that Jesus is alive. Paul used the same word for "sound speech," words as healthy as a restored body. Christ makes people whole, and He expects that wholeness to show in how they live and speak afterward.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hygiḗs is an ordinary Greek adjective for health. The English word "hygiene" comes from the same family, although the biblical word speaks of restored health rather than disease prevention. In thirteen of its fourteen New Testament uses, it appears in stories of healing in the Gospels and Acts. Half of all its uses are in John, and six of those come from one story: the healing at the pool of Bethesda in John 5. The one remaining use, Titus 2:8, applies the idea of health to a believer's speech.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word describes a sound, healthy condition. The NKJV renders it "whole," "well," "healed," and "sound," depending on context.

1. Physically healthy or restored. This describes a body or limb that is free from disease or disability and working properly, as when a withered hand is "restored as whole as the other" (Matthew 12:13).

2. Morally or doctrinally sound. This describes something free from corruption or error that cannot fairly be faulted, as in "sound speech that cannot be condemned" (Titus 2:8).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Gospels, hygiḗs usually appears in a phrase such as "made whole" or "made well" and names the state a person is left in after Jesus acts. Matthew 15:31 lists the crowd's healed sick: "the mute speaking, the maimed made whole, the lame walking, and the blind seeing." Each item in that list names something working again that had been broken.

Mark 5:34 shows how this word differs from the broader language of salvation. Jesus tells the woman who touched His garment, "your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be healed of your affliction." The first "made you well" translates a different verb, sōzō, which commonly means "save." Hygiḗs stands behind "be healed." Jesus speaks of her deliverance in the wider sense and of her bodily soundness in particular, and He gives her both. Physical wholeness is real and good in itself, and it also points beyond itself.

4. Theological Significance

Each time Jesus makes someone hygiḗs, He shows that God is not content to leave His creation broken. God looked at what He had made and saw that "indeed it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). Disease, paralysis, and withered limbs belong to a world that has fallen from that goodness. The healings of Jesus restore that goodness in a concrete way. He does not hand people a substitute ability; He gives back the function the body was designed to have.

These passages also show that God's restoration has a purpose. A body made whole becomes evidence of who Jesus is, a reason for people to glorify "the God of Israel" (Matthew 15:31), and the start of a life lived toward God. The word moves from bodies to speech in Titus because the same God who heals also intends to make His people sound in character.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The withered hand (Matthew 12:13; Mark 3:5; Luke 6:10). All three Synoptic Gospels record the same measure of the healing: the hand was "restored as whole as the other." The man's own healthy hand became the standard. Jesus put back what had been lost and matched the healed hand to the way it was meant to be. Mark adds that Jesus first "looked around at them with anger, being grieved by the hardness of their hearts." The healing happened in a synagogue on the Sabbath, in front of people watching to accuse Him. Their hearts were withered too, and that grieved Him. Each healing of this kind gives a small, real preview of the full renewal God has promised.

A whole man made well on the Sabbath (John 7:23). Months after Bethesda, Jesus answered the anger that healing had stirred: "If a man receives circumcision on the Sabbath, so that the law of Moses should not be broken, are you angry with Me because I made a man completely well on the Sabbath?" His critics already accepted that the law of Moses required circumcision on the eighth day, even when that day fell on a Sabbath. That command dealt with one small part of the body. Jesus argued from the lesser to the greater: if the law allowed that, how could restoring a man's entire body break the Sabbath? The Sabbath was given for life and restoration, and Jesus showed its purpose by making a man completely well on it. His opponents kept the day and missed what it was for.

A man standing whole before the council (Acts 4:10). Peter and John were on trial for healing a man lame from birth. Peter said, "by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom you crucified, whom God raised from the dead, by Him this man stands here before you whole." The healed man stood in the room as evidence, and no one could deny it. His sound body testified that the crucified Jesus was alive and acting. Peter then moved straight to the deeper claim: salvation is found in no one else (Acts 4:12). The man's physical wholeness pointed to the salvation offered only in Jesus' name.

Sound speech (Titus 2:8). Paul told Titus to show "sound speech that cannot be condemned, that one who is an opponent may be ashamed, having nothing evil to say of you." Healthy speech is free from error, malice, and corruption, just as a healthy body is free from disease. The goal is protective and outward-facing. When believers speak this way, critics of the gospel have no fair charge to bring.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The healings described by hygiḗs are signs of Jesus' kingdom breaking into a broken world. The restored hands and legs look back to the "very good" creation and forward to the day when God makes all things new. The Sabbath controversy in John 5 and 7 places Jesus at the center of God's rest. The day that celebrated God's finished work of creation became the day Jesus restored a man to wholeness. Rest and restoration meet in Him.

Acts 4:10 carries the story forward after the resurrection. Jesus no longer walks Galilee, yet a man stands whole "by Him." The risen Christ continues His restoring work through His name and His people. Titus 2:8 shows that work reaching into the church's daily life. The Healer of bodies forms a people whose speech is sound.

7. Spirit & Life

At Bethesda, Jesus saw a man who "had been in that condition a long time" and asked him, "Do you want to be made well?" (John 5:6). The question can seem strange, but thirty-eight years is long enough for a person to settle into an affliction and build a life around it. The man's answer shows this. He did not say yes. He explained why he could not be healed: he had no one to help him into the water (John 5:7). Jesus' question reached past his excuses to his will. Christ still asks people who have grown used to their brokenness whether they actually want what He offers. Wholeness is His gift, but He does not treat people as objects to be fixed.

The story does not end with the healing. Jesus later found the man in the temple, reminded him that he had been made well, and told him to sin no more so that nothing worse would come on him (John 5:14). Elsewhere Jesus refused to trace every illness to personal sin (John 9:3), so this warning should not be turned into a rule about sickness. It does show that restoration carries responsibility. A healed body was meant to lead to a surrendered life. Receiving Christ's mercy and then returning to rebellion leads somewhere worse than paralysis.

Put together, the uses of this word describe what Christ does with a person. He sees the broken one first, engages the will, restores, and then calls that person to live as someone made whole. That wholeness should reach even our words, so that people who watch us closely find nothing evil to say.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 15:31: Among the many healed, "the maimed made whole." The crowd's response was to glorify the God of Israel.

  • Mark 5:34: "Be healed of your affliction." Jesus' word to the woman confirmed that her long bleeding was truly over.

  • John 5:4: The NKJV describes whoever first entered the stirred pool being "made well." Many early manuscripts lack this verse, which helps explain the man's hopeless wait.

  • John 5:9: "Immediately the man was made well, took up his bed, and walked. And that day was the Sabbath." The healing was instant, and the timing set up the conflict.

  • John 5:11: "He who made me well said to me, 'Take up your bed and walk.'" The man's defense rested entirely on the One who had healed him.

  • John 5:15: The man went and told the Jewish leaders that it was Jesus who had made him well, and the opposition then turned toward Jesus Himself.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G5198 (hygiainō), lexical relative: The related verb "to be healthy or sound." The Pastoral Epistles use it often for "sound doctrine," which develops the metaphor seen in Titus 2:8.
