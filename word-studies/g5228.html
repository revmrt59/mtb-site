FOR, ON BEHALF OF, ABOVE, BEYOND - (hypér, Strong's G5228)

Summary

Hypér is the Greek preposition behind many of the New Testament's most personal statements about Christ and His people. It most often means "for" in the sense of "on behalf of" or "for the sake of." Christ died for us, believers pray for one another, and Paul suffers for the churches. It can also introduce the subject someone is speaking about ("concerning"). In a different construction it means "above," "beyond," or "more than," and measures rank or degree. When an NKJV reader meets "for" in a passage about Christ's death or about prayer, knowing that hypér often stands behind it brings the meaning into focus. Someone is acting in another person's interest, standing in for them, and giving himself for their good.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hypér connects an action to the person or thing it concerns. Usually that connection is personal: the action is done for someone's benefit, in their interest, or as their representative. The direction of the word depends on the grammatical form of the noun that follows it. With one form, it points to the person whose good is being served ("on behalf of," "for the sake of"). With another, it points to a standard that something rises past ("above," "beyond," "more than"). Translators usually make the second sense clear in English. The first sense is easier to miss, because the NKJV often renders it with the plain word "for." English uses "for" in many different ways, and the personal weight of hypér can disappear into that ordinary word.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

On behalf of, for the sake of (NKJV: "for," "on behalf of," "sake")

This is the word's dominant use. In Romans 5:8 Paul writes, "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." The preposition names the people whose good Christ's death served. The same idea appears in Luke 22:19, where Jesus says of the bread, "This is My body which is given for you." Hypér also marks representation. In 2 Corinthians 5:20, "we are ambassadors for Christ... we implore you on Christ's behalf." Paul speaks as one sent to act in Christ's interest and with His authority. The same use governs prayer for others (Luke 6:28; Acts 12:5; James 5:16) and suffering for others (Colossians 1:24).

Concerning, about (NKJV: "concerning," "about," "of")

Sometimes hypér simply introduces a topic. In 2 Thessalonians 2:1, "Now, brethren, concerning the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ" opens Paul's teaching on that subject. In 2 Corinthians 8:23, "If anyone inquires about Titus" marks Titus as the person being asked about. This use is less frequent. Even here, a note of interest or advocacy is often close at hand, as when Paul boasts of the Corinthians to others (2 Corinthians 9:2).

Above, beyond, more than (NKJV: "above," "beyond," "than")

In its other construction, hypér measures one thing against another. Jesus says, "A disciple is not above his teacher, nor a servant above his master" (Matthew 10:24). That sets a limit on rank. Paul uses the same sense for God's ability, which overflows every human measure: He "is able to do exceedingly abundantly above all that we ask or think" (Ephesians 3:20). In 1 Corinthians 10:13 it marks a limit God sets: He will not allow His people to be tempted beyond what they are able to bear (paraphrase).

3. Practical Reading Insight

The richest payoff comes where the New Testament speaks of Christ's death. Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 5:14, "if One died for all, then all died." Paul does more than say Christ's death was good for us. He reasons that because Christ died on behalf of all, His death counts as theirs. The preposition marks whose death this is for; Paul's argument shows how far that reaches. The next verse carries the thought forward: Christ died for all so that those who live would no longer live for themselves, but for Him who died for them and rose again (2 Corinthians 5:15, paraphrase). The one who gave Himself for us becomes the one we now live for.

The same word then describes the life of Christ's people. Believers pray for one another, and Paul endures hardship for the churches. John says plainly that because Christ laid down His life for us, we ought to lay down our lives for the brethren (1 John 3:16, paraphrase). Hypér traces a line from the cross to ordinary Christian love. The Lord who acted on our behalf calls us to act on behalf of others.

4. Reading Guidance

English "for" translates several Greek words, so do not assume hypér stands behind every one. Second Corinthians 5:14 shows this clearly: the opening "For the love of Christ compels us" uses a different word that gives a reason, while "One died for all" is hypér. Hypér itself means "on behalf of." In some passages the person acting also stands in another's place. Paul, for example, wanted Onesimus to serve him on Philemon's behalf (Philemon 13, paraphrase), doing what Philemon himself would have done. Whether Christ's death is substitutionary is settled by passages such as 2 Corinthians 5:14 and Galatians 3:13, which speak of Christ becoming a curse for us. The preposition alone does not settle it.
