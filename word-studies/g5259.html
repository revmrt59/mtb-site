BY, UNDER - (hypó, Strong's G5259)

Summary

Hypó tells the reader either who is acting or what someone stands under. Most often, NKJV renders it "by" and uses it to name the one who performs an action: Jesus "was baptized by John," and prophecy is "spoken by the Lord." Its other main use is rendered "under." That can mean a physical position, such as a lamp under a basket. It can also mean living under someone's authority or under the control of a power such as sin or the law. When readers notice hypó, they can see who is really acting in a sentence and what is ruling over a person's life. These two questions matter often in the Gospels and in Paul's letters.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hypó is a Greek preposition, a word that links a noun to the rest of the sentence and shows how the two relate. Greek nouns change their endings depending on their role, and hypó changes its job with them. With one set of endings (the genitive case), it usually follows a verb that describes something being done to someone and names the one doing it. English expresses this with "by." With another set of endings (the accusative case), it marks position beneath something, either literally or as subjection to authority. English expresses this with "under."

The two uses are related, but each one answers its own question. "By" tells you who acted. "Under" tells you what holds sway.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Naming the one who acts (by)

This is the most frequent use. Mark records that Jesus "was baptized by John in the Jordan" (Mark 1:9). Here hypó identifies John as the person who performed the baptism. Matthew uses the same construction when he points to fulfilled prophecy: "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet" (Matthew 1:22; compare Matthew 2:15). Hypó names the Lord as the speaker. A different preposition, translated "through," names the prophet as the one through whom He spoke.

Physical position beneath (under)

Sometimes "under" is simply about location. Jesus says no one lights a lamp to put it under a basket (Matthew 5:15, paraphrased). He also tells Nathanael that He saw him under the fig tree before Philip called him (John 1:48, paraphrased). In these passages hypó describes a place, and nothing more.

Under authority or a ruling power (under)

The centurion explains his faith in military terms: "For I also am a man under authority, having soldiers under me" (Matthew 8:9). He lives in a chain of command, so he understands that Jesus' word carries authority. Paul extends this picture to spiritual conditions: "the Scripture has confined all under sin" (Galatians 3:22). Romans 6:14 sets two such realms side by side. Believers are not under law but under grace (paraphrased). The same idea appears in the fixed expression "under His feet," which describes Christ's rule over all things (1 Corinthians 15:27; Ephesians 1:22, paraphrased).

Impersonal forces that act on people (by, with)

Occasionally hypó names a force rather than a person. In Jesus' parable, some hearers "are choked with cares, riches, and pleasures of life" (Luke 8:14). James pictures ships driven by fierce winds (James 3:4, paraphrased). These cares and winds are presented as real pressures that act on someone.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Matthew's fulfillment formula rewards careful reading. Matthew could have said only that the prophet spoke. Instead, he names the Lord as the one speaking and the prophet as the one through whom He spoke. Isaiah and Hosea were real men who used their own words, yet Matthew treats their words as God's own speech. Peter says something similar when he writes that holy men spoke as they were moved by the Holy Spirit (2 Peter 1:21, paraphrased). When we open the prophets, we are hearing God speak through human messengers.

"Under" carries equal weight in Paul's letters. In those letters it usually describes who rules a person. Galatians 3 and 4 trace a movement from being confined under sin and kept under guard to being set free. Christ Himself was "born under the law" in order to redeem "those who were under the law" (Galatians 4:4–5, paraphrased). The Son willingly placed Himself under the very condition He came to free us from.

4. Reading Guidance

English "by" can represent several Greek constructions, so not every "by" in the NKJV is hypó. Hypó usually names a personal agent, but passages like Luke 8:14 show it can also name an impersonal force, so read the context before deciding who or what is acting. "Under" in the Epistles usually describes rule or condition rather than location. Even so, a single preposition cannot carry a whole doctrine on its own. Paul's surrounding argument supplies the full meaning.
