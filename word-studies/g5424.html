UNDERSTANDING, THINKING - (phrḗn, Strong's G5424)

Summary

The Greek word phrḗn means the thinking part of a person: the understanding, the capacity to reason, weigh, and judge. In the New Testament it appears only twice, and both uses fall in a single sentence where Paul corrects a church that had come to prize spiritual excitement over spiritual sense: "Brethren, do not be children in understanding; however, in malice be babes, but in understanding be mature" (1 Corinthians 14:20).

That one verse carries a great deal. Paul tells believers to be like infants in one area and full-grown adults in another. In evil they should stay inexperienced, but in their thinking they should be sharp, discerning, and mature. The context shows why this matters. The Corinthians were enjoying uninterpreted tongues that left the rest of the church uninstructed, and Paul treats that as both childish and unloving. The Holy Spirit does not go around the mind of a believer. He engages it, so that the church is built up and the truth is made plain.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

In older Greek, phrḗn referred to the midriff, the area around the heart. Over time it became a common word for a person's inner thinking and judgment, much as English speakers still talk about "gut" decisions without meaning anything about digestion. That history explains how the word came to be used. Its meaning comes from how it was used, and by the New Testament period it simply meant understanding.

The noun is rare in the New Testament, but its word family is not. The related verb phroneō, "to think" or "to set one's mind," stands behind passages such as "Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 2:5) and "set their minds on the things of the flesh, but those who live according to the Spirit, the things of the Spirit" (Romans 8:5). Those verses do not use phrḗn itself, but they show how much weight Scripture places on the direction of a believer's thinking.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Phrḗn means the faculty of understanding: a person's ability to think, reason, and form sound judgments. It does not mean spiritual wisdom in itself. It names the human capacity that can be undeveloped or mature, foolish or well-trained. Its only New Testament setting calls for one definition:

1. Understanding, thinking, the capacity for judgment. "In understanding be mature" (1 Corinthians 14:20).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Both uses come in 1 Corinthians 14:20, and Paul builds the verse around them. He opens with "do not be children in understanding" and closes with "in understanding be mature." Between the two he places the exception: "in malice be babes." The word for understanding forms the frame, and childlikeness toward evil sits inside it.

The verse is a string of commands. Paul is not describing what the Corinthians already were. He is calling them to grow into something. Understanding in this verse can be developed, neglected, or left in childhood.

A different Greek word for "understanding," nous, appears in the verses just before this one (14:14–19). The two words overlap. Paul moves from describing how the mind takes part in prayer and praise to calling the church to grow up in its thinking.

4. Theological Significance

Paul's sentence shows that God values a thinking faith. The God who speaks, explains, and reveals Himself does not want worshipers who switch off their minds in His presence. The surrounding chapter assumes that worship should be intelligible enough for someone to say "Amen" with understanding (14:16). Spiritual life is not measured by how far it moves past comprehension.

The verse also shows that maturity in God's eyes is uneven in the right way. God wants His people to grow up in discernment while staying unpracticed in wickedness. The world often treats experience with evil as the price of wisdom. Paul separates the two, and so does Jesus, who sent His disciples out to be wise as serpents and harmless as doves (Matthew 10:16).

Finally, Paul ties right thinking to love. In 1 Corinthians, maturity of mind cannot be separated from concern for others. Clear thinking serves the church.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Corinthians 14:13–20: The mind in worship. The Corinthians were drawn to tongues that no one could interpret. Paul does not dismiss the gift; he says, "I thank my God I speak with tongues more than you all" (14:18). Yet he admits that when he prays in a tongue, "my spirit prays, but my understanding is unfruitful" (14:14). His conclusion is to use both: "I will pray with the spirit, and I will also pray with the understanding" (14:15). Then comes his most pointed line: "in the church I would rather speak five words with my understanding, that I may teach others also, than ten thousand words in a tongue" (14:19). Verse 20 follows directly. Being "children in understanding" meant chasing what felt most spiritual while ignoring whether anyone was helped. Paul calls this immature thinking, whatever spiritual label it carried.

1 Corinthians 13–14: Maturity as love. Chapter 14 opens with "Pursue love" (14:1), and that command depends on everything chapter 13 has just said. Tongues without love are "sounding brass or a clanging cymbal" (13:1), and love "does not seek its own" (13:5). Paul observes that the one speaking in a tongue speaks mysteries no one understands (14:2), while "he who prophesies speaks edification and exhortation and comfort to men" (14:3). A small child thinks first about its own experience. The Corinthians' spiritual immaturity looked the same way: they enjoyed their gifts while the person beside them went away uninstructed. A mature understanding asks what will build up the brother or sister in the next seat. In this chapter, growing up in one's thinking is one of the ways love acts.

1 Corinthians 14:20 with Romans 16:19: Innocence without ignorance. Paul's two-sided command in 14:20 has a close parallel in his letter to Rome: "I want you to be wise in what is good, and simple concerning evil" (Romans 16:19). In both places he asks for a particular kind of person. Toward malice, the believer should be like a baby, with no skill, practice, or appetite for it. Toward understanding, the believer should be an adult, able to weigh teaching, recognize error, and build others up. Paul has no interest in a gullible faith. Ephesians 4:14 describes what spiritual childhood looks like: being "tossed to and fro and carried about with every wind of doctrine, by the trickery of men." Innocence toward evil protects a believer from corruption, and a mature understanding protects them from deception. Paul wants both together.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus Himself fits the pattern Paul describes. He was wise enough to answer His opponents' traps and see through hidden motives, yet no evil ever took hold in Him. When He told His disciples to be wise as serpents and harmless as doves (Matthew 10:16), He described a life He was already living. Paul's call in 1 Corinthians 14:20 applies the same standard to the church.

Scripture also sets out where this maturity leads. Paul tells the Ephesians that God gave gifts to the church until believers come "to a perfect man," measured by the fullness of Christ (Ephesians 4:13, paraphrased). The following verse names the alternative: remaining children, blown around by every new teaching (4:14). Mature understanding is part of growing into Christ's likeness, and Philippians 2:5 points the whole mind in that direction: "Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus." Jesus welcomed children and said the kingdom must be received like a child. Paul's command does not contradict that. The trust of a child is commended, and the shallow, self-centered thinking of a child is not.

7. Spirit & Life

The Holy Spirit does not ask believers to set their minds aside in order to meet God. Some Christians quietly assume that the more spiritual a moment feels, the less it should involve thinking. Paul, who spoke in tongues more than anyone in Corinth, still chose five understandable words over ten thousand that no one could follow. When the Spirit fills a believer, He opens the understanding, and the believer in turn helps others understand.

There is also a needed word here about how wisdom is gained. Many people assume that to be discerning you must know evil up close, watch it, and become fluent in it. Paul says to stay a baby there. Discernment grows from knowing what is good and true, from Scripture, from Christ, and from the life of the church. Long familiarity with darkness does not produce it. A believer can be sharp, perceptive, and hard to fool while remaining unpracticed in malice.

Love is the test of a mature mind. The question Paul presses on Corinth applies in any setting where believers speak, teach, pray, or sing together: is anyone else being built up? Making the things of God clear to someone else is the work of a mind that has grown up in love.
