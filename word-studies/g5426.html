MIND, THINK, BE MINDFUL, CARE - (phronéō, Strong's G5426)

Summary

The Greek verb phronéō means to think, but in the New Testament it usually describes the settled direction of a person's mind: what someone is intent on, what they care about, and what they live toward. It joins thought, desire, and loyalty. When Paul writes that people "set their minds" on the flesh or on the Spirit, he is describing a way of life, not a passing idea.

This is one of Paul's favorite words, especially in Romans and Philippians. With it he warns against a mind set on earthly things, calls proud believers to "think soberly," and urges quarreling Christians to "be of the same mind." His most famous use is Philippians 2:5: "Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus." That mind is described by what Jesus did. He emptied Himself, became a servant, and obeyed the Father to the death of the cross. The same word appears in the Gospels when Jesus rebukes Peter for being "not mindful of the things of God, but the things of men." What a person sets the mind on determines which way their life goes. For the Christian, a renewed mind means learning to value what God values, and the clearest picture of that is the cross.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Phronéō is an ordinary Greek verb for thinking, holding an opinion, or having something in mind. Twenty-six of its twenty-nine New Testament uses come from Paul, and eleven of them are in the short letter to the Philippians. Outside Paul's letters it appears in Acts 28:22 and in the parallel accounts of Jesus' rebuke of Peter (Matthew 16:23; Mark 8:33). It shows up wherever people's loyalties, attitudes, or relationships are at stake.

The NKJV translates it in many ways, including "mind," "think," "mindful," "like-minded," "observe," "understood," and "care." That range is a clue. English needs several words to cover what this one verb holds together.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its simplest, phronéō means to think. In use, it carries three related senses:

1. To hold an opinion or judgment, to think something about a person or matter. The Jewish leaders in Rome wanted to hear what Paul thought about the Christian movement (Acts 28:22), and Paul says it is right for him to "think this" of the Philippians (Philippians 1:7).

2. To set one's mind on something, to be intent on or devoted to it. This is the sense in "set your mind on things above" (Colossians 3:2) and in Jesus' words about being "mindful of the things of God" (Matthew 16:23).

3. To have a shared attitude or concern, either with others or for them. Paul repeatedly calls believers to "be of the same mind" (Romans 12:16), and in Philippians 4:10 the verb describes the Philippians' care for him.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The object of the verb changes its weight. When it describes thinking about something, it is fairly neutral. When it is used with "the things of" (the flesh, the Spirit, God, men, the earth), it describes allegiance. When used with "the same" or "toward one another," it describes harmony in a community.

Commands matter here. Paul does not treat the mind's direction as something that simply happens. He tells believers to set their minds above (Colossians 3:2), to think soberly (Romans 12:3), and to let Christ's mind be in them (Philippians 2:5). He also prays that God will grant it (Romans 15:5). The mind is to be directed, yet the ability to direct it rightly is God's gift.

Romans 14:6 shows a quieter nuance. In a dispute over special days, Paul writes, "He who observes the day, observes it to the Lord." Here the verb means to regard a day as significant. What matters to Paul is that the person regards it to the Lord. A believer who keeps the day and one who does not can both honor God when their minds are turned toward Him with thanks.

4. Theological Significance

This word shows that God is concerned with the direction of the heart as well as the outward deed. Romans 8 sets two minds side by side and attaches life and peace to one and death to the other. The carnal mind, Paul says, "is enmity against God; for it is not subject to the law of God, nor indeed can be" (Romans 8:7). The human mind left to itself cannot simply decide to please God.

That is why unity among believers is also God's work. Paul prays, "may the God of patience and comfort grant you to be like-minded toward one another, according to Christ Jesus" (Romans 15:5). The standard of that shared mind is Christ, and its source is God. Paul does not ask for agreement on every question; Romans 14 openly allows differing convictions. The same mind he asks for in Philippians 2:2 is joined to "the same love" and being "of one accord." Believers share a common direction because they are turned toward the same Lord.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Philippians 2:5–8: the mind of Christ. "Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus." Paul defines this mind by telling a story. Christ, "being in the form of God, did not consider it robbery to be equal with God," but "made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant," and "humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross" (2:6–8). The mind of Christ is the attitude that moved the Son of God downward, from glory to servanthood to a cross. Paul's immediate concern is ordinary church life: believers looking out for one another's interests (2:3–4). Nobody can claim the mind of Christ while insisting on status and rights, because Christ's own mind led Him to let go of His.

Romans 8:5–7, with Philippians 3:19 and Colossians 3:2: two directions for a life. "Those who live according to the flesh set their minds on the things of the flesh, but those who live according to the Spirit, the things of the Spirit" (Romans 8:5). Paul describes two kinds of people, each recognized by where the mind is fixed, and the outcome follows: "to be carnally minded is death, but to be spiritually minded is life and peace" (8:6). In Philippians 3:19 he describes the enemies of the cross as people "whose god is their belly... who set their mind on earthly things." Colossians 3:2 turns that warning into a command for believers: "Set your mind on things above, not on things on the earth." The direction of the mind is never neutral. It leads either toward God or away from Him.

Matthew 16:23 and Mark 8:33: Peter's rebuke. Moments after confessing Jesus as the Christ, Peter tried to talk Him out of the suffering He had just predicted. Jesus answered, "Get behind Me, Satan!... you are not mindful of the things of God, but the things of men." Peter's thinking was natural, loyal, and wrong. He reasoned the way people usually reason, and to him a crucified Messiah was a defeat to prevent. Matthew follows immediately with the call to deny oneself and take up the cross (16:24). Peter's mind and Christ's mind in Philippians 2 point in opposite directions.

Romans 12:3 and 12:16: the humble mind. Paul warns each believer "not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think, but to think soberly, as God has dealt to each one a measure of faith" (12:3). The verb appears twice in that one sentence. A few verses later: "Do not set your mind on high things, but associate with the humble" (12:16). A sober mind measures itself by what God has given and is willing to spend time with lowly people.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The two Gospel uses and Philippians 2 together tell the whole story. In the Gospels, the disciple closest to Jesus cannot think His thoughts and resists the cross. In Philippians, after the cross and resurrection, Paul calls ordinary believers to share the very mind that led Jesus there. Something changed in between. The Spirit was given, and the mind that "cannot" submit to God (Romans 8:7) became able to be set on the things of the Spirit.

This fits God's promise in Jeremiah 31:33, repeated in Hebrews 8:10, that He would put His law in His people's minds and write it on their hearts. Paul does not quote that promise when he uses phronéō, but his teaching shows it being fulfilled. A new mind is part of salvation, and it takes on the likeness of Christ.

7. Spirit & Life

Wherever the mind is set, the life eventually follows. Paul's command in Colossians 3:2 is daily work. Every believer is surrounded by "earthly things" that compete for attention, and Paul expects us to turn our minds deliberately toward Christ, who is seated above. The Spirit makes this possible, but we still have to choose it.

Peter's example deserves honesty. His wrong thinking came from love for Jesus. Our natural instincts toward comfort, safety, and success can sound reasonable and even devout while still being "the things of men." When obedience leads toward loss, the question to ask is which way Christ's mind went.

The same word carries real affection. Paul could "think this" of the Philippians "because I have you in my heart" (Philippians 1:7), and he rejoiced that their care for him had flourished again, noting that they had cared all along but lacked opportunity (Philippians 4:10, paraphrased). The Christian mind thinks warmly about other believers and acts on it. That is why Paul pleads by name with Euodia and Syntyche "to be of the same mind in the Lord" (Philippians 4:2). Two quarreling women are not too small a matter for an apostle, because the mind of Christ is shown in such ordinary reconciliation.

Paul also uses the word for growing up. When he was a child, he says, he understood as a child, and when he became a man he put childish things away (1 Corinthians 13:11, paraphrased). His point there concerns our partial knowledge now compared with the fullness to come, yet the picture fits the whole Christian life: God is maturing what we value and how we think.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 28:22: The Jewish leaders in Rome ask to hear what Paul thinks about the Christian movement, which is spoken against everywhere (paraphrased). Here the word means simply to hold a view.

  • 1 Corinthians 4:6: Paul wants the Corinthians to learn not to think beyond what is written, so that no one becomes puffed up (paraphrased). Pride is again the danger.

  • Galatians 5:10: Paul expresses confidence in the Lord that the Galatians will have no other mind than the gospel he taught them (paraphrased).

  • Philippians 3:15–16: Paul urges the mature to be minded as he is and trusts God to reveal it to any who think otherwise. Then he adds, "let us be of the same mind."

  • 2 Corinthians 13:11: "Be of one mind, live in peace; and the God of love and peace will be with you."
