SOUL, LIFE - (psychḗ, Strong's G5590)

Summary

The Greek word psychē means the life of a living being and, by extension, the person who has that life. Depending on context, the NKJV renders it "life," "soul," and occasionally "mind," "heart," "being," or "persons." The same word can describe the life Herod wanted to take from the infant Jesus, the inner self that loves God with all its strength, the soul that God alone can destroy, and the martyrs John saw beneath the heavenly altar. English needs two words, "life" and "soul." Greek uses one, and that shapes some of Jesus' sharpest sayings.

The word matters most at the point where Jesus asks for it. "Whoever desires to save his life will lose it" (Mark 8:35). The life a person clutches and the soul a person can forfeit are the same psychē. Jesus asks for the whole self because He gave His whole self. As the Good Shepherd, He lays down His psychē for the sheep, and His soul was troubled as His hour came. In the letters, the soul becomes the thing God anchors, shepherds, and finally saves. In Revelation it lives on after death to be vindicated. To study psychē is to see what Jesus means when He asks for everything a person is.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Psychē was an ordinary Greek word for life and the living self. By the time of the New Testament, it had been shaped by the Greek Old Testament, where it regularly translates the Hebrew nephesh, the "living being" of Genesis 2:7. That background matters. The New Testament does not treat the soul as a pure spark trapped in a worthless body. It treats psychē as the living person: someone who eats, fears, loves, grieves, worships, and stands before God.

The word appears across the Gospels, Acts, Paul, the general letters, and Revelation. It shows up in discipleship sayings, shipwreck narratives, church greetings, warnings about judgment, and visions of heaven.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is the life that makes someone a living being, and that living being considered as a person. Context decides which part of that meaning comes forward.

1. Physical life, sustained by food and threatened by danger or death. Paul tells the crowd at Troas about Eutychus, "his life is in him" (Acts 20:10).

2. The inner self: the seat of desire, emotion, devotion, and will. Paul tells bondservants to do "the will of God from the heart" (Ephesians 6:6), where "heart" translates psychē.

3. The soul as the person before God, which continues beyond bodily death. John saw "the souls of those who had been slain for the word of God" (Revelation 6:9).

4. A person or living creature, used in counting. "That day about three thousand souls were added to them" (Acts 2:41).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Acts, the word is often practical. Lives are at risk in a storm (Acts 27:10, 22), and the ship's company is counted as 276 "persons" (Acts 27:37). In Paul's letters it often names the inner source of service. Colossians 3:23 says "do it heartily," which means doing it out of the psychē. Philippians 1:27 pictures believers striving together "with one mind," where "mind" also translates psychē.

The Gospels contain the word's most important usage pattern. Jesus moves between the sense of physical life and the sense of the soul before God within a single saying. Translators then have to choose different English words for one Greek term in neighboring verses. That shift is examined in Section 5.

4. Theological Significance

Through this word, Scripture shows that God values the whole person and judges the whole person. Jesus warns that people can kill the body but cannot kill the soul, and that God can destroy both soul and body in hell (Matthew 10:28). The soul does not belong finally to persecutors or to circumstances. It answers to God.

The word also shows that God wants the inner person as well as outward compliance. The Great Commandment calls for love "with all your soul" alongside heart and mind (Matthew 22:37; Mark 12:30). Paul applies the same demand to ordinary work: "not with eyeservice, as men-pleasers" (Ephesians 6:6). A slave doing chores can serve Christ from the psychē. God is not satisfied by performance that looks right while the self is held back.

The soul is also where people experience the deepest distress and the deepest rest. Lot tormented his righteous soul day after day as he watched the deeds of Sodom (2 Peter 2:8). Unbelievers in Iconium poisoned the minds of the Gentiles against the brethren (Acts 14:2). Into that vulnerable place Jesus speaks His promise that those who take His yoke and learn from Him will find rest for their souls (Matthew 11:29). That rest comes from Christ and is found under His yoke, not by escaping all burden.

Finally, psychē shows the limits of natural human life. Paul quotes Genesis: "The first man Adam became a living being" (literally, a living psychē). Then he adds, "The last Adam became a life-giving spirit" (1 Corinthians 15:45). Adam received life. Christ gives it. The natural soul we inherit from Adam is real and precious, but it cannot raise itself into resurrection glory.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Matthew 16:25–26 (with Mark 8:35–37; Luke 9:24). "Whoever desires to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for My sake will find it. For what profit is it to a man if he gains the whole world, and loses his own soul?" The NKJV says "life" in verse 25 and "soul" in verse 26, but Jesus uses one word throughout. The self a person protects through comfort, safety, and control is the same self that can be lost forever. Jesus does not let His hearers separate their earthly life from their eternal soul. Holding on to the one means losing the other. The question that follows, "what will a man give in exchange for his soul?" (Mark 8:37), puts the whole world on one side of the scale and a single psychē on the other, and the world loses. The rich fool in Luke 12 illustrates it. He speaks to himself: "Soul, you have many goods laid up for many years." God answers, "This night your soul will be required of you" (Luke 12:19–20). The soul he tried to comfort with barns was never his to keep.

John 10:11–18 (with Mark 10:45; John 12:27; Matthew 26:38). "The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). Jesus repeats that He lays down His psychē (vv. 15, 17) and insists that no one takes it from Him. He has authority to lay it down and authority to take it again. His death was a deliberate gift by the Shepherd, not a defeat imposed on Him. Mark states its purpose: "to give His life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Yet this authority did not make the offering painless. "Now My soul is troubled," Jesus says as His hour approaches, and He immediately adds, "But for this purpose I came to this hour" (John 12:27). In Gethsemane He tells His disciples that His soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death (Matthew 26:38). The same psychē that was given as a ransom was first crushed with grief. Christ gave both His body and His inner self, knowing fully what the cup held.

Revelation 6:9 and 20:4. John sees "under the altar the souls of those who had been slain for the word of God and for the testimony which they held." In the next verse they cry out to the Lord, asking how long until He judges. Later he sees the souls of those beheaded for their witness living and reigning with Christ (20:4). Their persecutors ended their bodily lives, but they did not end the martyrs. These souls are known to God, aware of injustice, and kept for vindication. The contrast in 18:13 is chilling: Babylon's merchants trade in "bodies and souls of men," treating human beings as cargo. The world may price a psychē in its markets. God gathers the souls of His witnesses at His altar.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of psychē begins with Adam receiving the breath of God and becoming a living soul. It continues through a race that tries to save that life on its own terms and loses it. The turning point comes when the Son takes a human psychē, lets it be troubled and sorrowful, and gives it as a ransom. In Christ, God did more than repair the natural soul. The Last Adam, raised from the dead, became a life-giving spirit (1 Corinthians 15:45).

Peter's sermon at Pentecost draws on Psalm 16: God would not leave the Messiah's soul in Hades (Acts 2:27, 31). Jesus gave His soul up willingly and received it back. The rest of the New Testament follows from that. Believers return to Christ as the Shepherd and Overseer of their souls (1 Peter 2:25). Their faith reaches its end in the salvation of their souls (1 Peter 1:9; Hebrews 10:39). The souls of the faithful dead wait with Him (Revelation 6:9–11), and John later sees them vindicated, living and reigning with Christ (20:4). The Shepherd who laid down His psychē now keeps the psychē of His sheep.

7. Spirit & Life

Every person spends a lifetime trying to preserve something that cannot be kept, and most people neglect the part of themselves that will last. Jesus' one-word paradox addresses both. The life you hold back from Him is the soul you are in danger of losing. The life you hand over is the soul He keeps.

That call would be crushing if it came from a distant master. It comes from the Shepherd whose own soul was troubled before He gave it. This is why the early believers could hold their lives so loosely. Paul said, "nor do I count my life dear to myself, so that I may finish my race with joy" (Acts 20:24). John reasons straight from the cross to the believer's life: because Christ laid down His psychē for us, we ought to lay down ours for the brethren (1 John 3:16). Jesus' call to hate one's own life in comparison with Him (Luke 14:26) comes to its full expression in Revelation 12:11. The saints overcome the accuser "by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony," and "they did not love their lives to the death." The threat of death loses its grip on those whose psychē is already given to Christ.

Surrender is not the end of the story. The soul given to Christ is held. Suffering believers are told to commit their souls to a faithful Creator while continuing to do good (1 Peter 4:19). Hope in Christ is "an anchor of the soul, both sure and steadfast," fastened where Jesus has gone, "behind the veil" (Hebrews 6:19). A soul tossed by grief, by the evil around it, or by its own weariness (Hebrews 12:3) is not asked to hold itself steady. It is anchored in Christ's presence before the Father.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 2:20 — Those who sought the young Child's life are dead. The same word later used for the life Jesus gives was first the life Herod tried to take.

  • Luke 1:46 — Mary's soul magnifies the Lord. Her worship rises from her inmost self.

  • Acts 4:32 — The believers were of one heart and one soul, a unity of inner life that showed itself in shared possessions.

  • 1 Thessalonians 2:8 — Paul and his companions were willing to share their own lives along with the gospel. Ministry gives the self as well as the message.

  • Hebrews 4:12 — God's word pierces even to the division of soul and spirit, reaching the depths of the person.

  • 3 John 2 — John prays that Gaius will prosper in health "just as your soul prospers." John treats the health of the soul as the standard by which to measure everything else.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

Lexical relatives:

  • G5591 (psychikos), "natural," "soulish." Used in 1 Corinthians 15:44, 46 for the natural body, in contrast to the spiritual body.

  • H5315 (nephesh), the Hebrew word that psychē regularly translates in the Greek Old Testament. Genesis 2:7 is quoted in 1 Corinthians 15:45.

Canonical pairings:

  • G4983 (sōma), "body." Paired with soul in Matthew 10:28.

  • G4151 (pneuma), "spirit." Contrasted with soul in 1 Corinthians 15:45 and paired with it in 1 Thessalonians 5:23 and Hebrews 4:12.

  • G2588 (kardia), "heart." Paired with soul in Matthew 22:37 and Acts 4:32.
