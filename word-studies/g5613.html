AS, LIKE, WHEN, ABOUT, HOW - (hōs, Strong's G5613)

Summary

Hōs is one of the most flexible connecting words in the Greek New Testament. Most often it sets one thing beside another so we can see how they correspond, and the NKJV renders it "as" or "like." It also marks time ("when," "while"), signals an estimate before a number ("about"), and voices wonder ("How unsearchable…"). Its most important work, however, can be easy to miss. When Scripture says believers are to live "as bondservants of God," hōs is often not offering a figure of speech. It is naming who we actually are and the role we are to live out. Careful readers learn to ask each time whether "as" introduces a picture of something else or a description of the real thing.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hōs links a word, phrase, or whole clause to something that helps the reader understand it. Usually that link is a comparison: one thing is said to resemble another in manner, degree, or appearance. The same word can also link an event to the moment it happened, or link a number to the acknowledgment that it is an estimate. Translators therefore have to choose among very different English words, so "as," "like," "when," and "about" in the NKJV may all stand for this one Greek word.

What joins these uses is correspondence. Hōs tells the reader that what follows is measured against, timed by, or identified with something else. Context decides which kind of correspondence is in view.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Comparison and simile (as, like, as though)

Hōs often introduces a picture that helps us see a quality. Jesus told the Twelve, "Behold, I send you out as sheep in the midst of wolves. Therefore be wise as serpents and harmless as doves" (Matthew 10:16). The disciples are not literally sheep, serpents, or doves. Each comparison isolates one trait: vulnerability, shrewdness, innocence. Revelation uses the word more than any other New Testament book. When John describes the risen Christ's eyes and voice in Revelation 1:14–15, he reaches for comparisons because what he saw outran ordinary description.

Identity or capacity (as, in the role of)

In this use, hōs names a real role rather than a figure of speech. Peter writes, "as free, yet not using liberty as a cloak for vice, but as bondservants of God" (1 Peter 2:16). Believers truly are free and truly are God's servants. In the same way, Peter speaks of suffering "as a Christian" (1 Peter 4:16, paraphrased), and Paul calls the church to imitate God as dear children (Ephesians 5:1, paraphrased). In none of these is "as" pretending. It describes what is actually so.

Time (when, while)

Hōs can introduce a clause that fixes when the main action occurred. "And it happened, when Elizabeth heard the greeting of Mary, that the babe leaped in her womb" (Luke 1:41). Luke ties John's leap to the very moment Mary's greeting reached Elizabeth. In Acts 1:10, "while they looked steadfastly toward heaven," hōs keeps the disciples' upward gaze in view as the two men in white suddenly appear beside them.

Approximation (about)

Before numbers and lengths of time, hōs marks an estimate. The herd of swine numbered "about two thousand" (Mark 5:13). Sapphira arrived "about three hours later" (Acts 5:7). The writers report honestly; the wording presents approximate amounts rather than exact totals.

Exclamation (how)

Occasionally hōs expresses wonder at the degree of something. After three chapters on God's dealings with Israel and the nations, Paul breaks into praise: "How unsearchable are His judgments and His ways past finding out!" (Romans 11:33). Paul is not asking a question. He is marveling.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The most valuable habit hōs teaches is to ask what kind of "as" you are reading. In 1 Peter 2:16, Peter uses it three times in one breath, and the three uses are not all the same. Believers live as free people, which is true. They must not use that freedom as a cloak, a covering that disguises sin as liberty. And they live as bondservants of God, which is equally true. Christian freedom and Christian service are not in competition. Both describe the same person. If a reader treats every "as" like a simile, these commands lose their force. Peter is not telling us to act like free servants of God. He is telling us to live out what God has already made us.

Revelation teaches the opposite caution. John's repeated "like" marks visionary description: the Son of Man's hair was white like wool, as white as snow, His eyes like a flame of fire, and His voice as the sound of many waters (Revelation 1:14–15). Those comparisons invite us to grasp the glory and terror of what he saw, not to treat each image as a literal inventory. The same small word calls for literal identity in Peter and vivid comparison in John, and only the surrounding passage tells us which.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume that every "as" means "pretend" or "resemble," and do not assume it never does. Some English renderings, such as "when" or "about," hide hōs entirely, and some English "as" words translate other Greek terms. In Acts 1:10, for example, "while they looked steadfastly" translates hōs, whereas "as He went up" comes from a different Greek construction. Let the sentence and its setting decide. In Romans 1:9, the NKJV's "that without ceasing" could also be read "how unceasingly," so no single rendering settles Paul's exact nuance.
