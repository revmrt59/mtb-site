FROM, AWAY FROM, SINCE, BY - (apó, Strong's G0575)

Summary

Apó is the Greek preposition most often translated "from" in the New Testament. Its basic idea is movement or distance away from a starting point. That starting point may be a place Jesus leaves, a person who sends a gift or message, a moment when a period of time begins, or the cause that produces a reaction. Most of the time the NKJV renders it "from," but it also appears as "of," "since," "by," "for," and "ago," because English needs different words for these different relationships. When readers ask where something comes from, the answer is often a person rather than a place. James uses this word to say that every perfect gift comes down from the Father of lights, and in the same chapter he insists that temptation does not come from God. Noticing what apó points back to helps readers see where blessing, authority, time, and even human emotion find their source.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Apó connects an action or thing to the point it comes from or moves away from. In some passages that point is physical: a riverbank, a mountain, a city, or a group of people. In others it is a person who gives or sends, such as God the Father in the greetings of Paul's letters. It can also mark the moment a stretch of time begins, as in "since the beginning," or the cause behind a response, as in being unable to believe "for joy." The surrounding verb and noun decide which relationship is in view. The English word "from" covers much of this ground, but not all of it. That is why the NKJV sometimes chooses "since," "by," or "for" to convey what apó is doing.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Separation and movement away (NKJV: "from," "away from," "off")

This is the most concrete and frequent use. At Jesus' baptism, "Jesus came up immediately from the water" (Matthew 3:16). The word marks the place He leaves as the heavens open. At the ascension, Luke writes that "He was parted from them and carried up into heaven" (Luke 24:51). Here the separation is personal: Jesus is taken from the disciples' physical company.

Source and origin (NKJV: "from," "of")

Apó often identifies where a person or thing comes from. The crowds at the triumphal entry say, "This is Jesus, the prophet from Nazareth of Galilee" (Matthew 21:11), which names His hometown. The same use reaches beyond geography to the giver of a gift: "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and comes down from the Father of lights" (James 1:17). Only the second "from" in that verse translates apó; "from above" renders a different Greek word. Paul's familiar letter greeting also belongs here. In a paraphrase of Romans 1:7 and similar openings, he wishes grace and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. That greeting names the Source of every blessing he goes on to describe.

Starting point in time (NKJV: "since," "from," "ago")

With time words, apó marks where a period begins. Jesus warns of "great tribulation, such as has not been since the beginning of the world until this time" (Matthew 24:21). John opens his first letter with "That which was from the beginning" (1 John 1:1). Apó also combines with time words to form set expressions, such as the phrase rendered "hereafter" in Matthew 26:64, which carries the sense "from now on."

Cause and agency (NKJV: "for," "because of," "by")

Less often, apó names what produces a condition. After the resurrection, Luke writes that "they still did not believe for joy" (Luke 24:41). Their joy is the cause of their hesitation. James uses the word where we might expect a simple agent: "Let no one say when he is tempted, 'I am tempted by God'" (James 1:13). Even here apó keeps its sense of source, so the claim being denied is that temptation originates with God.

3. Practical Reading Insight

James 1 places two uses of apó only a few verses apart, and together they make a pointed argument. In verse 13, no one may say temptation comes from God. In verse 17, every good and perfect gift comes down from the Father of lights. James is tracing things back to their source. Evil desire has its origin elsewhere, as verses 14–15 explain, while everything good traces back to a Father "with whom there is no variation or shadow of turning." A believer under trial can know that God is not setting a trap but giving good gifts.

Luke 24:41 shows a more human side of the word. The disciples were not doubting out of cold unbelief. Their joy was so overwhelming that the risen Jesus seemed too good to be real. He responds gently by asking for food and eating in front of them. Knowing that apó can mark cause lets readers see the tender realism in the scene.

The separation sense also gives weight to great promises. Paul declares that nothing in all creation can separate believers from the love of God in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:39, paraphrased). Here apó describes a distance that will never open up.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not limit "from" to travel or physical distance. Apó can mark origin, time, or cause, and the context decides which one is meant. Greek also uses ek, often rendered "out of" or "from," and in New Testament Greek the two words frequently overlap. Avoid building an interpretation on a sharp "away from the outside" versus "out from inside" contrast unless the passage itself supports it.
