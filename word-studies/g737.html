NOW, JUST NOW, HEREAFTER - (árti, Strong's G0737)

Summary

Árti is a Greek word for "now." It points to the present moment, often sharply, so that "right now" stands out against what came before or what is still to come. The NKJV usually renders it "now," but also "just," "to the present," and, when it combines with a small preposition meaning "from," "hereafter." The word carries no theology by itself. Its weight comes from who is speaking and what moment they are pointing to.

In the New Testament, Jesus and the apostles use árti to describe what kind of moment believers live in. It is a "now" of real transformation: the healed man says, "though I was blind, now I see." It is also a "now" of partial sight and present trials: "For now we see in a mirror, dimly." Jesus uses "from now on" to mark the hinge of history, even while He stands condemned before the high priest. In its last use in Scripture, heaven cries out, "Now salvation, and strength, and the kingdom of our God... have come," even as the defeated devil turns his wrath on the earth for a short time. Followed through the Bible, this small word helps believers understand the present: God is still working in it, God still holds evil back in it, and it is heading toward His victory.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Árti is a common Greek adverb of time. It appears mostly in spoken words. In the Gospels it is nearly always on the lips of Jesus or the people around Him, and in Paul's letters it carries the urgency of a pastor writing to a church he knows. John uses it more than any other writer, which fits his Gospel's steady attention to Jesus' "hour" and to what the disciples can and cannot grasp yet. A more frequent Greek word for "now" (nyn) overlaps with it. Árti tends to fix attention on the present instant itself.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The present moment, now. The ordinary sense is "at this time," usually set against an earlier or later time: "What I am doing you do not understand now, but you will know after this" (John 13:7).

2. Just now, a moment ago. Occasionally the word reaches back to something that has only just happened. The ruler tells Jesus his daughter has "just" died (Matt 9:18).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Two phrases built on árti matter for interpretation. With a preposition meaning "until," it becomes "until now" and stresses a line running unbroken from the past into the present. The steward says, "You have kept the good wine until now!" (John 2:10). Paul says the apostles have been "the offscouring of all things until now" (1 Cor 4:13).

With a preposition meaning "from," it becomes "from now on," marking a turning point that begins at that moment and continues forward. The NKJV renders this phrase "hereafter" (Matt 26:64; John 1:51), "from now on" (John 14:7), and "no more" (Matt 23:39). The first phrase looks back to the present. The second looks forward from it. That difference often carries the force of the sentence.

The word also works in contrasts. "Now" stands against "then" in 1 Corinthians 13:12, against "after this" in John 13:7, and against "I was blind" in John 9:25.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture's "now" is never empty of God. Accused of breaking the Sabbath, Jesus answered, "My Father has been working until now, and I have been working" (John 5:17). God rested from creating on the seventh day, but He has never stopped sustaining, showing mercy, and giving life. That work runs right up to the present. Because it does, the Son's healing on the Sabbath is His Father's own work. The leaders heard the claim clearly and sought to kill Him for making Himself equal with God (John 5:18).

The present is also bounded by God. Paul admits that "the mystery of lawlessness is already at work," yet adds, "only He who now restrains will do so until He is taken out of the way" (2 Thess 2:7). Paul does not name the restrainer, and interpreters still debate who it is. The comfort does not depend on settling that question. Evil is active, but it is not unrestrained. The present age runs inside limits God has set, until His appointed time.

God also reveals Himself to His people in stages. Jesus told the disciples, "I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now" (John 16:12). Holding things back was an act of care, fitted to what the disciples could carry, and He promised the Spirit would guide them further.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

John 9:19, 25 — the "now" that cannot be argued away. The leaders interrogate the healed man's parents: "How then does he now see?" (9:19). They cannot explain the present fact, so they attack the Healer. The man refuses their theological debate and stands on the one thing he knows: "Whether He is a sinner or not I do not know. One thing I know: that though I was blind, now I see" (9:25). His entire testimony rests on the gap between "was" and "now." A changed present is evidence. First John 2:9 turns the same logic into a warning: anyone who claims to be in the light while hating his brother "is in darkness until now." The present tells the truth about a person, whether it confirms a claim or exposes it.

1 Corinthians 13:12, with John 13:7 and 1 Peter 1:6–8 — the "now" of partial sight. Paul writes, "For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I shall know just as I also am known." Ancient mirrors gave a real image, but not a clear one. The same pattern appears at the foot-washing, where Jesus tells Peter, "What I am doing you do not understand now, but you will know after this" (John 13:7). Peter later writes to suffering believers that they have been grieved "now for a little while, if need be" (1 Pet 1:6). Two verses later he says they love and rejoice in Christ even though they do not see Him now (1 Pet 1:8). Put together, these passages describe the present as a season God has designed. It is real, limited, and temporary, and it is the place where faith grows. We want full clarity today. God gives enough light to trust Him, along with the promise of "then."

Revelation 12:10 — the triumphant "now." The last árti in Scripture rings out after the dragon is thrown down: "Now salvation, and strength, and the kingdom of our God, and the power of His Christ have come, for the accuser of our brethren... has been cast down." Heaven is announcing a real and decisive victory. The accuser who worked "day and night" has lost his place, and the next verse credits the victory to "the blood of the Lamb" (12:11). Yet the same voice goes on to say, "Woe to the inhabitants of the earth and the sea! For the devil has come down to you, having great wrath" (12:12), and the enraged dragon goes to make war with the woman's offspring (12:13–17). The accuser is defeated, but suffering on earth has not yet been removed. Heaven's "now" declares that the decisive battle has been won through the Lamb, while God's people still endure and overcome until the end.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus uses "from now on" to mark the turning point of history, and He locates it in unexpected places. To Nathanael He promises, "hereafter you shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man" (John 1:51). This echoes Jacob's ladder: Jesus Himself is now the meeting place of heaven and earth. On the night before the cross He tells Philip, "from now on you know Him and have seen Him" (John 14:7), because to see the Son is to see the Father.

The sharpest instance comes at His trial. Bound, accused, and about to be condemned, Jesus tells the high priest, "hereafter you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of the Power, and coming on the clouds of heaven" (Matt 26:64). By every earthly measure this is His lowest moment, and He names it as the beginning of His enthronement. The cross does not interrupt God's plan. It opens the new age.

Jesus also accepted the limits of the present to reach that turning point. At the Jordan He told John to permit His baptism "now," because it was fitting to fulfill all righteousness (Matt 3:15). In Gethsemane He said He could "now" ask His Father for more than twelve legions of angels (Matt 26:53), and He did not ask. He set aside instant rescue so that the Scriptures would be fulfilled. The Son who could have ended His humiliation in a moment chose to walk through it on the Father's schedule.

7. Spirit & Life

Believers live between two "nows." One is the "now I see" of John 9, the real change grace has made. The other is the "now we see... dimly" of 1 Corinthians 13, the season of unanswered questions and unrelieved trials. Neither one cancels the other. Faith does not pretend the present is clearer than it is, and it does not forget what Christ has already done.

Peter's protest, "Lord, why can I not follow You now?" (John 13:37), is the cry of every eager disciple who wants God's timing to match his own readiness. Jesus had to tell him he would deny Him that very night. Our sense of being ready is not the measure of God's "now." Jesus Himself waited on the Father in the Jordan and in the garden. When we accept that the present carries limits we did not choose, we are following His steps, not merely putting up with them.

Paul's "until now" in 1 Corinthians 4:11–13 keeps this honest. The apostles were hungry, beaten, and homeless "to the present hour." The present age does not promise comfort to faithful servants. Yet the same phrase that describes Paul's hardships also describes God's untiring work (John 5:17). The present is hard, but the Father is at work in it. At Cana the steward said, "You have kept the good wine until now!" (John 2:10), and the line hints at how Jesus works. What He gives later is better than what came first.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 11:12 — Jesus speaks of the kingdom from the days of John the Baptist "until now," treating His own ministry as a moment of fierce spiritual conflict.

  • Matthew 26:29 — At the Last Supper Jesus says He will not drink the fruit of the vine "from now on" until He drinks it new in His Father's kingdom. The cup looks forward to a promised feast.

  • John 16:24 — Until now the disciples had asked nothing in Jesus' name. He invites them to ask, so that their joy may be full.

  • 1 Corinthians 15:6 — Most of the five hundred who saw the risen Christ remained alive "to the present," living witnesses Paul's readers could still question.

  • Galatians 1:10 — Paul asks whether he is now seeking to please men or God, and his present conduct answers the charge.

  • 1 Thessalonians 3:6 — Timothy has just returned with good news of the Thessalonians' faith and love, and the timing brings Paul fresh comfort.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G3568 (nyn, "now") — A near-synonym, not a cognate. It is the more common Greek word for the present time and often covers the same ground as árti.
