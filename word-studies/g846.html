HE, SHE, IT, THEY, SELF, SAME - (autós, Strong's G0846)

Summary

Autós is the Greek pronoun New Testament writers use most often to keep track of people and things as a passage unfolds. In most places it is the ordinary "him," "his," "her," "it," "them," or "their" of a sentence. It tells us whom Jesus spoke to, whose hand held the winnowing fan, and whom the crowds followed. In certain constructions it does more. It can add emphasis, as in "David himself," marking out one person and no other. With the definite article it means "the same," pointing to one identical thing shared by many. Careful readers benefit in two ways. Following the pronoun shows who is acting in a passage, and noticing its emphatic and identifying uses brings out arguments that depend on exactly who said something or exactly what was shared.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Most of the time autós carries a story or argument forward without repeating names. Greek verbs already show their subject ("he said" is built into the verb), so autós usually appears as the object ("said to him"), as the possessor ("his house"), or after a preposition ("with them"). In these uses it is plain and unemphatic. It simply points back to someone already in view.

Two further uses depend on how the word is placed in the sentence. When autós stands apart from the article, or stands as an added subject, it underlines identity: "he himself," "the man himself." When it follows the article directly, it means "the same." The same Greek word therefore can mean "him," "himself," or "the same," and its position in the sentence tells the reader which meaning is intended.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Ordinary personal pronoun: him, her, it, them, his, their

Matthew 9:9 shows the pronoun at its most typical: "And He said to him, 'Follow Me.'" Autós points to Matthew, who has just been named, so the text does not need to repeat his name. In the same way, Matthew 3:12 speaks of the coming One who "will thoroughly clean out His threshing floor, and gather His wheat into the barn." These possessives tie the floor, the wheat, and the judgment to Him. Nothing about them is emphatic. They do the everyday work of reference.

Emphatic "self": himself, themselves, He Himself

In Luke 20:42 Jesus says, "Now David himself said in the Book of Psalms." The pronoun fixes attention on David as the speaker, and Jesus' whole question depends on that point. In 1 Corinthians 2:15 Paul writes that the spiritual person "judges all things, yet he himself is rightly judged by no one." Here the emphatic pronoun sets the person being judged against the things he judges.

Identifying "the same"

When autós follows the article, it marks identity. In 1 Corinthians 10:3 Paul says the Israelites "all ate the same spiritual food." In 2 Corinthians 3:18 believers "are being transformed into the same image from glory to glory." In the first passage many people share one provision. In the second, many believers are being conformed to one likeness, the glory of the Lord they behold.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The emphatic use carries real weight in Luke 20:41–44. Jesus asks how the Christ can be David's son when David himself calls Him "my Lord." The argument works only if David is the one speaking in Psalm 110. Jesus presses that point, and with it He exposes how thin His hearers' picture of the Messiah was. The promised Son of David is also David's Lord.

The identifying use matters just as much in 1 Corinthians 10:1–5. Paul stresses that "all" the Israelites shared "the same" food and drink, and he adds that "that Rock was Christ." Yet with most of them God was not well pleased. The Corinthians were relying on their spiritual privileges. Paul shows them that the wilderness generation shared identical privileges and still fell under judgment. Shared blessing does not take the place of faithful obedience. The word "same" makes that warning hard to escape.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not read emphasis into every "He" or "His." Capital letters in the NKJV show the translators' recognition of deity. They do not mark a stressed Greek word, and most uses of autós carry no special weight. Even nominative autós sometimes works as a simple "and he" rather than "he himself," as the discussion of Ephesians 4:11 ("And He Himself gave some to be apostles") shows. Trace each pronoun back through the whole context. The closest noun is not always the person meant.
