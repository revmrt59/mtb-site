BASIN, GOBLET, CUP - (ʼaggân, Strong's H0101)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼaggân names a bowl-shaped vessel for holding liquid. The NKJV renders it "basins," "goblet," and "cups." The word occurs 3 times in 3 verses of the Hebrew Bible, each in a very different setting. At Mount Sinai, Moses pours half the sacrificial blood into basins and then sprinkles that blood on the people as "the blood of the covenant." In the Song of Solomon, the bride's beauty is compared to a rounded goblet that never runs dry. In Isaiah, small cups hang from a peg fastened in a secure place, an image the prophet himself applies to the dependent people of a household.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

The word means only "bowl" or "cup." Its three uses, though, show something about what a vessel is for. A vessel matters because of what it holds and what holds it up. In the Sinai basins, God's people see the blood that binds them to Him. Jesus later lifts a cup and speaks of "My blood of the new covenant." Isaiah's hanging cups picture the weakest members of a household, whose safety depends on the strength of the peg, not on their own size. Scripture finally points to Christ as the One who holds "the key of David" and never gives way.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼaggân is an ordinary household and ritual noun in Hebrew. It shows up in three parts of the Old Testament: the Law (Exodus), the Writings (Song of Solomon), and the Prophets (Isaiah). Because the word is so rare, we cannot fix its exact size, shape, or material. In Exodus the vessel is large enough to hold half the blood of several sacrificed animals. In the Song it is a drinking goblet. In Isaiah it is one of the smallest vessels hanging in a house. Readers should not picture the same object in all three places. What the three have in common is a rounded container made to hold liquid.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʼaggân means a bowl or basin for liquids. Its context shows which kind of vessel is meant:

1. A basin for ritual use: a vessel that receives and holds sacrificial blood (Exodus 24:6).

2. A drinking vessel: a goblet or cup for wine or a mixed drink, used in poetry and in a household setting (Song of Solomon 7:2; Isaiah 22:24).

The word carries no built-in sacred meaning. Its significance comes from the scenes in which Scripture places it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Each use depends on what the vessel holds or where it hangs. At Sinai the basins are literal objects in the ceremony that seals Israel's covenant with God, and their contents are blood. In the Song the goblet is a comparison, and its contents are "blended beverage," a mixed wine served at celebrations. In Isaiah the cups are again a comparison. They hold nothing in the verse. Their whole significance lies in hanging on Eliakim, the "peg." So the word moves from a literal ceremony to two pictures, one of joyful fullness and one of dependence.

4. Theological Significance

Read together, the three passages share one idea: a vessel has no life of its own. It is filled, or it is held up. The Sinai basins hold blood that God's own instructions made the means of atonement: "the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement for your souls" (Leviticus 17:11). The goblet in the Song is valued because "it lacks no blended beverage." The cups in Isaiah are safe only as long as the peg holds.

This idea says something about God's ways with people. He does not ask His people to supply their own life or their own stability. He fills them, and He holds them. The Bible's wider imagery of the cup makes the same point from both sides. David's "cup runs over" with God's provision (Psalm 23:5). Jerusalem, by contrast, drinks "the cup of His fury" down to the dregs (Isaiah 51:17). A cup is defined by what God pours into it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 24:6–8 — The basins of covenant blood. Israel stands at the foot of Sinai, and the agreement God has spoken is about to be sealed. "Moses took half the blood and put it in basins, and half the blood he sprinkled on the altar" (24:6). The division matters. The blood on the altar goes toward God. The blood in the basins is held back for the people. Moses then reads the Book of the Covenant, the people pledge, "All that the LORD has said we will do, and be obedient" (24:7), and Moses takes the blood from the basins and sprinkles it on them, saying, "This is the blood of the covenant which the LORD has made with you according to all these words" (24:8).

The basins held the portion of the sacrificial blood reserved for the people, while the other portion was sprinkled on God's altar. The same sacrificial blood marked both altar and people, and the basins kept the people's portion until Moses sprinkled it on them. In that way the basins linked the altar and the congregation in one sacrifice. Hebrews 9:18–20 looks back on this exact scene and concludes that "not even the first covenant was dedicated without blood."

Isaiah 22:20–25 — Cups on a secure peg. Isaiah announces that the proud steward Shebna will be removed and that God will set Eliakim in his place. God says, "The key of the house of David I will lay on his shoulder; so he shall open, and no one shall shut" (22:22), and "I will fasten him as a peg in a secure place" (22:23). Then comes the verse with our word: "They will hang on him all the glory of his father's house, the offspring and the posterity, all vessels of small quantity, from the cups to all the pitchers" (22:24).

Isaiah explains his own image. The vessels are "the offspring and the posterity," the whole extended family and everyone who depends on Eliakim's position. The cups are the smallest of them. They contribute nothing to the peg's strength. They simply hang.

The very next verse is sobering, and it should not be skipped. Isaiah goes on to say that in that day the peg fastened in the secure place will be removed, cut down, and fall, and the load hanging on it will be cut off (22:25). Eliakim was God's servant (22:20), but he was still a man, and the peg falls because "the LORD has spoken." Some readers suggest that the weight of the household hanging on him played a part, though the verse does not say so. Isaiah's picture of security ends by exposing the limits of every human peg.

Song of Solomon 7:2 — The goblet that lacks nothing. The bride is praised: "Your navel is a rounded goblet; it lacks no blended beverage." The image is one of finely shaped form and unfailing fullness. The phrase "lacks no blended beverage" turns the vessel into a picture of delight that does not run out. Here ʼaggân belongs to the world of the wedding feast, a vessel for joy rather than for ritual.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Sinai basins lead directly to the upper room. Moses said, "This is the blood of the covenant" (Exodus 24:8). At the Last Supper Jesus takes the cup and echoes those words: "this is My blood of the new covenant, which is shed for many for the remission of sins" (Matthew 26:28; compare Luke 22:20, "This cup is the new covenant in My blood"). The cup Jesus used is a different vessel and a different word, but the pattern is plainly the same. Blood is offered, gathered, and brought to the people so that God and His people are bound together. At Sinai the blood came from animals, and Moses carried it in basins. In the upper room Jesus is both the sacrifice and the One who hands the cup to His disciples. Paul calls that cup "the cup of blessing," "the communion of the blood of Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:16).

Isaiah's peg finds its answer in Revelation 3:7. The risen Christ describes Himself as "He who has the key of David, He who opens and no one shuts, and shuts and no one opens," taking Eliakim's words from Isaiah 22:22 as His own. Eliakim foreshadows Christ, and the end of Isaiah 22 shows why a greater fulfillment was needed. Even Eliakim's peg, Isaiah says, would be removed and fall (Isaiah 22:25). Christ's place cannot be cut down, and everything that hangs on Him is held.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah lists "all vessels of small quantity, from the cups to all the pitchers." Every church has both kinds. Some believers carry a great deal, and others feel like the smallest cup on the wall, unable to hold much or do much. Isaiah's picture answers that feeling directly. The cup and the pitcher hang on the same peg, and neither one is safer than the other. Paul describes believers as vessels in "a great house" (2 Timothy 2:20) and writes, "we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellence of the power may be of God and not of us" (2 Corinthians 4:7). A believer's security has never depended on how much he or she can hold.

Isaiah 22:25 also carries a warning. People still hang their whole weight on a pastor, a leader, a family member, or a reputation, and human pegs still fall. Only Christ can bear the full weight of anyone's life.

The Sinai basins remind us that God is the One who supplies what His people need to come near Him. The people did not sprinkle themselves. Moses applied to them the blood of the same sacrifice whose other half had been sprinkled on God's altar. The Lord's Table works the same way. We come to receive what Christ has already given.

The goblet of the Song adds joy to the picture. God's vessels are not only rescued and held. They are filled. The David who wrote "my cup runs over" (Psalm 23:5) knew a God who gives generously. Christians have long seen Christ as drinking, at the cross, the cup of fury that Isaiah 51:17 describes, so that His people could receive the cup of blessing instead. A life that rests on Him and is filled by Him will not run dry.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

ʼaggân occurs only three times: Exodus 24:6 ("basins"), Song of Solomon 7:2 ("goblet"), and Isaiah 22:24 ("cups"). All three are developed above. The other cup and vessel passages cited in this study, such as Psalm 23:5, Luke 22:20, 1 Corinthians 10:16, and 2 Corinthians 4:7, use different Hebrew or Greek words. They appear here as related biblical imagery, not as further occurrences of this word.
