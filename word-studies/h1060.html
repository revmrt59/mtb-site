FIRSTBORN, ELDEST, OLDEST - (bᵉkôwr, Strong's H1060)

Summary

The Hebrew word bᵉkôwr means "firstborn." In family records it usually names a father's eldest son. He held a place of honor, received a double share of the inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:15–17), and was expected to lead the family after his father. In the laws of consecration it names the male that first "opens the womb" of his mother, whether human or animal (Exodus 13:2). The two uses overlap but are not identical: in a household with more than one wife, each mother could have a first child, while only one son was the father's legal firstborn. Most of the word's uses appear in family records, in the Passover story, and in the laws that followed it. In those laws God claimed every firstborn in Israel as His own because He had spared them on the night He struck Egypt.

The word reaches well beyond birth order. God calls the whole nation of Israel "My son, My firstborn" (Exodus 4:22). He promises to make David, the youngest of Jesse's sons, "My firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth" (Psalm 89:27). Again and again He passes over the natural firstborn to give the blessing to a younger brother. Through these passages "firstborn" becomes a word for cherished sonship, rank that God confers, and life redeemed by a price. That background explains why the New Testament calls Jesus "the firstborn over all creation" (Colossians 1:15).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bᵉkôwr is an ordinary Hebrew family word. It appears most often in genealogies, especially in Genesis and 1 Chronicles, where it marks the eldest son in a line. It also clusters heavily in Exodus and Numbers, where it belongs to the Passover and to the laws about consecrating and redeeming the firstborn. Smaller groups of uses occur in the Law, the historical books, the Psalms, Job, and the Prophets. The NKJV almost always translates it "firstborn," occasionally "eldest" or "oldest" (2 Kings 3:27; Job 1:13, 18).

In Israel the firstborn son was the family's hope for the next generation. Jacob calls Reuben the beginning of his strength (Genesis 49:3), and Psalm 78:51 describes Egypt's firstborn as "the first of their strength in the tents of Ham."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The firstborn male of humans or animals. In family and inheritance settings this is the father's eldest son, who holds the right of the firstborn and a double portion (Deuteronomy 21:15–17). In the laws of consecration it is the male that first "opens the womb" of its mother: "Consecrate to Me all the firstborn, whatever opens the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and beast; it is Mine" (Exodus 13:2).

2. One given the standing of a firstborn, meaning the place of chief honor, special love, and heirship, whatever the actual birth order: "Also I will make him My firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth" (Psalm 89:27).

3. The chief or most extreme instance of something, used in poetry: "The firstborn of death devours his limbs" (Job 18:13), probably a deadly disease pictured as death's foremost offspring.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the genealogies the word simply identifies the eldest: Eliab, Jesse's firstborn (1 Samuel 17:13); Amnon, David's firstborn (2 Samuel 3:2). In the law it identifies both sons and livestock that belong to God (Numbers 18:15–17; Deuteronomy 15:19). Deuteronomy 21:15–17 protects the firstborn's double portion. A man with two wives could not give it to the son of the wife he loved if the son of the unloved wife was born first.

The poets stretch the word. Bildad's "firstborn of death" (Job 18:13) and Isaiah's "firstborn of the poor" (Isaiah 14:30), meaning the very poorest, show that "firstborn" could name whatever stands foremost in its class. This is how the word can become a title of supreme rank without any reference to birth.

A grammatical detail matters in Psalm 89:27. God does not say David is His firstborn. He says, "I will make him My firstborn." The rank is given.

4. Theological Significance

Three truths run through this word. First, God claims what He saves. Israel's firstborn lived through the night of judgment in Egypt, so their lives were His. The Passover was more than a rescue. It established ownership.

Second, God gives His blessing by choice, not by natural entitlement. The law guarded the firstborn's rights against a father's favoritism (Deuteronomy 21:15–17), yet God Himself repeatedly sets the firstborn aside. A man was forbidden to do this out of personal preference. God does it in keeping with His own purpose, and Paul draws the conclusion from Jacob and Esau: "not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11).

Third, God loves as a Father. When He calls Israel His firstborn, He speaks of tenderness, honor, and an inheritance He intends to give.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 4:22–23 and the redemption laws. Before a single plague falls, God sends Moses to Pharaoh with this message: "Israel is My son, My firstborn." Pharaoh is warned that if he will not release God's firstborn, God will take Pharaoh's (Exodus 4:23). The tenth plague is therefore a father defending his son. Afterward God declares that all Israel's firstborn are His (Exodus 13:2). Parents were to redeem their firstborn sons, and when their children asked why, they were to tell how the LORD killed Egypt's firstborn and spared theirs (Exodus 13:13–15). Later God took the Levites "instead of every firstborn" (Numbers 3:12). In Numbers 3:13 He explains, "On the day that I struck all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, I sanctified to Myself all the firstborn in Israel." When the firstborn outnumbered the Levites by 273, each of the extra firstborn was redeemed with five shekels (Numbers 3:46–47). This was substitution counted out to the last person. Every Israelite home with a firstborn son lived with a reminder that a life had been spared and a price had been paid.

Genesis 48:14 and 1 Chronicles 5:1–2. The aged Jacob lays his right hand on Ephraim, "who was the younger," and his left on Manasseh, "guiding his hands knowingly, for Manasseh was the firstborn." Joseph protests, and Jacob does not move his hands. Chronicles gives the larger picture. Reuben "was indeed the firstborn, but because he defiled his father's bed, his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph," and yet "Judah prevailed over his brothers, and from him came a ruler" (1 Chronicles 5:1–2). The firstborn's privileges were split, and neither part went to the eldest. The same pattern appears with Isaac over Ishmael and with Jacob over Esau, where God had announced beforehand, "the older shall serve the younger" (Genesis 25:23). Eliab the firstborn also looked like a king to Samuel, but God told Samuel He looks on the heart (1 Samuel 16:7; 17:13).

Psalm 89:27. "Also I will make him My firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth." David was the youngest of Jesse's sons, the boy left keeping the sheep. Here "firstborn" has nothing to do with birth order. The second line defines it: the highest of the kings, set above every throne. God promises this to David and to the line that will come from him (Psalm 89:29). This verse shows clearly that in Scripture "firstborn" can be a royal title, a position of supremacy that God grants.

Zechariah 12:10. God promises to pour out a spirit of grace and supplication on Jerusalem. They will look on the One they pierced and mourn for Him as one mourns an only son, grieving as for a firstborn. Scripture shows what that grief meant. A desperate king of Moab sacrificed his eldest son on the city wall (2 Kings 3:27), and Micah's worshiper asks whether he should give his firstborn for his transgression (Micah 6:7). Losing a firstborn was the deepest loss a family could suffer. Zechariah uses that grief to describe a people who finally recognize whom they wounded.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The threads of this word come together in Jesus. Paul calls Him "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn over all creation" (Colossians 1:15). Read against Psalm 89:27, the title speaks of rank and inheritance. It does not mean that Christ was the first thing God created. He is the promised Son of David, raised above all, the heir of everything, and the verses that follow say all things were created through Him and for Him (Colossians 1:16).

The Passover thread leads to the cross. Israel's firstborn lived because a lamb died, and their lives were bought back with a price. Micah asks whether he should give his firstborn for his transgression (Micah 6:7), and the prophet's own answer is no: the LORD requires him to do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with God (6:8). No father's offering of his child can atone for sin. The gospel's answer is God's own, distinct provision: the Father gives His Son. Micah does not predict that gift, but read across the whole Bible, it meets the need his question exposes. John applies Zechariah's pierced One to Jesus on the cross (John 19:37). The grief Zechariah foresaw is grief over a Firstborn who was given. The pattern of the younger brother receiving the blessing also comes into focus. People with no natural claim receive the inheritance, and the church is called the "church of the firstborn" (Hebrews 12:23).

7. Spirit & Life

Jeremiah 31:9 gathers much of this word into one sentence. To exiles coming home "with weeping," God says, "I am a Father to Israel, and Ephraim is My firstborn." Ephraim was the younger son, blessed only because Jacob crossed his hands. Ephraim also became the name of the northern kingdom that strayed furthest and was scattered first. Yet God calls this people His firstborn and promises to lead them by rivers of water on a straight path where they will not stumble. The firstborn standing God gives does not depend on who arrived first or who behaved best. It rests on the Father's word.

A believer can live in both halves of this word's story. You belong to God because He spared you; you are not your own, any more than an Israelite's firstborn was. You are also an heir by grace, chosen with the younger sons. Belonging to God is safe because the One who owns you also calls you His firstborn and treats you as an heir.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 38:7 — Er, Judah's firstborn, was wicked, and the LORD killed him. Being born first gave him no protection before God.

  • Joshua 6:26; 1 Kings 16:34 — Joshua's curse said that whoever rebuilt Jericho would lay its foundation at the cost of his firstborn. Centuries later Hiel lost his firstborn, Abiram, exactly as the word of the LORD had said.

  • 2 Chronicles 21:3 — Jehoshaphat gave the kingdom to Jehoram because he was the firstborn. This shows the ordinary rule of succession, which God often overturned.

  • Nehemiah 10:36 — The returned exiles promised again to bring their firstborn sons and animals to God's house, renewing the claim first made at the Passover.

  • Psalm 136:10 — Israel praises God, who struck Egypt in their firstborn, "for His mercy endures forever." The judgment on Egypt was mercy toward Israel.
