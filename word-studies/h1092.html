BILHAN - (Bilhân, Strong's H1092)

Summary

Bilhan is the name of two distinct men in the Old Testament, and they should not be confused. The first was a Horite, the first-listed son of Ezer, one of the chiefs of the ancient people who lived in Mount Seir before Esau's descendants took the land (Genesis 36:27; 1 Chronicles 1:42). The second was an Israelite of the tribe of Benjamin. He was the son of Jediael and the father of seven sons, whose family line produced 17,200 "mighty men of valor fit to go out for war and battle" (1 Chronicles 7:10–11). The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is usually linked to the Hebrew root behind the name Bilhah, and lexicons offer glosses such as "modest," "timid," or "troubled," but none of these is firmly established.

Neither man speaks or acts in the biblical story. Both appear only in genealogies, but those lists still carry weight. The Horite Bilhan belongs to a people God removed from Seir when He gave that land to Esau (Deuteronomy 2:12, 22). The Benjamite Bilhan stands at the head of a large fighting clan that the Chronicler preserved for Israel's returning remnant. Between them, the two records show that God governs the rise and fall of peoples, and that He remembers the families of His own people by name.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H1092 covers two distinct individuals. The first is Bilhan the Horite, son of Ezer and brother of Zaavan and Akan. First Chronicles 1:42 spells the third brother's name as Jaakan. The second is Bilhan the Benjamite, son of Jediael, who was a son of Benjamin (1 Chronicles 7:6, 10). The two men are separated by people, land, and probably centuries, and Scripture presents them as distinct people who share the name.

The lexicon records four occurrences in three verses. This is because 1 Chronicles 7:10 uses the name twice: "The son of Jediael was Bilhan, and the sons of Bilhan were…"

The name's etymology is uncertain. It is generally associated with the root b-l-h, the same root connected with Bilhah, Rachel's maidservant. Bilhah is a separately indexed name (H1090). Glosses such as "modest," "timid," or "troubled/terrified" appear in reference works, but they describe a possible derivation, not a confirmed meaning. Scripture makes no wordplay on the name, so it tells us nothing about either man's character.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Bilhan the Horite. Genesis 36 lists Bilhan among "the sons of Seir the Horite," the inhabitants of the land before Esau's line ruled it. His father Ezer is named among the Horite chiefs in the land of Edom (Genesis 36:20–21, 27), and 1 Chronicles 1:42 repeats the record. Deuteronomy explains what happened to this people: the Horites once lived in Seir, but the descendants of Esau dispossessed and destroyed them and settled in their place (Deuteronomy 2:12, 22).

Bilhan the Benjamite. First Chronicles 7:6–12 records the military register of Benjamin. Through Jediael's son Bilhan came seven sons: "Jeush, Benjamin, Ehud, Chenaanah, Zethan, Tharshish, and Ahishahar" (1 Chronicles 7:10). One son carries the tribe's own name. Another, Ehud, shares his name with the left-handed Benjamite judge of Judges 3, but nothing in the text identifies the two, and this Ehud is best treated as a namesake. The family's strength was considerable: "seventeen thousand two hundred mighty men of valor fit to go out for war and battle" (1 Chronicles 7:11).

Two of the sons' names stand out. Chenaanah is related to the word for "Canaanite," and Tharshish matches the name of the distant Gentile seaport Tarshish. Scripture gives no explanation for these choices. They may reflect contact with surrounding peoples, or the cultural blending that marked Israel's early life in the land, but the text does not say.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states that the LORD gave Mount Seir to Esau as a possession (Deuteronomy 2:5) and that He destroyed the Horites before Esau's descendants (Deuteronomy 2:22). Bilhan's name therefore belongs to a people whose time in the land ended by God's ordering of the nations. Moses even uses that history to show Israel how God gives land to peoples. The Bible's careful memory of a minor Horite chief's son reflects how seriously it treats the real history of the nations around Israel.

The Benjamite record served a different purpose. Chronicles was written for those returning from exile, and a list like 1 Chronicles 7:10–11 reminded them of their tribal identity and their past strength. It would be reasonable to infer that such lists encouraged a small remnant. The text itself simply preserves the names and the numbers.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No connection to Christ, and no later prophetic or New Testament use of the name Bilhan, is established in Scripture.

5. Spirit & Life

The Horite genealogy shows that the nations do not rise and fall by chance. The same God who kept His word to Abraham also kept His lesser promises to Esau, and He ruled over peoples like the Horites of whom little else is known (Deuteronomy 2:5, 12, 22).

The Benjamite list shows God's people being counted one family at a time, down to a descendant of Benjamin whose own deeds are never told. In the Chronicler's record, belonging to Israel was never abstract. It was carried in real households with real names.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:20–21, 27 – Identifies Bilhan as a son of Ezer among the Horite chiefs of Seir.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:42 – Repeats the Horite record, spelling one brother's name as Jaakan.

  • Deuteronomy 2:5, 12, 22 – Explains God's gift of Seir to Esau and the removal of the Horites.

  • 1 Chronicles 7:6, 10 – Places Bilhan the Benjamite as Jediael's son and names his seven sons.

  • 1 Chronicles 7:11 – Records the 17,200 mighty men of valor in Jediael's line.
