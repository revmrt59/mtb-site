ADBEEL - (ʼAdbᵉʼêl, Strong's H0110)

Summary

Adbeel was the third-listed of Ishmael's twelve sons and Abraham's grandson through Hagar the Egyptian. Scripture names him only twice, in Genesis 25:13 and again in 1 Chronicles 1:29, both times in the list of Ishmael's sons. The meaning of his name is uncertain. It most likely ends with ʼel, "God," and scholars have proposed meanings such as "disciplined by God" or "invited by God." None of these can be settled, so the name's meaning is best treated as unknown.

Adbeel's place in Scripture is small, but it is not empty. Years before Ishmael had a single son, God told Abraham, "He shall beget twelve princes, and I will make him a great nation" (Genesis 17:20). Genesis 25 records twelve sons, and Adbeel is one of them. He stands in the record as part of the evidence that God keeps even the promises that fall outside the main line of Israel's story. God's chosen line ran through Isaac, yet He did not forget the son Abraham prayed for.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Adbeel is one individual, the third-listed son of Ishmael. The brothers named before him are Nebajoth and Kedar, and those named after him are Mibsam, Mishma, Dumah, Massa, Hadar, Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah (Genesis 25:13–15). He was a grandson of Abraham and Hagar. Scripture does not name his mother, although Ishmael's wife came from Egypt (Genesis 21:21). No other person in the Bible bears this name.

The name's meaning is debated. It appears to contain the common Semitic element ʼel, "God." Proposed derivations rely mostly on Arabic cognates. One links the name to a root for discipline, which would give "disciplined" or "chastened of God." Another connects it to a root for inviting someone to a feast, which would give "invited of God." Other suggestions exist, but none is secure. Because the meaning is unknown, it should not be read as a key to Adbeel's life or to Ishmael's spiritual condition when the boy was born.

Genesis 25:16 calls Ishmael's sons "princes" and associates their names with "their towns and their settlements." That suggests Adbeel also gave his name to a tribal group. Many scholars identify this group with an Arab people called Idibi'ilu in the inscriptions of the Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser III in the eighth century BC. Those records appear to connect an Idibi'ilu leader with oversight of the frontier facing Egypt. This identification is widely held, but it remains a scholarly proposal rather than a statement of Scripture.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Scripture tells us nothing Adbeel did. Genesis 25:12 opens with "the genealogy of Ishmael, Abraham's son, whom Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah's maidservant, bore to Abraham." The twelve names follow, and then a summary: "twelve princes according to their nations" (Genesis 25:16). Ishmael's descendants lived in the desert country from Havilah to Shur, east of Egypt (Genesis 25:18, paraphrased). That is the same region where the Angel of the LORD had found the pregnant Hagar, near "the way to Shur" (Genesis 16:7).

Centuries later, the Chronicler repeated the list: "These are their genealogies: The firstborn of Ishmael was Nebajoth; then Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam" (1 Chronicles 1:29). Some of Adbeel's brothers appear again in the prophets. Kedar, Tema, and Dumah are examples (Isaiah 21:11–17). Adbeel does not.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

What Scripture states plainly is that God promised Ishmael twelve princes and that the promise was kept. Genesis places the fulfillment directly beside the story of Isaac, whom God had named as the heir of His covenant (Genesis 17:21). Moses did not have to record Ishmael's sons at all. By naming each one, Genesis shows that God's word to Abraham on Ishmael's behalf did not fail in a single detail.

Two further observations require more care. First, the Chronicler kept these Arabian tribes in Israel's family record after the exile, even though Israel and the Arabs had often been rivals. That choice fits the Bible's steady insistence that the nations belong to God's world and that He sets their place (compare Deuteronomy 32:8). Second, the ʼel in Adbeel's name may reflect the memory of God in Abraham's wider family. Ishmael's own name, given by God, means "God hears" (Genesis 16:11). Even so, ʼel-names were common across Semitic peoples, so the name alone cannot tell us what Adbeel believed.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Neither the New Testament nor the prophets connect Adbeel to Christ. The larger story still reaches toward his people. God promised that all the families of the earth would be blessed through Abraham (Genesis 12:3). Isaiah pictures the flocks of Kedar and Nebaioth, Adbeel's brothers, accepted on God's altar (Isaiah 60:7), and Arabians were among those who heard the apostles at Pentecost (Acts 2:11). These passages do not mention Adbeel. They show that the peoples descended from Ishmael were not outside the reach of God's purpose in Christ.

5. Spirit & Life

Adbeel's name sits in a list most readers pass over, and that is exactly its value. God told a pleading father, "As for Ishmael, I have heard you" (Genesis 17:20), and then He kept His word, son by son. If God was this careful with a promise made outside the main line of redemption, His people can trust Him with the promises He has made to them in Christ.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:20 — God's promise of twelve princes from Ishmael, which Adbeel's birth helped fulfill.

  • Genesis 25:12–16 — The genealogy naming Adbeel third and calling the sons tribal princes.

  • Genesis 25:18 — The region where Ishmael's descendants settled.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:29 — The Chronicler's preservation of Adbeel's name after the exile.

  • Genesis 16:7, 11 — Hagar near Shur and the meaning of Ishmael's name, the background to the family's story.
