BELA - (Belaʻ, Strong's H1106)

Summary

Bela is the name of four separate subjects in the Old Testament: a city on the plain near the Dead Sea, an early king of Edom, the firstborn son of Benjamin, and a later member of the tribe of Reuben. The Hebrew name בֶּלַע comes from the verb bala, "to swallow up," and is generally understood to mean "swallowing" or "destruction." Scripture never explains why any of these people or the city received the name, so the meaning should not be treated as a description of what each one was. Each person and the city should be understood in their own biblical setting.

The city carries the most narrative weight. Genesis introduces it as "Bela (that is, Zoar)" (Genesis 14:2), one of five cities whose kings rebelled and lost a war. When the Lord overthrew the cities of the plain, this one alone was spared, because Lot pleaded for it as "a little one" (Genesis 19:20–22). The name Bela also stands at the head of the list of Edom's kings, who reigned long before Israel had a throne (Genesis 36:31–33). It also marks the first of Benjamin's family lines, whose descendants Chronicles still counted as "mighty men of valor" (1 Chronicles 7:7). Together these records show God's mercy in the middle of judgment, His unhurried faithfulness to His promises, and His careful knowledge of the families He preserves.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name Belaʻ (בֶּלַע) is built from the verb bala, which means "to swallow up" or "to engulf." Strong's connects the proper name with that root and gives the sense "swallowing" or "destruction." That derivation is the standard explanation. What remains unknown is the reason the name was given. Scripture offers no wordplay on "Bela" itself, and nothing in the text says whether the city's name was its original local name or how it was understood by the people who used it. For the three men named Bela, the text gives no explanation at all.

The fourteen uses of this Hebrew name describe three men and a city:

  • The city of Bela, later called Zoar, one of the five cities of the plain (Genesis 14:2, 8).

  • Bela son of Beor, the first king listed for Edom, who ruled from Dinhabah (Genesis 36:32–33; 1 Chronicles 1:43–44).

  • Bela son of Benjamin, Jacob's grandson and the ancestor of the Belaite clan (Genesis 46:21; Numbers 26:38, 40; 1 Chronicles 7:6–7; 8:1, 3). The NKJV spells his name "Belah" in Genesis 46:21.

  • Bela son of Azaz, a Reubenite whose family lived east of the Jordan (1 Chronicles 5:8).

The city's later name, Zoar, is indexed separately (H6820). It does carry an explicit biblical wordplay: Lot calls the town "a little one," and the text says that is why it was named Zoar (Genesis 19:20–22). That account belongs to the related name, but it is essential to the story of the city called Bela.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The city of Bela (Zoar). Genesis 14 opens with the first war Scripture describes. For twelve years the kings of the plain had served Chedorlaomer, and in the thirteenth year they rebelled (Genesis 14:4). Genesis 14:2 names them: "Bera king of Sodom, Birsha king of Gomorrah, Shinab king of Admah, Shemeber king of Zeboiim, and the king of Bela (that is, Zoar)." Only the king of Bela is left without a personal name. The five kings marched out to the Valley of Siddim (Genesis 14:8), were routed, and in the plunder that followed, Lot was carried off until Abram rescued him (Genesis 14:11–16). The words "that is, Zoar" are a note for later readers who knew the town by its newer name.

In Genesis 19 the city returns under that newer name. As the angels hurried Lot out of Sodom, he begged to escape to a nearby town instead of the mountains: "is it not a little one?" (Genesis 19:20). The angel agreed not to overthrow it and said he could do nothing until Lot arrived there (Genesis 19:21–22). Lot reached Zoar as the sun rose, and then fire fell on the plain (Genesis 19:23–25). Later Scripture confirms the exception. When Moses describes the overthrow, he lists Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim, but not Zoar (Deuteronomy 29:23). The town survived into later history. Moses saw it from Mount Nebo (Deuteronomy 34:3), and Isaiah and Jeremiah mention it in their oracles against Moab (Isaiah 15:5; Jeremiah 48:34). Lot himself did not stay. He grew afraid and moved to a cave in the mountains (Genesis 19:30).

Bela son of Benjamin. Bela heads the list of Benjamin's sons who went down to Egypt with Jacob (Genesis 46:21), and Chronicles calls him Benjamin's firstborn (1 Chronicles 8:1). In the wilderness census his descendants formed "the family of the Belaites" (Numbers 26:38). The genealogies of his line differ in ways that reflect how Israel kept family records. Naaman and Ard appear as Benjamin's sons in Genesis 46:21 but as sons of Bela in Numbers 26:40. First Chronicles 7:7 lists five sons of Bela, and 1 Chronicles 8:3 opens a different list: "The sons of Bela were Addar, Gera, Abihud," followed by several more. In Hebrew genealogies, "son" can mean descendant, and lists often trace clans from different periods, so these records do not have to contradict each other. Chronicles describes Bela's five heads of houses as "mighty men of valor," numbering "twenty-two thousand and thirty-four" (1 Chronicles 7:7).

Bela son of Beor, king of Edom. Genesis 36:31 introduces the kings who reigned in Edom before any king reigned over Israel, and Bela comes first: "Bela the son of Beor reigned in Edom, and the name of his city was Dinhabah" (Genesis 36:32). The next verse records the succession: "And when Bela died, Jobab the son of Zerah of Bozrah reigned in his place" (Genesis 36:33). Several rulers in the list are associated with different named cities or regions, though some entries give none, which suggests a kingship that did not pass from father to son. Bela's father shares a name with Balaam's father, Beor (Numbers 22:5). That similarity has invited speculation, but Scripture never connects the two men.

Bela son of Azaz. This Reubenite appears in the genealogy of 1 Chronicles 5, which begins by explaining that Reuben lost his birthright (1 Chronicles 5:1–2). Bela's family lived in Aroer and extended as far as Nebo and Baal Meon (1 Chronicles 5:8). The surrounding verses describe Reubenite herds spreading toward the wilderness and Gilead, and a war with the Hagrites in Saul's day (1 Chronicles 5:9–10).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

What Scripture states about the city is plain. Zoar belonged to a rebellious alliance, stood within the region marked for judgment, and was spared at Lot's request. The text also roots Lot's rescue in something larger than Lot: "God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out of the midst of the overthrow" (Genesis 19:29). Abraham had pleaded for the righteous of Sodom only a chapter earlier (Genesis 18:23–32). The fact that a town called "Bela" was the one place left standing when the plain was destroyed is a thematic irony that readers have rightly noticed. It is an observation, though, not a claim the text makes about the name.

The list of Edom's kings carries real theological weight. Esau's descendants had a royal succession before any king reigned over Israel (Genesis 36:31), and in Moses' day Israel had to ask permission from "the king of Edom" to pass through his land (Numbers 20:14). God had promised Jacob that "kings shall come from your body" (Genesis 35:11). A reasonable implication is that Genesis 36 shows God's faithfulness to Esau's line, to whom He had given Mount Seir (Deuteronomy 2:5), while Israel's own promise waited. Israel's kingship came later, and in David's day Edom became subject to Israel (2 Samuel 8:14).

The Benjaminite Bela shows God's care for the ordinary building blocks of His people. The tribe of Benjamin was nearly wiped out in the civil war of Judges 20–21, and only six hundred men survived (Judges 20:47). Yet Chronicles, compiled after the exile, still records Bela's line by name. Benjamin returned from Babylon alongside Judah (Ezra 1:5). The Chronicler's lists reminded a small, struggling community that God had preserved a remnant and remembered its families. The record does not prove that every named household survived each disaster or returned from exile, but keeping these names was itself a sign of God's care.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament does not mention Bela by name, but it interprets the event that made Zoar famous. Jesus pointed to "the days of Lot" as a picture of sudden judgment on the day the Son of Man is revealed, and He warned, "Remember Lot's wife," who looked back on the road to Zoar (Luke 17:28–32; Genesis 19:26). Peter draws the other lesson from the same story: the Lord who turned Sodom and Gomorrah to ashes also rescued righteous Lot, and so He "knows how to deliver the godly" (2 Peter 2:6–9). Zoar is where that deliverance ended. The town was a small refuge granted inside a ruined plain, and that rescue came through the intercession of another man.

The Edomite king list also belongs to a story that leads to Christ. The promise that kings would come from Jacob waited through centuries of Edom's head start. It reached David and finally the Son of David, whose kingdom has no end (Luke 1:32–33).

The root behind the name Bela resonates thematically, though not as typology. The verb bala, "to swallow," often describes judgment that engulfs. Isaiah reverses it: "He will swallow up death forever, And the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from all faces" (Isaiah 25:8). Paul applies this to the resurrection: "Death is swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). The proper name Bela is not part of that prophecy. Still, the Bible's own vocabulary shows the One who judges swallowing up the greatest destroyer of all.

5. Spirit & Life

The sparing of Zoar shows the kind of mercy God shows. Lot was not a heroic intercessor. He lingered, the angels had to pull him by the hand, and he bargained for a place he soon abandoned (Genesis 19:16–20, 30). Even so, the Lord granted his request and held back judgment until Lot was safe. Behind it all, Scripture says, "God remembered Abraham" (Genesis 19:29), and the context points back to Abraham's pleading for the righteous in the chapter before (Genesis 18:23–32). Mercy at Zoar was never Lot's achievement. It is a picture of how God delights to save, and how He hears the prayers of those who plead for others.

The record of Bela king of Edom calls for patience. God's people sometimes watch others build cities and dynasties while their own promises seem slow. Genesis 36 does not hide that contrast. It sets Edom's kings plainly in view and then turns back to the story of Jacob's family (Genesis 37:1–2). God was not late. He was working on His own timetable toward a King greater than any in Edom's list.

The genealogies of Bela the Benjaminite and Bela the Reubenite may look like dry records, but they show that God knows His people by name and by family. Thousands of names in Chronicles were remembered after war, exile, and loss. Not one of them was forgotten by the God who kept the record.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 14:2, 8 — Identifies Bela as Zoar and places its king in the rebellion and battle of the Valley of Siddim.

  • Genesis 19:20–30 (related name, Zoar) — Lot's plea, the sparing and naming of Zoar, and Lot's later departure.

  • Deuteronomy 29:23 — Lists the overthrown cities without Zoar, confirming it was spared.

  • Genesis 36:31–33; 1 Chronicles 1:43–44 — Bela son of Beor, first listed king of Edom, before Israel had a king.

  • Genesis 46:21; Numbers 26:38, 40 — Bela son of Benjamin and the Belaite clan.

  • 1 Chronicles 7:6–7; 8:1, 3 — Bela as Benjamin's firstborn, his descendants, and the "mighty men of valor" of his line.

  • 1 Chronicles 5:8 — Bela son of Azaz, the Reubenite, and his territory.

  • Luke 17:28–32; 2 Peter 2:6–9 — The New Testament's interpretation of Lot's rescue and the judgment on the plain.

  • Isaiah 25:8; 1 Corinthians 15:54 — The verb "swallow up" turned toward the defeat of death (thematic connection to the root only).
