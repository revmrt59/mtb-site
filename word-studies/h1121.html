SON, CHILDREN, DESCENDANTS - (bên, Strong's H1121)

Summary

The Hebrew word bên means "son." It is one of the most common words in the Old Testament, and it reaches far beyond the family. A bên can be a son or grandson, a member of a tribe or nation ("the children of Israel"), or someone marked by a quality or condition. Hebrew calls brave men "sons of valor," and it gives a person's age by saying he was "a son of" so many years. It can even describe things that come out of a source, such as sparks that are "sons" of a flame or arrows that are "sons" of a quiver. Behind all of these uses is one idea: a son shows where someone comes from and what he belongs to.

That idea carries great spiritual weight. God calls Israel His son and keeps faith with "the sons of Jacob" even when they fail Him. He promises David a royal son and says, "I will be his Father." He addresses Ezekiel as "son of man," a frail human being standing before a holy God. Again and again He passes over the natural firstborn to choose a younger son by grace. These threads meet in Jesus Christ, the beloved Son, the Son of Man, and the King in David's line. Through Christ, people who were never sons by birth are received as God's children, and they begin to bear their Father's likeness.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bên is the ordinary Hebrew word for a male child. It appears in every part of the Old Testament: genealogies, laws, battle accounts, psalms, proverbs, and prophecy. Much of its use comes in family records such as "the sons of Levi" or "the son of Jesse." These lists can look dry, but they follow the lines through which God kept His promises across generations.

In Israel a son carried his father's name, received his inheritance, and continued his household. Older lexicons connect bên with a Hebrew verb meaning "to build," which is why Strong's describes a son as "a builder of the family name." That link is uncertain, so the word's meaning is best learned from how Scripture actually uses it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its heart bên names someone or something by its source or by the group to which it belongs. Context decides which sense is meant:

1. A son, grandson, or descendant by birth. Abraham's reply to Isaac, "Here I am, my son" (Genesis 22:7), uses the word in its most familiar sense.

2. A member of a people, tribe, or company. The phrase "children of Israel" means the Israelites (Exodus 2:23), and "the people of Ammon" means the Ammonites (Deuteronomy 2:19).

3. Someone belonging to a quality, condition, or stage of life. "Sons of valor" describes courageous men; the NKJV renders the phrase "courageous and valiant" in 2 Samuel 13:28, and "a son of twenty-three years" is a man twenty-three years old (2 Kings 23:31).

4. The young of animals, or something that comes out of a source. The word can mean a calf (Genesis 18:7), and in poetry it can describe sparks or arrows.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's most distinctive use is the phrase "son of [something]." When Hebrew calls a person or thing the "son" of a quality, it means that the quality defines him. When Saul says of David, "he shall surely die" (1 Samuel 20:31), the Hebrew says that David is "a son of death," a man marked out for death. The worthless men called "scoundrels" in some verses are literally "sons of worthlessness."

The same phrase reaches into nature and poetry. In Jonah 4:10 the LORD speaks of the plant "which came up in a night and perished in a night." In Hebrew the plant is "a son of a night." It sprang up overnight and perished just as quickly. Job 5:7 says, "man is born to trouble, As the sparks fly upward," and the sparks are literally "sons of the flame," leaping out of the fire that produced them. When Jacob blesses Joseph, "Joseph is a fruitful bough" (Genesis 49:22) is literally "a fruitful son," and his "branches" are "daughters." Family language described the growth of a tree. In Lamentations 3:13 the LORD "has caused the arrows of His quiver To pierce my loins," and those arrows are "the sons of His quiver," sent out by the One who holds them.

These uses do not turn every son into a symbol. They do show how naturally Hebrew connected identity with source. A son came from somewhere, belonged somewhere, and carried the character of that place.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats a son as the continuation of his father's life and strength. Jacob calls Reuben "my might and the beginning of my strength" (Genesis 49:3), and the law uses the same phrase to protect the firstborn's double portion (Deuteronomy 21:17). Psalm 127 calls children "a heritage from the LORD" and compares them to "arrows in the hand of a warrior" (Psalm 127:3–4). The man with "his quiver full of them" is called happy, and "They shall not be ashamed, But shall speak with their enemies in the gate" (Psalm 127:5). Sons carried a father's name and defense into the future. Ruth 4 shows how deep this ran. When Ruth's son is born, the women bless the LORD for giving Naomi "a restorer of life," and they call Ruth herself "better to you than seven sons" (Ruth 4:14–15). Naomi had lost her sons, and God rebuilt her household through a Moabite daughter-in-law.

God takes up this language for His relationship with His people. He calls Israel "My son, My firstborn" (Exodus 4:22), and the whole nation becomes "the children of Israel." That name rests on God's faithfulness, not on Israel's performance. "For I am the LORD, I do not change; Therefore you are not consumed, O sons of Jacob" (Malachi 3:6). The same family language can also bring rebuke. Through Jeremiah the LORD laments that His people are foolish, silly children who do not know Him (Jeremiah 4:22). He grieves as a Father over sons who act nothing like Him.

Scripture also repeatedly overturns the natural order of sons. God chooses Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Ephraim over Manasseh, and David, the youngest of Jesse's sons, over his older brothers (Genesis 21; 25; 48; 1 Samuel 16). The pattern appears so often that it teaches by repetition: the inheritance God gives comes by His choice and promise, not by birth order.

The phrase "sons of God" also describes heavenly beings. They "came to present themselves before the LORD" (Job 1:6), and at creation "all the sons of God shouted for joy" (Job 38:7). They are most likely called His sons because they come directly from His creative hand and serve in His presence. Interpreters still debate who the "sons of God" in Genesis 6:2 were, but the Job passages clearly describe the heavenly court.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 22:2 — "your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love." God's command to Abraham piles up one phrase after another, and each one increases the cost: "Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love... and offer him there as a burnt offering." Isaac was not Abraham's only child, but he was the only son of promise, the one through whom every promise was to come. The son carries the wood up the mountain, and the father carries the fire and the knife. The Angel of the LORD stops Abraham's hand, and a ram caught in a thicket is offered instead of his son. The New Testament later calls Isaac Abraham's "only begotten" (Hebrews 11:17), and the Father's voice at Jesus' baptism calls Him "My beloved Son." Moriah prepares us for a day when God would not spare His own Son.

1 Chronicles 28:6 and Psalm 72:1 — the royal son. God's promise to David reaches its center in a son. David recalls God's words about Solomon: "I have chosen him to be My son, and I will be his Father" (1 Chronicles 28:6; compare 2 Samuel 7:14). The king of Israel was meant to rule as God's son, reflecting the Father's justice on the throne. Psalm 72 turns that calling into prayer: "Give the king Your judgments, O God, And Your righteousness to the king's Son" (72:1). The psalm goes on to ask for a reign that defends the poor and lasts as long as the sun. No son of David fulfilled that prayer. Solomon himself turned aside, and the kings after him declined until the throne was lost. Psalm 2 had already looked past them to a King to whom the LORD says, "You are My Son, Today I have begotten You" (2:7), a King who would receive the nations as His inheritance. The failure of David's sons left the promise open and waiting for the true Son. Hebrews 1:5 joins Psalm 2:7 with the promise to David and applies both to Jesus.

Ezekiel — "Son of man." God addresses Ezekiel as "son of man" more than ninety times. Ezekiel 30:2 is typical: "Son of man, prophesy and say, 'Thus says the Lord GOD.'" The Hebrew phrase means "son of Adam," a mortal human being. Ezekiel has just seen the blazing glory of God (Ezekiel 1), and the title would keep him aware of what he is: a man of dust, sent to speak God's words. Psalm 8:4 uses the same phrase with wonder: "What is man that You are mindful of him, And the son of man that You visit him?" Ezekiel's title humbles him, and the psalm marvels that God would care for such a creature at all.

Proverbs — "My son." Proverbs passes on wisdom as a father speaking to a son: "My son, pay attention to my wisdom; Lend your ear to my understanding" (Proverbs 5:1; compare Proverbs 1:8). Sonship here means receiving instruction, and the father's heart is involved. He rejoices when his son's heart becomes wise (Proverbs 23:15). Wisdom grows inside a relationship where correction comes from love. Hebrews takes up Proverbs 3 and speaks to believers "as to sons": "For whom the LORD loves He chastens, And scourges every son whom He receives" (Hebrews 12:5–6).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Every strand of bên comes together in Jesus. Israel was God's son, called out of Egypt (Hosea 11:1). Matthew sees that pattern fulfilled when the child Jesus returns from Egypt (Matthew 2:15). Jesus is the faithful Son of Israel, obeying where the nation failed.

He is also the royal Son promised to David. The words of Psalm 2:7 sound again at His baptism and transfiguration, and the prayer of Psalm 72 finds its answer in His righteous rule. The beloved son of Genesis 22 is matched by the beloved Son whom the Father gave. This time no ram was caught in a thicket, because the Son Himself was the provided Lamb.

Jesus' most frequent name for Himself, "the Son of Man," draws mainly on Daniel 7:13, where an Aramaic phrase describes one like a son of man receiving an everlasting kingdom. The Hebrew uses help us hear the full sound of the title. Like Ezekiel, Jesus is truly human and sent with God's word. Like the man of Psalm 8, He shares the frailty of our race. Hebrews 2:6–9 reads Psalm 8 as fulfilled in Jesus, who was made lower than the angels, suffered death, and is now crowned with glory. The heavenly "sons of God" were created, but the Son is the One through whom all things were made (Hebrews 1:2, 5).

Benjamin's birth offers a fitting picture of this path. As Rachel died, "she called his name Ben-Oni; but his father called him Benjamin" (Genesis 35:18). "Son of my sorrow" became "son of the right hand." Genesis does not present this as a prophecy, but it rhymes with the gospel. The Man of sorrows is the Son to whom the LORD says, "Sit at My right hand" (Psalm 110:1).

The pattern of the passed-over firstborn also prepares for the gospel. If God's inheritance never depended on birth order, it need not depend on birth at all. Those who receive Christ are given the right to become children of God, born "not of blood... but of God" (John 1:12–13). God sent His Son so that we might receive adoption as sons and cry, "Abba, Father" (Galatians 4:4–7). That name of inheritance belongs to every believer, men and women alike.

7. Spirit & Life

Hebrew called a man a "son of valor" when courage defined him and a "son of death" when he was marked out for death. So when Jesus says that peacemakers will be called sons of God, and that loving enemies shows we are sons of our Father in heaven (Matthew 5:9, 44–45), He echoes the idiom of bên. A son resembles his source. To be God's child is to share His standing and also, increasingly, His character. The question is not only whose family we are in but whose likeness we show.

That likeness is formed through the Father's correction. Proverbs and Hebrews teach the same lesson: discipline is evidence that God has received us as sons. A believer under God's hand need not read hardship as rejection. The Father who corrects is the Father who rejoices when wisdom takes root.

Our security rests where Israel's did. The sons of Jacob were not consumed because the LORD does not change (Malachi 3:6). Believers stand before God in the Son He fully approves, and their place in the family depends on His faithfulness, not on their record.

Children are also like arrows in the hand of a warrior (Psalm 127:4). A father aims and sends his sons so that his strength reaches places he will never go. Parents, teachers, and spiritual fathers and mothers can take this to heart. Those we raise in the faith carry the Father's name into a future we will not see. Ruth reminds us that God's household is not limited to blood. A foreign daughter-in-law who loved well proved better than seven sons and became an ancestor of David and of Christ.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 2:23 — The children of Israel groaned under their bondage and cried out, and their cry came up to God. God heard their groaning and remembered His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Exodus 2:24).

  • Numbers 6:27 — After the priestly blessing, the LORD says that the priests will put His name on the children of Israel, and He will bless them. The children of Israel carry the LORD's name.

  • Deuteronomy 6:7 — Israel is to teach God's words diligently to their children. Faith passes from parent to son in the ordinary moments of daily life.

  • Lamentations 3:33 — In the middle of judgment, the poet confesses that the LORD does not afflict or grieve the children of men from His heart.

  • Joel 2:28 — God promises to pour out His Spirit so that "your sons and your daughters" will prophesy. Peter declares this fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 2:17).

  • Psalm 128:6 — The blessing of seeing one's children's children describes a life God has kept and multiplied.
