BUILD, BUILT, REBUILD - (bânâh, Strong's H1129)

Summary

The Hebrew verb bânâh means "to build." It is the Old Testament's ordinary word for raising altars, houses, walls, cities, palaces, and the temple. Hebrew also uses the same verb for building a family. A household with children is a "built" house. Scripture uses bânâh for God's own work as well: He forms the woman in the garden, raises up David's royal line, and promises to rebuild a ruined people after judgment.

Across Scripture, the word keeps asking who is doing the building and for whom. At Babel, people build to make a name for themselves, and God scatters them. The patriarchs build altars before they build anything else. David offers to build God a house, and God answers that He will build David one. Psalm 127:1 sums up the lesson: "Unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain who build it." The word's story leads to Jesus Christ. He is the stone the builders rejected, the promised Branch who builds the temple of the LORD, and the cornerstone of a living house where God dwells by His Spirit.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bânâh is a common Hebrew verb found in nearly every part of the Old Testament. Most of its uses gather around Israel's major building projects:

  • the patriarchs' altars in Genesis;

  • the temple and royal buildings in 1 Kings and 2 Chronicles, where the word is used most heavily;

  • the rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls in Nehemiah;

  • the prophets' promises of restoration.

Many have connected bânâh with the Hebrew words for "son" (bēn) and "daughter" (bat). Scholars debate whether these words are actually related. The idiom of "building" a family does not depend on that connection, however. It stands clearly in the biblical text, as Sarai's words in Genesis 16:2 and the blessing in Ruth 4:11 show.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To construct or rebuild a physical structure. This is the most common sense. It covers altars, houses, cities, walls, and the temple, and also siege works built against a city (2 Kings 25:1; Ezekiel 4:2). The word applies to new construction and to restoring what has been destroyed (Nehemiah 2:17).

2. To build a family or household. This sense means gaining children and continuing a family line. In Genesis 16:2 Sarai hopes to "obtain children" through Hagar. The Hebrew reads roughly, "perhaps I will be built from her." Deuteronomy 25:9 speaks of a man who refuses to build up his brother's house by providing him an heir.

3. To establish or restore a people or a person. This figurative sense appears mostly with God as the builder. He builds up Israel after judgment (Jeremiah 24:6; 31:4). He also promises that repentant nations will be "established" among His people (Jeremiah 12:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the verb stays the same. Its weight changes with the builder and the purpose. When Hiel rebuilt Jericho, he lost his firstborn at the foundation and his youngest son at the gates, "according to the word of the LORD, which He had spoken through Joshua" (1 Kings 16:34; compare Joshua 6:26). The work itself was ordinary construction. It became a judgment because it defied God's word.

The prophets often pair bânâh with opposite verbs. Jeremiah is commissioned to root out and pull down, and also to build and to plant (Jeremiah 1:10). God later promises to watch over His people "to build and to plant" just as He once watched over them to tear down (Jeremiah 31:28). Building is regularly joined with planting and with living in what has been built. Without that last step, building means nothing.

That last point leads to a repeated warning. Deuteronomy 28:30 says, "you shall build a house, but you shall not dwell in it." Amos aims the same judgment at leaders who oppressed the poor: "Though you have built houses of hewn stone, yet you shall not dwell in them" (Amos 5:11). Zephaniah 1:13 repeats it. Building that is cut off from faithfulness to God becomes labor done for someone else.

4. Theological Significance

God is the first and greatest Builder in Scripture. He "builds His layers in the sky" (Amos 9:6), and "the LORD builds up Jerusalem; He gathers together the outcasts of Israel" (Psalm 147:2). The second verse shows that, for God, building a city and gathering scattered people are one act. What He builds lasts. What people build against Him does not. When Edom boasts, "we will return and build the desolate places," the LORD replies, "They may build, but I will throw down" (Malachi 1:4).

The first things God's people build show what comes first for them. Noah built an altar to the LORD as soon as he left the ark (Genesis 8:20). Abram received the promise of the land and built altars at Shechem and near Bethel, while he himself lived in a tent (Genesis 12:7–8). Isaac and Jacob did the same (Genesis 26:25; 35:7). Before cities, fortresses, or palaces, the patriarchs built places to meet God.

God also controlled how those altars were built: "you shall not build it of hewn stone; for if you use your tool on it, you have profaned it" (Exodus 20:25). Joshua followed that command on Mount Ebal (Joshua 8:30–31). The neighboring nations decorated their worship sites with carefully crafted work. Israel's altar was made from stones exactly as God had made them. Human skill could not improve the place where sacrifice met mercy. This rule applied to the altar, not to the temple, which used finely cut stone, cedar, and gold (1 Kings 6). Even there, the stones were shaped at the quarry, so no iron tool was heard at the building site (1 Kings 6:7).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 2:22 — God builds the woman. The first time bânâh appears in Scripture, God is the builder and a person is what He builds. The LORD God took the rib from the man and built it into a woman. The NKJV translates the verb "made." From the very beginning, building and family are connected. God's first construction was a companion. Paul later calls the joining of man and wife "a great mystery," adding, "but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). Genesis does not state this typology directly. Still, the pattern is worth noticing: the first bride was built from the side of the first man, and the church is formed from the life of Christ.

Genesis 11:4–8 — Babel. "Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered" (Genesis 11:4). The builders used baked bricks in place of stone (11:3). Their goal was security, fame, and unity without God. The text then says, "the LORD came down to see the city and the tower which the sons of men had built" (11:5). The tower meant to reach heaven was so small that God had to come down to look at it. He confused their language, and they stopped building the city (11:8). Babel is Scripture's first picture of building as a way to escape dependence on God.

2 Samuel 7 — Two houses. David, settled in his palace, wants to build a house for the ark (7:2, 5). God turns the offer around. He had never asked for a house of cedar (7:7). Instead, the LORD will make David a house, meaning a dynasty, and David's son will build the house for God's name (7:11–13). David responds in prayer that God has revealed He will build him a house (7:27).

The double meaning of the word carries the whole exchange. David offered stone and timber, and God promised descendants and a throne. The idiom behind this promise also appears when the elders bless Ruth: "like Rachel and Leah, the two who built the house of Israel" (Ruth 4:11). The nation's true builders were the mothers of its tribes and, through Ruth, the line that would produce David. Behind both was the God who gave children.

1 Kings 8 — A house for the Name. Solomon repeatedly describes the temple as built "for the name of the LORD" (1 Kings 5:5; 8:16–20). He knew no building could contain God: "heaven and the heaven of heavens cannot contain You. How much less this temple which I have built!" (8:27). God's name stands for His revealed character and His promised presence. Because God put His name there, the temple became a place to turn toward. Even captives in a foreign land could "pray to You toward... the temple which I have built for Your name" (8:48). The building mattered because God had attached His promise to it.

Nehemiah 4 — The trowel and the sword. When the returned exiles rebuilt Jerusalem's walls, Sanballat mocked them and enemies plotted to attack. Nehemiah organized the work so that those who built on the wall worked with one hand and held a weapon with the other. Every builder had his sword at his side as he built (Nehemiah 4:17–18). Rebuilding what God had restored required watchfulness. The work went on in plain view of enemies who wanted Jerusalem to stay in ruins.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets take up the language of building for God's promise beyond judgment. They do not speak only of starting fresh. They speak of restoring what sin destroyed. "They shall rebuild the old ruins... the desolations of many generations" (Isaiah 61:4). "Again I will build you, and you shall be rebuilt, O virgin of Israel! You shall again be adorned with your tambourines" (Jeremiah 31:4). God promises to restore joy along with walls.

Amos promises, "I will raise up the tabernacle of David, which has fallen down... and rebuild it as in the days of old" (Amos 9:11). He adds that this restoration will include "all the Gentiles who are called by My name" (9:12). At the Jerusalem council, James quotes this passage to show that Gentiles coming to Christ is God rebuilding David's fallen house (Acts 15:16–17).

Two prophecies bring the builder and the building together in one Person. Psalm 118:22 says, "The stone which the builders rejected has become the chief cornerstone." Israel's leaders, whose task was to build the nation's life, threw away the very stone God had chosen. Jesus applied this verse to Himself (Matthew 21:42), and Peter announced its fulfillment to the council: "This is the 'stone which was rejected by you builders'" (Acts 4:11).

Zechariah adds the other side: "Behold, the Man whose name is the BRANCH!... He shall build the temple of the LORD... So He shall be a priest on His throne" (Zechariah 6:12–13). The Messiah is both the stone rejected by human builders and the One who builds God's true temple. Jesus spoke of "the temple of His body," destroyed and raised in three days (John 2:19–21). His people are now "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ Himself being the chief cornerstone... being built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:20–22).

God's promise to build David a house, and Solomon's house for God's name, both come together in Christ. Abraham's tent-dwelling faith looked ahead to the city whose builder and maker is God (Hebrews 11:10).

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 127:1 does not tell believers to stop working. The builders still lay bricks, and the watchman still keeps watch. The psalm tells them where results come from. Much anxious effort in Christian life is an attempt to secure our own name and safety, the same project as Babel. The patriarchs give the alternative. They built altars first and let God decide what else would stand.

The unhewn altar applies directly to the way we approach God. We cannot improve the ground of our acceptance with Him. The sacrifice of Christ needs no decoration from human cleverness, and trying to add to it spoils it.

David's story offers another kind of freedom. Sometimes God declines what we offer to build for Him and then promises to build something in us. The prophets add hope for anyone whose life, family, or church lies in ruins. God's way is to rebuild what was ruined, not to abandon it, and to bring back the tambourines along with the walls.

Nehemiah's builders show what this work requires in practice. Restoring prayer, a marriage, or a congregation calls for steady labor and alert resistance against what would tear the work down. Christ is building His house. Those who build with Him need both the trowel and the sword.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 6:10 — God gives Israel great cities they did not build. The land itself was a gift of grace, and Israel was warned not to forget the Giver once settled in it.

  • Jeremiah 29:5 — God commands the exiles in Babylon to build houses and live in them. Even in judgment, He gives His people ordinary, faithful work to do.

  • Isaiah 65:21–22 — In the new creation, God's people will build houses and live in them, and others will not take them over. The old warnings of Deuteronomy 28:30 are reversed.

  • Micah 3:10 — Jerusalem's rulers "build up Zion with bloodshed and Jerusalem with iniquity." Building for God's city with unjust means brings judgment.

  • Haggai 1:8 — The returned exiles are told to bring wood and build the temple. They had been building their own houses while God's house lay in ruins (1:4).

  • Ezekiel 13:10 — False prophets are like people building a flimsy wall and coating it with whitewash. Their reassuring words look solid but cannot survive God's storm.
