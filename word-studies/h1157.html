THROUGH, AROUND, BEHIND, FOR - (bᵉʻad, Strong's H1157)

Summary

The Hebrew word bᵉʻad is a preposition, a word that shows how one person or thing relates to another. In some passages it marks a physical position relative to a window, door, wall, hedge, or veil, so the NKJV renders it "through," "behind," "around," "over," or "upon." In others it names the person on whose behalf someone acts, especially in prayer and atonement, and the NKJV renders it "for." Abraham will pray for Abimelech, and Aaron makes atonement for himself. Because English needs so many different words to carry this one Hebrew word, readers rarely notice it. Yet the word repays attention: it shows who is being prayed for, who is shielded, and who stands before God on someone else's behalf.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Bᵉʻad links an action to a person or object by describing how the two stand in relation. In physical scenes it usually involves a barrier or an opening. Someone looks out through a window, a door closes behind someone, or a hedge surrounds a household. In passages about prayer and worship, the word identifies who benefits from what is done before God. The English rendering depends heavily on the verb beside it. Hebrew uses one word where English must choose between "through," "around," and "for," so the translation choice reflects English idiom as much as any change in the Hebrew.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Through an opening ("through")

Isaac is seen when Abimelech looks out through a window (Genesis 26:8). Rahab lets the spies down by a rope through her window (Joshua 2:15). In both scenes the word places the action at the window. The opening is where seeing or escaping happens.

Enclosing, covering, or closing off ("around," "behind," "over," "upon")

Satan's complaint in Job 1:10 uses the word three times: "Have You not made a hedge around him, around his household, and around all that he has on every side?" The repetition shows that nothing belonging to Job lies outside God's protection. In the Song of Solomon the bride has "dove's eyes behind your veil" (Song of Songs 4:1; compare 4:3; 6:7), where the veil partly hides what lies beneath it. Elsewhere a door is shut behind the widow and her sons (2 Kings 4:5) and upon the dead boy (2 Kings 4:21), and the fat closes over Ehud's blade (Judges 3:22). David also uses the word when he says that even the night becomes light about him as darkness threatens to fall (Psalm 139:11).

On behalf of someone ("for")

God tells Abimelech that Abraham is a prophet who will pray for him (Genesis 20:7). Pharaoh asks Moses to intercede for him (Exodus 8:28), and Zedekiah's messengers ask Jeremiah to inquire of the LORD for them (Jeremiah 21:2). On the Day of Atonement, Aaron first makes atonement for himself and his house (Leviticus 16:6). In each case bᵉʻad names the person whose need is brought before God. The same word can mark what the action addresses: Moses hopes to make atonement for the people's sin (Exodus 32:30).

A rarer and debated use appears in Proverbs 6:26: "For by means of a harlot A man is reduced to a crust of bread." Here the word expresses cause or price. The verse traces how a man comes to ruin, so it is not describing an act on anyone's behalf.

3. Practical Reading Insight

The "for" passages place real people side by side before God. Abraham, whose half-truth endangered Abimelech's household, is the one who must pray for him (Genesis 20:7). Pharaoh, who repeatedly hardened his heart, still depends on Moses to bring his request to God. The preposition makes these relationships visible. One person stands before God with another person's name.

Leviticus 16:6 adds a sobering detail. Aaron cannot bring atonement for Israel until atonement has first been made for himself. The high priest needed the same mercy he carried to others. Hebrews 7:27 draws on this very pattern when it contrasts earthly high priests, who offered first for their own sins, with Christ, who had no such need.

Jeremiah 7:16 shows the word in a hard setting. God tells Jeremiah not to pray for the people of Judah. Intercession does not override the Lord's judgment, and that command shows how far Judah's rebellion had gone.

4. Reading Guidance

The word's physical uses, such as the hedge around Job, do not prove that every prayer "for" someone builds a protective covering around that person. Each sense of bᵉʻad should be read from its own verse. Remember also that English "for" often renders other Hebrew words, so the presence of "for" in a verse does not show that bᵉʻad is there.
