BEOR - (Bᵉʻôwr, Strong's H1160)

Summary

Beor is the name of two men in the Old Testament, and neither one ever speaks or acts in the biblical story. Each appears only as a father. One Beor was the father of Bela, the first king listed as reigning in Edom (Genesis 36:32; 1 Chronicles 1:43). The other was the father of Balaam, the diviner from Pethor near the Euphrates whom Balak king of Moab hired to curse Israel (Numbers 22:5). Scripture gives no reason to treat the two fathers as the same man. The name is usually connected to the Hebrew root ba'ar, "to burn," so it most likely means "burning" or "a torch." That meaning is probable rather than certain, and the Bible itself never comments on it.

Beor's importance lies in the phrase "Balaam the son of Beor," which follows Balaam from the Moabite crisis in Numbers through Deuteronomy, Joshua, and Micah and on into 2 Peter. The phrase fixes Israel's memory on a specific man and a specific moment in history. A famous seer was paid to curse God's people, and the LORD would not allow it. "Remember," God says through Micah, "what Balaam the son of Beor answered him... that you may know the righteousness of the LORD" (Micah 6:5). The Lord's protection of His people stayed in their remembered history, and in the New Testament the same name becomes a warning against spiritual gifts put up for sale (2 Peter 2:15).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew lemma Bᵉʻôwr names two separate individuals:

  • Beor, father of Balaam, connected with Pethor, "which is near the River" (Numbers 22:5), meaning the Euphrates. Deuteronomy 23:4 locates Pethor in Mesopotamia. All of the lemma's occurrences in Numbers, Deuteronomy, Joshua, and Micah refer to him.

  • Beor, father of Bela, the first Edomite king named in the king list of Genesis 36:31–39, repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:43.

These are two people from two regions: Edom lies south and southeast of the Dead Sea, while Pethor lies far to the northeast in Mesopotamia. The text links them by nothing except the shared name. Some readers have noticed that Bela and Balaam are both non-Israelites with similar-sounding names and have wondered whether the families were related, but Scripture gives no basis for that idea. Balaam's opposition to Israel is explicit; the king list says nothing about Bela's dealings with Israel. The list names several cities, Bela's being Dinhabah, and it never connects Bela with Pethor or with Balaam.

The name probably comes from ba'ar, a common Hebrew verb meaning "to burn," and so would mean something like "burning" or "torch." The same root can also mean "to consume" or "to clear away," and some have rendered the name "destroyer." That rendering stretches the evidence further than "burning" does. The Bible makes no wordplay on Beor's name, and the name says nothing about the character of either man.

In 2 Peter 2:15 some Greek editions give the father's name in a different form, Bosor (indexed separately as G1007), while others read Beor. The NKJV renders it "Beor," matching the Old Testament, and either way Peter means the same father.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Beor, father of Balaam. As Israel camped on the plains of Moab, Balak sent messengers "to Balaam the son of Beor at Pethor... to call him" (Numbers 22:5). He wanted Balaam to curse a people who seemed to "cover the face of the earth." Balaam's later oracles open by naming him formally as the son of Beor, the man whose eyes are opened (Numbers 24:3, 15). Yet every attempt at cursing became blessing. The fourth oracle looks ahead to a Star coming out of Jacob and a Scepter rising from Israel, and it names Edom among the lands that would become his possession (Numbers 24:17–18).

The full name returns at Balaam's end. Israel killed "Balaam the son of Beor" with the sword alongside the kings of Midian (Numbers 31:8), and Joshua 13:22 records the same event, calling him the soothsayer. Numbers 31:16 explains why. After failing to curse Israel, Balaam had counseled the Midianites to draw Israel into sin at Peor.

After that, Israel told the story in the father's name. Deuteronomy 23:4–5 bars Ammonites and Moabites from the assembly partly because they hired Balaam the son of Beor against Israel, and it adds that the LORD turned the curse into a blessing because He loved His people. Joshua's farewell recounts how Balak called Balaam the son of Beor to curse Israel, and how God would not listen to Balaam, so that he kept on blessing them (Joshua 24:9–10). Centuries later, Micah calls Israel to remember the episode as one of God's saving acts (Micah 6:5).

Outside the Bible, an inscription found at Deir Alla in Jordan, usually dated several centuries after the events of Numbers, refers to a "Balaam son of Beor" as a seer of the gods. It shows that the name and its patronymic were remembered in the region beyond Israel.

Beor, father of Bela. This Beor appears only in a royal list. "Bela the son of Beor reigned in Edom, and the name of his city was Dinhabah" (Genesis 36:32). Genesis introduces the list with a note of time (Genesis 36:31), which the Chronicler repeats: these kings "reigned in the land of Edom before a king reigned over the children of Israel" (1 Chronicles 1:43). Nothing else is known of him.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture plainly states that Balaam the son of Beor was hired to curse Israel, that God refused to hear him, and that the curse became a blessing because the LORD loved His people (Deuteronomy 23:5; Joshua 24:10). The repeated full name keeps the episode tied to a real man and a datable event. Israel did not remember a vague danger. They remembered a named opponent and a named deliverance.

Micah shows how that memory was meant to work. In a passage where God brings His case against His people, He points them to what Balaam answered Balak so that they "may know the righteousness of the LORD" (Micah 6:5). The story of the son of Beor is presented as evidence of God's faithful dealings with Israel.

The father of Bela touches a quieter theme. Edom had kings while Israel waited for one. Balaam's oracle, spoken by the son of a different Beor, foresees a ruler from Jacob to whom Edom would belong (Numbers 24:17–18). Scripture does not draw a line between the two Beors, so this is an observation about how the two passages sit together in the canon, not a claim the text makes. Still, the juxtaposition fits the Bible's larger picture of Esau's line rising early and Israel's King coming later by God's appointment.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament carries the name forward in warning. Peter describes false teachers who have "gone astray, following the way of Balaam the son of Beor, who loved the wages of unrighteousness" (2 Peter 2:15). Jude 11 and Revelation 2:14 also refer to Balaam, though without naming his father. In these passages Balaam has become the pattern of someone who uses spiritual gifts for gain and leads God's people into compromise.

The Star and Scepter oracle (Numbers 24:17) was widely read as a promise of a coming King. Christians have long seen it fulfilled in Christ, who calls Himself "the Bright and Morning Star" (Revelation 22:16). The New Testament does not directly quote Numbers 24:17, so this is best understood as strong canonical development rather than an explicit citation. Even so, the irony is real and rooted in the text. A diviner hired to curse Israel was made to announce Israel's greatest King.

5. Spirit & Life

Beor himself gives us nothing to imitate. He is only a name. What the name preserves is a story about God. The LORD would not hear the hired curse, and He turned it into blessing "because the LORD your God loves you" (Deuteronomy 23:5). His people's safety rested on His love and His word, not on the silence of their enemies.

Micah's "remember now" still applies. God's people are strengthened when they recall specific acts of His faithfulness, with names and places attached, and let those memories teach them His righteousness.

Peter's use of the same name is a sober warning. Balaam truly heard from God and still loved "the wages of unrighteousness." Spiritual insight and a willingness to obey are two different things, and only one of them reflects a heart that belongs to God.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:32; 1 Chronicles 1:43 — Beor as father of Bela, Edom's first listed king, reigning before Israel had a king.

  • Numbers 22:5 — Balak summons Balaam the son of Beor from Pethor near the River.

  • Numbers 24:3, 15 — Balaam's oracles open with his formal patronymic.

  • Numbers 24:17–18 — The Star and Scepter from Jacob, with Edom as his possession.

  • Numbers 31:8, 16; Joshua 13:22 — Balaam's death and the counsel that led Israel to sin at Peor.

  • Deuteronomy 23:4–5 — The hiring of Balaam and God's love turning the curse into blessing.

  • Joshua 24:9–10 — God would not listen to Balaam, and so he kept blessing Israel.

  • Micah 6:5 — Remembering the son of Beor in order to know the LORD's righteousness.

  • 2 Peter 2:15 — The New Testament warning about "the way of Balaam the son of Beor."
