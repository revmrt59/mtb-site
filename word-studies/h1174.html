BAAL-HAMON - (Baʻal Hâmôwn, Strong's H1174)

Summary

Baal-hamon is the place where King Solomon owned a vineyard, named near the close of the Song of Solomon (Song of Solomon 8:11). The name combines two Hebrew words. Baal means owner, master, lord or husband, and hamon means multitude, crowd or abundance. Taken together, the name most likely means lord of a multitude or owner of abundance. The verse uses the name only to locate Solomon's vineyard, not to speak of the Canaanite god Baal, whose title came from the same word. How this place first got its name remains uncertain. No one knows where the place was, and some readers think the poet chose or coined the name mainly for its meaning.

The vineyard appears in a single verse, but that verse matters. Solomon leased his vineyard to keepers, and each one owed him "a thousand silver coins" for its fruit (Song of Solomon 8:11). The next verse, most naturally in the bride's voice, sets this huge estate beside her own vineyard, meaning her own person and love. That vineyard is right in front of her, and it is not for rent. Solomon is welcome to his thousand. The scene puts into a picture what the Song has just said outright: love cannot be bought, even by the wealthiest king in Israel (Song of Solomon 8:7). Later Scripture takes up the image of a vineyard owner who expects its fruit. In Isaiah's song of the vineyard, God Himself plants and tends the vineyard and looks for good grapes (Isaiah 5:1–7). Jesus draws on Isaiah's picture in His parable of the vinedressers and adds tenants who lease the vineyard. In that parable the owner's son comes in person, and the tenants kill him (Matthew 21:33–41). Read alongside these passages, Baal-hamon reminds readers that God wants a people who give themselves to Him, not a delivery of produce from tenants at a distance.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

Baal-hamon is a named location, and Scripture connects it with only one thing: a vineyard owned by Solomon. The NKJV spells it "Baal Hamon" and mentions it only in Song of Solomon 8:11. No other person or place in Scripture carries this name. Some historical geographers have connected it with a town called Balamon near Dothan, mentioned outside the Bible in the book of Judith. Others connect it with Baal-gad. Both suggestions are guesses. The Bible gives no location, and there is no archaeological confirmation of the estate.

The name joins two common Hebrew words. Baal is an everyday noun for an owner, master, lord, or husband. The Canaanite god Baal took his title from this same word, and some place names containing baal may once have honored that god. Others may use the word simply as a title. We do not know which is true of Baal-hamon. Song of Solomon 8:11 uses the name only to say where Solomon's vineyard was. Hamon refers to a multitude, a noisy crowd, or an abundance of wealth. The most likely meaning of the name is therefore lord of a multitude or owner of abundance. The meanings of the two parts are clear. We cannot know whether the meaning of the whole name described a real town's history or was chosen by the poet for effect.

Whichever is true, the name fits its setting closely. In the Song's closing lines, the owner of abundance is the king who owns more than he can tend. This is wordplay drawn from the context. The verse itself does not explain the name.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

The compound name appears once, in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Song of Solomon 8:11 gives the only details we have: "Solomon had a vineyard at Baal Hamon; He leased the vineyard to keepers; Everyone was to bring for its fruit A thousand silver coins." The estate was large enough that Solomon worked it through tenants. Each tenant owed a thousand pieces of silver. Isaiah later used the same figure for a valuable vineyard, describing land with a thousand vines worth a thousand shekels of silver (Isaiah 7:23). The amount points to wealth on a royal scale, the kind of prosperity described in Solomon's reign (1 Kings 10:14–27).

Verse 12 follows immediately and gives verse 11 its point. The speaker, most naturally the bride, says that her own vineyard is in front of her. Solomon may keep his thousand, and the keepers may have their two hundred for their work (Song of Solomon 8:12, paraphrased). The bride's vineyard has been a theme since the opening of the Song. There she admitted that while others made her keep their vineyards, she had not kept her own (Song of Solomon 1:6). By the end her own vineyard is before her, hers to give. The book moves from a neglected vineyard to a vineyard that is hers to give, and Baal-hamon is the backdrop for that final contrast.

Just before this, the Song states its central claim plainly. Love is as strong as death, many waters cannot quench it, and anyone who offered all the wealth of his house for love would be utterly scorned (Song of Solomon 8:6–7). Baal-hamon then shows the point in a picture. Solomon's vineyard runs on contracts and payments. The bride's love is not part of that system.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states the facts plainly: Solomon owned the vineyard, leased it out, and was owed a fixed sum for its fruit. It also states that love cannot be purchased (Song of Solomon 8:7). Placing Baal-hamon next to the bride's vineyard in 8:12 makes the comparison obvious. The estate stands for ownership from a distance, care handed off to others, and value measured in silver. The bride offers presence, attention, and a gift no one could buy.

A further implication fits the larger story of Solomon, although the Song does not spell it out. God's law warned that Israel's king must not multiply horses, wives, or silver and gold, because these would turn his heart away (Deuteronomy 17:16–17). Solomon did all three. In the end he had seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines, and they turned his heart after other gods (1 Kings 11:1–4). Elsewhere the Song pictures sixty queens, eighty concubines, and countless maidens, and then the beloved who is "one" (Song of Solomon 6:8–9). Read against this history, the name lord of a multitude carries an irony. Because baal can also mean husband, the title could even be heard as husband of a multitude. Solomon came to own a multitude in land, silver, and wives, yet the Song ends by honoring the single, faithful love that his multitude could not supply. This reading is a thoughtful inference, not a stated claim of the text. Still, it matches what Scripture records about where Solomon's accumulation led.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

No New Testament passage mentions Baal-hamon. The picture of a lord who owns a vineyard, entrusts it to keepers, and expects its fruit does run through Scripture, however, and it reaches its fullest meaning in Christ.

Isaiah sings of his Well-beloved's vineyard on a fruitful hill. God planted it with care and looked for good grapes, but it produced only wild grapes. Isaiah explains that the vineyard is the house of Israel (Isaiah 5:1–7). Isaiah's vineyard has no tenants; God tends it Himself. Jesus draws directly on Isaiah's picture in His parable and adds tenants: "There was a certain landowner who planted a vineyard and set a hedge around it, dug a winepress in it and built a tower. And he leased it to vinedressers and went into a far country" (Matthew 21:33). At harvest he sent servants "that they might receive its fruit" (Matthew 21:34). The tenants beat, killed, and stoned them. Then "last of all he sent his son to them, saying, 'They will respect my son'" (Matthew 21:37). Instead they said, "This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and seize his inheritance" (Matthew 21:38). Mark and Luke tell the same parable (Mark 12:1–9; Luke 20:9–16).

The parallels to Song of Solomon 8:11 are structural, not a matter of direct quotation. Both passages have an owner, a leased vineyard, keepers, and an expected return of fruit. The meaning is reversed, though. In the Song, Solomon's keepers owe their silver, and the estate serves as a contrast to personal love. In the parable, the vineyard is God's own, the tenants refuse Him what He is owed, and the owner does not stay away. He sends His beloved Son into the vineyard, where He is killed and cast out (Mark 12:6–8). Solomon expected his silver through others. God came in the person of His Son.

Older Christian interpreters often read Solomon at Baal-hamon as a picture of Christ. In that reading the keepers are ministers of the church who must give account and keep two hundred as their wages. The image of faithful stewards giving account is biblical. In the Song itself, however, Baal-hamon is the contrast to the bride's love rather than the model for it, so that allegory sits uneasily with the passage's own direction.

A further thematic echo comes from Hosea. God promised that His people would one day call Him "My Husband" rather than "My Master," using a word other than baali (Hosea 2:16, paraphrased). The ordinary word baal behind Baal-hamon is the same word God sets aside there. Hosea is not commenting on the Song, but both passages show that God wants His people joined to Him in love, not tied to Him only as tenants to a landlord.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Baal-hamon can illustrate how love is counterfeited. A vineyard can be productive and profitable, with every payment made on time, while the owner never walks its rows. Religious life can look the same. Duties get done and offerings get given, all at a distance, as though God were a landlord collecting rent. The bride chooses differently. Her vineyard is right in front of her, and she gives it herself. God told Israel through Hosea that He desired mercy and the knowledge of God more than sacrifices and burnt offerings (Hosea 6:6). He has always wanted the person and not only the payment.

Read with Solomon's history, the passage also warns against quietly trusting in abundance. Solomon's multitude of silver, land, and wives looked like blessing, yet his wives turned his heart after other gods (1 Kings 11:4). A crowded life, a busy ministry, or a growing estate does not measure faithfulness. The Song honors the one over the many.

Stewardship still matters. Even at Baal-hamon, keepers had to tend the vines and bring the fruit, and Jesus' parable presses this point with real seriousness: the owner will come for his fruit (Matthew 21:40–41). The gospel adds what the Song could not. The Owner of the vineyard sent His Son, and the Son gave Himself. People who know that kind of love are drawn to give themselves to Him freely and gladly rather than paying grudging rent.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Song of Solomon 8:11 — The only mention of Baal-hamon. Solomon leases his vineyard to keepers for a thousand silver coins each.

  • Song of Solomon 8:12 — The bride sets her own vineyard against Solomon's estate. This contrast gives Baal-hamon its meaning in the Song.

  • Song of Solomon 8:6–7 — Love is as strong as death and cannot be bought, the claim that Baal-hamon illustrates.

  • Song of Solomon 1:6; 6:8–9 — The bride's once-neglected vineyard, and the one beloved set against a multitude of queens and concubines.

  • Deuteronomy 17:16–17; 1 Kings 11:1–4 — The warning against a king's multiplying wealth and wives, and Solomon's fall. These passages give the background for the irony in the name.

  • Isaiah 5:1–7 — God's vineyard, Israel, from which He looked for good fruit.

  • Matthew 21:33–41; Mark 12:1–9; Luke 20:9–16 — Jesus' parable of the leased vineyard and the son whom the tenants killed.

  • Hosea 2:16; 6:6 — God's desire to be known as Husband rather than Master, and His preference for steadfast love over ritual.
