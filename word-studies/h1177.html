BAAL-HANAN - (Baʻal Chânân, Strong's H1177)

Summary

Baal-hanan is the name of two different men in the Old Testament, and they should not be confused. The first was an early king of Edom, "the son of Achbor," who reigned after a king named Saul (not Israel's Saul) and before Hadar (Genesis 36:38–39; 1 Chronicles 1:49–50). The second was Baal-hanan the Gederite, one of King David's royal stewards, who "was over the olive trees and the sycamore trees that were in the lowlands" (1 Chronicles 27:28). The name means "Baal is gracious" or "the lord is gracious." It joins baʻal ("lord, master") to a form of the Hebrew verb chanan, "to be gracious."

Scripture tells us almost nothing about either man's personal life, but each one sits in a meaningful place in the biblical story. The Edomite king belongs to a list that Moses introduces with a pointed remark: these kings reigned in Edom "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). Esau's family had kings long before Jacob's did, yet God's promise ran through Jacob. The Davidic steward belongs to the years when that promised kingdom finally stood, organized and fruitful. His work over olive groves and fig orchards shows God's provision for His people being cared for under royal administration.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Two separate individuals bear this name. Baal-hanan son of Achbor was a king of Edom in the period before Israel had a monarchy (Genesis 36:38–39; 1 Chronicles 1:49–50). Chronicles repeats the Genesis list almost exactly. Baal-hanan the Gederite served in David's administration later (1 Chronicles 27:28). Nothing links the two men except the name.

The name means "Baal is gracious" or "the lord is gracious." Baʻal means "lord, master, owner," and chanan means "to be gracious" or "to show favor." In Canaanite religion Baal became the name of a storm god. In earlier Israelite usage, though, the word could also serve as an ordinary title for "lord." Hosea later looks forward to a day when Israel would no longer call the LORD "My Master" (Baali) and would remove the names of the Baals from her mouth (Hosea 2:16–17). That verse suggests the word had once been used in ways the prophets came to reject. We cannot tell which "lord" either man's parents had in mind. For the Edomite, a local deity or a general title is likely. For David's official, the name may reflect older usage or a non-Israelite background.

"Gederite" points to a hometown called Geder or Gederah. A Gederah lay in the lowland of Judah (Joshua 15:33, 36), which fits his assignment, but the exact place is uncertain.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The king of Edom. Baal-hanan followed Saul of Rehoboth-by-the-River (Genesis 36:37–38). When Baal-hanan died, "Hadar reigned in his place" (Genesis 36:39). Chronicles spells the successor's name Hadad (1 Chronicles 1:50). Unlike several kings on the list, Baal-hanan has no royal city named. The city Pau in verse 39 belongs to Hadar. Where the list names fathers and cities, they vary from king to king, though several fathers and cities are not given. This pattern suggests that Edom's kingship did not pass from father to son.

David's steward. First Chronicles 27:25–31 lists the men who were "over King David's property": treasuries, fields, vineyards, wine, herds, and flocks. Baal-hanan oversaw the olive trees and sycamore-fig trees of the lowlands, the Shephelah foothills toward Philistia. Joash, named in the same verse, kept the store of oil. Olives supplied food, light, trade, and anointing oil. The sycamore-fig provided fruit and common building timber. The region's fig trees became proverbial: under Solomon, cedars became "as abundant as the sycamores which are in the lowland" (1 Kings 10:27, paraphrased).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Genesis 36:31 is the key to the first Baal-hanan. Scripture states plainly that Edom had kings before Israel did. Esau's line, which received a lesser blessing (Genesis 27:39–40), had kings earlier. Jacob's line passed through slavery, the wilderness, and the judges before receiving a king. God had said, "the older shall serve the younger" (Genesis 25:23). Balaam foresaw that Edom would become a possession (Numbers 24:18), and under David it did (2 Samuel 8:14). Scripture does not make this contrast explicit, but the arc is easy to trace: the kingdom that arrived first did not hold the promise.

The second Baal-hanan shows that same promised kingdom in operation. Scripture states only his job. We can reasonably infer that David's reign had brought enough peace and order for the land's produce to be managed carefully. Whether Baal-hanan was a non-Israelite brought into royal service, like Uriah the Hittite or Ittai the Gittite, remains possible but unproven.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Neither man is mentioned again in Scripture, and no direct link to Christ is established. The broader theme they touch still matters. Genesis 36:31 looks ahead to a king in Israel. That hope ran through David's house and ultimately to the Son of David, whose kingdom will have no end (Luke 1:32–33).

5. Spirit & Life

Edom's early kings remind us that an earlier rise to power is no sign of God's favor. God's people often wait while others prosper. The LORD kept His word to Jacob in His own time, and His timing proved better than Edom's head start.

Baal-hanan the Gederite reminds us that God's kingdom was sustained by more than generals and priests. Someone had to tend the olive trees. Scripture thought that work worth recording by name. Faithful care of ordinary things belongs to the service of the King.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:31 – Edom's kings reigned before Israel had a king.

  • Genesis 36:38–39; 1 Chronicles 1:49–50 – Baal-hanan son of Achbor, king of Edom, and his succession.

  • 1 Chronicles 27:28 – Baal-hanan the Gederite, steward of the lowland olive and sycamore trees.

  • Genesis 25:23; 2 Samuel 8:14 – The promise that the older would serve the younger, fulfilled under David.

  • Hosea 2:16–17 – The prophetic rejection of "Baal" language.
