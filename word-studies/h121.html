ADAM - (ʼdâm, Strong's H0121)

Summary

Adam was the first man, whom God formed from the dust of the ground and from whom the whole human family descends. The name is the ordinary Hebrew word for "man" or "humankind." Genesis ties it to ʼadamah, "ground," because God formed the man from the ground, and many readers also link it to the Hebrew root for "red," perhaps the color of earth or of human skin. The connection to the ground is a deliberate wordplay in Scripture. The precise linguistic derivation is less certain.

Scripture presents Adam as the man who received God's breath, God's presence, and God's commission, and who brought sin and death into the world by his disobedience. Scripture calls him "a type of Him who was to come" (Romans 5:14). Where the first Adam brought death, the "last Adam," Jesus Christ, brings righteousness and resurrection life (1 Corinthians 15:45). The town of Adam has a separate history. It marks the place where God stopped a flooded river so that His people could cross into their inheritance (Joshua 3:16).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Adam the man and Adam the town. Scripture uses the name Adam for both the first man and a town beside the Jordan. In 1 Chronicles 1:1 Adam is the first human being, the father of Seth and the head of every genealogy that follows. In Joshua 3:16 Adam is a town, "the city that is beside Zaretan," upstream from where Israel crossed the Jordan opposite Jericho. Scripture never connects the town's name with the first man, and the two should not be merged historically.

Why the count is only two. Strong's numbering separates the Hebrew word ʼadam into two entries. H0120 covers the common noun "man, mankind," and that entry also includes the Genesis passages where the word functions as the first man's name. H0121 is reserved for occurrences treated as strictly proper names. As a result, the familiar story of Adam in Genesis 1–5 does not appear in the H0121 count, and neither do the New Testament references (Luke 3:38; Romans 5:14; 1 Corinthians 15:22, 45; 1 Timothy 2:13–14; Jude 14), which use Greek G76. This study draws on those passages because the person cannot be understood without them. They are related entries and are not counted as occurrences of H0121.

The name's meaning. ʼAdam is the common Hebrew word for "man" or "humanity," so the first man's name is simply "Man," or "Human." Genesis connects the name to ʼadamah, "ground": "the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground" (Genesis 2:7, paraphrased). The same wordplay returns at the Fall, when the ground is cursed because of the man and he is told he will return to it (Genesis 3:17–19). Many interpreters also associate the word with the Hebrew root for "red" (ʼadom), possibly referring to reddish earth or to the color of human flesh. The wordplay with "ground" is plainly part of the text. The exact derivation behind both words is a matter of scholarly discussion and should be held more loosely. For the town, the name is the same word, and Scripture gives no explanation of why it was so called.

The town's location. Joshua places Adam beside Zaretan, far enough upstream that the text calls it "very far away" from the crossing point near Jericho (Joshua 3:16). It is commonly identified with Tell ed-Damiyeh in the central Jordan Valley, roughly eighteen miles north of Jericho, though that identification comes from geography and scholarship and is not stated in Scripture.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Adam, the First Man

Created and commissioned. Genesis presents humanity as the crown of creation. God created man "in His own image" and gave them dominion over the earth (Genesis 1:26–28). Genesis 2 then focuses on the man himself: God formed him from the dust, breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and he became a living being (Genesis 2:7). God placed him in Eden "to tend and keep" the garden (Genesis 2:15). Later Scripture uses the same pair of verbs for the Levites' service at the tabernacle, which suggests that Adam's work had a priestly dimension of guarding the place where God was present. Adam named the animals, showing that God had given him real authority (Genesis 2:19–20). Because no suitable helper was found among the animals, God made the woman from the man's side, and the man received her with joy (Genesis 2:21–23).

Life in God's presence. Eden was more than a farm. God spoke with the man directly and walked in the garden (Genesis 3:8). Adam lived with unhindered access to God, with abundant provision, and with one clear command: he was not to eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, "for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die" (Genesis 2:16–17, paraphrased).

The Fall. When the serpent deceived the woman, Adam, who was with her, also ate (Genesis 3:6). The result was immediate. Their eyes were opened, shame entered, and the man who had walked with God now hid "from the presence of the LORD God" (Genesis 3:8–10). God's questioning brought out blame-shifting rather than confession (Genesis 3:12). Judgment followed. The ground was cursed, work became toil, and the man was told he would return to the dust he came from (Genesis 3:17–19). Mercy came with the judgment, though. God promised that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15), and He clothed Adam and Eve with tunics of skin (Genesis 3:21). Adam named his wife Eve, "the mother of all living" (Genesis 3:20). He named her that after hearing the sentence of death, which reads as an act of faith in God's promise of life.

Exile and legacy. God drove the man out of the garden and stationed cherubim at its east side to guard the way to the tree of life (Genesis 3:22–24). Outside Eden the sorrow multiplied. Cain murdered Abel, and God then gave Adam and Eve another son, Seth (Genesis 4:25). Genesis 5 opens "the book of the genealogy of Adam." It recalls that God made man in His likeness, notes that Adam fathered Seth "in his own likeness, after his image," and ends Adam's 930 years with the refrain that echoes through the chapter: "and he died" (Genesis 5:1–5, paraphrased).

Adam in later Scripture. First Chronicles opens with the bare words "Adam, Seth, Enosh" (1 Chronicles 1:1). The Chronicler wrote for a community rebuilding after the Babylonian exile. Before telling Israel's story through Abraham, David, and the temple, he goes back to the first man. Hosea 6:7 may refer to Adam as well: "But like men they transgressed the covenant." The Hebrew can be read "like Adam," and some translations render it that way, but the NKJV's "like men" is also a legitimate reading. In the New Testament, Luke traces Jesus' ancestry past Abraham all the way to "Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38). Matthew, by contrast, begins his genealogy with Abraham. Paul builds his explanation of sin, death, and salvation on Adam (Romans 5:12–21; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49) and appeals to the order of creation and deception in Eden (1 Timothy 2:13–14). Jude identifies Enoch as "the seventh from Adam" (Jude 14).

Adam, the Town by the Jordan

Joshua 3 describes Israel camped on the east bank, ready to enter Canaan at the worst possible time, "for the Jordan overflows all its banks during the whole time of harvest" (Joshua 3:15). God told the priests to carry the ark ahead of the people. The moment their feet touched the water, the river stopped: "the waters which came down from upstream stood still, and rose in a heap very far away at Adam, the city that is beside Zaretan. So the waters that went down into the Sea of the Arabah, the Salt Sea, failed, and were cut off; and the people crossed over opposite Jericho" (Joshua 3:16). The priests "stood firm on dry ground in the midst of the Jordan" until the whole nation had crossed (Joshua 3:17).

The town of Adam marks how far upstream the river was held back, which shows that the miracle covered a long stretch of the river. Scripture presents the event as God's direct act. The water stopped exactly when the priests' feet touched it, at flood season, and it stayed back until the last Israelite crossed. Joshua 4 adds that the crossing showed Israel and the nations that the hand of the LORD is mighty (Joshua 4:24, paraphrased). The town does not appear again in the narrative.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

What Scripture states. Adam is the source of the whole human family and its representative before God. Paul says it plainly: "through one man sin entered the world, and death through sin, and thus death spread to all men, because all sinned" (Romans 5:12). He adds that "by one man's disobedience many were made sinners" (Romans 5:19) and that "in Adam all die" (1 Corinthians 15:22). The death announced in Genesis 3 and repeated in Genesis 5 is not limited to Adam. It describes every one of his children, which means all of us. Scripture also states that Adam was "a type of Him who was to come" (Romans 5:14). His life was shaped from the beginning to point beyond himself to Christ.

What the genealogies imply. By beginning with Adam, both the Chronicler and Luke widen the frame. Israel's history, with its promises to Abraham and David, is not a private religious story. It belongs to God's purpose for the whole human race that came from one man. For the returning exiles, opening with "Adam, Seth, Enosh" was a reminder that the God who began humanity had not abandoned His plan, and that Israel's calling was tied to the blessing of all nations. Luke draws out the same scope when he connects Jesus to "Adam, the son of God." This reading of the Chronicler's purpose is an inference from his structure. He does not explain it himself, but the structure supports it well.

What Joshua's crossing shows. The town of Adam is significant because of what God did there. It stands at the boundary between the wilderness years and life in the land God had promised. The river at flood stage was a real obstacle. God's presence, carried in the ark, went ahead of the people into the water, and the way opened. The scene shows that God kept His word to bring Israel into the inheritance He had sworn to their fathers.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Explicit New Testament teaching. Paul calls Adam "a type of Him who was to come" (Romans 5:14). He then shows that the comparison is mainly a contrast: "the free gift is not like the offense" (Romans 5:15). One man's offense brought condemnation and the reign of death. One Man's "righteous act" brings "justification of life" (Romans 5:18). Where sin abounded, "grace abounded much more, so that as sin reigned in death, even so grace might reign through righteousness to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord" (Romans 5:20–21). In 1 Corinthians 15 Paul carries the contrast into the resurrection: "For since by man came death, by Man also came the resurrection of the dead" (15:21). He sets two heads of humanity side by side: "The first man Adam became a living being. The last Adam became a life-giving spirit" (15:45). Adam received life from God's breath. Christ gives life. Adam hid from God's presence in a garden, while Christ obeyed in a garden and on a cross where Adam had disobeyed. Every person belongs to one of these two men, and Paul treats that as a matter of life and death.

Strongly supported canonical development. The promise of the woman's offspring who would crush the serpent (Genesis 3:15) runs through the line Chronicles and Luke both trace: Adam, Seth, Enosh, and on to Christ. Adam's exile from God's presence also opens a long biblical storyline. God dwells among His people in the tabernacle and temple, then in the Son who "dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and finally in the new creation, where the tree of life is open again (Revelation 22:2). Adam's story explains why humanity needs that restoration and why only God could accomplish it.

Thematic resonance at the town of Adam. Joshua does not say that the town's name means anything theologically. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, though, the scene invites reflection. The leader's name, Joshua, is the Hebrew form of the name Jesus, and Hebrews 4:8 uses the same Greek name for both. In Joshua 3 the ark of God's presence goes down into the water first. The river that flowed down into the Salt Sea, a sea of death later healed in Ezekiel's vision (Ezekiel 47:8–9), is cut off and held back all the way to a place called Adam. The people then cross on dry ground into their inheritance. Christians can hardly miss the echo. In Christ, God entered death ahead of His people, and the reign of death that began with Adam was broken so that we could pass from death to life. This is a canonical reflection, not something the author of Joshua declared. The shared name brings the two scenes into conversation. Within those limits it is a fitting picture of the gospel.

A related theme concerns direction. Adam was driven out with the way back guarded on the east (Genesis 3:24). Cain went out "on the east of Eden" (Genesis 4:16). Israel at the Jordan moved the other way, west across the river into the land where God would dwell among them. Scripture does not spell this pattern out, and it should be held lightly. It does fit the larger story of God bringing exiles home.

5. Spirit & Life

Adam's story tells us the truth about ourselves. We were made from dust and made for God. We carry His image and breathe because He gave us breath. We also share Adam's instinct to hide when we have sinned and to blame someone else when God asks what we have done. Genesis does not flatter humanity, and Romans does not let anyone stand outside Adam's family. That honesty is a mercy, because no one looks for a rescuer until they admit they need one.

God's response to Adam shows His heart. He came looking for the man who was hiding: "Where are you?" (Genesis 3:9). He judged sin truly, but before Adam left the garden God had already promised a Deliverer and clothed the guilty pair with His own provision. That pattern holds through all of Scripture. God's grace does not deny His judgment; it goes beyond it. Paul's phrase "much more" (Romans 5:15, 17) is the heart of the gospel. What we lost in Adam does not measure what we receive in Christ, because grace "abounded much more."

The Jordan crossing adds an encouragement for anyone facing an impossible obstacle. Israel had to step toward a river in flood with nothing but God's word and God's presence going ahead of them. The waters did not part while the people stood on the bank; they stopped when the priests stepped in. Faith still acts on what God has said. Behind that obedience stands a greater certainty: Christ has already gone into the deepest waters for us. Those who belong to the last Adam do not cross alone, and death no longer has the final word.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 1:26–28; 2:7, 15–23 (H0120 passages): Adam's creation in God's image, his formation from the ground (the source of the name's wordplay), and his calling to tend and keep the garden.

  • Genesis 3:1–24 (H0120): The Fall, hiding from God's presence, the curse on the ground, the promise of Genesis 3:15, and the exile from Eden.

  • Genesis 5:1–5 (H0120): Adam's genealogy, the image passed to Seth, and the refrain of death.

  • Joshua 3:14–17 (H0121 at 3:16): The town of Adam as the point where the Jordan's waters were held back for Israel's crossing.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:1 (H0121): Adam heads the Chronicler's genealogy, placing Israel's story within God's purpose for all humanity.

  • Hosea 6:7 (H0120): A possible comparison of Israel's unfaithfulness to Adam's transgression, depending on translation.

  • Luke 3:38 (G76): Jesus' genealogy traced to "Adam, the son of God," linking Him to all humanity.

  • Romans 5:12–21 (G76): Adam as the "type of Him who was to come" and the contrast between his offense and Christ's obedience.

  • 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45 (G76): Death in Adam, life in Christ, and the first and last Adam.

  • 1 Timothy 2:13–14; Jude 14 (G76): Later apostolic references to Adam's creation, the Fall, and the genealogical line.
