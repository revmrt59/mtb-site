RED, RUDDY - (ʼâdôm, Strong's H0122)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼâdôm simply means "red." Scripture uses it for a pot of food, a heifer's coat, water lit by the morning sun, the healthy glow of a young man's face, a warrior's stained clothing, and horses in a prophet's vision. Although it is an ordinary color word, it appears in several of the Bible's most memorable scenes, and it is often tied to blood, life, and death.

Esau trades his birthright for "that same red stew," and the color becomes his name, Edom. God commands a "red heifer without blemish" whose ashes cleanse His people from the defilement of death, a ritual Hebrews sets beside the blood of Christ. Isaiah sees the Lord coming from Edom in garments red with the blood of His enemies, an image Revelation applies to the returning Christ. Taken together, these red scenes warn against trading God's promises for a moment's appetite. They also point to a cleansing God Himself provides, and they announce a King who will judge evil and save His people.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼâdôm is a Hebrew adjective of color. Like many ancient color words, it probably covered a wider range than our "red." The lentil stew of Genesis 25 was likely reddish-brown, and the "ruddy" complexion of Song of Solomon 5:10 is a warm, healthy flush.

The word sounds like the name Edom, and the Bible makes deliberate use of that likeness. Esau, Jacob's brother and the ancestor of the Edomites, receives the name Edom because of his request for red food (Genesis 25:30). Centuries later, Isaiah sees the Lord coming "from Edom" in "red" garments (Isaiah 63:1–2). Hebrew words for "man" and "ground" sound similar, but the meaning of ʼâdôm should not be built on those resemblances. The wordplay Scripture actually draws is with Edom.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Red or reddish, describing the color of objects, animals, or substances. Examples include the stew in Genesis 25:30, the heifer in Numbers 19:2, the water in 2 Kings 3:22, the garments in Isaiah 63:2, and the horses in Zechariah 1:8.

2. Ruddy, describing a person's complexion. The word conveys a glow of health and vigor, as in "My beloved is white and ruddy" (Song of Solomon 5:10).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses are plain descriptions, but the setting gives the color its weight. In narrative passages, red is something people see and react to. Esau craves red food, and the Moabites misread red water. In the law, red is a requirement God sets for a sacrifice. In poetry it signals beauty, and in prophecy it marks judgment and the deployment of heaven's forces.

Genesis 25:30 uses a striking pattern. Esau does not name the food. He says, in effect, the red, this red, using the color word twice as a noun. The NKJV's "that same red stew" smooths the phrase, but the repetition shows a hungry man pointing at something he wants and grabbing for words.

Twice the color is tied directly to blood. In 2 Kings 3:22 the water looks "as red as blood," and in Isaiah 63 the red garments are explained by blood. Song of Solomon 5:10 shows that the word carries no built-in darkness. There the color is a picture of life and attractiveness.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show God treating blood with great seriousness. Blood stands for life poured out. It appears in the cleansing provided by the red heifer and in the judgment of the red garments. Leviticus 17:11 gives the principle behind both: "For the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood that makes atonement for the soul." Sin and death are never ignored in Scripture. They are dealt with either by a sacrifice God provides or by the judgment He carries out.

The red scenes also show God ruling over history. He turns the appearance of red water into a trap for Moab (2 Kings 3:22). He sends red horses throughout the earth (Zechariah 1:8; 6:2). In Isaiah 63 He treads the winepress of judgment alone. His enemies' plans and the movements of nations remain under His command.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 25:29–34 — The red that became a name. Esau comes in from the field weary and says, "Please feed me with that same red stew, for I am weary." The narrator adds at once, "Therefore his name was called Edom." Esau's birthright gave him the firstborn's place as heir of the promise God made to Abraham. Esau trades it away with a shrug: "Look, I am about to die; so what is this birthright to me?" He eats, drinks, rises, and goes his way, and the verdict follows: "Thus Esau despised his birthright." His name preserves the moment, so that a whole nation carries the memory of one meal. Hebrews 12:16 holds Esau up as a warning to the church: a "profane person like Esau, who for one morsel of food sold his birthright." Being profane in this sense means treating holy things as common. The red stew was not evil. Esau's sin lay in letting an immediate craving outweigh what God had promised.

Numbers 19:1–10 — The red heifer. God commands "a red heifer without blemish, in which there is no defect and on which a yoke has never come." It is slaughtered outside the camp and burned whole. Cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet are thrown into the fire (Numbers 19:6), and the ashes are stored "for the water of purification; it is for purifying from sin" (Numbers 19:9). This water cleansed anyone defiled by contact with a dead body. The color requirement is unusual among Israel's sacrifices. A reasonable reading is that an animal entirely red, the color of blood, fittingly answers the defilement of death, which is life's absence. Hebrews 9:13–14 builds directly on this ritual: if "the ashes of a heifer" purified the flesh, "how much more shall the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered Himself without spot to God, cleanse your conscience from dead works to serve the living God?" The heifer cleansed a body for worship, and Christ cleanses the conscience. Hebrews 13:11–12 also notes that Jesus, like the sacrifices burned outside the camp, "suffered outside the gate."

Isaiah 63:1–4 — The red garments of the Lord. Someone is seen approaching "from Edom, With dyed garments from Bozrah," Edom's chief city, and the question comes, "Why is Your apparel red, And Your garments like one who treads in the winepress?" The answer is sobering: "I have trodden the winepress alone… Their blood is sprinkled upon My garments, And I have stained all My robes." Edom, the people named for red, has met the Lord in red. The nation descended from the man who despised his birthright had become a byword for hostility to God's people (Obadiah 1:10). The same Warrior introduces Himself as "mighty to save," and His purpose is double: "the day of vengeance is in My heart, And the year of My redeemed has come." The Lord comes as Judge of evil and Rescuer of His people, and He acts with no human ally.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The red passages follow the whole movement of redemption. In Genesis, a man throws away God's promise for a meal. In Numbers, God provides cleansing from death through a spotless sacrifice burned outside the camp. Hebrews names Christ as the One whose blood accomplishes what the heifer's ashes only pictured.

Isaiah 63 reaches toward the end of the story. Revelation 19 presents the returning Christ "clothed with a robe dipped in blood" (Revelation 19:13), treading "the winepress of the fierceness and wrath of Almighty God" (Revelation 19:15). The robe is already dipped before the battle is described, and many readers have seen in it the blood of His own cross alongside the blood of judgment. That reading is not certain, but it fits the gospel: the One who judges is the One who first bled.

The timing is also significant. In Nazareth, Jesus read from Isaiah 61, a few chapters earlier, and stopped at "the acceptable year of the LORD" (Luke 4:19). He did not read the next line, "the day of vengeance of our God" (Isaiah 61:2). His first coming opened the year of the redeemed. The red garments of vengeance await His return.

The ruddy Beloved of Song of Solomon 5:10, "Chief among ten thousand," has long been read as a picture of Christ's matchless beauty. That application rests on Christian interpretation of the Song rather than on any direct statement. Even so, it reminds us that the One who is coming in judgment is the One His people find altogether worth loving.

7. Spirit & Life

Esau's red stew asks what we are willing to trade. His craving was ordinary hunger, and he said, "I am about to die." Pressure magnified the appetite until God's promise seemed worthless. Few believers sell their inheritance in a single dramatic act. More often it is surrendered a bowlful at a time, whenever the urgent wins over the eternal. Hebrews applies Esau's choice to Christians so that we will value what God has given us.

The red heifer answers the conscience that feels stained by "dead works." Under the old ritual, someone who touched death could not simply decide to be clean. God provided the ashes, and God provided the water. In the same way, Hebrews sends the troubled believer to the blood of Christ, which frees us "to serve the living God." Cleansing has a purpose, which is restored worship.

Isaiah 63 steadies us when evil seems unanswered. Zechariah's riders reported the nations resting at ease while Jerusalem lay in ruins (Zechariah 1:11–15), and God answered that He was zealous for His people. The Warrior who comes "mighty to save" will deal with evil Himself. Believers can leave vengeance to Him and keep their hands clean.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Kings 3:22 — "The Moabites saw the water on the other side as red as blood." God had filled the dry valley with water, which came "by way of Edom" (2 Kings 3:20), and the Moabites mistook the sunlit water for the blood of kings who had slaughtered one another. They rushed in to plunder and were routed, so their greed became the means of their defeat.

  • Zechariah 1:8 — "A man riding on a red horse… among the myrtle trees in the hollow." The riders patrol the earth at the Lord's command, and their report leads to God's declaration of zeal for Jerusalem.

  • Zechariah 6:2 — "With the first chariot were red horses." The chariots go out as the spirits of heaven sent from the Lord (Zechariah 6:5). Revelation's "fiery red" horse that takes "peace from the earth" (Revelation 6:4) uses a related image in Greek, showing red as a color of war under God's sovereign permission.

  • Song of Solomon 5:10 — "My beloved is white and ruddy." The bride praises a glowing, healthy complexion. A closely related Hebrew word describes young David as "ruddy" when God chose him (1 Samuel 16:12).
