BOZRAH - (Botsrâh, Strong's H1224)

Summary

Bozrah was a leading city of Edom, the nation descended from Esau, in the rugged highlands southeast of the Dead Sea. Its name comes from a Hebrew root that means to cut off, enclose, or make inaccessible, so Bozrah is generally understood to mean "enclosure," "fortress," or "fold." The name fits a stronghold that seemed out of reach. Bozrah first appears as the hometown of Jobab, one of the kings who ruled Edom before Israel had a king (Genesis 36:33; 1 Chronicles 1:44). In the prophets it becomes something more. Amos foresees fire devouring "the palaces of Bozrah" (Amos 1:12). Jeremiah pictures God swooping over it like an eagle and leaving it a desolation (Jeremiah 49:13, 22). Isaiah makes it the scene of the LORD's great judgment on His enemies: "the LORD has a sacrifice in Bozrah" (Isaiah 34:6).

The name's deepest use comes in Isaiah 63:1, where a lone, mighty figure strides out of Edom "with dyed garments from Bozrah." He has trodden the winepress of God's wrath alone, and He announces that "the year of My redeemed has come." Revelation 19 takes up this picture of blood-stained garments and the winepress and applies it to the returning Christ. One more use of the word stands in contrast. In Micah 2:12 the same Hebrew word, rendered "the fold" in the NKJV, describes God gathering His scattered people "like sheep of the fold." The proud fortress that could not escape God's judgment shares its name with the safe enclosure where the Shepherd keeps His flock.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Bozrah is a place name. In most of its occurrences it refers to a major city of Edom, the land of Esau's descendants south and east of the Dead Sea (Genesis 36:33; 1 Chronicles 1:44; Isaiah 34:6; 63:1; Jeremiah 49:13, 22; Amos 1:12). The Hebrew text names it alongside Edom and Teman, and the prophets treat it as a chief city, perhaps the capital, that stands for the nation's power. The site is commonly identified with modern Buseirah in southern Jordan, though the biblical text itself gives only its Edomite setting.

Two occurrences need separate handling. Jeremiah 48:24 lists Bozrah among "all the cities of the land of Moab," in an oracle against Moab rather than Edom. Scripture does not explain the connection. It may be a different town with the same name, a period when Moab controlled the Edomite city, or a form related to Bezer, a town in the Moabite region. The question remains open, and this verse should not be merged into Edom's story. Micah 2:12 is the second special case. Strong's indexes the word there under the same entry, but the NKJV translates it as a common noun, "the fold," while some translations keep the place name, "sheep of Bozrah."

The name comes from the Hebrew root batsar (Strong's H1219), which carries the ideas of cutting off, enclosing, or making inaccessible. From that root Bozrah means "enclosure," "fortress," or "sheepfold." The meaning is well supported, and it suits both a fortified mountain city and the "fold" of Micah 2:12. The same root is also used for gathering grapes at the vintage. That makes a wordplay with Isaiah 63 possible, since the figure coming from Bozrah has been treading the winepress. Isaiah does not use the root in that passage, so the link is a possible echo of sound and idea rather than a stated pun, and the Isaiah passage does not depend on it. It still deserves to be noticed.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

A royal city of Esau's descendants. Bozrah enters Scripture in the list of "the kings who reigned in the land of Edom before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). Jobab the son of Zerah "of Bozrah" succeeded Bela as king (Genesis 36:33), and Chronicles repeats the record (1 Chronicles 1:44). The detail is small, but it shows that Esau's line had organized power and royal cities before Israel had any king. Edom had an early start.

Fire on the palaces. In the eighth century B.C., Amos names the sin that brings judgment on Edom: "he pursued his brother with the sword, And cast off all pity; His anger tore perpetually, And he kept his wrath forever" (Amos 1:11). The brother was Israel, Jacob's family. God's answer follows: "But I will send a fire upon Teman, Which shall devour the palaces of Bozrah" (Amos 1:12). Edom's grudge was carried for generations, and the fortress where its rulers lived would not protect them. Obadiah does not name Bozrah, but he describes the same pride in the Edomites who lived in high, rocky places: "You who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?'" (Obadiah 1:3).

A sacrifice in Bozrah. Isaiah 34 opens by summoning all the nations to hear of the LORD's anger (Isaiah 34:1–2), and then focuses on Edom as the clearest example. "For My sword shall be bathed in heaven; Indeed it shall come down on Edom, And on the people of My curse, for judgment" (Isaiah 34:5). The next verse uses the language of the altar: "The sword of the LORD is filled with blood, It is made overflowing with fatness, With the blood of lambs and goats... For the LORD has a sacrifice in Bozrah, And a great slaughter in the land of Edom" (Isaiah 34:6). Isaiah explains the purpose: it is the LORD's day of vengeance and a year of recompense for the cause of Zion (Isaiah 34:8). Bozrah has become a stand-in for every power that sets itself against God and His people.

The Warrior from Bozrah. Isaiah 63 returns to the scene after the battle. A watchman asks, "Who is this who comes from Edom, With dyed garments from Bozrah, This One who is glorious in His apparel, Traveling in the greatness of His strength?" The answer comes back: "I who speak in righteousness, mighty to save" (Isaiah 63:1). The watchman asks why His clothes are red "like one who treads in the winepress" (63:2). The LORD answers that He has "trodden the winepress alone, And from the peoples no one was with Me," and that their blood has stained His robes (63:3). Then He gives His reason: "For the day of vengeance is in My heart, And the year of My redeemed has come" (63:4). No one stood with Him: "Therefore My own arm brought salvation for Me" (63:5). Right after this the prophet turns to remember "the lovingkindnesses of the LORD" toward Israel (Isaiah 63:7). The God who judges is also the God who saves and shows mercy.

Desolation in Jeremiah. About a century later Jeremiah's oracle against Edom repeats the theme. The LORD swears by Himself that Bozrah will become a desolation, a reproach, a waste, and a curse, and its cities perpetual ruins (Jeremiah 49:13). He pictures Himself rising like an eagle and spreading His wings over Bozrah, while the mighty men of Edom are as helpless as a woman in labor (Jeremiah 49:22). The separate mention in the Moab oracle, "On Kerioth and Bozrah, On all the cities of the land of Moab, Far or near" (Jeremiah 48:24), belongs to God's judgment on Moab and should be read in that setting.

The fold of the remnant. Micah 2:12 uses the word in a completely different way: "I will surely assemble all of you, O Jacob, I will surely gather the remnant of Israel; I will put them together like sheep of the fold, Like a flock in the midst of their pasture." The next verse shows the gathered flock led out by "the one who breaks open," with their King passing before them and the LORD at their head (Micah 2:13). Whether this verse names the city or simply means "fold," it gives the word a gentle and protective sense.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly why Bozrah was judged. Edom hunted his brother and refused to let go of his anger (Amos 1:11). The prophets connect Bozrah's fall to "the cause of Zion" (Isaiah 34:8) and to "the year of My redeemed" (Isaiah 63:4). God's judgment on Bozrah was not random anger. It was the other side of His faithfulness to Jacob's family. When He defended His people against a brother who wished them harm, He was keeping His promises.

The text also shows Bozrah being used for more than Edom. Isaiah 34 begins with the nations, and Isaiah 63 speaks of treading down "the peoples" (Isaiah 63:3, 6). Bozrah remains a real city, but the prophets let it stand for all organized hostility to God. A careful conclusion follows from this pattern. Bozrah's high walls and difficult approach are not the point of the prophecies. The point is that no height is beyond God's reach, as God's answer to Edom's boast declares and as Jeremiah's eagle shows (Obadiah 1:3–4; Jeremiah 49:22).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest canonical development is in Revelation 19. Revelation does not name Bozrah, but John's vision draws directly on Isaiah 63. The rider on the white horse "was clothed with a robe dipped in blood, and His name is called The Word of God" (Revelation 19:13). Most significantly, "He Himself treads the winepress of the fierceness and wrath of Almighty God" (Revelation 19:15). The lone Warrior of Isaiah, whose garments came red from Bozrah, is seen in Revelation as Jesus Christ returning to judge the nations and rule them. What Isaiah attributed to the LORD, John attributes to the Son.

The winepress is a wider biblical pattern. Joel calls for the sickle "for the harvest is ripe... the winepress is full, The vats overflow— For their wickedness is great" (Joel 3:13). Revelation 14 shows "the great winepress of the wrath of God" trampled outside the city (Revelation 14:19–20). Bozrah stands inside this line of imagery. The possible sound-link between its name and the grape harvest adds color, but the connection rests firmly on the winepress language itself.

Isaiah's emphasis on the Warrior acting alone also has a fitting resonance with the gospel. "I looked, but there was no one to help... Therefore My own arm brought salvation for Me" (Isaiah 63:5). Isaiah 59:16 uses nearly the same words about God's own arm bringing salvation when no intercessor could be found. In Isaiah 63 the blood belongs to God's enemies, and that should not be blurred. Still, Christian readers can rightly see that the One who will tread the winepress in judgment is the same One who first stood alone at the cross and bore sin for His people. Isaiah does not make this point, but the New Testament's picture of Christ's solitary saving work leads naturally to it.

5. Spirit & Life

Bozrah tells the truth about human security. Edom trusted its heights and nursed a grudge against its brother, and God named both sins. The fortress was real and its walls were strong, Edom said in its heart, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (Obadiah 1:3), and God answered, "From there I will bring you down" (Obadiah 1:4). That answer still stands over every proud heart. The safety we build for ourselves cannot shelter us from the God who sees.

Isaiah 63 also shows that God's judgment and God's rescue come from the same heart. The Warrior speaks "in righteousness," yet He calls Himself "mighty to save" (Isaiah 63:1). The day of vengeance and the year of His redeemed arrive together (63:4). For those who belong to Him, His fierce strength is not a threat. It is the reason they can be sure evil will not have the last word.

Read side by side, the two uses of the word invite a comparison, though Micah does not draw it himself. For proud Edom, Bozrah was an enclosure that became a trap. For the remnant of Jacob, the "fold" in Micah 2:12 is an enclosure that keeps them safe, with their King walking ahead of them (Micah 2:13). The difference is not the strength of the walls but who is inside them and who stands guard. Jesus said He is the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11). The safest place in the world is inside His fold.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:31–33; 1 Chronicles 1:44 — Bozrah as the city of Jobab, an early king of Edom before Israel had a king.

  • Amos 1:11–12 — Edom's merciless hatred of his brother and the promise of fire on Bozrah's palaces.

  • Obadiah 1:3 — Edom's pride in its high, rocky dwelling places (Bozrah is not named, but the setting matches).

  • Isaiah 34:5–8 — The LORD's "sacrifice in Bozrah," tied to vengeance for the cause of Zion.

  • Isaiah 63:1–6 — The Warrior from Bozrah who trod the winepress alone; the day of vengeance and the year of the redeemed.

  • Jeremiah 49:13, 22 — Bozrah's coming desolation and God rising over it like an eagle.

  • Jeremiah 48:24 — A Bozrah listed among the cities of Moab; its identity is uncertain.

  • Micah 2:12–13 — The same word used for the "fold" where God gathers the remnant, led by their King.

  • Joel 3:13; Revelation 14:19–20 — The wider biblical image of judgment as a winepress.

  • Revelation 19:13–15 — Christ in a robe dipped in blood, treading the winepress of God's wrath, drawing on Isaiah 63.
