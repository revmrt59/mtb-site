EDOM - (ʼĔdôm, Strong's H0123)

Summary

Edom is first a man and then a nation. Genesis gives the name to Esau, Jacob's twin brother, and then to the people descended from him and the rugged land they held south and southeast of the Dead Sea. The name means "red." Genesis ties it directly to the moment Esau begged Jacob for "that same red stew" and sold his birthright for it (Genesis 25:30). Edom grew strong early. It had chiefs and kings "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). Yet its history with Israel became a long record of brotherhood refused. Edom barred Israel's way in the wilderness, was conquered by David, threw off Judah's rule, and finally cheered and helped when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem.

The prophets answer that betrayal with some of the Bible's severest words of judgment. Obadiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Joel, and Malachi expose Edom's pride, its trust in mountain strongholds, and its cruelty toward a brother in distress. In Isaiah 63 the Lord Himself comes from Edom in blood-stained garments after treading the winepress alone, a picture Revelation 19 echoes in its vision of Christ. The story does not end in judgment alone, though. Amos foresees the restored house of David possessing "the remnant of Edom" along with the nations called by God's name (Amos 9:12). James applies that promise to Gentiles turning to the Lord (Acts 15:17), and Mark records that people from Idumea, the later name of Edom's region, came to Jesus (Mark 3:8). Edom teaches both how seriously God takes pride and cruelty and how far His mercy can reach.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The Hebrew name ʼĔdôm (אֱדוֹם) refers to three closely related things in Scripture. It is a second name of Esau, son of Isaac and Rebekah and twin brother of Jacob (Genesis 25:30; 36:1, 8). It names the people descended from him, organized first under chiefs and then under kings (Genesis 36:15–43). It also names their territory, the mountainous region southeast and south of the Dead Sea that bordered Judah's southern edge (Numbers 34:3; Joshua 15:1, 21). Scripture often uses Seir or Mount Seir, a separately indexed name (H8165), in parallel with Edom for the same land. Esau's personal name is likewise indexed separately (H6215), as is the later Greek name Idumea (G2401). These names belong to the same story, but they are not occurrences of this Hebrew entry.

The name means "red." It comes from the Hebrew root for redness, and Genesis states the connection outright: Esau asked for "that same red stew… Therefore his name was called Edom" (Genesis 25:30). The wordplay is intentional and it sticks. Esau was already described with a related word for red at his birth (Genesis 25:25), but Scripture attaches the name itself to the meal for which he traded his birthright. Writers often note that the reddish sandstone of the region suits the name. That is a fitting observation, though the Bible does not give it as the reason for the name. The meaning "red" describes the origin of the name. It is not a verdict on Edom's character, which Scripture judges by Edom's actions.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Origins: The Brother Who Sold His Birthright

Before the twins were born, the Lord told Rebekah that two nations were in her womb and that the older would serve the younger (Genesis 25:23). Esau came home weary from the field and traded his birthright to Jacob for bread and red stew. Genesis sums up the scene by saying that Esau despised his birthright (Genesis 25:34). Hebrews later points back to this moment and calls Esau a "profane person… who for one morsel of food sold his birthright" (Hebrews 12:16).

Even so, Genesis does not tell Esau's story as pure villainy. When Jacob returned from Haran, he sent messengers "to Esau his brother in the land of Seir, the country of Edom" (Genesis 32:3). Esau ran to meet him and embraced him, and the brothers parted in peace (Genesis 33:4). Genesis 36 then traces Esau's descendants, their chiefs, and a line of Edomite kings who reigned before Israel had any king (Genesis 36:31–43; compare 1 Chronicles 1:43–54). Edom prospered early and visibly while the heirs of the promise were still waiting.

The Wilderness: A Brother's Request Refused

After the exodus, Moses' song pictures the chiefs of Edom dismayed at what God had done (Exodus 15:15). When Israel reached Kadesh, Moses sent a carefully worded message: "Thus says your brother Israel: 'You know all the hardship that has befallen us'" (Numbers 20:14). He retold how the Lord had heard Israel's cry and brought them out of Egypt, and he promised to stay on the King's Highway and touch nothing: "We will not pass through fields or vineyards, nor will we drink water from wells" (Numbers 20:17). Edom refused and came out against Israel with a strong army (Numbers 20:18–21). Israel turned away. Judges 11:17–18 remembers the same refusal centuries later.

That detour had consequences. Aaron died at Mount Hor "by the border of the land of Edom" (Numbers 20:23). Then Israel traveled "to go around the land of Edom," and the people grew discouraged along the way (Numbers 21:4). Their complaining led to the plague of serpents and to the bronze serpent lifted on a pole, which Jesus later used to describe His own lifting up (John 3:14). Shortly afterward, Balaam's oracle announced a Star coming out of Jacob and declared that "Edom shall be a possession" (Numbers 24:17–18, paraphrased). The land of Edom also marked the southern boundary of Judah's inheritance (Numbers 34:3; Joshua 15:1, 21).

One more early note stands out. Deborah's song celebrates the Lord who "went out from Seir" and "marched from the field of Edom" when the earth trembled (Judges 5:4, paraphrased). In Israel's worship, the southern wilderness near Edom was remembered as the direction from which God came to save His people.

The Monarchy: Conquest, Revolt, and Snare

Saul fought against Edom (1 Samuel 14:47). David conquered it, put garrisons throughout the land, and "all the Edomites became David's servants" (2 Samuel 8:14, paraphrased; 1 Chronicles 18:11–13). The ancient word to Rebekah that the older would serve the younger now took visible form. The title of Psalm 60 links that psalm to this war, and the psalm speaks of God casting His shoe over Edom and asks, "Who will lead me to Edom?" (Psalm 60:8–9, paraphrased; repeated in Psalm 108:9–10).

Solomon's fleet sailed from Ezion Geber "on the shore of the Red Sea, in the land of Edom" (1 Kings 9:26, paraphrased; 2 Chronicles 8:17). But Edom also produced Hadad, a royal survivor of Joab's slaughter of Edom's males, whom the Lord raised up as an adversary against Solomon because of Solomon's turning to other gods (1 Kings 11:14–16). Later, Edom had no king, only a deputy (1 Kings 22:47). An Edomite king joined Israel and Judah against Moab in a campaign in which God sent water "by way of Edom" (2 Kings 3:8–26).

Under Jehoram of Judah, Edom revolted and made its own king (2 Kings 8:20–22; 2 Chronicles 21:8–10). This fulfilled Isaac's word to Esau that he would one day break his brother's yoke from his neck (Genesis 27:40). Amaziah later struck ten thousand Edomites in the Valley of Salt and captured Sela (2 Kings 14:7). Victory went straight to his head. Jehoash of Israel warned him that defeating Edom had lifted up his heart (2 Kings 14:10). Chronicles adds a sobering detail: Amaziah brought back the gods of Edom and worshiped them, and his downfall came "because they sought the gods of Edom" (2 Chronicles 25:14, 20, paraphrased). The conqueror was conquered by the idols of the people he defeated.

The Exile: The Day of Jerusalem

Edom appears among the nations who must drink the cup of God's wrath (Jeremiah 25:21) and among the envoys who came to Zedekiah as Babylon pressed in (Jeremiah 27:3). For a time some Judeans even found refuge in Edom (Jeremiah 40:11). But when Jerusalem fell, Edom chose the wrong side. Psalm 137 preserves the memory with painful clarity: "Remember, O LORD, against the sons of Edom the day of Jerusalem, who said, 'Raze it, raze it, to its very foundation!'" (Psalm 137:7).

Obadiah lays the charge out in detail. Edom stood aloof while strangers cast lots for Jerusalem, "even you were as one of them" (Obadiah 1:11). Then it went further: "You should not have gazed on the day of your brother… nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction" (Obadiah 1:12). Edom entered the gate, laid hands on Judah's goods, and stood "at the crossroads to cut off those among them who escaped" (Obadiah 1:13–14). Ezekiel adds that Edom took vengeance on Judah (Ezekiel 25:12) and seized the Lord's land "with wholehearted joy" (Ezekiel 36:5). Lamentations bitterly tells the "daughter of Edom" to rejoice while she can, because the cup will pass to her too (Lamentations 4:21–22).

The Prophets: Judgment on Pride and Violence

Edom's sin was old. Amos indicts Edom because "he pursued his brother with the sword, and cast off all pity" (Amos 1:11), and he mentions Edom in the oracles against Gaza, Tyre, and Moab as well (Amos 1:6, 9; 2:1). Obadiah exposes the root: the pride of a people living "in the clefts of the rock" who said in their hearts, "Who will bring me down?" (Obadiah 1:3, paraphrased). God's answer is that He will bring them down even if they nest among the stars (Obadiah 1:4). Jeremiah asks whether wisdom has vanished from Teman (Jeremiah 49:7) and announces desolation over Edom and Bozrah (Jeremiah 49:17–22). Ezekiel places Edom among the nations in the pit (Ezekiel 32:29) and declares Mount Seir desolate because it rejoiced when Israel's inheritance became desolate (Ezekiel 35:15). Joel names Edom's violence and innocent bloodshed against Judah (Joel 3:19).

Isaiah carries this further. The Lord's sword descends on Edom, with a great sacrifice in Bozrah (Isaiah 34:5–6). Then a lone warrior comes "from Edom, with dyed garments from Bozrah," having trodden the winepress alone because there was no one to help (Isaiah 63:1–6, paraphrased). Here Edom stands for every enemy of God's people.

Even the oracles of judgment hold a thread of mercy. In the middle of Jeremiah's word against Edom, the Lord says He will preserve Edom's fatherless children and invites its widows to trust in Him (Jeremiah 49:11, paraphrased). Isaiah, Amos, and Daniel speak of Edom's place in the future as well (Isaiah 11:14; Amos 9:12; Daniel 11:41). Malachi, the Old Testament's last word on Edom, looks back on its ruin. Edom vows, "We will return and build the desolate places," but the Lord answers, "They may build, but I will throw down; they shall be called the Territory of Wickedness" (Malachi 1:4).

After the Old Testament

The New Testament uses the Greek name Idumea once. Among the crowds who came to Jesus were people from "Jerusalem and Idumea and beyond the Jordan… when they heard how many things He was doing" (Mark 3:8). Outside the Bible, historical sources describe Herod the Great as Idumean by descent. Matthew does not mention Herod's ancestry, but the historical background adds weight to the scene of a king in Jerusalem trying to destroy the newborn King of the Jews (Matthew 2:16).

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states plainly that Edom was Israel's brother and that God treated that bond as real. Moses appealed to it (Numbers 20:14). Amos and Obadiah condemn Edom precisely because the victim was "your brother Jacob" (Obadiah 1:10). Israel was commanded not to abhor an Edomite for that same reason (Deuteronomy 23:7, paraphrased). Edom's guilt was heavier because of the relationship it betrayed. This is the main thread of its history: closeness to the family of promise gave Edom no advantage when it turned against that family.

Scripture also states that God's purposes for the two brothers differed from the beginning (Genesis 25:23), and Malachi frames Edom's ruin as a demonstration of the Lord's love for Jacob (Malachi 1:2–5). Paul quotes Malachi in Romans 9:13 to show that God's saving purpose rests "not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11). Interpreters differ on how far Paul's argument concerns individuals and how far it concerns the nations descended from them. What is clear is that Edom's story displays both God's freedom in choosing and human responsibility for choices made. Esau despised his birthright, and Edom chose violence. Scripture never presents Edom as a helpless victim of decree.

A further implication follows from the pattern of the prophets. God disciplined Jerusalem by Babylon, but He did not license spectators to join in. Edom's delight in Judah's fall drew a sentence that matches its deed: "As you have done, it shall be done to you" (Obadiah 1:15, paraphrased). When God disciplines His own people, He stays fiercely protective of them. Finally, Edom's fall comes in the context of the coming kingdom. Obadiah closes with deliverers on Mount Zion and the declaration that "the kingdom shall be the LORD's" (Obadiah 1:21, paraphrased). Edom's judgment serves the rule of God, not merely Israel's revenge.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

The clearest New Testament use of Edom comes at the Jerusalem Council. Amos had promised that the rebuilt tabernacle of David would "possess the remnant of Edom, and all the Gentiles who are called by My name" (Amos 9:12). James quotes the Greek version, which reads, "So that the rest of mankind may seek the LORD, even all the Gentiles who are called by My name" (Acts 15:17). The two wordings differ, with "possess" beside "seek" and "Edom" beside "mankind." The Hebrew words for Edom and for mankind (Adam) look very similar, which may be related to the difference, though the texts themselves do not explain how it arose. James finds in the passage God's promise to gather the Gentiles under the rule of David's greater Son, and Christian readers have seen Edom, the nation that stood for hostility to God's people, included in that wider ingathering. James draws the conclusion that Gentiles turning to God should not be burdened (Acts 15:19). Mark's note that people from Idumea came to Jesus (Mark 3:8) gives a small, concrete picture of that promise beginning to come true.

The second major connection is strongly supported canonical development. Isaiah's warrior returns from Edom with garments stained from treading the winepress alone (Isaiah 63:1–6). Revelation takes up the same imagery for Christ's return: "He was clothed with a robe dipped in blood… He Himself treads the winepress of the fierceness and wrath of Almighty God" (Revelation 19:13, 15). Revelation does not name Edom, but the borrowed picture is unmistakable. The Lord who once judged Edom will judge every power that sets itself against Him.

Other connections are prophetic or thematic. Balaam's oracle joins the rising Star from Jacob with Edom becoming a possession (Numbers 24:17–18). The Christian church has long read that Star as messianic, and David's conquest of Edom gave the promise an early, partial fulfillment. The detour "around the land of Edom" led to the bronze serpent, which Jesus explicitly applied to His cross (Numbers 21:4–9; John 3:14). It is worth noticing that Edom's refusal set the stage for that picture of healing, though Scripture does not draw the link itself. The rivalry of Esau and Jacob also echoes, as a historical resonance rather than a stated type, when a king of Idumean descent seeks to kill the infant Christ (Matthew 2:16). The older brother's long hostility meets its answer in the true Heir, who wins not by ruling over Edom with a garrison but by drawing people from Idumea to Himself.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Esau's bowl of red stew is the Bible's plainest picture of trading what is eternal for what is immediate. Hebrews uses it as a warning to believers. Esau was not ignorant of his birthright. He simply cared more, in that hour, about his hunger. "Profane" in Hebrews 12:16 describes a person who treats holy things as common. That danger does not belong only to the obviously wicked. It belongs to anyone who decides God's promises can wait while the appetite cannot.

Edom's pride grew out of its geography. A nation hidden among cliffs could ask, "Who will bring me down?" and expect no answer. God answered anyway (Obadiah 1:3–4). Whatever makes us feel untouchable, whether money, position, intelligence, or the walls we have built around our lives, becomes dangerous the moment it replaces dependence on God. Teman's famous wisdom could not save it either (Jeremiah 49:7).

Obadiah also condemns something many people never think of as sin: gazing at another's ruin with satisfaction. Edom did not destroy Jerusalem. Babylon did. But Edom watched, rejoiced, spoke proudly, and then helped itself. God counted all of it. When a brother or sister falls, even under God's own discipline, the Lord does not invite us to enjoy it. Galatians 6:1 calls the spiritual to restore such a person gently, and that is the opposite of Edom's way.

Amaziah's story adds a quieter warning. He beat Edom on the battlefield and then bowed to its gods (2 Chronicles 25:14). Spiritual victories bring their own temptation, because pride after triumph can open the door to the very thing we overcame.

Edom does not leave us only with warning. God told Edom's widows to trust in Him (Jeremiah 49:11). He promised that a remnant of Edom would belong to the house of David (Amos 9:12). And when Jesus began His ministry, people from Idumea walked a long way to hear Him (Mark 3:8). The gospel reaches even the descendants of Israel's bitterest brother, and that should silence any notion that a family's history, or our own, places us beyond the grace of Christ.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 25:23–34 — The prophecy over the twins and the sale of the birthright. Genesis 25:30 gives the name and its meaning, "red."

  • Genesis 32–33 — Jacob's message to Esau in Edom and the brothers' reconciliation.

  • Genesis 36:1–43 — Esau identified as Edom. Edom's chiefs and its kings before Israel's monarchy.

  • Numbers 20:14–21; 21:4 — Moses' appeal to "your brother Israel," Edom's armed refusal, and the detour that preceded the bronze serpent.

  • Numbers 24:17–18 — Balaam's Star from Jacob and Edom as a possession.

  • 2 Samuel 8:14; 1 Kings 11:14–16 — David's conquest of Edom and Hadad the Edomite as Solomon's adversary.

  • 2 Kings 8:20–22; 14:7–10; 2 Chronicles 25:14–20 — Edom's revolt, Amaziah's victory and pride, and Judah's turn to Edom's gods.

  • Psalm 60; Psalm 137:7 — Prayer for victory over Edom, and the memory of Edom's cry on the day of Jerusalem.

  • Obadiah 1–21 — The fullest exposure of Edom's pride, betrayal, and judgment, ending with the Lord's kingdom.

  • Isaiah 34:5–6; 63:1–6 — God's judgment on Edom and the solitary warrior returning from Bozrah.

  • Jeremiah 49:7–22 — The oracle against Edom, including mercy toward its fatherless and widows (49:11).

  • Ezekiel 25:12–14; 35; 36:5 — Edom's vengeance and joy over Israel's desolation.

  • Amos 1:11; 9:12 — Edom's pitiless pursuit of his brother, and the remnant of Edom under David's restored tabernacle.

  • Malachi 1:2–5 — The Lord's love for Jacob shown in Edom's lasting desolation.

  • Acts 15:17 — James applies Amos 9:12 to the Gentiles seeking the Lord.

  • Romans 9:10–13; Hebrews 12:16–17 — Esau as the New Testament's example of God's calling and of the danger of a profane heart.

  • Mark 3:8; Revelation 19:13–15 — People from Idumea coming to Jesus, and Christ treading the winepress in fulfillment of Isaiah's imagery.
