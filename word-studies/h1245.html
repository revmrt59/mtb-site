SEEK, REQUIRE - (bâqash, Strong's H1245)

Summary

The Hebrew word bâqash is one of the Old Testament's ordinary words for seeking. It means going after something you want and do not yet have. Saul uses it when he goes looking for his father's lost donkeys, and later when he hunts David through the wilderness. David uses it when he answers God, "Your face, LORD, I will seek" (Psalm 27:8). When someone with authority seeks something from another person, the word can mean "require": a demand that someone give an account.

Scripture's uses of bâqash show what a heart is really after. Violent men seek a life to destroy, while the faithful seek the face of God. God promises that those who seek Him with all their heart will find Him (Jeremiah 29:13). He also warns that a people who refuse His word may one day seek it and not find it (Amos 8:12). Most striking of all, God Himself seeks. He requires the blood of the wicked from a watchman who stayed silent. When Israel's shepherds abandoned the flock, He promised, "I will seek what was lost" (Ezekiel 34:16). Jesus' words echo that promise when He says He came "to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bâqash is a common Hebrew verb found from Genesis to Malachi. It appears most often in 1 Samuel, the Psalms and Jeremiah, the books where David is hunted, where God's people cry out to Him, and where a nation is called back before judgment falls. The settings are very ordinary: herdsmen and lost animals, royal courts and petitions, legal disputes, fugitives and pursuers. That ordinariness matters, because Scripture takes a word used for everyday searching and uses it for a person's relationship with God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, bâqash means to make an effort to find, obtain or secure something. Context determines what kind of effort is meant.

1. To search for something or someone missing, as Saul looks for the donkeys (1 Samuel 10:14) or Joseph looks for his brothers (Genesis 37:16).

2. To pursue someone in order to harm or kill, often with the word life, as in "seek my life" (Psalm 63:9).

3. To seek a person's favor or presence, whether a king's or God's, by asking, pleading or drawing near. Mordecai charges Esther to go in to the king and plead before him for her people (Esther 4:8), and the psalmist seeks the LORD's face (Psalm 27:8).

4. To require or demand, especially to hold someone accountable for a debt or for a life (Genesis 43:9; Ezekiel 3:18).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The object of the verb usually reveals the heart of the one seeking. Proverbs pairs the same verb with very different goals. The wise seek knowledge (Proverbs 18:15), an evil man seeks rebellion (Proverbs 17:11), and those who get treasures by a lying tongue are, in the end, people who seek death (Proverbs 21:6). Wisdom calls for seeking with the energy of a miner: "If you seek her as silver, And search for her as for hidden treasures" (Proverbs 2:4). The search pays off, because the next verse promises that such a seeker will "find the knowledge of God" (Proverbs 2:5). The comparison does not cheapen wisdom. It implies that wisdom deserves at least the effort people spend on buried silver.

The phrase "seek the face" comes from life at court. Literally, Proverbs 29:26 says that many seek the face of the ruler, meaning they want his favor. Likewise, all the earth sought the presence of Solomon to hear his wisdom (1 Kings 10:24). When Israel is told to seek God's face, the court picture suggests a subject asking for an audience with the King.

At the other end of life, the same verb describes desperation. David has never seen the righteous forsaken, "Nor his descendants begging bread" (Psalm 37:25). In that verse, seeking has shrunk to scraping for food. God's care for the righteous is seen in His keeping them from that humiliation.

4. Theological Significance

Bâqash shows that God takes seeking seriously in both directions. He is the One whom people are called to seek, and He is also the God who seeks.

He seeks as Judge. When God "requires" blood, He is not a distant observer of human wrongs. Psalm 9:12, using a different Hebrew verb, says that when He avenges blood, "He remembers them; He does not forget the cry of the humble." Justice in Scripture is personal. A holy God goes after the accounting that is owed.

He also seeks as Lord and Shepherd. Samuel tells Saul that the LORD "has sought for Himself a man after His own heart" (1 Samuel 13:14). In Ezekiel 22:30, God looks for a man to "stand in the gap" and finds none. Ezekiel 22:31 shows the judgment that followed.

God's hiddenness has a purpose. "I did not say to the seed of Jacob, 'Seek Me in vain'" (Isaiah 45:19). When He withdraws from His own people, as in Hosea 5:15, He does so to draw them back, not because He wishes to stay lost to them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 27 and the promise to the wholehearted. Psalm 27 is a song sung under threat, with enemies, armies and false witnesses all around. Yet David's deepest desire is the LORD Himself: "One thing I have desired of the LORD, That will I seek: That I may dwell in the house of the LORD All the days of my life, To behold the beauty of the LORD" (27:4). Verse 8 shows who speaks first: "When You said, 'Seek My face,' My heart said to You, 'Your face, LORD, I will seek.'" God's invitation comes before David's search. David's seeking is a response to God's call.

That order explains the promises made to Israel in exile. Moses foresaw a scattered people and said, "from there you will seek the LORD your God, and you will find Him if you seek Him with all your heart and with all your soul" (Deuteronomy 4:29). Centuries later, Jeremiah wrote the same hope to captives in Babylon: "you will seek Me and find Me, when you search for Me with all your heart" (Jeremiah 29:13). Both promises are spoken to people far from the land and the temple. Distance from Jerusalem does not put God out of reach. A divided heart does.

Saul's hunt for David. In 1 Samuel the verb repeatedly describes the king hunting the man God had anointed. "Saul sought him every day" (1 Samuel 23:14). Saul then "took three thousand chosen men from all Israel, and went to seek David and his men on the Rocks of the Wild Goats" (24:2). Yet in that same chapter Saul walks unaware into the cave where David is hiding, and David spares his life. The hunted man shows mercy to the man hunting him. David carried that language into his prayers: "oppressors have sought after my life; They have not set God before them" (Psalm 54:3). The verb exposes what sin does to human desire. People were made to seek God, but the same energy can be turned into a manhunt.

The watchman and the life God requires. God tells Ezekiel that if he fails to warn the wicked, "that same wicked man shall die in his iniquity; but his blood I will require at your hand" (Ezekiel 3:18; repeated in 33:8). The wicked man is still responsible for his own sin. Even so, God holds the silent watchman accountable for the life he could have warned. Judah uses the same verb when he offers himself as surety for Benjamin: "from my hand you shall require him" (Genesis 43:9). In both cases, a life has been entrusted to someone, and that person must answer for it.

Ezekiel 34: the Shepherd who seeks. God condemns Israel's shepherds because they fed themselves and never sought what was lost (Ezekiel 34:4). The sheep were scattered over the mountains, and no one went looking for them (34:6). So God makes the search His own: "I will seek what was lost and bring back what was driven away, bind up the broken and strengthen what was sick" (34:16). The leaders' failure did not end the search. Instead, God Himself took it over.

Seeking too late. Hosea describes people going with their flocks and herds to seek the LORD but not finding Him, because He has withdrawn (Hosea 5:6). Later in the chapter God explains why: "I will return again to My place Till they acknowledge their offense. Then they will seek My face; In their affliction they will earnestly seek Me" (Hosea 5:15). Amos pushes this warning to its limit: "They shall run to and fro, seeking the word of the LORD, But shall not find it" (Amos 8:12). This famine falls on a people who had long refused the prophets. Their frantic searching after God has fallen silent is the opposite of the wholehearted seeking that finds Him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The hostile sense of bâqash follows God's chosen deliverers from Moses in Egypt to the Child of Bethlehem. Pharaoh sought to kill Moses, and God later told him that all who sought his life were dead (Exodus 2:15; 4:19). Matthew echoes those words when the angel tells Joseph to return from Egypt, because those who sought the young Child's life were dead (Matthew 2:20). The pattern of the hunted David reaches its height in Jesus. Rulers sought His life, and He gave it up willingly at the cross.

The redemptive story turns on Ezekiel 34. Human shepherds failed, so God promised to seek His flock Himself and to set over them "My servant David" (Ezekiel 34:23). Hosea likewise foresaw Israel returning in the latter days to seek "the LORD their God and David their king" (Hosea 3:5). Jesus stands where these promises meet. In the parable, the shepherd leaves "the ninety-nine in the wilderness" and goes "after the one which is lost until he finds it" (Luke 15:4). In Zacchaeus's house, Jesus declares that He has come "to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10). Luke writes in Greek, yet Jesus' words carry the clear sound of Ezekiel 34:16. The God who said "I will seek" has arrived in person. Malachi had promised that "the Lord, whom you seek," would suddenly come to His temple (Malachi 3:1).

The story ends with the nations. Zechariah foresees "many peoples and strong nations" coming to seek the LORD of hosts in Jerusalem (Zechariah 8:22). Nations that once sought Israel's ruin will come seeking Israel's God. Isaiah had already heard God say He was found by those who did not seek Him (Isaiah 65:1), and Paul applies those words to the Gentiles' welcome into the gospel (Romans 10:20).

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 27:8 shows that David's seeking began with God's own call. The believer who longs for His face is answering a call already spoken. That is why seeking God is not anxious guesswork. Jeremiah 29:13 lays a promise under every honest search.

Earlier in Psalm 119, the psalmist says he has sought God's precepts, using the related Hebrew verb dârash (H1875; Psalm 119:94). The psalm ends with his confession that he has gone astray like a lost sheep and his request that God seek His servant, using bâqash (Psalm 119:176). Mature faith includes both prayers: "Your face, LORD, I will seek" and "Seek Your servant." The Shepherd of Ezekiel 34 answers the second prayer.

The Song of Songs gives this seeking its warmth: "By night on my bed I sought the one I love; I sought him, but I did not find him" (Song of Solomon 3:1). She does not stay in bed. She rises and searches the streets and squares (3:2). The Song is first a picture of human love, and it should be read that way. Still, it shows the kind of longing that Psalm 27 brings before God. At its best, seeking Him is the restlessness of love rather than a chore done for duty's sake.

Scripture also warns against seeking the wrong things. God asked Baruch, "do you seek great things for yourself? Do not seek them" (Jeremiah 45:5). Zephaniah redirects the same desire: seek the LORD, seek righteousness, seek humility (Zephaniah 2:3). Amos adds urgency. God's patience is real, but His word is not a resource we can call up whenever we like. Today, while He invites us to seek His face, is the time to answer.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Chronicles 7:14 — God promises healing when His people humble themselves, pray and "seek My face" and turn from wickedness.

  • Psalm 34:14 — "Seek peace and pursue it": peace is something to chase, not merely wait for.

  • Psalm 105:4 — Israel is called to seek God's face continually, not only in a crisis.

  • Jeremiah 5:1 — God calls for a search of Jerusalem's streets for one person who does justly and seeks truth.

  • Ezra 8:21–23 — Ezra's company fasted to seek from God a safe journey, and "He answered our prayer."

  • Nehemiah 2:4 — When the king asks what Nehemiah requests, he prays to God before answering.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1875 (dârash), "seek, inquire" — a different Hebrew verb that often stands alongside bâqash in parallel lines. Dârash tends to stress inquiring or consulting, while bâqash stresses the effort to find and secure. The two verbs are paired in Deuteronomy 4:29 and Jeremiah 29:13, and in Psalm 105:4 they appear in back-to-back lines.
