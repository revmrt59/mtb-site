PURE, CLEAN, CLEAR - (bar, Strong's H1249)

Summary

The Hebrew word bar describes something pure in the sense of unmixed, with nothing foreign blended in. Something bar has been sifted clean: a heart with no rival loyalty, a commandment with no trace of falsehood, a bride singled out above all others, even a feeding trough swept empty. The word appears only seven times, all in the poetic books of Job, Psalms, Proverbs, and the Song of Solomon. Its few uses are carefully chosen.

The richest uses are in the Psalms. Psalm 24:4 names "a pure heart" as the mark of the person who may stand in God's holy place, and defines that heart as one that "has not lifted up his soul to an idol." Psalm 73:1 says God's goodness is seen by "such as are pure in heart," and Psalm 19:8 calls God's commandment "pure, enlightening the eyes." Together these passages teach that God, whose Word has no mixture of darkness, draws near to people whose hearts are no longer divided. Jesus took up the same hope when He promised that the pure in heart would see God (Matthew 5:8). The Song of Solomon adds that this purity is radiant and chosen. It is the brightness of someone deeply loved.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bar is a Hebrew adjective related to the verb barar, which means to purify, sift, or select. The family resemblance helps explain why bar can mean both "pure" and "choice." In both senses, something has been separated from what does not belong with it.

The word stands apart from the vocabulary of ceremonial cleanness used for washings and sacrifices in Leviticus. Bar appears only in poetry and wisdom writing. It describes inner quality, clarity, and singleness more than ritual status.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning of bar is "pure, clean, clear," with the underlying idea of being unmixed or free of anything extra. Context brings out several distinct senses:

1. Pure in heart or conduct, meaning free of divided loyalty or falsehood. Example: "a pure heart" (Psalm 24:4).

2. Pure in quality, meaning without flaw or error. Example: "The commandment of the LORD is pure" (Psalm 19:8).

3. Choice or favored, meaning singled out from others. Example: "The favorite of the one who bore her" (Song of Solomon 6:9).

4. Clear or bright, meaning unclouded in its shining. Example: "Clear as the sun" (Song of Solomon 6:10).

5. Clean in the plain physical sense of empty or bare. Example: "the trough is clean" (Proverbs 14:4).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The thing being described changes how the word lands. Applied to a heart, bar is moral and relational: the person's loyalty belongs to God alone. Applied to God's commandment, it describes the commandment's own nature as true and without error. Applied to the beloved woman in the Song, it is the language of affection and admiration. Applied to a manger, it simply means nothing is in it.

The word has two neighbors that are worth noticing. In Psalm 24:4, "clean hands" uses a different Hebrew word (naqi) for innocence in action, and bar is reserved for the heart. In Song 6:10, bar stands beside "fair as the moon." That pairing links purity with light.

4. Theological Significance

These few verses show a consistent pattern in how God relates to people. God's revelation is bar, with no mixture of deceit or error in it. The person who comes near Him is called to a heart that is bar as well, undivided in loyalty. Fellowship with God involves a kind of matching. A God who is wholly true is approached by a heart that is wholly His.

The Psalms define this purity by what the heart refuses. In Scripture's broader picture, purity is often pictured as refined metal separated from dross. It is less about a spotless record than about allegiance with no rival mixed in. That is why the danger Psalm 24 names is idolatry and deceit. These are the two great ways a heart becomes mixed.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 24:3–4 — Who may stand in His holy place? The psalm asks, "Who may ascend into the hill of the LORD? Or who may stand in His holy place?" The answer moves from the outside to the inside: "He who has clean hands and a pure heart, Who has not lifted up his soul to an idol, Nor sworn deceitfully." Israel's worshipers knew the washings required at the sanctuary. Here David presses past them to the heart itself. The definition that follows explains what bar means here. A pure heart has not lifted itself toward a false god and does not speak with a double tongue. God's presence welcomes the one whose devotion contains no second master.

Psalm 73:1 — Seeing God's goodness. Asaph opens with a settled conclusion: "Truly God is good to Israel, To such as are pure in heart." He then admits how close he came to losing that conviction: "my feet had almost stumbled... For I was envious of the boastful, When I saw the prosperity of the wicked" (73:2–3). Envy had begun to mix a second desire into his heart, and his view of God's goodness clouded. Clarity returned when he went into the sanctuary of God (73:17). In this psalm, a pure heart is the eye that can see God's goodness when the world's evidence seems to argue against it. Purity and perception go together.

Psalm 19:8 — The commandment that gives sight. "The commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes." David has just praised the sun running its course across the heavens (19:4–6). He now turns to a second light. Because God's commandment carries no falsehood, it does more than avoid misleading. It gives sight, and the one who receives it comes to see clearly. The same psalm calls these words "More to be desired... than gold" (19:10). The pure Word is a treasure because it lets a person see.

Song of Solomon 6:9–10 — Chosen and radiant. The bridegroom sets his beloved apart from "the queens and the concubines" (6:8–9): "My dove, my perfect one, Is the only one... The favorite of the one who bore her." Here bar means choice, the one singled out. The next verse gives the effect: "Who is she who looks forth as the morning, Fair as the moon, Clear as the sun." In this passage the beloved's purity is the brightness of one who is chosen and cherished. Her value comes from being loved and singled out, more than from anything she has achieved.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Psalm 24 asks a question that only one person has fully answered. The psalm ends with the King of glory entering the gates (24:7–10). Jesus Christ, whose loyalty to the Father was never divided, is the one who truly ascends the hill of the LORD with clean hands and a pure heart. In the Beatitudes He carries the promise of Psalms 24 and 73 forward: the pure in heart shall see God (Matthew 5:8).

The Song's chosen, radiant beloved finds a fitting echo in the church as Christ's bride. Paul says Christ gave Himself "that He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle" (Ephesians 5:27). At the marriage of the Lamb, "to her it was granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright" (Revelation 19:8). The word "granted" matters. The bride's brightness is given to her. The New Testament does not cite Song 6 directly, so this is a canonical resonance rather than a stated fulfillment. Still, the pattern holds: the chosen one shines because the King has chosen her and made her clean.

7. Spirit & Life

Asaph's near-fall is a mirror for many believers. His hope did not fade because God stopped being good. It faded because envy crept in alongside his trust, and a mixed heart sees poorly. The remedy was to come back into God's presence. There his single focus returned, and with it his sight. When God's goodness seems hard to see, it is worth asking what second desire has slipped in beside Him.

God supplies what He asks for. He calls for an unmixed heart, and He gives an unmixed Word that enlightens the eyes. Time in Scripture is one of the ordinary ways He clears away the dross and steadies a divided heart.

Proverbs 14:4 offers a fitting caution by analogy: "Where no oxen are, the trough is clean." A spotless manger means there is no ox and no harvest. The purity God seeks is singleness of heart toward Him. It does not mean a life kept sterile by avoiding the mess of real work, real people, and real service. A believer can pursue an undivided heart while still filling the trough.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The occurrences in Psalms and the Song of Solomon are discussed above. The two remaining uses add range.

  • Job 11:4 — Zophar accuses Job of claiming, "My doctrine is pure, And I am clean in your eyes." The word translated "clean" is bar, used here of moral innocence before God. Zophar presents the claim as arrogance, yet God later says the friends had not spoken rightly of Him (Job 42:7). The verse warns against proud claims of purity. It warns just as strongly against harshly judging someone else's integrity.

  • Proverbs 14:4 — "Where no oxen are, the trough is clean; But much increase comes by the strength of an ox." This is the word's plainest physical sense: empty and bare. The proverb teaches that productive work brings untidiness and cost, and that a perfectly clean trough means an empty field.
