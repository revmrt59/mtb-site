FIR, CYPRESS - (bᵉrôwth, Strong's H1266)

Summary

The Hebrew word bᵉrôwth names a fragrant evergreen wood, the cypress or fir. In Song of Solomon 1:17 the bride looks at the shelter she shares with her beloved and says, "The beams of our houses are cedar, And our rafters of fir." The word is a spelling variant of the more common Hebrew bᵉrôwsh (H1265), the cypress or fir used throughout the Old Testament for royal and sacred building.

This one use carries a lot of meaning. Cedar and cypress were the woods Solomon used to line the temple of God, so when the bride describes their shared dwelling in those terms, the poetry gives their love the dignity and permanence of a sanctuary. The verse before it says "our bed is green," which suggests they may be lying outdoors under living trees. If so, creation itself has become their roofed chamber. The single word draws together garden and temple, nature and holiness, and it gives a glimpse of what love looks like when it rests within God's good design.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bᵉrôwth is a Hebrew noun. It appears to be an alternate form of bᵉrôwsh (H1265), the ordinary Hebrew word for cypress or fir. The difference is a single letter at the end, a variation that probably reflects regional or Aramaic-influenced speech. This form occurs once in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Song of Solomon 1:17. Because this form occurs only once, its meaning is learned from its close relative and from the verse where it stands.

The tree in question belongs to the tall evergreens of Lebanon and the surrounding hills. Its wood was prized for strength, fragrance, and durability. The King James Version and the NKJV say "fir." Many modern translations prefer "cypress" or "pine," because the exact species is uncertain, but every option refers to a noble, lasting timber.

The setting is the Song of Solomon, a book of love poetry. Its images are meant to be felt and pictured, and it often borrows the language of kings, gardens, and spices to describe the joy of a man and woman given to each other.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Bᵉrôwth means a cypress or fir tree, or the timber cut from it. In Song of Solomon 1:17 it describes the "rafters," the upper framework overhead, while cedar forms the "beams." The word itself names only the wood. The richer associations come from how the poem uses it and from the way Scripture elsewhere speaks of this wood.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

With one occurrence, the usage of bᵉrôwth comes down to the context of a single verse. Three details stand out.

First, the fir is paired with cedar. Throughout the Old Testament, cedar and cypress appear together as prized building materials for palace and temple. Mentioning them together signals beauty and quality.

Second, the wood is overhead. The cedar beams and fir rafters form a roof, so the verse is about shelter and covering. The lovers lie beneath something strong and sweet-smelling.

Third, the bride uses the word "our": "our bed" (v. 16), "our houses," "our rafters" (v. 17). The dwelling belongs to both of them. Her delight is in a place they share, and the repeated "our" makes the setting an expression of their union.

4. Theological Significance

Choosing cedar and fir for the lovers' shelter links married love with the materials God's people used for holy places. Scripture does not treat the love of husband and wife as something shameful that must be kept away from God. The Song describes it with the vocabulary of palace and temple, which honors it as part of His good creation.

The verse also gives a sense of permanence. Cypress and cedar were prized because they last. Love that is pictured under such a roof is not a passing feeling sheltered by flimsy materials. It is meant to endure, and God, who builds for permanence, is honored by love that does the same.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:16–17: the living canopy. The bride praises her beloved, "Behold, you are handsome, my beloved! Yes, pleasant!" and then says, "Also our bed is green" (v. 16). A green bed is most likely a bed of grass or leaves. When she goes on to speak of beams of cedar and rafters of fir, the most natural picture is of two lovers resting outdoors, with the branches of tall evergreens arching above them like the framework of a house. The poem may be describing a royal chamber, a forest bower, or both at once, and the Song often blends such images. The outdoor reading still gives the verse its freshness. On that reading the lovers have no need of a man-made palace, because God's own trees have built them one. Creation becomes a royal chamber, and the scene recalls the first garden, where a man and woman lived together unashamed in a world God had called good.

1 Kings 6:15 and 2 Chronicles 3:5: the wood of the temple. When Solomon built the house of the LORD, "he built the inside walls of the temple with cedar boards... and he covered the floor of the temple with planks of cypress" (1 Kings 6:15). The Chronicler adds that "the larger room he paneled with cypress which he overlaid with fine gold, and he carved palm trees and chainwork on it" (2 Chronicles 3:5). These passages use the related word bᵉrôwsh, not bᵉrôwth, but the pairing of cedar and cypress is the same one found in Song of Solomon 1:17. A reader of the Song who knew Israel's history could hear an echo of the temple in the lovers' roof of cedar and fir.

The two passages also illuminate each other. Song of Solomon 1:17 turns living trees into a house. The temple turned the same trees into a house and then carved palm trees into its walls, and 1 Kings 6:29 adds cherubim and open flowers. Those carvings made Solomon's sanctuary something like a garden in wood and gold, recalling Eden, where God first walked with humanity. The Song's forest chamber and the temple's carved garden point in the same direction from opposite sides. Both describe a place where nature and holiness meet and where love is at home.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The cypress has a place in the wider biblical story through its related word bᵉrôwsh. David and Israel celebrated before the LORD "on all kinds of instruments of fir wood" (2 Samuel 6:5). The same timber used in the temple also gave voice to worship. In the prophets, cypress marks God's restoration. Isaiah promises that the cypress will grow where thorns once grew (Isaiah 55:13) and that the glory of Lebanon, including the cypress, will come to beautify God's sanctuary (Isaiah 60:13). Hosea hears the LORD compare Himself to a green cypress tree from whom His people's fruit comes (Hosea 14:8). The evergreen that sheltered lovers in the Song becomes a sign of God's own life-giving shelter.

The story reaches its goal in Jesus Christ. He spoke of His body as the temple (John 2:19–21), the true meeting place of God and humanity. The New Testament presents Him as the Bridegroom and His people as the bride (Ephesians 5:25–32). Scripture closes with a bride prepared for her husband and the announcement that God's dwelling is with men (Revelation 21:2–3), followed by a river and the tree of life (Revelation 22:1–2). The Song's chamber of cedar and fir is not a stated prophecy of these things. Still, it fits the larger pattern: a garden, a sanctuary, and a love that finds its home in God's presence. The temple timbers have given way to the Son of God Himself, and the garden is restored in Him.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride does not complain about where she lies. She looks up and sees cedar and fir. Love that is rightly ordered sees the shelter God has provided and calls it a palace. Before marriages wear down, couples often stop noticing what God has built around them. Her words can invite husbands and wives to say "our" again, about their home, their bed, and their shared life, and to recognize God's provision in it.

The choice of wood also matters. Cedar and cypress were not cheap. They were chosen for a house meant to stand. Love between a man and a woman needs a roof of faithfulness, patience, and honesty, materials that do not rot after the first season of feeling. Couples who build with these qualities often find that their home carries a fragrance others notice.

Finally, this single verse, read alongside the temple passages, illustrates that God's holiness and His creation are not enemies. The trees He planted, the temple built from them, and the love sheltered beneath them all come from the same Creator. A believer can rest in married love, in a forest, or in worship and see in each the work of a God who delights to dwell with His people.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Bᵉrôwth occurs only in Song of Solomon 1:17, which is discussed above. The other passages in this study that mention fir or cypress (1 Kings 6:15; 2 Chronicles 3:5; 2 Samuel 6:5; Isaiah 55:13; Isaiah 60:13; Hosea 14:8) use the related word bᵉrôwsh and are cited as canonical background, not as further occurrences of this word.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1265 (bᵉrôwsh), cypress, fir. This is a lexical relative. Bᵉrôwth is a variant form of this more common word, which is used for the temple timber in 1 Kings 6:15 and for worship instruments in 2 Samuel 6:5.

  • Cedar. This is a canonical pairing, not a lexical relative. It appears alongside fir in Song of Solomon 1:17 and alongside cypress in the temple construction of 1 Kings 6:15.
