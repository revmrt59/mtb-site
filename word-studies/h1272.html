FLEE, FLED, MAKE HASTE - (bârach, Strong's H1272)

Summary

The Hebrew verb bârach means to flee, to get away quickly from danger, anger, or a power that threatens harm. Most of its uses are plain stories of people running: Hagar fleeing Sarai, Jacob fleeing Esau and later Laban, Moses fleeing Pharaoh, David fleeing Saul and then his own son Absalom. The word itself passes no moral verdict. A flight can be wise, cowardly, rebellious, or commanded by God, and the story around it decides which. The same word also stretches into poetry. Job's days "flee away," a man "flees like a shadow," and the bride in the Song of Solomon urges her beloved to "make haste."

What Scripture shows through this word is sobering and comforting at once. People can outrun kings, brothers, and armies, but no one can outrun God. David asks, "Where can I flee from Your presence?" (Psalm 139:7), and Jonah tried anyway, only to admit that he ran because God is gracious. Again and again, the road of the fugitive becomes the place where God finds, sees, corrects, and prepares His people. In the larger story, Christ is the Shepherd who did not flee. Because He stood His ground, His people have a refuge to run to.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bârach is a common Hebrew verb for fleeing. It appears mostly in the historical books and follows people who suddenly lose their safety or standing. The largest group of uses gathers around David, first as a hunted young man in 1 Samuel and then as a dethroned king in 2 Samuel. The word also appears in the superscriptions of Psalms 3 and 57, which tie those prayers to David's flights.

Outside the narratives, the word appears in Job's laments about how short life is, in the prophets' warnings and commands, and in two descriptions of the tabernacle. The prophets use it for kings and cities trying to escape judgment (Jeremiah 4:29; Jeremiah 39:4) and for a command to leave Babylon (Isaiah 48:20). In the tabernacle passages, the "middle bar" runs straight through the frame.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core idea is swift movement away from something, usually to escape. Context supplies the reason and the moral weight.

1. To flee or run away from danger or a threatening person. This is by far the most common sense: Moses "fled from the face of Pharaoh" (Exodus 2:15).

2. To slip away or vanish quickly, said poetically of life and time: "They flee away, they see no good" (Job 9:25).

3. To drive or chase someone away, causing them to flee: Nehemiah says of a compromised priest's son, "I drove him from me" (Nehemiah 13:28; compare Proverbs 19:26).

4. To pass or run straight through, said of the tabernacle's middle bar: "The middle bar shall pass through the midst of the boards from end to end" (Exodus 26:28; Exodus 36:33).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the subject is a person in danger, bârach simply reports escape, and often a successful one. Jacob gets away from Laban, and David gets away from Saul many times. The direction of the flight matters. Running from an enemy can be wisdom. Running from the presence of the LORD is rebellion, and in Jonah's story it fails. When the prophets give the word as a command, as in "Flee" (Isaiah 48:20), the action becomes obedience. Then flight is how God's people separate themselves from what He is judging.

The poetic uses carry the urgency of the fugitive into new settings. In Job, what runs away is a person's own life. In Song of Solomon 8:14, the NKJV renders the word "Make haste," probably because the English "flee" would suggest running away. The same speed is turned toward love.

4. Theological Significance

Three truths stand out. First, God's presence has no outer edge. A fugitive can cross a border and leave a king behind, but there is no border beyond God. Amos 9:1–3, using a different Hebrew verb for fleeing, presses this as a warning: whoever flees "shall not get away," whether he digs into the depths, climbs to heaven, or hides at the bottom of the sea.

Second, God sees fugitives. The people in these stories are often lonely, displaced, and powerless, and that is where He meets them.

Third, sin produces flight. The word follows the breakdown of David's household after his sin. It also gives Job language for the deepest kind of vulnerability, which is that human life itself is running out. A person who flees is exposed and unsheltered. Job sees that every mortal is in that condition, since "He flees like a shadow and does not continue" (Job 14:2).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 139:7 and Jonah 1–4: the runner who cannot get away. David asks, "Where can I go from Your Spirit? Or where can I flee from Your presence?" For him the question is a comfort. He goes on to picture heaven, the grave, and the far side of the sea, and finds God's hand holding him in every place. Jonah treats the same impossibility as a travel plan. He "arose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD," went down to Joppa, paid the fare, and went down into the ship (Jonah 1:3). The repeated "down" traces the direction of disobedience. The storm, the lot, and the fish show God's purpose overtaking him.

The surprise comes in Jonah 4:2. Jonah explains, "Therefore I fled previously to Tarshish; for I know that You are a gracious and merciful God, slow to anger and abundant in lovingkindness." Jonah did not misunderstand God's reach. He resented God's character. He ran from mercy that might be shown to Nineveh. Running from God is finally a matter of the heart and not of geography. The same presence that terrifies the rebel is the shelter David rested in.

Genesis 16 and 31: God intercepts the fugitive. Hagar "fled from her presence" (Genesis 16:6) into the wilderness. The Angel of the LORD found her by a spring and asked where she was going. She said she was "fleeing" from Sarai (Genesis 16:8). Then she named Him the God who sees (Genesis 16:13). The first person in Scripture recorded as giving God a name was a runaway slave woman. Jacob also fled twice, from Esau and then from Laban (Genesis 31:20–22). Each time God stepped in. He appeared to Jacob at Bethel as he fled from Esau (Genesis 28:10–15), an appearance Genesis 35:1, 7 later recalls, and He warned Laban in a dream not to harm him (Genesis 31:24).

Exodus 2:15 and 1 Samuel 19–27: flight into formation. Moses fled from Pharaoh into Midian and lost every advantage Egypt had given him. Forty years later, at Horeb, the God of his fathers spoke to him from the burning bush (Exodus 3). David fled from Saul to Ramah, to Achish, to caves and strongholds (1 Samuel 19:12, 18; 1 Samuel 21:10; 1 Samuel 27:4). Psalm 57 is titled for the time "when he fled from Saul into the cave," and in it David cries out that his soul trusts in God and takes refuge in the shadow of His wings. The court could no longer protect him, so he learned that God could. Hebrews 11:27, using a different word, says that Moses forsook Egypt by faith and "endured as seeing Him who is invisible."

2 Samuel 13–19: when the king becomes a fugitive again. After David's sin with Bathsheba, Nathan announced that the sword would never depart from his house (2 Samuel 12:10). The word bârach then follows that prophecy coming true. Absalom murders Amnon and flees to Geshur (2 Samuel 13:34, 37–38). Later Absalom rebels, and David tells his servants that they must flee or none of them will escape (2 Samuel 15:14). The man who once fled Saul as God's innocent anointed now flees his own son. He goes up the Mount of Olives weeping, with his head covered and his feet bare (2 Samuel 15:30). Sin had turned the king back into a fugitive.

Nehemiah 6:11: the man who would not run. Shemaiah, hired by Nehemiah's enemies, urged him to shut himself inside the temple to escape assassins. Nehemiah answered by asking whether a man like him should flee, and whether a man like him should enter the temple to save his life. As a layman he had no right to enter the holy place (compare Numbers 18:7). Running would have broken God's law, shown fear, and given his enemies "cause for an evil report" (Nehemiah 6:13). Wisdom sometimes runs from danger. Faithfulness sometimes refuses to run when fleeing would cost holiness or testimony.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

David's flight from Absalom has a striking parallel in the Gospels. David crossed the Kidron and climbed the Mount of Olives in tears (2 Samuel 15:23, 30). Jesus also crossed the Kidron to the Mount of Olives (John 18:1; Luke 22:39), but He went there knowing all that would come upon Him (John 18:4). His disciples fled, and He did not. Jesus described the hireling who "sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees," and then said that He, the Good Shepherd, lays down His life (John 10:11–13). The Greek uses a different word, but the contrast completes the pattern of the Old Testament fugitives. The true King stood firm where David had once fled.

Because Christ stood firm, fleeing takes on a new direction. Believers "have fled for refuge to lay hold of the hope set before us" (Hebrews 6:18). They are also told, "flee from idolatry" (1 Corinthians 10:14). Isaiah's command to the exiles to leave Babylon and flee from the Chaldeans, announcing that the LORD has redeemed His servant Jacob (Isaiah 48:20), sounds again in Revelation 18:4: "Come out of her, my people." The captives become witnesses as they leave.

The Song of Solomon ends with this same word turned toward love: "Make haste, my beloved" (Song of Solomon 8:14). Readers have long heard in this a likeness to the Bible's final prayer, "Even so, come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20). The connection is one of resonance and not stated fulfillment, but it fits the shape of the whole story. Scripture opens with people hiding from God and closes with His people begging Him to hurry.

7. Spirit & Life

Every believer runs toward something or away from something, and these passages ask which way we are going. Jonah's confession exposes the hidden motive. We often run from God when we do not like what He is doing, and that includes His mercy toward people we would rather see judged. The cure is not more distance. It is the honesty Jonah finally prayed, along with a fresh look at the gracious God he fled.

For the weary and displaced, Hagar's spring and David's cave speak plainly. Losing position, family peace, or safety does not put a person outside God's sight. The wilderness may be where He strips away what we leaned on so that we learn to lean on Him, as Psalm 57 shows. Job adds a sober realism. Our days run away from us, and they cannot be held. That is why the believer runs to the refuge of Hebrews 6:18 and not to anything that flees as fast as we do.

Discernment matters too. Nehemiah's example shows that some fear dressed up as prudence is really unfaithfulness. In Nehemiah 13:28 he also drove a compromised man away. There are things we must not flee from, and there are things we must drive from our presence.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 24:11 – Balak angrily tells Balaam to flee to his place after Balaam blesses Israel instead of cursing it. Human power cannot buy God's word.

  • Jeremiah 39:4; Jeremiah 52:7 – Zedekiah and his soldiers fled by night when Jerusalem fell, but they were overtaken, which shows how futile it is to flee judgment.

  • Amos 7:12 – Amaziah tells Amos to flee to Judah. Here the word becomes a tool for silencing a prophet.

  • Daniel 10:7 – Daniel's companions fled to hide in terror at a vision they could not see, while Daniel remained.

  • Job 20:24 – "He will flee from the iron weapon; A bronze bow will pierce him through." The wicked escape one danger only to meet another.

  • Hosea 12:12 – Jacob fled to Syria and served for a wife. Hosea reminds Israel of its humble, fugitive beginnings.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H4422 (mâlat) – a distinct Hebrew verb meaning to escape or slip away. It is related in meaning but is not a word-family relative. First Samuel 19:12 and 19:18 set the two verbs side by side when David "fled and escaped."

  • G5343 (pheugō) – the common Greek verb for flee. It is a thematic counterpart in the New Testament, used of the hireling who flees (John 10:12–13) and in the command "flee from idolatry" (1 Corinthians 10:14).

10. Other Relevant Insights

The tabernacle's middle bar was made "to pass through the boards from one end to the other" (Exodus 36:33). It ran through the center of the frame and held the separate boards together as one dwelling. As a pastoral analogy, and not a stated biblical symbol, it pictures what Paul describes when he writes of the whole building "fitted together" into "a holy temple in the Lord" (Ephesians 2:21) and of keeping "the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (Ephesians 4:3).
