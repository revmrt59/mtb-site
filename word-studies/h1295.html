POOL - (bᵉrêkâh, Strong's H1295)

Summary

The Hebrew word bᵉrêkâh means a pool or reservoir: a body of water gathered and held in place, usually near a town, and often built by human hands. In a land that depended on rain, a city's pool was a matter of life and death. People drank from it, watered gardens with it, and needed it to survive a siege. Because people came there for water, a pool could also be a public setting.

Scripture uses these pools as the setting for some of its most searching moments. At Jerusalem's Upper Pool, God sent Isaiah to call King Ahaz to trust Him. Years later, an Assyrian commander stood at the same spot and mocked King Hezekiah. In Isaiah 22, God rebukes His people for carefully securing their water supply while they "did not look to its Maker." Pools also became places of public judgment. Nahum pictures proud Nineveh draining away like a breached reservoir. The story reaches the New Testament at the Pool of Siloam, whose name John translates "Sent." There a man born blind, sent by Jesus, the One sent by the Father, washed and came back seeing. Through this ordinary word, Scripture keeps asking where our security lies: in the reservoir we have built, or in the God who sends the water.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Moses told Israel that Canaan was not like Egypt, "where you sowed your seed and watered it by foot." It was "a land of hills and valleys, which drinks water from the rain of heaven" (Deuteronomy 11:10–11). Rain came in seasons. A town therefore needed a way to catch and store water for the dry months and for times of war. A bᵉrêkâh met that need. It was a pool, whether natural or cut and walled by workers, that kept water within reach of the people.

The word appears in historical books, prophets, and poetry, and almost every use names a real place: Gibeon, Hebron, Samaria, Heshbon, and several pools in Jerusalem. Hebrew dictionaries connect the word to the root letters b-r-k, which are associated with kneeling and with blessing. Some have suggested that a pool was a place where animals knelt to drink, or that dependable water was itself a blessing. That connection is background only. It does not define what the word means in a given verse.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A literal pool or reservoir that holds water for a community. Most of these pools are named landmarks, such as "the pool of Gibeon" (2 Samuel 2:13) or "the Pool of Shelah by the King's Garden" (Nehemiah 3:15).

2. A figure of speech drawn from that ordinary object. A pool can picture gathered strength that suddenly drains away: "Though Nineveh of old was like a pool of water, now they flee away" (Nahum 2:8). It can also describe the still depth of a loved one's eyes (Song of Songs 7:4).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses mark a location. Descriptive words often tell the reader which pool is meant: the "upper pool" (Isaiah 7:3), the "lower pool" and the "old pool" (Isaiah 22:9, 11), the "King's Pool" (Nehemiah 2:14), and "the man-made pool" (Nehemiah 3:16). These labels suggest a city that tracked its water carefully and had built up a network of reservoirs over generations.

The word also appears among the great works of kings. Hezekiah "made a pool and a tunnel and brought water into the city" (2 Kings 20:20), and Solomon says, "I made myself water pools" (Ecclesiastes 2:6). Building a pool was a mark of royal power and wealth.

Pools often sat near roads and gates, so they could provide public settings for what happened there. That helps explain why Scripture sets several of its scenes there.

4. Theological Significance

A pool stores water, but it does not create any. Every reservoir in Israel depended on "the rain of heaven" that God sent (Deuteronomy 11:11). This simple fact runs through the passages that use bᵉrêkâh. Again and again, the danger is that people will trust the container and forget the Giver.

Solomon's pools show how that danger can look in a time of peace. In Ecclesiastes 2 he describes building houses, vineyards, gardens, and "water pools from which to water the growing trees of the grove" (Ecclesiastes 2:6). He was building a private paradise, a kind of Eden made by engineering and money. Yet when he looked at all his works, he judged them vanity and grasping for the wind (Ecclesiastes 2:11). The finest reservoirs in the kingdom could water his trees, but they could not satisfy his soul.

The pools also show that God's word is fulfilled in plain sight. Pools could be public places, and in Hebron the display at the pool made justice visible. In these passages God does not keep His justice hidden, and His word is carried out as He said.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The Upper Pool: two kings, one place (Isaiah 7:3; Isaiah 36:2; 2 Kings 18:17). When Aram and the northern kingdom of Israel marched against Jerusalem, the LORD told Isaiah, "Go out now to meet Ahaz… at the end of the aqueduct from the upper pool, on the highway to the Fuller's Field" (Isaiah 7:3). Ahaz was most likely inspecting his water supply, the very thing a king checks before a siege. God met him at that spot and told him not to fear, warning that if he would not believe, he would not be established (Isaiah 7:4, 9). Ahaz chose another path and sent tribute to the king of Assyria (2 Kings 16:7–8).

A generation later, Assyria itself came against Jerusalem. The Rabshakeh, the Assyrian king's spokesman, "stood by the aqueduct from the upper pool, on the highway to the Fuller's Field" (Isaiah 36:2; 2 Kings 18:17). The location is described in exactly the same words. The power Ahaz had trusted was now standing at the same water source and demanding surrender from Ahaz's son. Hezekiah responded by going into the house of the LORD and praying (Isaiah 37:1, 14–20), and God delivered the city. The same pool saw a king who refused to trust God and a king who brought his crisis to Him.

"You did not look to its Maker" (Isaiah 22:9, 11; 2 Kings 20:20). Scripture records Hezekiah's water project with honor (2 Kings 20:20). Isaiah 22 shows the other side of that kind of preparation. Many readers connect the chapter to the same crisis, though its exact date is debated. Facing siege, the people inspected the broken walls, "gathered together the waters of the lower pool," and "made a reservoir between the two walls for the water of the old pool" (Isaiah 22:9, 11). Every step was sensible. Then God says, "But you did not look to its Maker, nor did you have respect for Him who fashioned it long ago" (Isaiah 22:11).

God does not condemn the engineering. He condemns the turning away. In that day He called for weeping and repentance, but the people feasted instead, saying, in effect, "Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" (Isaiah 22:12–13). With their water secured, they acted as if they had no need of God. Wise preparation became a snare because it took the place of trust in the One who had planned the city's history long before.

Pools as places of public judgment (1 Kings 22:38; 2 Samuel 4:12; 2 Samuel 2:13). Elijah had told Ahab, "In the place where dogs licked the blood of Naboth, dogs shall lick your blood" (1 Kings 21:19). After Ahab died in battle, "someone washed the chariot at a pool in Samaria, and the dogs licked up his blood… according to the word of the LORD" (1 Kings 22:38). The prophecy was fulfilled at a pool in Samaria, Ahab's capital.

David also used a pool to make justice visible. The men who murdered Ishbosheth were executed and "hanged… by the pool in Hebron" (2 Samuel 4:12), where anyone coming for water could see that David had no part in their crime. At Gibeon, Joab's men and Abner's men "sat down, one on one side of the pool and the other on the other side" (2 Samuel 2:13). The bloodshed that followed set brother against brother. The pool that should have served both groups became the line that divided them.

A pool that cannot hold (Nahum 2:8). "Though Nineveh of old was like a pool of water, now they flee away. 'Halt! Halt!' they cry; but no one turns back." A pool exists to hold what has been gathered. For generations Nineveh had collected people, wealth, and military power. When God judged the city, all of it ran out like water through a broken wall, and no command could stop it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

After the exile, Nehemiah walked through the ruins of Jerusalem past "the King's Pool" (Nehemiah 2:14). The repairs that followed included "the wall of the Pool of Shelah by the King's Garden" (Nehemiah 3:15). The name Shelah is linked to the Hebrew idea of sending, and this pool is generally identified with the waters of Siloam. Isaiah had used a related name in Ahaz's day. He said that the people had refused "The waters of Shiloah that flow softly," the quiet supply God had given them, and that they would instead face the flood of Assyria (Isaiah 8:6–7). The gentle waters of Shiloah stood for God's quiet provision, and the people turned it down.

The Gospel of John returns to this same water. Jesus told a man born blind, "Go, wash in the pool of Siloam" (which is translated, Sent). "So he went and washed, and came back seeing" (John 9:7). John's translation of the name draws the reader's attention to it. Throughout his Gospel, Jesus is the One the Father sent. The water system whose wall Nehemiah's builders repaired became the place where a blind man, sent by the Sent One, washed and received his sight. At another pool, Bethesda (John 5:2), Jesus healed a man who had been ill for thirty-eight years (John 5:5). In both scenes the water does not save. Jesus does.

God had once complained that His people forsook "the fountain of living waters" and dug "broken cisterns that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). The Hebrew word there is a different one from bᵉrêkâh, but the image fits Nineveh's breached pool and Jerusalem's misplaced trust. The answer to that long story is a Person: Jesus, who sends the blind man to Siloam and heals the helpless man at Bethesda, and gives what no reservoir can hold.

7. Spirit & Life

The Upper Pool shows something about God's ways. He met Ahaz at the very spot where Ahaz was most likely checking his defenses. God often comes to us where we keep our security and asks whether we trust that security or Him. Ahaz answered with a treaty. Hezekiah answered with prayer. The question is asked just as plainly in our own lives.

Isaiah 22 does not ask believers to stop preparing. Hezekiah's tunnel was a good work. The warning concerns the heart that inspects the walls, counts the water, and never looks up. Savings, plans, and skills are reservoirs. They are useful, but they are not the source. When our preparations leave us feeling no need to pray, they have already become a snare.

Solomon's pools add a quieter warning. A person can build everything they want and still find it empty. Only the One who sends the rain can satisfy the soul.

The Hebrew root behind the word also suggests a fitting picture, though it is a picture and not a definition. Water that God provides is received by those who bend down for it. Jesus sent the blind man to stoop and wash at Siloam. He obeyed the word of the One who sent him there, and he came back seeing.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Nehemiah 2:14 — "Then I went on to the Fountain Gate and to the King's Pool, but there was no room for the animal under me to pass." The rubble around the city's water shows how complete Jerusalem's ruin was before God restored it.

  • Nehemiah 3:16 — The repairs reached "the man-made pool," near the tombs of David. Even the city's built reservoirs were part of the rebuilding God made possible.

  • Song of Songs 7:4 — "Your eyes like the pools in Heshbon by the gate of Bath Rabbim." Among the battle scenes and judgments, this one tender use pictures the still, refreshing depth of the beloved's eyes.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1293 (bᵉrâkâh), blessing — Hebrew dictionaries connect this word with the same root letters as bᵉrêkâh. The link is in the background of the language. It does not mean that every pool in Scripture stands for blessing (compare Ezekiel 34:26, where God sends "showers of blessing").

  • Thematic connections — The fountain and cisterns of Jeremiah 2:13, the waters of Shiloah in Isaiah 8:6, and the Greek word for the pools of Siloam and Bethesda in John 5:2 and John 9:7 belong to the same family of images. They are not occurrences of bᵉrêkâh.
