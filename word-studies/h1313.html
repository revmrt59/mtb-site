SPICE - (bâsâm, Strong's H1313)

Summary

The Hebrew word bâsâm means "spice" or "balsam," a costly, fragrant plant product prized in the ancient world. It appears only once in the Old Testament, at the high point of the Song of Solomon's garden poem: "I have gathered my myrrh with my spice" (Song of Solomon 5:1). The Bridegroom has just described his bride as a "garden enclosed" filled with rare plants and "all the chief spices" (4:12–14). She has called the winds to blow so that "its spices may flow out" and has asked her beloved to come (4:16). He comes at once, gathers the spice, and calls his friends to share the feast.

Read as Christians have long read the Song, as a picture of Christ and His people, this single verse shows how gladly the Lord receives the love of those who welcome Him. He calls the garden "my garden" and the spice "my spice." He treats the graces His people bring as a treasure that belongs to Him. Scripture elsewhere calls believers "the fragrance of Christ" (2 Corinthians 2:15). The spice the Bridegroom gathers is fruit His own grace has grown, and His enjoyment of it becomes a table spread for others.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bâsâm belongs to a small Hebrew word family for fragrant spices and balsam. Its close relative, besem (H1314), is far more common. That word names the "quality spices" of the holy anointing oil (Exodus 30:23), the treasure the Queen of Sheba brought Solomon (1 Kings 10:10), and the spices throughout the Song of Solomon's garden scenes. Lexicons generally treat the two as forms of the same word, though some connect bâsâm more specifically with the balsam plant. The difference does not change the meaning in Song 5:1.

In the biblical world, spices were imported, expensive, and associated with kings, temples, and burial. Hezekiah kept "the spices and precious ointment" beside his silver and gold (2 Kings 20:13). When the Song speaks of spice, it speaks of something rare and valuable.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Bâsâm means a fragrant spice or balsam, an aromatic substance valued for its scent. Its one use carries only this basic sense. The poem around the word supplies its richer significance. The bride and her love are being compared to the spice, but the word itself still means spice.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The only occurrence comes in the Bridegroom's speech in Song 5:1. Three of his declarations follow the same paired pattern: "my myrrh with my spice," "my honeycomb with my honey," "my wine with my milk." The repeated "my" is the key to the verse, because he speaks as one taking possession of what is his.

The verse also answers chapter 4. The related word besem appears three times in the lead-up. The bride's perfumes are better "than all spices" (4:10). Her garden holds "all the chief spices" (4:14). She asks the winds to blow "that its spices may flow out" (4:16). When bâsâm appears in 5:1, it marks the moment of gathering, after the spices have been praised and offered.

4. Theological Significance

The garden scene reveals a God who delights in His people. Zephaniah says of the Lord, "He will rejoice over you with gladness... He will rejoice over you with singing" (Zephaniah 3:17). The Bridegroom of the Song comes to His garden with that same gladness. He is not inspecting a field out of duty. He enjoys what He finds.

Across Scripture, fragrance marks what is pleasing to God. The anointing oil and incense of Exodus 30 were blended from "quality spices" and set apart for the sanctuary. Paul calls the Philippians' gift "a sweet-smelling aroma, an acceptable sacrifice, well pleasing to God" (Philippians 4:18). In Revelation the bowls of incense before the Lamb "are the prayers of the saints" (Revelation 5:8). The spice gathered in Song 5:1 fits this pattern, in which God receives the love, prayer, and giving of His people as something fragrant and precious.

Spice was also royal tribute. The Queen of Sheba brought "spices in great quantity," and "there never again came such abundance of spices" (1 Kings 10:10). That a king gathers spice from his bride's garden tells us how highly he values her love. In his eyes it is a treasure fit for a throne.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The garden enclosed (Song of Solomon 4:12–15). The Bridegroom states plainly what the garden means: "A garden enclosed is my sister, my spouse" (4:12). The garden is the bride herself, and its plants are her loveliness and her love. He names spikenard, saffron, calamus, cinnamon, frankincense, myrrh, and aloes (4:14). Most of these grew far from Israel and came by caravan at great cost. The garden is "enclosed" and "sealed," kept for him alone. The spices therefore picture a love that is costly, carefully tended, and reserved for one person.

The invitation and the claim (Song of Solomon 4:16–5:1a). The bride's words in 4:16 contain a small but telling shift. She begins, "Blow upon my garden," and ends, "Let my beloved come to his garden." In one breath the garden passes from her keeping into his. He answers at once: "I have come to my garden, my sister, my spouse; I have gathered my myrrh with my spice" (5:1). He accepts her gift and owns it gladly, calling the garden and the spice his own. Nothing in the text delays his coming. When the garden is offered, he enters.

In Christian reading, the Song pictures Christ and His Church, with the Song's first meaning still the love of a bridegroom and bride. On that reading, the passage shows the Lord's readiness to come where He is welcomed and His joy in claiming His people's devotion as His own possession.

From gathering to feast (Song of Solomon 5:1b). The verse does not end with the Bridegroom alone in the garden. He turns outward: "Eat, O friends! Drink, yes, drink deeply, O beloved ones!" The harvest of spice, honey, wine, and milk becomes a banquet. The intimacy between bridegroom and bride does not shut others out. It becomes the occasion for a feast.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament presents Christ as the Bridegroom who gave Himself for His bride "that He might present her to Himself a glorious church... holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27). His own self-giving was "an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma" (Ephesians 5:2). The fragrance His people carry is first His fragrance: "we are to God the fragrance of Christ" (2 Corinthians 2:15). Through them God "diffuses the fragrance of His knowledge in every place" (2:14).

Spices also mark the course of Jesus' earthly life. The Magi opened their treasures and gave Him "gold, frankincense, and myrrh" (Matthew 2:11), the royal homage once brought to Solomon now laid before the true King. At His death, Nicodemus brought a large amount of myrrh and aloes (John 19:39), and the body of Jesus was bound "in strips of linen with the spices, as the custom of the Jews is to bury" (John 19:40). These are the same kinds of spices named in the bride's garden in Song 4:14. The King who gathers spice from His bride first had spices wrapped around His own body in the tomb.

The Song's pattern of invitation and feast reappears in Christ's words to the church: "If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20). The story moves toward a wedding feast where the Bridegroom finally receives His bride and shares His joy with all His guests.

7. Spirit & Life

The spices in the bride's garden did not appear by accident. They were planted, tended, and guarded. In 4:16 the bride calls on the north and south winds to blow so the fragrance may be released. Christians have often heard in that wind a picture of the Holy Spirit, whose fruit is "love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control" (Galatians 5:22–23). The picture fits Scripture's teaching. The graces Christ enjoys in His people are graces His Spirit produced. When He says "my spice," He is gathering what He Himself has grown. That truth leaves no room for pride, and no room for despair. The believer's part is to open the garden.

The verse pairs "my myrrh with my spice." Myrrh was bitter and tied to burial, while the other spices were sweet. The Bridegroom gathers both. The pairing supports a fair pastoral reading. Christ values the obedience that comes with joy, and He also values the surrender that comes through suffering. Paul longed to know Christ in His resurrection power and in the fellowship of His sufferings (Philippians 3:10). A season of grief may yield myrrh, and the Lord does not count it as wasted. He gathers it with the spice.

The Bridegroom's joy also spills over to others. "Eat, O friends!" follows directly on "I have gathered." John writes that "our fellowship is with the Father and with His Son Jesus Christ," and he declares it so "that you also may have fellowship with us" (1 John 1:3). Private devotion to Christ is meant to feed His people. A life that has been opened to Him becomes a place where others are nourished.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Bâsâm occurs only in Song of Solomon 5:1, which is discussed above. The surrounding uses of the related word besem in Song 4:10, 4:14, and 4:16, along with Exodus 30:23 and 1 Kings 10:10, supply its context and are treated in Sections 1, 4, and 5.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H1314 (besem), lexical relative.** This is the common word for spice and fragrance. It is used for the holy anointing oil (Exodus 30:23), for royal treasure (1 Kings 10:10), and for the bride's garden (Song of Solomon 4:10, 14, 16).

  • Myrrh, canonical pairing. Myrrh is set directly beside spice in Song of Solomon 5:1, and the two appear together again among the gifts and burial spices given to Christ (Matthew 2:11; John 19:39–40).
