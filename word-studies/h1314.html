SPICES, PERFUMES - (besem, Strong's H1314)

Summary

The Hebrew word besem means a sweet-smelling spice, a perfume, or the fragrance that such costly substances give off. These were luxury goods, the kind of treasure kings stored beside gold and silver. They were not the ordinary seasonings of a family kitchen. In the NKJV the word is usually translated "spices," but it is also rendered "sweet-smelling," "perfumes," and "sweet smell."

Scripture uses this fragrance in four main settings. God commanded that these spices be given for the holy anointing oil of the tabernacle. Royal courts treasured them, from the Queen of Sheba's gift to Solomon to Hezekiah's storehouse. The Song of Solomon uses them as its richest picture of the delight between a bride and her beloved. Isaiah warns that when pride rules, the "sweet smell" of luxury will be replaced by a stench.

Taken together, these settings show that something precious and fragrant can honor God, express love, or become a snare, depending on whose glory it serves. The word's path leads to Christ, who "has loved us and given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma" (Ephesians 5:2). It also leads to His people, whose devotion is meant to rise to Him like fragrance from a garden.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Besem belongs to the world of ancient aromatics. These were resins, barks, and oils carried by camel caravans from distant lands such as Sheba in southern Arabia (1 Kings 10:2; Ezekiel 27:22). Because they were rare and had to travel so far, they were extremely valuable.

Scholars still debate the exact plants behind the word. It may sometimes mean balsam in particular, and at other times aromatics in general. Scripture uses the word for what these substances do and what they are worth. It does not classify them by plant.

The word appears most often in three places. Exodus uses it for the tabernacle. The accounts of Solomon and Hezekiah use it for royal treasure. The Song of Solomon gathers six occurrences, in six verses, into its love poetry.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A costly aromatic substance: spice or balsam used in sacred oil, as perfume, or as a trade good (Exodus 30:23; 1 Kings 10:10).

2. The fragrance itself: the sweet smell that comes from such substances (Isaiah 3:24; Song of Solomon 4:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Where besem appears tells us what it means in each passage. In Exodus, the spices are given to God and set apart for His sanctuary. In Kings and Chronicles, they show up in lists of treasure: "the silver and gold, the spices and precious ointment" (2 Kings 20:13). In Esther 2:12 they are beauty treatments used in a long preparation to meet a king. In the Song of Solomon the substance becomes imagery: a fragrance describes the beloved, and the lovers' garden is full of spices. In Isaiah 3:24 the word is translated simply "sweet smell" and set against "a stench."

4. Theological Significance

God is not indifferent to beauty and delight. He asked for "spices for the anointing oil and for the sweet incense" (Exodus 25:6). That request shows that worship involved the sense of smell as well as sight. The God who made fragrance claimed the finest of it for His dwelling place.

The same spices also expose the human heart. Precious things can be received as gifts and given back in honor, or they can be hoarded as proof of one's own greatness. The pattern grows larger in Ezekiel 27:22. There, the merchants of Sheba and Raamah trade "the choicest spices, all kinds of precious stones, and gold" in the markets of proud Tyre, where they are goods for profit. Revelation 18:13 lists cinnamon and incense among the cargo of fallen Babylon. Something that was once set apart for God's house becomes, in the world's markets, one more thing to buy and sell.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 30:23 — the fragrance of holiness. God told Moses, "take for yourself quality spices," and the passage lists exact weights of myrrh, "sweet-smelling cinnamon," and "sweet-smelling cane." The precise measures matter. The offering was costly, and it had to be prepared according to God's design rather than human preference. Later, in 1 Chronicles 9:29–30, Levites were placed in charge of the spices, and "some of the sons of the priests made the ointment of the spices."

God then guarded this formula. Anyone who made oil like it for common use was to be cut off from his people (Exodus 30:32–33). Fragrance cannot be seen, yet it fills every corner of a room. The tabernacle therefore carried a scent that belonged to the Lord alone. When someone breathed it in, they were standing where God had chosen to meet His people.

Song of Solomon 4:10–16 — the garden of love. The groom exclaims, "How much better than wine is your love, And the scent of your perfumes Than all spices!" (Song of Solomon 4:10). He calls his bride an enclosed garden (Song of Solomon 4:12) and lists its plants, ending with "all the chief spices" (Song of Solomon 4:14). She answers, "Blow upon my garden, That its spices may flow out. Let my beloved come to his garden" (Song of Solomon 4:16).

The delight goes both ways. She later says his cheeks are like a bed of spices (Song of Solomon 5:13). Fragrance in the Song belongs to love that is rightly ordered.

The setting also deserves attention. After the Fall, the ground was cursed with thorns and thistles (Genesis 3:18). The Song pictures a cultivated garden that produces precious spices instead. Scripture does not state this contrast directly, but it is a fruitful echo. The Song's garden recalls Eden, which was lost, and shows the kind of beauty God intends love to produce.

1 Kings 10:10 and Isaiah 39:2 — glory received and glory displayed. The Queen of Sheba brought "spices in great quantity," and "There never again came such abundance of spices." She came because she had heard of Solomon's fame "concerning the name of the LORD" (1 Kings 10:1). Her lavish gift honored a kingdom resting under God's favor.

Generations later, Hezekiah "was pleased with" the Babylonian envoys. He showed them "the spices and precious ointment." Indeed, "There was nothing in his house… that Hezekiah did not show them." Isaiah's response was that all of it would be carried to Babylon (Isaiah 39:6). The same kind of treasure that once honored God's king had become a stage for a king's own pride, and it was to be carried off by the very nation whose envoys had seen it.

Isaiah 3:24 — the sweet smell taken away. The "daughters of Zion are haughty," walking "with outstretched necks And wanton eyes" (Isaiah 3:16). God promises to strip away their finery, including "the perfume boxes" (Isaiah 3:20). The verdict follows: "Instead of a sweet smell there will be a stench." Perfume could cover the body, but it could not cover the pride that God saw. When judgment came, the fragrance that had advertised their status was replaced by the smell of ruin.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The spices of Exodus 30 went into the oil that anointed priests. The very title "Messiah," which means Anointed One, comes from this world of fragrant anointing. Jesus fulfills what that oil pointed to. Peter tells "how God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power" (Acts 10:38). In Jesus, anointing is no longer a matter of fragrant oil alone; it is the presence and power of the Holy Spirit. Paul says that Christ "has loved us and given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma" (Ephesians 5:2). His self-giving love is the fragrance that pleases the Father.

The royal passages lead to Him as well. Jesus told His generation that the queen of the South came from the ends of the earth to hear Solomon's wisdom, "and indeed a greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42). Long before He said this, wise men had already brought gold, frankincense, and myrrh to the young Child-King (Matthew 2:11). Those gifts recall the caravans that came to Solomon.

The burial accounts also resonate. Asa was laid "in the bed which was filled with spices" prepared by perfumers (2 Chronicles 16:14). He was honored in death even though, in his final illness, he had looked to physicians and not to the Lord (2 Chronicles 16:12). The true King, by contrast, was faithful to the end. Nicodemus brought about a hundred pounds of myrrh and aloes for Jesus' body (John 19:39–40). The Gospel writes in Greek, not with this Hebrew word, but the royal honor is the same. Unlike Asa, Jesus did not stay in His tomb.

Paul also calls the union of husband and wife "a great mystery… concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). Read in that light, the Song's garden of spices pictures the delight Christ takes in His bride, and the devotion she returns to Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Fragrance cannot be seen, yet everyone in the room notices it. Paul says God spreads "the fragrance of His knowledge" through believers, and that "we are to God the fragrance of Christ" (2 Corinthians 2:14–15, paraphrased). A life given to God affects the people around it in ways no one can easily point to.

In the Song, the bride asks both the cold north wind and the warm south wind to blow, "That its spices may flow out" (Song of Solomon 4:16). The hard wind and the gentle one both release her fragrance for her beloved.

Ancient perfumers also had to grind and blend their aromatics before the full scent was released (compare Exodus 30:25, 36). The comparison is an analogy rather than something the text states, but it is a faithful one. Christ "was bruised for our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:5), and His suffering became the aroma of our salvation. In the same way, Paul says that "our light affliction… is working for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory" (2 Corinthians 4:17). Trials do not waste a believer. They can release what grace has grown.

Esther 2:12 adds a sober note. A young woman spent twelve months in preparation, "six months with perfumes," before she could enter the king's presence. That was the price of approaching a Persian king. Christ, our King, washes and prepares His bride Himself (Ephesians 5:26–27). His people still give themselves to that preparation, but they do so with confidence that He will make them ready, not with anxiety about whether they will be accepted.

Hezekiah's story is a warning to every believer who has received good gifts. Whatever God gives can be returned to Him in worship, or it can be put on display to win the admiration of Babylon.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 35:28 — The leaders brought spices and oil for the light and the incense. The tabernacle's fragrance came from willing gifts.

  • 1 Chronicles 9:29 — Levites were appointed over "the incense and the spices." Caring for the sanctuary's fragrance was holy service.

  • 2 Chronicles 9:24 — The kings of the earth brought Solomon gifts year by year, including spices, as they sought to hear his wisdom (2 Chronicles 9:23–24).

  • 2 Chronicles 32:27 — Hezekiah built treasuries for silver, gold, precious stones, and spices. This verse sets up the later display of his wealth.

  • Song of Solomon 6:2 — The beloved goes down "to the beds of spices." In the Song, love seeks out the place of fragrance.

  • Song of Solomon 8:14 — The Song closes by calling the beloved to come quickly over "the mountains of spices." The book ends in longing.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The "spices" carried by the Ishmaelites in Genesis 37:25, and sent by Jacob in Genesis 43:11, come from a different Hebrew word. These passages show that aromatics were valued from the patriarchs' time onward, but they are not uses of besem. In the same way, the incense recipe in Exodus 30:34 uses another Hebrew term. That is why this study ties besem to the anointing oil of Exodus 30:23 in particular.
