DAUGHTER, TOWNS, VILLAGES - (bath, Strong's H1323)

Summary

Bath is the ordinary Hebrew word for "daughter." Most often it means a man's or woman's female child or descendant. It also has wider uses. It can name the women of a people ("the daughters of Canaan"). It can describe the small towns that depend on a larger city, which the NKJV translates as "villages" or "towns." It also appears in vivid idioms. Sarah is literally "a daughter of ninety years," and the pupil of the eye is "the daughter of the eye." Most significantly, the prophets call Jerusalem and God's people "the daughter of Zion" and "the daughter of my people."

That prophetic title carries the word's deepest weight. God speaks of His people as a father speaks of a daughter. He guards her, grieves over her ruin, and finally calls her to rejoice. Jeremiah weeps over "the daughter of my people" as the city falls. Zechariah tells the same daughter, "Behold, your King is coming to you," and the Gospels see that promise fulfilled when Jesus rides into Jerusalem. Along the way the word also marks real women in Israel's story: foreign daughters who drew Israel toward idols, and the daughters of Zelophehad, whose plea God Himself upheld.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bath is a common Hebrew noun found throughout the Old Testament. It appears in genealogies, marriage arrangements, laws, poetry, and prophecy. Genesis uses it constantly, in the steady refrain of "sons and daughters" in chapters 5 and 11 and in the patriarchs' marriages. The historical books use it for royal mothers and political alliances. The prophets, especially Jeremiah and Lamentations, turn it into a name for Jerusalem herself.

The word's geographic use comes from a family picture. A fortified city was pictured as a parent, and the unwalled settlements that depended on it were its "daughters." When Israel took "Heshbon and in all its villages" (Numbers 21:25), the Hebrew speaks of Heshbon's daughters. Scripture can also call a city "a mother in Israel" (2 Samuel 20:19).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A female child or descendant. This is the ordinary sense, as with Dinah, "the daughter of Leah" (Genesis 34:1). It can also mean a granddaughter, as with Maachah, whom the NKJV calls the granddaughter of Abishalom (1 Kings 15:2).

2. A woman as a member of a people, place, or kind. The phrase "the daughters of Canaan" (Genesis 28:1) means Canaanite women, and "daughters of Jerusalem" means the women of that city. An older man may use the word as a warm address to a younger woman, as Boaz does with Ruth (Ruth 2:8; 3:10).

3. A city or people personified as a woman, as in "the daughter of Zion" or "the daughter of Babylon" (2 Kings 19:21; Isaiah 47:1).

4. A dependent settlement, the "towns" or "villages" that belong to a larger city (Numbers 21:25; Joshua 15:45).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Hebrew often uses "daughter of" to describe what something is or what belongs to it, and several idioms grow from this habit:

  • Age: Sarah is "a daughter of ninety years," which the NKJV renders "ninety years old" (Genesis 17:17).

  • The eye: the pupil is the "daughter of the eye" (Psalm 17:8).

  • Plants: Joseph's "branches" are a tree's daughters running over the wall (Genesis 49:22).

  • Wild birds: "a companion of ostriches" (Job 30:29) is literally a companion of the ostrich's daughters.

  • Character: Hannah begs Eli not to count her a "daughter of Belial," meaning a wicked woman (1 Samuel 1:16).

In prophecy, the personal tone of the word is deliberate. A city called "daughter" can be shamed, abandoned, mourned, or rescued. When the prophets add "virgin" (2 Kings 19:21) or "my people," the address becomes more tender and more urgent.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture first shows the word as a warning. God forbade Israel to take the daughters of Canaan for their sons, because those daughters would lead their sons into the worship of other gods (Exodus 34:16; Deuteronomy 7:3). In Genesis 34, Dinah's visit to "the daughters of the land" ends with Shechem's family proposing that the two peoples exchange daughters (34:9). At Acacia Grove, "the people began to commit harlotry with the women of Moab" (Numbers 25:1), and Baal worship followed. After the exile, Ezra tore his garments when he learned that "the holy seed is mixed with the peoples of those lands" (Ezra 9:2). By Malachi's day, marrying "the daughter of a foreign god" counted as profaning what the LORD loves (Malachi 2:11). The danger was never the woman's ethnicity. It was a union that carried the heart to another god.

The same word then becomes God's name for His own people. The daughter of Zion is left "as a booth in a vineyard" (Isaiah 1:8), cries out like a woman in labor (Jeremiah 4:31), and yet laughs at the Assyrian king who threatens her (2 Kings 19:21). The title holds together chosenness and frailty. Her beauty and her danger are both measured by whose daughter she is. Even her suffering is not final. Micah tells the laboring daughter of Zion that she must go to Babylon, and then he adds that she will be delivered there, because the LORD will redeem her (Micah 4:10).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The daughters of Zelophehad (Numbers 27:1–11; 36:1–12). Zelophehad died with no sons. Under the existing pattern of inheritance, his name and his share of the Promised Land would have disappeared. His five daughters came to Moses, Eleazar, and the leaders at the tabernacle door and asked for a possession among their father's brothers. Moses did not rule on his own authority. He brought their case before the LORD, and God answered that the daughters spoke what was right (27:7). God then made their case into a standing law: when a man dies without a son, his inheritance passes to his daughter (27:8). When the tribal leaders raised a fair concern about land passing to other tribes, God refined the ruling so that the daughters would marry within their father's tribe (36:6–8). Their inheritance and the tribal allotments were both preserved. These women believed the land promise applied to them, and God agreed.

"The apple of Your eye" (Psalm 17:8). David prays, "Keep me as the apple of Your eye; Hide me under the shadow of Your wings." The Hebrew speaks of the "little man, the daughter of the eye," which is the pupil, where a person sees his own small reflection. No part of the body is guarded more quickly. The eyelid shuts and the hand comes up before a person has time to think. David asks God for exactly that kind of reflexive, jealous protection. (Deuteronomy 32:10 and Zechariah 2:8 use the same picture with other Hebrew words.)

"The daughter of my people" (Jeremiah 8–9; Lamentations). Jeremiah condemns the false prophets who healed the hurt of "the daughter of My people" only slightly (Jeremiah 8:11). Then the grief breaks open. He is hurt because she is hurt, and he asks whether there is no balm in Gilead (8:21–22). He wishes his head were waters so that he could weep day and night for her slain (9:1). In this passage the prophet's voice and the LORD's voice blend; "Why have they provoked Me to anger?" interrupts the lament (8:19). The judgment is real. Lamentations says the Lord Himself covered the daughter of Zion with a cloud in His anger (2:1–4). Yet the one who weeps does not call the city "the rebels" or "that nation." He calls her "daughter." In Lamentations his eyes fail with tears over her destruction (2:11; 3:48), even as compassionate mothers are driven to unspeakable acts in the famine (4:10). Holiness does not cancel grief here, and grief does not soften the judgment. God's sorrow and God's verdict stand side by side.

From lament to song (Zephaniah 3:14–17; Zechariah 2:10; 9:9). The same daughter who wept is later commanded to sing. Zephaniah calls the daughter of Zion to shout and rejoice, because the LORD has taken away her judgments and the King of Israel is in her midst (3:14–15). He even says that God will rejoice over her with singing (3:17). Zechariah promises that the LORD will come and dwell in her midst (2:10). Then he announces: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! Shout, O daughter of Jerusalem! Behold, your King is coming to you; He is just and having salvation, Lowly and riding on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9). The King comes to a daughter, not to a conquered province. He comes lowly, and He brings salvation with Him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Gospels state the fulfillment directly. When Jesus enters Jerusalem on a donkey, John 12:15 cites Zechariah: "Fear not, daughter of Zion; Behold, your King is coming, Sitting on a donkey's colt" (see also Matthew 21:5). The long story of the daughter of Zion, from Isaiah's lonely booth through Jeremiah's tears, arrives at a particular day and a particular Man.

Jesus also carries Jeremiah's grief. As He neared the city, He wept over it (Luke 19:41). The tears that once fell for "the daughter of my people" now fall from the eyes of the King Himself.

The word also looks toward the wider family of God. Through Isaiah, God calls the scattered "My daughters" whom He will bring from the ends of the earth (Isaiah 43:6). Joel foretold that "your sons and your daughters shall prophesy" when the Spirit is poured out (Joel 2:28), and Peter proclaimed that promise fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 2:17). Paul's promise that God will be a Father to His "sons and daughters" (2 Corinthians 6:18) echoes this language. The daughter of Zion becomes a household of daughters and sons gathered to the Father through Christ.

7. Spirit & Life

God does not judge His people from a distance. The voice that pronounces sentence in Jeremiah is the same voice that weeps over the daughter of His people. A believer under God's discipline can know that the hand that corrects belongs to a Father whose heart is breaking. The same believer can pray David's prayer with confidence and ask to be kept as the pupil of God's eye. That prayer is not presumption. God taught His people to ask for it.

The daughters of Zelophehad show what faith looks like when the rules seem to leave a person out. They did not grumble in their tents. They brought their case into God's presence and trusted Him to be just, and God called their request right. Petitions grounded in His promises are welcome before Him.

The Song of Solomon adds a quieter note. The bride speaks to the "daughters of Jerusalem" again and again (Song 1:5). She asks them not to stir up love before its time (2:7; 3:5; 8:4). She asks for their help when her beloved is gone (5:8), and she describes his worth to them (5:16). Love that honors God is not hidden. It is guarded, tested, and celebrated before people who watch and care.

The warning about foreign daughters still speaks. What a person joins his heart to will, in time, draw that heart in its own direction.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 17:17 — Sarah is literally "a daughter of ninety years." Her age made the promise seem impossible, and God kept it anyway.

  • Ruth 3:10–11 — Boaz calls the Moabite widow "my daughter" as he blesses her and commends her character. In his mouth, a foreigner is received with the tenderness the word carries.

  • Psalm 45:10 — The royal bride is told, "Listen, O daughter," and called to forget her father's house for the sake of the King.

  • Isaiah 47:1 — The "virgin daughter of Babylon" must come down and sit in the dust. Proud nations can bear the title too, and God can humble them.

  • Isaiah 56:5 — God promises faithful eunuchs a name better than sons and daughters. Belonging to Him outweighs even family descent.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1121 (ben, son) — a canonical pairing. "Sons and daughters" is a constant pairing in Scripture (Genesis 5:4; Joel 2:28).

  • H1330 (bethulah, virgin) — a canonical pairing in the title "the virgin, the daughter of Zion" (2 Kings 19:21).

  • H380 ('ishon, pupil, "apple") — a canonical pairing. It joins bath in the idiom of Psalm 17:8.
