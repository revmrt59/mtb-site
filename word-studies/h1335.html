PART, PIECE - (bether, Strong's H1335)

Summary

The Hebrew word bether means a part or piece, something cut off from a whole. It is a very concrete word. As a common noun, it occurs 3 times in 3 verses of the Hebrew Bible (Genesis 15:10; Jeremiah 34:18–19), and each time it names the halves of animals cut in two when a covenant was made. In Genesis 15, Abram lays the pieces opposite each other, and God's own presence, appearing as "a smoking oven and a burning torch," passes between them after a deep sleep has fallen on Abram. In Jeremiah 34, the leaders of Judah walk between the parts of a calf to seal a promise, then break that promise. God answers by holding them to the meaning of the pieces they walked through.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

Together these passages set God's faithfulness beside human unfaithfulness in one vivid image. In Genesis, God binds Himself to His promise and Abram receives it. In Jeremiah, people take the oath and fail. That contrast points toward the gospel, where God keeps His promise to Abraham at His own cost through Jesus Christ. A related place name with the same spelling, "the mountains of Bether" in the Song of Solomon, belongs to a love poem that waits for the beloved's coming "until the day breaks."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bether is a Hebrew noun closely related to the verb bathar, to cut in two. Genesis 15:10 uses both words together: Abram "cut them in two" (the verb), then "placed each piece" (the noun) opposite the other. The noun therefore names exactly what the verb produces.

The setting behind each use of the common noun is an ancient way of making a binding agreement. Hebrew speaks of "cutting" a covenant, and Genesis 15:18 uses that idiom when it says the Lord made a covenant with Abram. Animals were divided, and the one making the pledge walked between the halves. Jeremiah 34 shows that the act was understood as a solemn self-curse, in effect saying, "Let this happen to me if I break my word."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Bether has one basic sense, and a related place name shares its spelling:

1. A piece or part cut from something, specifically the halves of animals divided for a covenant ceremony (Genesis 15:10; Jeremiah 34:18–19). The word describes the divided piece itself. Its covenant weight comes from the ceremony in which the pieces were used.

2. Related place name: "the mountains of Bether" (Song of Solomon 2:17). Here the same Hebrew spelling is a place name, which Strong's lists as a separate entry, H1336 (Bether). The NKJV keeps it as a place name. Because the common noun means piece, some interpreters take the phrase to describe cleft or rugged mountains, hills that look broken apart. Whether Bether names a real place or describes the terrain, the poem pictures a distant, broken landscape.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The common noun and the related place name appear in very different settings. In Genesis and Jeremiah, bether belongs to oath-taking and shows up at the most serious moment of the ceremony, when someone passes between the pieces. What matters in those passages is who walks between the parts. In Genesis, the answer is God alone. In Jeremiah, it is the king's officials, the priests, and the people. The same object carries opposite outcomes depending on who has bound himself by it.

Genesis 15 also shows a closely related word at work. Verse 10 uses bether for the pieces Abram arranges, and verse 17 uses a different Hebrew word for "pieces" (gezer) when the smoking oven and burning torch pass "between those pieces." Both words describe the same divided animals, so the two verses read as one scene.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God takes promises with the utmost seriousness, both the ones He makes and the ones people make in His name. The divided animals turned a promise into a picture of death. Whoever walked between them was, in effect, declaring that he would rather be torn apart than be unfaithful.

That is why God's action in Genesis 15 matters so much. The Lord who cannot lie still chose to give Abram a sign Abram could see, binding Himself in the strongest terms Abram's world understood. Hebrews 6:13 draws out the same truth: "because He could swear by no one greater, He swore by Himself." God's promises rest on His own character, and He goes out of His way to help weak people believe them.

Jeremiah 34 shows the other side of the same seriousness. God treats a broken oath as a direct offense against Himself, and in this case against the dignity of the poor who were wronged by it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 15:7–18: God passes between the pieces. God has promised Abram the land, and Abram asks, "how shall I know that I will inherit it?" (Genesis 15:8). God does not answer with an explanation. He tells Abram to prepare a covenant ceremony. Abram "cut them in two, down the middle, and placed each piece opposite the other" (Genesis 15:10). Then he waits, driving away the birds of prey that come down on the carcasses (Genesis 15:11). As the sun is going down, a deep sleep and a terrifying darkness fall on Abram (Genesis 15:12). Then, when the sun has gone down and it is dark, "a smoking oven and a burning torch... passed between those pieces" (Genesis 15:17).

Abram never walks between the pieces. The fire and smoke represent the presence of God, the same imagery that later marks God's presence at Sinai and in the pillar that led Israel. In this scene God is the only one who passes through. He places Himself under the oath, as if to say, "Let Me be torn apart before this promise fails." Abram, asleep, contributes nothing to the guarantee except the animals God told him to bring. The promise is God's to keep.

Jeremiah 34:8–22: the people walk between the parts. Centuries later, with Babylon's army surrounding Jerusalem, King Zedekiah and the people made a covenant to free their Hebrew slaves, as God's law required. Afterward they changed their minds and took those servants back into bondage (Jeremiah 34:10–11), and God's words to them mention that Babylon's army had for a time withdrawn from the city (Jeremiah 34:21). God reminds them that they had cut a calf in two and passed between its parts (Jeremiah 34:18–19). Because they broke the promise sealed by that ceremony, He will hand them over to their enemies, and their bodies will become food for the birds and beasts (Jeremiah 34:20).

The judgment fits the oath they swore. They had walked between the parts of a dead animal and invited its fate on themselves, and now that fate would come on them. Read side by side, the two scenes echo each other. The birds Abram drove away from the pieces in Genesis 15 are, in Jeremiah 34, given the bodies of the oath-breakers. When God alone walks between the pieces, the promise stands. When people walk between them in their own strength, the oath turns against them.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pieces in Genesis 15 raise a question that only the gospel answers. If God pledged Himself under the penalty of the divided animals, and if Abraham's descendants proved to be like the leaders in Jeremiah 34, how would the promise survive? Only three chapters before Jeremiah 34, the same prophet announces God's answer: a new covenant, in which God Himself will write His law on His people's hearts and forgive their sin (Jeremiah 31:31–34).

At the Last Supper, Jesus took the cup and called it "the new covenant in My blood" (Luke 22:20). Paul writes that Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us, "that the blessing of Abraham might come upon the Gentiles in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:13–14, paraphrased). The New Testament does not directly connect the cross to the divided animals of Genesis 15. Even so, the parallel is hard to miss. The God who once passed alone between the pieces later came in the flesh and bore the curse that covenant-breakers deserved. Hebrews ties the oath to Abraham directly to Christian hope: God confirmed His promise by an oath "that by two immutable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we might have strong consolation" (Hebrews 6:18).

7. Spirit & Life

Abram's sleep is one of the most encouraging details in Genesis. The man who would become the father of the faithful was in the deep sleep that had fallen on him when God guaranteed the promise (Genesis 15:12, 17). The believer's assurance works the same way. It does not depend on how strongly we hold on to God; it depends on God having bound Himself to His word. Hebrews calls those who rely on this oath people "who have fled for refuge to lay hold of the hope set before us" (Hebrews 6:18). Refugees bring nothing with them but their need.

Jeremiah 34 gives the needed warning. Judah's leaders made a good vow while Jerusalem was under siege and dropped it afterward. Many people make promises to God in the middle of a crisis. Their story shows how fragile a vow made in our own strength can be. It also shows that God watches how His people treat the vulnerable, because the broken promise here was a promise to free fellow Israelites.

The Song of Solomon adds a different note. The bride calls, "Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, Turn, my beloved... Upon the mountains of Bether" (Song of Solomon 2:17). The poem is about human love, but its longing fits the church's present life. We live in the time before the day breaks, across rough country from the One we love, asking Him to come quickly.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All three occurrences of the common noun bether, and the related place name in Song of Solomon 2:17, have been discussed above. One more detail deserves notice. Jeremiah 34:19 lists who passed between the parts of the calf: the princes of Judah and of Jerusalem, the eunuchs, the priests, and all the people of the land. The whole community took the oath, which is why the judgment that followed reached the whole community.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1334 (bathar, to cut in two): the verb related to bether. Abram "cut them in two" in Genesis 15:10, producing the pieces.

  • H1506 (gezer, piece): a different Hebrew word used for the same divided animals in Genesis 15:17, when the smoking oven and torch pass between them.

  • H1285 (berith, covenant) and H3772 (karath, to cut): distinct words paired in the idiom "to cut a covenant." Genesis 15:18 uses this phrase right after the pieces scene, and Jeremiah 34:18 refers to the covenant sealed by the divided calf.
