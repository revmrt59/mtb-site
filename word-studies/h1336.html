BETHER - (Bether, Strong's H1336)

Summary

Bether appears only once in the Bible, in the closing line of a love song in Song of Solomon 2:17. There the bride asks her beloved to be like a gazelle or a young stag "upon the mountains of Bether." The name comes from a Hebrew root meaning "to cut in pieces" or "to divide," so Bether probably means "division," "cleft," or "separation." The ancient Greek translation read the phrase as "mountains of ravines" or "hollows." Scripture does not make clear whether Bether named a real range of hills, perhaps the rugged country of Judah, or whether it is a poetic picture of "mountains of separation" lying between two people who love each other. The meaning is reasonably clear, but the exact location is unknown.

The setting is night, "until the day breaks and the shadows flee away," and the bride wants her beloved to cross the broken country between them with a stag's speed. The Song does not leave the picture there. Its final verse repeats the plea almost word for word but changes the place: the beloved is now called to "the mountains of spices" (Song 8:14). For Christian readers, this small name gives honest words for times when Christ seems far away. It also points to a Bridegroom who crosses the distance we cannot cross ourselves, and to a dawn when the waiting ends.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Bether is a place name found only in Song of Solomon 2:17. The NKJV, like many English versions, keeps it as a proper name, "the mountains of Bether." It is not connected with any recorded event, city, or people, and the verse gives no further geographic detail.

Bether is most likely built on the Hebrew verb meaning "to cut in pieces" or "to divide" (Strong's H1334). On that basis the name probably means "division," "cleft," or "separation." The ancient Greek translation known as the Septuagint did not treat the word as a name. It rendered the phrase "mountains of hollows," meaning ravines or clefts. The sense of divided, broken ground is therefore well supported. What remains uncertain is whether the writer meant a specific, known range or a descriptive phrase such as "the cleft mountains." Some interpreters think of the craggy hills around Jerusalem and Judah, cut through by deep ravines. Others read the phrase as purely poetic. A few scholars suggest a copyist's slip from "spices," the word in Song 8:14. The Hebrew text as we have it, however, reads Bether, and this study treats that reading as intended.

Bether should be kept distinct from Bithron (Strong's H1338). Bithron is a separately indexed name for a region of ravines connected with the Jordan Valley, which Abner's men crossed in 2 Samuel 2:29. The two names may share the idea of a "cleft" or "gorge," but they are different entries and do not refer to the same place.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Bether has no story of its own. It appears in a single cry of love at the end of the Song's second chapter. Earlier in the chapter the bride hears her beloved coming, leaping on the mountains and bounding over the hills, and she compares him to a gazelle or a young stag (Song 2:8–9). He calls her to come away with him because winter is past (2:10–14). She answers with confidence that "my beloved is mine, and I am his" (2:16). Then the chapter closes:

Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, Turn, my beloved, And be like a gazelle Or a young stag Upon the mountains of Bether. (Song 2:17)

Interpreters differ on exactly what "turn" means here. She may be calling him to come back to her, or she may be sending him off over the hills until morning. Either way, the picture is one of distance and night, with rough, broken country lying between the lovers. She asks him to move across that terrain with the sure-footed speed of a stag. The Song later repeats her plea in its very last verse: "Make haste, my beloved, And be like a gazelle Or a young stag On the mountains of spices" (Song 8:14).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

On its plain level, the Song celebrates love between a man and a woman as a good gift of God. That love includes desire, absence, and longing, and Bether names the ground that separates the lovers. Scripture states this much directly.

Read alongside the rest of the Bible, where God often speaks of His people as His bride, Jewish and Christian readers have long heard in this verse the longing of God's people for His presence. That reading is an implication drawn from the wider canon rather than something Song 2:17 says outright. It is a well-grounded implication, however, because the Bible uses marriage language for God and His people from the prophets to Revelation.

The change from Bether in 2:17 to "spices" in 8:14 is worth noticing. Both verses carry the same request in nearly the same words, yet the landscape has changed from a place of cleft and division to a place of fragrance. The Song does not explain the change. A reasonable reading sees movement in the book from separation toward a richer, sweeter communion. Even so, the book still ends with "Make haste." The longing is not finished, but the ground between the lovers now smells of spices.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never quotes Song 2:17. Its connections to Christ are canonical and thematic rather than explicit fulfillments.

The first connection is the night. The bride waits "until the day breaks and the shadows flee away." Paul uses similar language for the church's present life: "The night is far spent, the day is at hand. Therefore let us cast off the works of darkness, and let us put on the armor of light" (Romans 13:12). The church lives between Christ's first coming and His return. It belongs to Him, as the bride says "my beloved is mine," yet it still waits to see Him face to face. Christian preachers through the centuries have heard the mountains of Bether as everything that seems to stand between the believer and the conscious enjoyment of Christ, including sin, weakness, trouble, and death.

The second connection is the one who crosses the mountains. Isaiah sings, "How beautiful upon the mountains Are the feet of him who brings good news, Who proclaims peace... Who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'" (Isaiah 52:7). In Scripture, mountains often stand for what people cannot cross by their own strength, and God's grace comes over them to His people. Christ is the Beloved who comes across the ground of separation, and He comes all the way to the cross.

A third connection is more delicate and belongs to the level of resonance rather than plain statement. The same Hebrew root behind Bether appears in Genesis 15, where God confirms His promise to Abram. Abram took the animals, "cut them in two, down the middle, and placed each piece opposite the other" (Genesis 15:10). After the sun had gone down and it was dark, a smoking oven and a burning torch, signs of God's presence, passed between the pieces (Genesis 15:17). God Himself moved through the divided pieces in the dark and bound Himself to keep His promise. The Song never refers to Genesis 15, and we should not claim the writer intended the link. Still, the shared language invites reflection. In both passages the darkness is real, the ground is divided, and the faithful One is the one who crosses.

5. Spirit & Life

Song 2:17 gives believers permission to be honest. Scripture does not pretend that God's people always feel His nearness. The same bride who has just said "my beloved is mine, and I am his" goes on to speak of night, shadows, and broken hills between her and the one she loves. Belonging to Christ and longing for Him can sit side by side in the same heart. A believer who feels distance from God has not necessarily lost Him.

Notice also what the bride does not do. She does not set out to climb the mountains of Bether herself. She asks her beloved to come with the speed and sure footing of a gazelle. This pictures the way grace works. Our communion with God rests on His movement toward us, which Christ has already made and still makes, rather than on our ability to scale the heights. Prayer in the dark sounds like her words: "Turn, my beloved."

Finally, the verse is full of hope. The night has a limit, "until the day breaks." The Song's last word moves from the mountains of division to the mountains of spices, yet it still cries "Make haste." The Bible itself ends the same way, with the church still calling for the Lord to come (Revelation 22:20). Christians live in that tension, but they do not live in it without hope. Morning is coming, and the One who crosses mountains is not slow.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Song of Solomon 2:17 — The only occurrence of Bether. It gives the night setting, the plea to the beloved, and the picture of divided, broken mountains between the lovers.

  • Song of Solomon 2:8–9, 16 — The surrounding context, where the beloved is pictured as a gazelle leaping over hills and the bride declares mutual belonging.

  • Song of Solomon 8:14 — The book's final verse, which repeats the plea but names "the mountains of spices," showing a movement from separation toward communion.

  • 2 Samuel 2:29 — Bithron, a separately indexed and distinct place of ravines, useful for comparison but not the same location.

  • Genesis 15:9–10, 17 — The verb from the same root used for cutting the animals in two, with God passing between the pieces in the dark. This passage supplies a thematic echo, not a direct link.

  • Romans 13:12 — Paul's "the night is far spent, the day is at hand," which echoes the Song's waiting for daybreak in the life of the church.

  • Isaiah 52:7 — Good news coming over the mountains, a canonical picture of God's salvation crossing what separates His people from Him.
