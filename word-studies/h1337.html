BATH RABBIM - (Bath Rabbîym, Strong's H1337)

Summary

Bath Rabbim was a gate of the city of Heshbon, east of the Jordan River. Scripture names it only once, in the Song of Solomon, where the bridegroom tells his bride, "Your eyes like the pools in Heshbon By the gate of Bath Rabbim" (Song of Songs 7:4). The name joins two Hebrew words: bath, "daughter," and rabbim, "many" or "great." It is usually understood as "daughter of many" or "daughter of multitudes." Scripture does not explain why the gate carried this name. A gate called "daughter of multitudes" may have been a busy main entrance where crowds came and went, but that is a likely reading of the name rather than a stated fact.

The gate has no story of its own. It matters because of what it helps the bridegroom say. He reaches for a well-known landmark, the pools of water beside a Heshbon gate, to describe how his bride's eyes look to him: deep, clear and refreshing. The image belongs to a book that receives married love as a good gift from God and is not embarrassed to praise it. Bath Rabbim reminds readers that the Bible's poetry is rooted in real places that real people knew.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

Bath Rabbim is a named gate connected with Heshbon. The verse links the gate to "the pools in Heshbon," which suggests that the pools lay at or near this entrance to the city. The gate should be kept distinct from Heshbon itself, which has its own entry in Strong's (H2809). Although the name begins with "daughter," Bath Rabbim is a gate, not a woman.

The name is a Hebrew compound. Bath is the ordinary Hebrew word for "daughter." Rabbim is a plural form of the word for "many" or "great," so the phrase can be read as "daughter of many," "daughter of multitudes," or possibly "daughter of the great ones." "Gate of many" is a loose paraphrase, because the word "gate" is not part of the name itself. Hebrew sometimes speaks of a city or its people as a "daughter," as in the familiar phrase "daughter of Zion." That habit may explain the name here, but the reasoning behind it is not given. It is also unknown whether Bath Rabbim was the gate's official civic name or a name the poet used for it.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Bath Rabbim appears only in Song of Songs 7:4. The verse belongs to a poem in which the bridegroom praises his bride feature by feature. In the same verse he compares her neck to "an ivory tower" and her nose to "the tower of Lebanon Which looks toward Damascus." Each comparison draws on a landmark his hearers would have recognized, and the pools at the gate of Bath Rabbim are one of them.

Heshbon has a long history elsewhere in Scripture. It was the royal city of Sihon, king of the Amorites, until Israel took it during the journey to Canaan (Numbers 21:25–30). Afterward it was rebuilt and assigned among the tribes east of the Jordan (Numbers 32:37; Joshua 13:17), and it was later listed among the cities given to the Levites (Joshua 21:39). By the time of the prophets, Heshbon belonged to Moab and was named in oracles of judgment against that nation (Isaiah 15:4; 16:8–9; Jeremiah 48:2, 34, 45). None of those passages mentions Bath Rabbim. They do show that Heshbon was a city people knew, which helps explain why its pools could serve as a picture of beauty.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture records no event, promise or judgment connected to this gate. What the text states is simple: the bride's eyes are like the pools beside it. From that, readers can draw a careful conclusion. City pools stored water for the people who lived there and for travelers passing through, so the comparison points to eyes that are clear, deep and life-giving to look into.

The larger significance comes from the book in which the name appears. The Song of Solomon celebrates the love between a husband and wife with honest delight. Even its geography serves that purpose. Towers, pools and mountains from across the land are used to honor a beloved person. The created world and human building are not treated as distractions from God's goodness but as fitting ways to describe it.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

No New Testament passage refers to Bath Rabbim, and no Christ-centered pattern can be drawn from the gate itself. Many early and medieval Christian interpreters read the Song as a picture of Christ and His church. Some of them treated the pools at the "gate of multitudes" as an image of the church's clear vision of God's Word, open to all peoples. That reading belongs to the history of how Christians have interpreted the Song. Scripture does not teach it, and it should not be presented as the verse's intended meaning.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

The verse sets a quiet picture beside a crowded one. If Bath Rabbim was a busy gate, then the travelers, traders and noise passed right by still water. The bridegroom finds that kind of calm, clear depth in his bride's eyes. This is an inference from the setting rather than an explicit point of the text, but it fits the poem well.

The plainer lesson is worth keeping. God gave the Song of Solomon a place in His Word, and in it a husband looks at his wife with attention and gratitude, searching his whole world for words good enough to describe her. Married love that sees, treasures and speaks honor is part of what God calls good.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Song of Songs 7:4 — The only occurrence of Bath Rabbim. It links the gate to the pools of Heshbon and uses them as a picture of the bride's eyes.

  • Numbers 21:25–30 — Heshbon as Sihon's royal city, taken by Israel. This passage gives the historical background of the city in which the gate stood.

  • Joshua 13:17; 21:39 — Heshbon among the territories assigned east of the Jordan and among the Levitical cities.

  • Isaiah 15:4; Jeremiah 48:2, 34, 45 — Heshbon in later prophetic oracles against Moab. These passages show the city's changing history, though they do not mention the gate.
