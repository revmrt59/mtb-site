MIGHTY, MIGHTY MAN, WARRIOR - (gibbôwr, Strong's H1368)

Summary

Gibbôwr is the Hebrew word for a mighty one: a warrior, a champion, a person of exceptional strength or standing. It names Nimrod at the dawn of empire, Goliath on the field of Elah, David's elite fighters, and Boaz, the respected landowner of Bethlehem. The word itself is neither good nor bad. Everything depends on whose purposes that strength serves and where the mighty person places his trust.

Scripture also does something striking with this word. It takes the vocabulary of the battlefield and applies it to God Himself. The LORD is "mighty in battle" (Psalm 24:8). He goes forth "like a mighty man" against His enemies (Isaiah 42:13). He is "the Mighty One" who saves His people and rejoices over them with singing (Zephaniah 3:17). Isaiah gives that same title, "Mighty God," to a Child who will be born to carry the government on His shoulder (Isaiah 9:6). Meanwhile the prophets warn that no human champion can deliver himself. The word moves the reader from trust in human strength toward the strength of God, which finally comes to us in Jesus Christ.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gibbôwr belongs to Israel's world of tribal militias, border wars, and royal armies. Much of its usage clusters in the historical books, especially 2 Samuel and 1 Chronicles, where it appears again and again in rosters of fighting men and tribal musters. It also appears in the Psalms, Proverbs, and the prophets, where poets and preachers pick up the familiar figure of the warrior to say something about God, about human pride, and about judgment. NKJV usually renders it "mighty" or "mighty men." It also uses "warrior," "strong," "valiant," and once "champion," for Goliath (1 Samuel 17:51).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is a person marked by superior strength, prowess, or prevailing power. Context determines what kind of strength is in view.

1. A warrior or fighting man, a hero of battle, such as David's mighty men (2 Samuel 23:8) or the defeated soldiers of Egypt in Jeremiah's oracle (Jeremiah 46:5–12).

2. A person of substance, standing, or capability, often in the phrase "mighty man of valor" or "man of great wealth," as with Boaz (Ruth 2:1).

3. A title of God as the mighty One who acts and prevails, as in Deuteronomy 10:17, Psalm 24:8, and Isaiah 9:6.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the historical books the word is mostly descriptive. It counts the soldiers of a tribe, ranks the king's guard, and honors the fallen. David's lament over Saul and Jonathan repeatedly mourns the fall of the mighty (2 Samuel 1:19, 25, 27).

In poetry and prophecy the same figure becomes a lens on trust. Some passages expose the limits of the human gibbôwr. In others, God Himself takes the warrior's place.

One construction is worth noticing. The word often pairs with chayil, a term for capability, valor, or wealth, giving phrases like "mighty man of valor" (Judges 6:12). Context decides whether the emphasis falls on fighting ability or on social and economic weight. NKJV's "man of great wealth" for Boaz reflects the second.

4. Theological Significance

Israel's Scriptures did not hesitate to call God a warrior. Moses calls Him "the great God, mighty and awesome, who shows no partiality nor takes a bribe" (Deuteronomy 10:17). His might is joined to justice. The strongest Being in the universe is also the one Judge who cannot be bought, and the surrounding verses show Him defending the orphan, the widow, and the stranger. Psalm 24 pictures the gates lifting their heads for a returning King and asks who He is: "The LORD strong and mighty, The LORD mighty in battle" (Psalm 24:8). Isaiah watches Him step into history: "He shall stir up His zeal like a man of war... He shall prevail against His enemies" (Isaiah 42:13). This is active strength. It moves, it fights, and it wins.

That revelation stands against every confidence placed in human might. "A mighty man is not delivered by great strength" (Psalm 33:16). Amos piles up the specialists of war, the swift, the archer, and the horseman, and says none will escape. Then he concludes that "the most courageous men of might Shall flee naked in that day" (Amos 2:16). The LORD tells Jeremiah, "Let not the mighty man glory in his might" (Jeremiah 9:23), and in the next verse points to the one thing worth boasting in, which is understanding and knowing the LORD. Human strength has real value in its place. It becomes dangerous when it is used to oppress others or asked to do what only God can do.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Nimrod and David's mighty men (Genesis 10:8; 2 Samuel 23:8–17). After an earlier use for "the mighty men who were of old" (Genesis 6:4), the word introduces Nimrod: "he began to be a mighty one on the earth" (Genesis 10:8). The next verse calls him a mighty hunter before the LORD, and his kingdom begins at Babel (Genesis 10:9–10). Interpreters debate whether "before the LORD" is praise or defiance. Genesis 10 does not say that Nimrod led the tower-building of Genesis 11, but many readers connect the kingdom that began at Babel with the later story of self-made greatness there. Read that way, Nimrod stands for strength gathered around human ambition, in contrast to strength offered to God. Set this beside 2 Samuel 23: "These are the names of the mighty men whom David had." Their feats are astonishing. Yet the most memorable scene is a quiet one. Three of them broke through the Philistine lines simply because their king, hiding in a cave, longed aloud for water from the well of Bethlehem. David would not drink it. He poured it out to the LORD, calling it the blood of men who had risked their lives (2 Samuel 23:15–17). Strength that served the king was handed on to God. The same roster ends with Uriah the Hittite (2 Samuel 23:39), a sober reminder that the king himself could betray such loyalty.

Gideon in the winepress (Judges 6:12). Gideon was threshing wheat out of sight, afraid of Midian, when the Angel of the LORD greeted him: "The LORD is with you, you mighty man of valor!" Nothing in Gideon's behavior had earned the title. It rests on the first clause, God's presence. Gideon's own protest that his clan is the weakest and he the least (Judges 6:15) only sharpens the point. God named him by what His presence would make of him.

Boaz of Bethlehem (Ruth 2:1). "There was a relative of Naomi's husband, a man of great wealth... His name was Boaz." The phrase behind "great wealth" is the "mighty man of valor" construction. Boaz fights no battles. His strength shows in a field left open to a foreign widow, in protection for a vulnerable woman, and in a costly decision to redeem. When he tells Ruth that the town knows her as a "virtuous woman" (Ruth 3:11), he uses the same family of language about her. Biblical might includes faithful, generous character used for others.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah names the coming Child "Wonderful, Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace" (Isaiah 9:6). One chapter later the prophet uses the identical title for the LORD Himself: "The remnant will return, the remnant of Jacob, To the Mighty God" (Isaiah 10:21). The Child carries the name of the God to whom the remnant returns. The Divine Warrior of Psalm 24 and Isaiah 42 does not stay at a distance. He comes as a Son given, with the government on His shoulder.

Jesus shows how this Warrior fights. Isaiah asks whether prey can be taken from the mighty, and the LORD answers that He will take the captives from the mighty and contend with His people's enemy (Isaiah 49:24–25). Jesus speaks of entering the strong man's house, binding him, and plundering his goods (Matthew 12:29), and He was casting out demons as He said it. At the cross, the One who looked weakest disarmed the powers and triumphed over them (Colossians 2:15). The royal psalm that hails the king as "O Mighty One" (Psalm 45:3) belongs to the same psalm Hebrews applies to the Son, quoting its verses 6–7 (Hebrews 1:8–9). The human empire that began at Babel, often read as the line of self-made greatness Nimrod represents, finally meets a King whose might is spent rescuing captives.

7. Spirit & Life

The fiercest strength in the universe is also the nearest. Hunted by enemies and mocked by friends, Jeremiah confessed that the LORD was with him like a mighty, awesome one, and so his persecutors would stumble (Jeremiah 20:11). He had no promise of rescue from a distance. God's might stood beside him. Zephaniah goes further: "The LORD your God in your midst, The Mighty One, will save; He will rejoice over you with gladness, He will quiet you with His love" (Zephaniah 3:17). The Warrior who prevails against His enemies sings over His people. Anxious believers often assume God's power and God's tenderness pull in opposite directions. In this verse they belong to the same Person, in the same breath.

Gideon's greeting speaks to anyone who feels like the least in their father's house. God does not wait for visible courage before He calls. He announces His presence and names His servant by what that presence will produce. Boaz and David's men then show what such strength looks like once it is given. It protects the weak, it serves the King at personal cost, and it is offered back to God. The call is not to despise strength. It is to stop glorying in it and to place every capacity we have in the hands of the true Mighty One.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 17:51 — Goliath is the Philistine "champion." His fall to a shepherd with a sling puts the theme of Psalm 33:16 into a single story.

  • Psalm 78:65 — After a season of judgment, the Lord is pictured awaking like a mighty man and driving back His enemies, a bold image of God rising to act for His people.

  • Psalm 103:20 — The angels who "excel in strength" are called to bless the LORD. Even heaven's mighty ones exist to obey His word.

  • Psalm 127:4 — Children are like arrows in the hand of a warrior, a picture of strength in the next generation that God has given.

  • Proverbs 16:32 — The one slow to anger is better than the mighty, and the one who rules his spirit better than one who takes a city. Self-mastery outranks conquest.

  • Ecclesiastes 9:11 — The battle is not always to the strong. Time and chance humble the confident.
