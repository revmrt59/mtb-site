MIGHT, STRENGTH, POWER, MIGHTY ACTS - (gᵉbûwrâh, Strong's H1369)

Summary

The Hebrew word gᵉbûwrâh means might, strength, or power: the capacity to act and to carry something through. Scripture uses it for the strength of a warrior, the force of a war horse, and the military record of kings. It also uses it, far more gloriously, for the power of God. In the plural it often means God's "mighty acts," the specific occasions when His strength broke into history to save His people.

The word draws a sharp line between two kinds of strength. Human might is real but brief. The books of Kings close one reign after another with "all his might" filed away in the royal records. God's might belongs to Him by nature, is joined to His wisdom, and is given to those who trust Him. David confessed, "Yours, O LORD, is the greatness, the power and the glory." Isaiah foresaw the Messiah resting under "the Spirit of counsel and might." The psalmist resolved to go forward in the strength of the Lord GOD. To understand gᵉbûwrâh is to learn where real strength comes from and how a believer lives by it.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gᵉbûwrâh is a Hebrew noun that appears 61 times in the Old Testament. It belongs to the same family as the familiar word for a "mighty man" or warrior. That is why Jeremiah 9:23 can say, "Let not the mighty man glory in his might," which plays on two related words in one line. The word appears in battle reports, royal annals, wisdom sayings, prophetic oracles, and above all in the Psalms, where it occurs seventeen times. That spread matters. The same word that describes a soldier's prowess is used to praise the God who rules the nations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, gᵉbûwrâh is strength put to use: the ability to accomplish one's will against resistance. Context settles whose strength it is and how it shows itself.

1. Strength or might as a capacity, whether in a man, a king, or an animal. Gideon is told that "as a man is, so is his strength" (Judges 8:21), and God asks Job whether he gave the horse its strength (Job 39:19).

2. Power as an attribute of God, belonging to Him by right and without limit: "You are great, and Your name is great in might" (Jeremiah 10:6).

3. Mighty acts, usually in the plural: deeds that display power, especially God's saving works. "One generation shall praise Your works to another, And shall declare Your mighty acts" (Psalm 145:4).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In battle settings the word can lean toward martial success. Coming down from the mountain, Moses hears the noise from the camp and says, "It is not the noise of the shout of victory, Nor the noise of the cry of defeat, But the sound of singing I hear" (Exodus 32:18). The NKJV rightly renders gᵉbûwrâh here as "victory," the shout of strength that has prevailed.

The historical books use the word in a repeated closing formula for kings: "The rest of all the acts of Asa, all his might, all that he did... are they not written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Judah?" (1 Kings 15:23; also 2 Kings 10:34; 13:8; 20:20). Asa's summary ends with a telling note about his diseased feet in old age. The might of a king becomes a line in an archive.

The shift to the plural is the most meaningful grammatical pattern. When the Psalms speak of God's "mighty acts" (Psalm 106:2; 145:4, 12; 150:2), His power is no longer only something He has. It is something He has done, and it can be recounted, named, and told to children.

The word also appears in the mouths of God's enemies. The Assyrian Rabshakeh mocks Hezekiah: "You speak of having plans and power for war; but they are mere words" (2 Kings 18:20; Isaiah 36:5). From proud lips, gᵉbûwrâh becomes a boast that God will answer.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats power as personal before it treats it as useful. God does not simply wield strength. Might is part of who He is, and every other strength in creation is on loan from Him. That is why the Bible's first response to power is worship.

God's might is never raw force. Job confesses that with God are wisdom and strength (Job 12:13). In Proverbs 8:14, Wisdom herself claims understanding and strength. Isaiah 11:2 pairs "counsel and might" in the Spirit who rests on the Messiah. The pairing sets God's power apart from the tyrant's. His strength always knows what it is doing and why. When the Rabshakeh sneers at Hezekiah's "plans and power for war," literally counsel and might in Hebrew, he mocks the very combination that God gives in its perfect form to His King.

The word also exposes the limits of human strength. Psalm 90:10 allows that a life may stretch to eighty years "by reason of strength," yet even that strength's boast "is only labor and sorrow; For it is soon cut off, and we fly away." Ecclesiastes 9:16 adds, "Wisdom is better than strength." Ezekiel pictures fallen nations lying in the pit, ashamed of their might (Ezekiel 32:29–30). God does not despise strength. He refuses to let it become a god.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Chronicles 29:11–12: All power belongs to the Lord. Near the end of his life, David gathers the materials for a temple he will never see built, and the people give with joy. Standing before that pile of gold and silver, David does not admire what Israel has achieved: "Yours, O LORD, is the greatness, The power and the glory, The victory and the majesty; For all that is in heaven and in earth is Yours." In the next verse he adds that riches and honor come from God, that power and might are in His hand, and that it is in His hand to make great and to give strength to all. God is the only source of strength, and He distributes it. A few verses later, the chronicler sums up David's reign with "his might" (1 Chronicles 29:30). The king whose life is remembered for its might had already confessed whose might it was.

Isaiah 11:2 and Micah 3:8: Might by the Spirit. Isaiah sees a shoot rising from the cut-down stump of Jesse's family line, and "The Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon Him... The Spirit of counsel and might." This might is not native to the house of David, which by then is little more than a stump. It rests on the Branch from outside. That is why He does not judge by what His eyes see but strikes the earth with the rod of His mouth (Isaiah 11:3–4). His strength is the power to do justice that cannot be bribed or deceived. Micah, facing prophets who preached peace for pay, claims the same source: "But truly I am full of power by the Spirit of the LORD, And of justice and might, To declare to Jacob his transgression And to Israel his sin." Here might is the courage to tell the truth to a nation that does not want to hear it. In both passages, Spirit-given might serves righteousness.

Isaiah 30:15 and Jeremiah 9:23: Strength surrendered and received. Judah was racing to Egypt for horses and alliances when God said, "In returning and rest you shall be saved; In quietness and confidence shall be your strength." The very thing they were frantically trying to manufacture would come through stillness and trust. The verse ends with the saddest words in the passage: "But you would not." Jeremiah sounds the same note: "Let not the mighty man glory in his might," and he directs all boasting toward knowing the Lord (9:24). God's people lay hold of His power only when they let go of their own.

Psalm 145 and Psalm 106: Mighty acts told and remembered. Psalm 145 pictures praise passing down the generations: "One generation shall praise Your works to another, And shall declare Your mighty acts." The purpose is spelled out: "To make known to the sons of men His mighty acts, And the glorious majesty of His kingdom" (v. 12). Psalm 106 shows why God acts at all. At the Red Sea Israel rebelled, "Nevertheless He saved them for His name's sake, That He might make His mighty power known" (v. 8). God's might is revealed in rescuing people who did not deserve it, and those rescues become the content of worship. "Praise Him for His mighty acts," the Psalter ends (Psalm 150:2).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The royal formula in Kings tells the story of human might: king after king, each reign summed up as "all his might," each one ending in a grave. Against that backdrop, the Psalms and Prophets look for a King whose strength does not pass away.

Isaiah's Branch is that King. Earlier, Isaiah gave the promised Child the name "Mighty God" (Isaiah 9:6), using a title from the same word family. Isaiah 11 then shows that this King's might comes through the Spirit resting on Him. The New Testament presents Jesus in exactly those terms. At the start of His ministry He reads Isaiah's words about the Spirit of the Lord being upon Him (Luke 4:18). Peter later summarizes His life as God anointing Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power (Acts 10:38). The might Isaiah described rested on Him fully.

The climactic display of God's might turned human expectations upside down. The prophets had insisted that trusting in human strength fails and that strength comes through quiet confidence in God. At the cross, the Son of David gave up every claim to self-saving power, and God raised Him from the dead. Paul learned the same pattern in his own weakness when the Lord told him that His strength is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9). David's doxology, with its kingdom, power, and glory, also echoes in the closing words of the Lord's Prayer as it appears in the NKJV (Matthew 6:13). Jesus' disciples end their prayers with the confession David made over the temple offerings.

7. Spirit & Life

The believer is invited to live in God's might as well as admire it. In Psalm 71, an aging saint whose natural strength is fading resolves to go in the strength of the Lord GOD and to declare God's power to the next generation (71:16, 18). The two belong together. The one who walks in God's strength becomes the one who tells of it. David prays the same way under pressure, asking God to save him by His name and vindicate him by His strength (Psalm 54:1). Strength for the believer comes as a gift received in trust.

This changes how we read our own weakness. Psalm 147:10 says the Lord takes no delight in the strength of the horse, the same creature whose might God celebrates in Job 39:19. God made that strength and is glad in it as His work. What He does not do is rest His purposes on it. The believer can therefore be honest about failing energy, limited gifts, and approaching age without despair. Isaiah 30:15 still stands: quiet confidence in God is strength.

Deborah's song ends with a picture worth keeping. She prays that those who love the Lord will be like the sun when it comes out in full strength (Judges 5:31). Strength is the destiny of those who love Him, and like the sun's light, it shines because it comes from a source beyond itself.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 20:6 — The psalmist is confident that the Lord answers His anointed with the saving strength of His right hand. God's strength is power directed toward rescue.

  • Psalm 89:13 — "You have a mighty arm." The arm is the biblical picture of strength in action, the same arm that redeemed Israel from Egypt.

  • Isaiah 28:6 — The Lord promises to be strength to those who turn back the battle at the gate. Courage in defending God's people is itself His gift.

  • Isaiah 63:15 — The prophet asks where God's zeal and strength have gone. Prayer can honestly plead with God to show again the might He has shown before.

  • Jeremiah 16:21 — God promises to make the nations know His hand and His might, and that they will know His name is the LORD. His power always serves the revelation of who He is.

  • Ecclesiastes 10:17 — A land is blessed when its princes eat at the proper time for strength and not for drunkenness. Even ordinary bodily strength is meant to serve responsibility, not indulgence.
