HILL, HILLS - (gibʻâh, Strong's H1389)

Summary

The Hebrew word gibʻâh simply means hill, a rise of ground usually smaller than a mountain. Many of its uses are plain geography. Moses stands on a hill during a battle, David hides in the hills from Saul, and the ark rests in a house on a hill. In Hebrew poetry the word usually stands beside the word for mountains, and the pair together describes the whole high landscape.

Because hills are high, old, and hard to move, Scripture uses them to make important points about God. Israel built idol shrines "on every high hill," and the prophets call this unfaithfulness to the Lord. The same hills skip and melt when He appears, and they are weighed in His scales. In the age to come, the mountain of the Lord's house will be lifted above them. God's love is even more permanent than the hills: "the mountains shall depart And the hills be removed, But My kindness shall not depart from you" (Isaiah 54:10). The hills that Israel polluted will one day flow with wine and break out in song. The word traces a contest over the high places that God Himself settles.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gibʻâh is an ordinary Hebrew landscape word. It appears in narrative, law, poetry, and prophecy, with a heavy concentration in Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. In the historical books it often helps name a place, such as "the hill of the foreskins" (Joshua 5:3) or "the hill of God" (1 Samuel 10:5). Hill country gave a person a view of the land, a defensive position, and a place to hide. Ancient people also chose high places for worship, and that practice explains much of how the prophets use the word.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Gibʻâh means a hill, a raised area of land. In prose it names a particular elevation. In poetry it usually pairs with the Hebrew word for mountain, har, to describe the high ground in general. In those poetic pairs, readers should not press a sharp difference in size or meaning between the two words. One definition covers every use. The richness of the word comes from the contexts where it appears.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the word in four main ways.

  • Historical places. Hills are sites of encampment, lookout, and refuge (Exodus 17:9; 1 Samuel 23:19; 2 Samuel 2:25).

  • Sites of idolatry. The repeated phrase "every high hill" condemns Israel's illegal shrines (1 Kings 14:23).

  • Responses to God's presence. In poetry the hills are pictured as living things. They tremble, melt, and rejoice.

  • Pictures of the age to come. Hills measure God's permanence and display the abundance of the restored creation.

The phrase "every high hill" matters. Its sweeping wording shows that idolatry had spread across the whole land rather than staying in a few isolated places.

Hills could also protect people. David hid from Saul in the hill of Hachilah (1 Samuel 23:19; 1 Samuel 26:1). Jeremiah reverses that picture for a rebellious nation. God says His hunters "shall hunt them from every mountain and every hill, and out of the holes of the rocks" (Jeremiah 16:16). The ground that once sheltered David gives no cover from God's judgment.

4. Theological Significance

Hills looked permanent to ancient people. Jacob and Moses both speak of the ancient, lasting hills when they bless Joseph (Genesis 49:26; Deuteronomy 33:15). Scripture uses that sense of age and strength to show how much greater God is.

When God moves, the hills give way. At the Exodus, "The mountains skipped like rams, The little hills like lambs" (Psalm 114:4). In Nahum 1:5, "The mountains quake before Him, The hills melt." Habakkuk sees the everlasting hills bowing as God marches out (Habakkuk 3:6). Isaiah shows the same truth more quietly. The Lord has "Weighed the mountains in scales And the hills in a balance" (Isaiah 40:12), the way a merchant weighs a few coins. The things people consider heaviest and most fixed are small and easily moved before their Maker.

The same poetry can also be joyful. When God sends rain, "the little hills rejoice on every side" (Psalm 65:12). In Hebrew the hills are pictured as wrapping themselves in joy like clothing. Creation answers God with fear when He comes in judgment and with gladness when He comes in kindness.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 17:9–10: the hill of intercession. While Joshua fights Amalek in the valley, Moses stands on top of the hill with the rod of God in his hand. Israel wins while his hands are raised and begins to lose when they drop. Aaron and Hur then hold his hands steady until sunset (Exodus 17:11–12). Joshua fights the battle in the valley, but its outcome depends on what happens on the hill. Moses' raised hands and the rod of God show that Israel's strength comes from God. Many readers take those raised hands as a picture of prayer, and read that way the passage also shows that those who pray need others to help hold them up.

The high hills of unfaithfulness (1 Kings 14:23; Jeremiah 2:20; Ezekiel 6:13). God told Israel to tear down the Canaanite altars "on the hills" (Deuteronomy 12:2) and to worship at the one place He would choose. Instead, Judah "built for themselves high places, sacred pillars, and wooden images on every high hill and under every green tree" (1 Kings 14:23). The words "for themselves" point to worship arranged on the people's own terms, in places they chose rather than the place God would choose. Jeremiah calls this harlotry. On every high hill Israel lay down like an unfaithful wife (Jeremiah 2:20). Ezekiel adds that the bodies of the slain will lie around those same altars on every high hill (Ezekiel 6:13). God treats false worship as a betrayal of His love. The places people chose for their own worship became the places of their judgment.

Isaiah 2 and Micah 4: the mountain above the hills. Isaiah and Micah record the same promise. "The mountain of the LORD's house Shall be established on the top of the mountains, And shall be exalted above the hills; And peoples shall flow to it" (Micah 4:1; compare Isaiah 2:2). A few verses later, Isaiah says the day of the Lord will come against every lofty hill, along with proud cedars, towers, and walls (Isaiah 2:12–15). The hills belong to a list of everything that exalts itself against God. God raises His own mountain and lowers every rival height. In the same vision the nations stream to His exalted house to learn His ways, and they turn their swords into plowshares (Isaiah 2:2–4).

Isaiah 54:10: love outlasting the hills. "For the mountains shall depart And the hills be removed, But My kindness shall not depart from you, Nor shall My covenant of peace be removed." Isaiah takes the most permanent things a person can see and calls them temporary. The word translated "kindness" is God's steadfast, loyal love. God's love for His people is the one thing in the picture that cannot be moved. The prophets then picture the hills renewed. They will break into singing as God's people go out in joy (Isaiah 55:12). "All the hills shall flow with" sweet wine (Amos 9:13), and "The hills shall flow with milk" (Joel 3:18). The hills Ezekiel described as covered with altars and the slain are the same hills that will flow with plenty.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament takes up these hill passages directly. Luke describes John the Baptist's work of preparing the way for Jesus with Isaiah's words about every mountain and hill being brought low (Isaiah 40:4; Luke 3:4–5). In Luke's Gospel, that leveling prepares the way for Christ's coming. On the road to the cross, Jesus quotes Hosea 10:8 to the weeping women: people will tell the hills to cover them (Luke 23:30; compare Revelation 6:16). The hills of Israel's false worship become the hills that sinners beg to hide them from judgment. Jesus walks toward Calvary to bear that judgment Himself.

Proverbs 8:25 sets Wisdom before the hills: "Before the mountains were settled, Before the hills, I was brought forth." The New Testament calls Christ the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:24) and says He existed before all things (Colossians 1:17). Proverbs 8 does not name Christ, but the connection is fruitful. The One who is older than the ancient hills is the One who made them.

The Song of Songs adds a tender picture. The beloved comes "Leaping upon the mountains, Skipping upon the hills" (Song of Songs 2:8). The hills do not slow him down. Later, in his own words of praise to the bride, he says, "I will go my way to the mountain of myrrh And to the hill of frankincense" (Song of Songs 4:6), a height filled with fragrance where the lovers meet. Christians have long read this love song in light of Christ and His church. Read that way, it shows the Bridegroom crossing every distance to reach His people. Isaiah's exalted mountain points the same direction. The church has come to Mount Zion, the city of the living God (Hebrews 12:22). As the gospel calls people from every nation, Christians see Isaiah's picture of the nations coming to learn of God beginning to take shape.

7. Spirit & Life

The story of the hills shows a choice every heart faces. A person can seek God on God's terms, or build a private "high hill" where religion feels meaningful but He is not obeyed. Jeremiah's charge that Israel lay down "playing the harlot" (Jeremiah 2:20) shows how God feels about that choice. He grieves it as a husband grieves unfaithfulness, because He wants His people's love for Himself alone.

Isaiah 54:10 also challenges where we place our security. People lean on whatever looks permanent to them, such as health, savings, institutions, or reputation. God says even the hills will be removed. His kindness remains when everything else gives way, so it is the only safe foundation.

Saul's story adds an encouraging detail. Samuel sent him to "the hill of God where the Philistine garrison is" (1 Samuel 10:5). At that same hill the Spirit of God came upon Saul, and he prophesied (1 Samuel 10:10). Enemy occupation did not keep God from working there. Exodus 17 adds a picture in which what happens on the hill decides what happens in the valley of battle. Paul describes the believer's struggle as one against spiritual powers (Ephesians 6:12). His words support the comparison: believers win their real battles through dependence on God.

Finally, God does not abandon the ground that sin has defiled. The hills of Ezekiel 6 become the hills of Amos 9. God's grace reclaims ruined places and makes them fruitful again, and it does the same for ruined people.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joshua 5:3. Joshua circumcised the new generation "at the hill of the foreskins." That hill marks Israel's renewal after crossing the Jordan and before the conquest of Jericho.

  • 1 Samuel 7:1. The returned ark was brought "into the house of Abinadab on the hill," where Eleazar was consecrated to guard it. The hill became a place of care for holy things.

  • Psalm 72:3. In the psalm of the righteous king, the mountains bring peace to the people, "And the little hills, by righteousness." The hills bring peace through righteousness. Even the landscape shares in his good reign.

  • Ezekiel 34:6. God's sheep wandered on every high hill with no one to seek them. That neglect prepares for God's promise to shepherd His flock Himself.

  • Psalm 148:9. The hills are called to praise the Lord along with all creation.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2022 (har, mountain). Canonical pairing. This is the standard poetic partner of gibʻâh. The two words appear together in passages such as Micah 4:1 and Isaiah 54:10 to describe the whole high landscape.

  • H2617 (chesed, steadfast love, kindness). Canonical pairing by contrast. In Isaiah 54:10, the removal of the hills is set against God's kindness, which will not depart.
