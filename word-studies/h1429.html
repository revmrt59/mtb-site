KID, LITTLE GOAT - (gᵉdîyâh, Strong's H1429)

Summary

The Hebrew word gᵉdîyâh means a young goat, or kid. It appears only once in the Old Testament, in Song of Solomon 1:8. In that verse the young woman of the Song has asked her beloved where he pastures his flock, and she is told: "Follow in the footsteps of the flock, And feed your little goats Beside the shepherds' tents." The word itself is ordinary farm language. It is the feminine form of the common Hebrew word for a kid, and the NKJV translates it "little goats."

This single use carries real weight. The woman longs to find the one she loves. The answer she receives does not send her alone into the hills. She is to take the young animals in her care, follow the trail the flock has already worn, and pasture them near the other shepherds. Read alongside Jesus' words to Peter, "Feed My lambs" (John 21:15), the verse holds a lasting truth for believers. Love for the Shepherd and care for the vulnerable ones He entrusts to us belong together. The place to find Him is among His people, doing His work.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gᵉdîyâh is a Hebrew noun. It is the feminine counterpart of gᵉdî (H1423), the usual Old Testament word for a young goat. Rebekah calls for two "choice kids of the goats" in Genesis 27:9, and Gideon prepares "a young goat" for the Angel of the LORD in Judges 6:19. In Israel, flocks commonly mixed sheep and goats, and tending them was ordinary family work. Young women did this work too, as Rachel did when she brought her father's sheep to the well (Genesis 29:9).

The Song of Solomon is love poetry. It is written as an exchange of voices between the woman, her beloved, and a group of companions. The speaker of 1:8 is not named. The words may come from the beloved himself, or from the companions who later address her as "fairest among women" (5:9; 6:1). Either way, the instruction answers her plea in verse 7.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Gᵉdîyâh means a young goat, a kid. Its feminine form most naturally points to young female goats. The word has one sense, and nothing more needs to be read into the definition. Its significance comes from the scene in which it appears. The word names the youngest and most dependent members of a shepherdess's flock.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In its one appearance, the word carries the possessive "your": "feed your little goats." This detail matters because verse 7 has just spoken of his flock: "Where you feed your flock." The beloved has his flock, and she has hers. She is not a spectator searching for a shepherd. She is a shepherdess herself, responsible for the young animals entrusted to her.

The NKJV's "little goats" captures the youth of these animals. They are kids, not grown goats, so the scene concerns care for the young.

4. Theological Significance

The scene reveals something about the way God's people find the One they love. The woman's desire is honest and urgent. She wants to know where her beloved rests his flock "at noon," during the heat of the day, and she does not want to drift like a stranger among other men's flocks. The answer she receives is practical and wise. She is told to keep to the path the flock has made and keep her own young animals near the shepherds' tents. Her love is not set against her responsibility. Her love is to be carried out through that responsibility.

Throughout Scripture, God shows particular care for the young and the weak of His flock. Isaiah pictures the LORD gathering the lambs in His arm and gently leading those with young (Isaiah 40:11). A shepherd who cares for kids must slow down, stay near water and shelter, and give attention to animals that cannot fend for themselves. When the Song places "little goats" at the center of the woman's task, it presents seeking the beloved and caring for the vulnerable as one movement of the heart.

Kids were also valued. A choice kid was prepared for a father's blessing (Genesis 27:9) and offered as hospitality to a heavenly visitor (Judges 6:19). The elder brother in Jesus' parable complained that he had never been given "a young goat, that I might make merry with my friends" (Luke 15:29). The woman's little goats are no burden to escape. They are something precious entrusted to her keeping.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:7–8 — Finding the beloved by following the flock. The woman asks, "Tell me, O you whom I love, Where you feed your flock, Where you make it rest at noon. For why should I be as one who veils herself By the flocks of your companions?" A veiled woman wandering among strangers' flocks would look lost, or worse, like someone of uncertain reputation. She wants to go straight to him. The reply gives her three connected directions. She is to "follow in the footsteps of the flock," trusting the trail already worn by those who have walked it before her. She is to "feed your little goats," keeping faith with her own charge. And she is to do this "beside the shepherds' tents," in the company of other shepherds and within reach of help and shelter. She will not find her beloved by abandoning her kids to search the wilderness alone. She will find him by tending them where shepherds gather.

Genesis 33:13 — Why the young cannot be driven hard. When Esau offered to travel with him, Jacob declined: "My lord knows that the children are weak, and the flocks and herds which are nursing are with me. And if the men should drive them hard one day, all the flock will die." Every shepherd knew this. Young animals set the pace. They need pasture close at hand, rest, and protection. That is why the woman's little goats belong near the shepherds' tents rather than out on a long, lonely search. Caring for the young means choosing the safe path.

John 21:15–17 — Love proven in feeding. By the Sea of Galilee, the risen Jesus asks Peter three times, "Do you love Me?" Each time Peter affirms his love, and each time Jesus answers with a shepherd's charge: "Feed My lambs," "Tend My sheep," "Feed My sheep." Jesus does not answer Peter's love with a private experience. He gives Peter work, and He begins with the lambs, the youngest of the flock. The Song and the Gospel illuminate one another. In the Song, a woman who longs for her beloved is told to feed her little ones near the shepherds. In John, a disciple who loves his Lord is told to feed the Lord's little ones. In both scenes, devotion to the Shepherd leads directly into care for the young.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Christians have long heard in the Song's beloved shepherd an echo of the Shepherd Christ, and the New Testament gives that echo solid ground. Jesus calls Himself the Good Shepherd who knows His sheep and lays down His life for them (John 10:11, 14). The Song does not name Him, so this reading is a resonance rather than a stated fulfillment. Still, the resonance is strong. Jesus feeds His flock, gives it rest, and calls His followers to share His work.

Peter, who once heard "Feed My lambs," later passes the same charge to the church's elders: "Shepherd the flock of God which is among you... nor as being lords over those entrusted to you, but being examples to the flock; and when the Chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the crown of glory that does not fade away" (1 Peter 5:2–4). The pattern is complete. Christ is the Chief Shepherd. Those who love Him become undershepherds who tend what He entrusts to them. They carry out that work among their fellow shepherds while waiting for His appearing. The shepherdess with her little goats beside the shepherds' tents is an early, gentle picture of that whole arrangement.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers assume that drawing closer to Christ means getting away: leaving behind ordinary responsibilities, stepping back from the church, and searching for Him alone. Song 1:8 points another way. The woman's longing is answered with a path, a task, and a community. Her nearness to the beloved is found while she feeds her kids near the other shepherds. Jesus confirmed this when He answered Peter's love with "Feed My lambs." Those who care for children, new believers, or the weak and struggling are not doing something that distracts them from the Lord. They are standing where He works.

"The footsteps of the flock" also deserve attention. Faithful people have walked this way before us, and their trail is not a cage. It is a mercy. A seeker who refuses every worn path in favor of her own wandering usually ends up like the veiled woman among strangers' flocks, close to the right place but lost.

Shepherds also knew that goats, unlike sheep, tend to be independent and wander off. Scripture does not teach this lesson from Song 1:8, but the analogy fits the verse well. The restless, self-reliant parts of our own hearts and of those we care for are kept safest close to the shepherds' tents, under the care of others who also answer to the Chief Shepherd.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Gᵉdîyâh occurs only in Song of Solomon 1:8, which is discussed above. The following verses use related words for young goats. They are not occurrences of H1429, but they show how the image of the kid was understood.

  • Genesis 27:9 — "Two choice kids of the goats" (H1423) are chosen for a father's favorite meal, showing that a kid was a prized animal.

  • Judges 6:19 — Gideon prepares "a young goat" (H1423) as his offering of hospitality to the Angel of the LORD.

  • Luke 15:29 — The elder brother's complaint about never receiving "a young goat" shows that a kid was the stuff of celebration among friends.

  • Song of Solomon 4:1 — "Your hair is like a flock of goats, Going down from Mount Gilead" uses a different word for goats, but it shows the Song treating goats as images of beauty.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H1423 (gᵉdî), the masculine and more common word for a young goat, as in Genesis 27:9 and Judges 6:19.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Elsewhere, goats appear in solemn settings. One goat bore the people's sins into the wilderness on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:8–10), and Jesus pictured the final judgment as a shepherd dividing "his sheep from the goats" (Matthew 25:32). These passages should not be read back into Song 1:8. The Song's little goats are cherished young animals in a scene of love and care, and the same book uses goats as a picture of beauty. Scripture's goat imagery depends on its setting, and in this setting the kids represent what is precious and entrusted, not what is guilty.
