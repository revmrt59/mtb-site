VALLEY - (gayʼ, Strong's H1516)

Summary

The Hebrew word gayʼ means "valley." It usually names a real ravine or valley in the land of Israel: a border line in Joshua, a gate in Jerusalem's wall, or the gap between two armies. A valley is low, closed in, and hard to see into from a distance. Scripture uses those features to teach about God. Valleys became places where people hid their worst sins, where God judged, and where God showed Himself present and saving.

The best-known valley in this word study is David's "valley of the shadow of death" in Psalm 23, where the Shepherd's presence takes the fear out of the darkest path. The most sobering is the Valley of the Son of Hinnom outside Jerusalem. Kings of Judah burned their children there, and its name later became the New Testament word for hell. The prophets also look ahead. Isaiah promises that "every valley shall be exalted" when the Lord comes, and Zechariah sees the Lord split the Mount of Olives to open a valley of escape for His people. Across Scripture, the low places of the earth reveal both how deep human sin goes and how far God will come down to rescue.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gayʼ is an ordinary Hebrew word for a valley. It is often used of a ravine or a narrower valley with hills or mountains on either side. Hebrew has other words for broad valleys and plains, and English translations do not always show the difference. For that reason, the English word "valley" in a verse does not prove that gayʼ is the word underneath.

The word appears mainly in historical books and the prophets. In Joshua it marks tribal boundaries, such as the Valley of the Son of Hinnom on Judah's border (Joshua 15:8). In Nehemiah it names the Valley Gate of Jerusalem (Nehemiah 2:13; 3:13). Many valleys carry proper names: Elah, Salt, Zephathah, Hinnom, and the "Valley of Craftsmen" (Ge Harashim, 1 Chronicles 4:14; Nehemiah 11:35). Because valleys were low and enclosed, they served as natural boundaries, battle lines, hiding places, and burial grounds.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Gayʼ has one basic meaning: a valley, a low place between higher ground. Contexts change what the valley is used for, but the dictionary meaning stays the same. The word itself does not mean "death," "judgment," or "trouble." Those associations come from what happened in particular valleys and from how the prophets and psalmists used the image.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses are geographical. The Valley of Salt is where David, and later Amaziah, defeated Edom (2 Samuel 8:13–14; 2 Kings 14:7; Psalm 60, superscription). The Valley of Zephathah is where Asa lined up for battle against a huge Ethiopian army (2 Chronicles 14:10).

The prophets give the valley a moral and spiritual weight. Ezekiel addresses the land itself, "the mountains... the hills... the ravines, and... the valleys." First he does so to announce judgment on idolatry (Ezekiel 6:3). Later he uses the same address to promise restoration (Ezekiel 36:4, 6). Isaiah speaks of the "verdant valleys" of proud Ephraim fading like a flower (Isaiah 28:1, 4). In the psalms the valley becomes a picture of the dangers a person passes through (Psalm 23:4).

4. Theological Significance

The valleys of Scripture show that no place is out of God's sight. People went down into the ravine outside Jerusalem to do what they would not do openly, yet God named the place and judged what was done there. In Jeremiah 2:23 God answers Israel's claim of innocence with an instruction to look at its way in the valley and know what it had done. The valley itself becomes evidence against them.

The valleys also show God's rule over the land itself. He fills valleys (Isaiah 40:4), opens new ones (Zechariah 14:4), and turns a valley of travelers into a graveyard for invading armies (Ezekiel 39:11, 15). Low ground is not beyond Him. Throughout these passages, God's judgment and His saving presence both meet people in the same low places.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The Valley of the Son of Hinnom (2 Kings 23; Jeremiah 7; 19). This ravine on Jerusalem's southern edge became the site of Judah's darkest worship. Ahaz "burned his children in the fire" there (2 Chronicles 28:3), and Manasseh did the same (2 Chronicles 33:6). Jeremiah says the people built the high places of Topheth there, which God says He never commanded, "nor did it come into My heart" (Jeremiah 7:31, paraphrased; see also 32:35). Josiah "defiled Topheth, which is in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom, that no man might make his son or his daughter pass through the fire to Molech" (2 Kings 23:10). God then gives the place a new name. It will be called "the Valley of Slaughter," because the dead will be buried there until no room remains (Jeremiah 7:32; 19:6). Jeremiah acts out the sentence. He takes a potter's clay flask out to that valley and breaks it, declaring that God will break the people and the city like a potter's vessel that cannot be made whole again (Jeremiah 19:2, 10–11). The place where they sacrificed their children becomes the place of their own judgment.

The Valley of Elah (1 Samuel 17). "The Philistines stood on a mountain on one side, and Israel stood on a mountain on the other side, with a valley between them" (1 Samuel 17:3). For forty days Goliath shouted across that gap and no one in Israel went down. The valley is where the fear was felt. David crossed it, going to meet the giant in the name of the LORD of hosts rather than in Saul's armor. Faith did not win this battle from the safety of the ridge. Afterward, Israel chased the Philistines as far as the entrance of the valley (17:52). The ground that had stood for Israel's paralysis became the route of its pursuit.

The valley opposite Beth Peor (Deuteronomy 3:29; 34:6). Moses begged God to let him cross the Jordan, and God refused. The next line reads simply, "So we stayed in the valley opposite Beth Peor" (Deuteronomy 3:29, paraphrased). At the end of the book that same setting returns: "And He buried him in a valley in the land of Moab, opposite Beth Peor; but no one knows his grave to this day" (34:6). The valley near the place of his disappointment became his burial place. God Himself buried him there. No monument was raised and no shrine was built. The hidden grave may also have protected Israel from honoring the man more than the God he served. Either way, the burial was God's own private care for His friend.

The Valley of Vision (Isaiah 22). Isaiah calls Jerusalem, the city of Mount Zion, "the Valley of Vision" (Isaiah 22:1, 5). The city was surrounded by valleys, but the title carries sharp irony. The place where God had given revelation could not see what was coming. Its people went up to the housetops as the siege approached, and they feasted instead of mourning (22:12–13). Living near the place of revelation did not give them spiritual sight.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Valley of Hinnom provides the New Testament's word for hell. "Gehenna" comes from the Hebrew name Ge Hinnom, "Valley of Hinnom." When Jesus warns, "fear Him who is able to destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28), and speaks of "hell, into the fire that shall never be quenched" (Mark 9:43), He is using the name of a real ravine where Judah had burned its children. A place of sin and judgment in Jeremiah's day became the name for final judgment.

Isaiah 40 points in the opposite direction. "Every valley shall be exalted / And every mountain and hill brought low" (Isaiah 40:4). The image is road-building for a coming king: low places filled and rough ground leveled. The Gospels apply this passage to John the Baptist, "the voice of one crying in the wilderness" (Matthew 3:3), and Luke quotes the valley line itself: "Every valley shall be filled" (Luke 3:5). John's preaching of repentance was the work of preparing that road. Proud hearts were brought down, and low, broken hearts were lifted up to receive the Lord.

Zechariah completes the picture. "His feet will stand on the Mount of Olives... And the Mount of Olives shall be split in two... Making a very large valley" (Zechariah 14:4). Valleys had so often been places of slaughter and burial. This one is opened by the Lord as a way out: "Then you shall flee through My mountain valley" (14:5). The same verse closes, "Thus the LORD my God will come, And all the saints with You." The Mount of Olives is also where Jesus taught, prayed, and ascended, and the hope of His return gathers around it.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 23:4 is the believer's word for the valley: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; For You are with me." David does not say the Shepherd leads His sheep around the valley. The path goes through it. The comfort is in the change of address. Earlier in the psalm David speaks about the LORD as "He." Inside the dark valley he begins to speak to Him: "You are with me." The rod that drives off predators and the staff that guides are close enough to be felt. The Shepherd's nearness is most personal in the place where the sheep can see the least.

This valley belongs with the others in this study. The same God who saw what Judah did in Hinnom sees His own sheep in their darkest ravine. He buried Moses in a valley no one else could find. He crossed the valley of Elah through a shepherd boy who trusted His name. He promises to fill every valley before His coming and to open a valley of escape at the end. A believer in a low place is not in territory God has left. Low ground is where God has repeatedly met His people.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 21:20 – Israel's wilderness route ends at a valley in Moab near the top of Pisgah, close to where Moses would later view the land and be buried.

  • 2 Kings 2:16 – The sons of the prophets fear the Spirit may have cast Elijah into some valley. Their proposed search reflects uncertainty about where he had gone.

  • 2 Chronicles 14:10 – Asa sets his battle lines in the Valley of Zephathah and cries out to the LORD against a far larger army.

  • Nehemiah 2:13, 15 – Nehemiah inspects Jerusalem's broken walls by night, going out and returning through the Valley Gate.

  • Micah 1:6 – God says He will pour Samaria's stones down into the valley. The proud capital of the north will be emptied into the low ground.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Ezekiel's famous valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37:1) uses a different Hebrew word, so it does not belong to this study, even though the English word is the same.

In Jeremiah 2:23 God points to "the valley" as proof of Israel's unfaithfulness. The hidden place of their idolatry becomes the witness against their denial. In Jeremiah 19 the broken flask in the Valley of Hinnom is an acted symbol that God Himself explains: a ruin that cannot be repaired.

Ezekiel's "Valley of Hamon Gog" ("multitude of Gog") pictures a valley filled with the dead of an invading army. The burial is so large that it blocks those passing through, and burying the dead cleanses the land (Ezekiel 39:11, 15). The pride of the nations ends up buried in the low ground. The Valley of Salt is a recurring site of Davidic victory over Edom (2 Samuel 8:13–14; 2 Kings 14:7; Psalm 60, superscription). God secured His king's borders on barren ground.

Ezekiel addresses mountains and valleys together, first in judgment (6:3) and later in restoration (36:4, 6). The heights were the visible places of idolatry, and the valleys were often where the darker rites were hidden. It is a fair reflection, though not the text's stated meaning, that God's restoring word reaches both kinds of sin: the pride people display and the shame they hide.
