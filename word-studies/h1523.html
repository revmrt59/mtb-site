REJOICE, BE GLAD - (gîyl, Strong's H1523)

Summary

The Hebrew verb gîyl means to rejoice, to be glad, or to exult. It describes joy that shows itself in voice, song, and celebration. The Psalms and the Prophets use it most often for joy in what God has done: His salvation, His mercy, His reign, and His coming to His people. The heavens, the earth, the desert, and the daughter of Zion are all called to rejoice this way. Scripture also shows its dark side. The same eager joy can attach to an enemy's fall or to an idol, and then it ends in grief.

The word's most surprising use has God as the one rejoicing. In Zephaniah 3:17 and Isaiah 65:19, the Lord Himself exults over His redeemed people. Habakkuk shows that this joy can rise from empty fields because it rests on "the God of my salvation." Zechariah calls Zion to "Rejoice greatly" because her King is coming lowly on a donkey, and the Gospels show that promise fulfilled in Jesus.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gîyl is a Hebrew verb found almost entirely in poetry: Psalms, Proverbs, Song of Solomon, and the Prophets, especially Isaiah. Outside these books it appears only once, in David's psalm of thanksgiving in 1 Chronicles 16:31. It is not the ordinary word for a happy day in Israel's history. It belongs to worship, to prophetic hope, and to wise reflection on what people take pleasure in.

Older lexicons connected the word with circling or whirling about, perhaps under strong emotion. That link is uncertain, and it should not be read into each use. Context shows the word clearly enough: gîyl is joy that is felt strongly and expressed openly.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Gîyl means to rejoice, be glad, or exult. It often stands beside śāmaḥ, the more common word for gladness, as in Isaiah 25:9, "We will be glad and rejoice in His salvation." In such paired lines, gîyl usually carries the louder note of exultation. Two senses deserve distinction:

1. Glad exultation in something good, especially in God and His saving acts. This is the word's normal use, as in Psalm 13:5: "My heart shall rejoice in Your salvation."

2. An intense, excited cry in a setting of loss or alarm. In Hosea 10:5 the NKJV renders the word "shriek," describing the priests of Samaria crying over their doomed calf idol.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The value of this joy depends entirely on what it fastens to. Most often the object is the Lord: His mercy (Psalm 31:7), His salvation (Psalm 21:1), His reign (Psalm 97:1). When the object is wrong, the same eagerness is condemned. Proverbs 2:14 describes people who delight in perversity. Habakkuk 1:15 pictures the Babylonian conqueror who drags nations into his net and is glad about it. Proverbs 24:17 forbids the heart to "be glad when he stumbles," and the next verse warns that the Lord sees such gloating with displeasure.

Psalm 13 places the two kinds side by side. In verse 4 David fears that his enemies will rejoice over his fall. In verse 5 he answers with his own rejoicing in God's salvation. The same word marks two opposite hearts.

Hosea 10:5 shows where idolatrous joy ends. The priests had once celebrated the calf of Beth Aven. Older translations read the line as speaking of the priests "who rejoiced over it." Now its glory has departed, and the NKJV hears their cry as a shriek. Either way, the excitement once spent on a false god ends in panic when that god is carried away.

Many uses are commands: "Be joyful, O earth!" (Isaiah 49:13); "Rejoice greatly" (Zechariah 9:9). Biblical joy can therefore be called for. God summons His people to rejoice because He has given them something real to rejoice in.

4. Theological Significance

Gîyl reveals a God whose rule produces gladness. "The LORD reigns; let the earth rejoice" (Psalm 97:1, paraphrased closely), and the call in 1 Chronicles 16:31 and Psalm 96:11 for heaven and earth to be glad, treat God's kingship as good news for creation itself. His reign brings judgment to evil, but for the world He made it means rescue and right order.

The word also shows that joy in Scripture is a response to God's acts. People rejoice in His salvation (Psalm 9:14), in His mercy that "considered my trouble" (Psalm 31:7), and in His comfort for the afflicted (Isaiah 49:13). Joy follows when God is seen for who He is and what He has done. That is why the prophets can command it: the reason for joy is God Himself.

Most striking of all, God is not only the object of this joy. He is also its subject. The Lord exults over His people. Salvation culminates in the joy of forgiven sinners and God's own delight in those He has brought home.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Zephaniah 3:17 and Isaiah 65:19 — God rejoices over His people. Zephaniah wrote to a Jerusalem headed for judgment, yet he ends with a picture of the Lord "in your midst." The verse stacks up God's actions: He "will save," He "will rejoice over you with gladness," He "will quiet you with His love," and "He will rejoice over you with singing." That last line uses gîyl. The quieted people are not left in silence; their God sings over them. Isaiah 65:19 makes the same move in the new creation: "I will rejoice in Jerusalem, And joy in My people," and weeping will no longer be heard there. Isaiah 62:5 supplies the picture: "as the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, So shall your God rejoice over you." Jesus said the same thing about one repentant sinner and the "more joy in heaven" that follows (Luke 15:7). The Lord does not simply put up with His redeemed people. He delights in them, and He says so aloud.

Habakkuk 3:17–18 and Psalm 13 — joy when nothing is left. Habakkuk lists everything that might fail: fig tree, vine, olive, fields, flock, and herd. Nothing that normally makes a farmer glad remains. Then comes "Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation." The word "joy" there is gîyl. The prophet is not describing a mood that arrived. He is declaring a resolve: he will exult in God when the harvest is gone. Psalm 13 follows the same path. David opens with "How long, O LORD?" four times, yet he closes with "I have trusted in Your mercy; My heart shall rejoice in Your salvation." Psalm 31:7 explains the ground of such joy: God has "considered my trouble" and "known my soul in adversities." This joy grows from trust in a faithful God, and so it can be chosen before the circumstances change.

Psalm 2:11 — rejoicing with trembling. Psalm 2 confronts rebellious kings with God's anointed King and tells them to serve the Lord with fear and to rejoice with trembling. Exuberance and awe belong together. The joy gîyl describes is loud and wholehearted, but it never becomes casual. The same psalm calls the nations to "kiss the Son," so this trembling joy is directed toward God's appointed King. Hebrews 12:28 carries the same balance into the church: grateful service "with reverence and godly fear."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Many uses of gîyl look forward. Isaiah 9:3 pictures people rejoicing as at harvest, and the passage moves straight to "unto us a Child is born" (Isaiah 9:6). Isaiah 25:9 places the cry "We will be glad and rejoice in His salvation" beside the promise that God will swallow up death forever. In Isaiah 35:1–2 the desert "shall rejoice and blossom as the rose," and the reason is that "They shall see the glory of the LORD."

Zechariah 9:9 brings the hope into focus: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! … Behold, your King is coming to you; He is just and having salvation, Lowly and riding on a donkey." Matthew 21:5 quotes this promise as fulfilled when Jesus entered Jerusalem. The long-promised joy has a face. It arrives with a humble King who goes to the cross.

Two psalms carry the story further. Psalm 118:24 calls the people to rejoice in "the day the LORD has made," the day when the rejected stone becomes the chief cornerstone (Psalm 118:22). Jesus applied that stone to Himself (Matthew 21:42). In Psalm 16:9 David says his glory rejoices because God will not leave his soul in the grave. Peter quotes that psalm in Acts 2:25–28 as a prophecy of Christ's resurrection.

Creation's joy also finds its place here. The heavens and earth called to rejoice in Psalm 96:11 match Paul's picture of a creation that "eagerly waits" to be "delivered from the bondage of corruption" (Romans 8:19, 21). The psalm and Paul's letter do not share a word, but they share a hope: the full reign of God through Christ will set the world free.

7. Spirit & Life

The deepest discovery in this word is that God rejoices over His people. Many believers can picture God forgiving them while still assuming He is disappointed. Zephaniah 3:17 answers that fear. The Lord who saves also quiets His people with love and rejoices over them with singing. A believer may come to God expecting welcome, because God has said He delights in His people.

Habakkuk shows how such joy survives loss. His rejoicing did not wait for the fig tree to bloom again; it rested on "the God of my salvation." Psalm 51:8 gives the same hope to the guilty: David asks that "the bones You have broken may rejoice." The joy God gives can rise from loss and from repentance, because it rests on His mercy and not on what we have kept.

The word also guards the heart. Gloating over an enemy, or celebrating what God calls evil, uses the same capacity for joy that God made for Himself. Hosea's shrieking priests show where misplaced delight ends. Psalm 2 adds that even the purest joy stands before a holy King. Joy and reverence are friends: the believer can sing freely and still bow low.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 32:11 — The forgiven are told to be glad in the Lord and rejoice, the joy that follows confession in that psalm.

  • Psalm 51:8 — David asks that the bones God has broken may rejoice, joy restored after repentance.

  • Proverbs 23:24–25 — A father of the righteous will greatly rejoice. Godly children bring their parents this kind of gladness.

  • Song of Solomon 1:4 — The beloved's companions say they will be glad and rejoice in him, the joy of love.

  • Joel 2:21, 23 — After locusts and judgment, the land and the children of Zion are told to be glad, because God will restore the rain.

  • Isaiah 61:10 — The redeemed soul is joyful in God, who clothes it with garments of salvation and a robe of righteousness.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H1524 (gîyl, noun), "joy" or "rejoicing," from the same root. Psalm 43:4 calls God "my exceeding joy."

  • Canonical pairings: H8055 (śāmaḥ), "be glad," often parallel in the same line (Isaiah 25:9). H7797 (śûś), "rejoice," paired with gîyl in Zephaniah 3:17 and Isaiah 65:19.

  • Thematic connection: G0021 (agalliaō), the Greek verb "rejoice greatly." Mary uses it in Luke 1:47 ("my spirit has rejoiced in God my Savior"), echoing the joy in the God of salvation found in Habakkuk 3:18.
