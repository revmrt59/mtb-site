HEAP, WAVES, SPRING - (gal, Strong's H1530)

Summary

The Hebrew word gal names two things that look very different: a heap of stones and a wave of the sea. Jacob and Laban pile stones into a gal to mark a peace agreement. A gal is raised over Achan and over Absalom. Prophets warn that proud cities, Jerusalem and Babylon among them, will become heaps of rubble. When the word turns to water, it describes the rolling waves that God bounds with sand, stirs up, and stills with a word.

Scripture uses both pictures to show God's rule over what people build and what threatens to overwhelm them. Fortresses and false altars collapse into shapeless piles at His command. The sea's waves stop where He says, "This far." Jonah and the psalmist, sinking under trouble, call those waves "Your waves." Isaiah reverses the image when God says His obedient people would have had righteousness "like the waves of the sea." The Lord who rules the waves is the Lord Jesus, who said to the sea, "Peace, be still!"

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gal is a common Hebrew noun found from Genesis to Zechariah. Lexicons usually connect it to a verb meaning to roll. That link fits both of its main pictures: stones rolled together into a pile, and water rolling toward shore. Still, each use gets its meaning from its context, not from that derivation.

Heaps appear in stories and prophetic warnings. In the ancient Near East, a pile of fieldstones was the simplest lasting marker. People used one to record an agreement, to cover a grave, or to show what was left of a destroyed town. Waves appear mainly in poetry and prophecy, where the sea stands for power that people cannot control. The word also forms part of the place name Gallim (Isaiah 10:30).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A heap or pile, usually of stones. The pile may serve as a marker (Genesis 31:46), cover a grave (Joshua 7:26), or be the ruins of a destroyed city (Jeremiah 9:11).

2. A wave or billow of the sea: the rolling, surging swell of water (Psalm 89:9; Jeremiah 5:22).

3. A spring, in poetry (Song of Solomon 4:12, "a spring shut up"), where the picture is water welling up from the ground.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The material in view decides the sense. When stones are involved, gal is a heap. When the sea is involved, it is a wave, usually in the plural, because waves come in sets. The purpose of the heap then decides its tone. A heap can record a promise or bury a rebel. It can also be the remains of a city that once stood proudly.

When the sea "stood upright like a heap" at the Red Sea (Exodus 15:8), Moses uses a different Hebrew word. That word describes water held motionless. Gal describes water in motion.

4. Theological Significance

God brings proud strongholds down to rubble. The prophets use gal to describe what remains when God judges human self-reliance. "You have made a city a ruin, a fortified city a ruin" (Isaiah 25:2). God says, "I will make Jerusalem a heap of ruins" (Jeremiah 9:11), and later He says the same of the empire that conquered her: "Babylon shall become a heap" (Jeremiah 51:37).

Assyria believed its own strength had flattened the nations. God corrects that belief: "From ancient times that I formed it... That you should be For crushing fortified cities into heaps of ruins" (2 Kings 19:25; Isaiah 37:26). The empire was a tool in a plan it never understood.

The same judgment reaches religious buildings. Hosea warns that because Israel sacrificed bulls in Gilgal, "their altars shall be heaps in the furrows of the field" (Hosea 12:11). An altar was built carefully and set apart for worship. God says those altars will become loose heaps lying in a farmer's furrows. In this way, false worship loses both its shape and its claim to holiness.

God rules the waves. To ancient people the sea was the clearest picture of chaos, yet every reference to its waves in this word study puts it under God's command. He has "placed the sand as the bound of the sea... though its waves toss to and fro, Yet they cannot prevail" (Jeremiah 5:22). To the sea He says, "This far you may come, but no farther, And here your proud waves must stop!" (Job 38:11). "When its waves rise, You still them" (Psalm 89:9).

Jeremiah 51 brings the two senses together. Babylon becomes a heap (v. 37), and the sea comes up over her until the waves cover her (v. 42). The ruined city and the overwhelming sea both picture order coming undone, and in both cases God decides how far the undoing goes.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 31:46–52 and Joshua 7:26: a heap of peace and a heap of judgment. After years of mistrust, Jacob told his relatives, "Gather stones." They "took stones and made a heap, and they ate there on the heap" (Genesis 31:46). Laban gave the pile an Aramaic name, and Jacob gave it a Hebrew one, Galeed, meaning heap of witness (vv. 47–48). It also received the name Mizpah, because Laban asked the LORD to watch between them when they were apart (v. 49). Neither man was to pass the heap to harm the other (v. 52). These stones held a shared meal and a promise, and God was called on as the unseen witness.

Joshua 7 shows the other use of a heap. Achan stole from what God had devoted to destruction, and Israel's army fell at Ai because of it. After the judgment, "they raised over him a great heap of stones, still there to this day. So the LORD turned from the fierceness of His anger" (Joshua 7:26). Israel raised a similar heap over the king of Ai (Joshua 8:29). Later Absalom, who had rebelled against his father David, was thrown into a pit with "a very large heap of stones over him" (2 Samuel 18:17). Most graves honor the person buried there. These piles left a lasting record of rebellion and its cost. The same kind of stones could seal a peace or mark a judgment, and the difference lay in how people had dealt with God.

Psalm 42:7 and Jonah 2:3: "Your waves." The psalmist, cast down and far from the house of God, writes, "All Your waves and billows have gone over me." Jonah, thrown into the sea for running from God, prays almost the same words: "All Your billows and Your waves passed over me." In Jonah's prayer the waves stand beside "the belly of Sheol" (v. 2) and "the Pit" (v. 6), the place of the dead. The water had become the grave closing over him. Yet both men say "Your." The small word "Your" means they did not treat their suffering as random. The flood that might seem to separate them from God came from His hand. That is why both men call out to Him from inside it, and why Jonah's prayer ends, "Salvation is of the LORD" (Jonah 2:9).

Isaiah 48:18: righteousness like the waves. Everywhere else, waves threaten. In Isaiah 48:18 God uses the same image to grieve over what Israel lost: "Oh, that you had heeded My commandments! Then your peace would have been like a river, And your righteousness like the waves of the sea." The image is of steady flowing water: a river that keeps running, and waves that keep arriving, set after set, day and night. God wanted His people's righteousness to have that kind of steady force. It would be renewed again and again instead of appearing as an occasional effort. The verse is a lament, but it also shows God's intent. The waves He wanted for His people were the ongoing goodness of a life that listens to Him.

Song of Solomon 4:12: a sealed spring. The bridegroom calls his bride "a garden enclosed... A spring shut up, A fountain sealed." The water here is fresh water rising from the ground, protected and kept for one beloved. The picture is usually read as describing the bride's purity and her faithfulness to one love. (Some ancient copies read "garden" in place of "spring" here; the NKJV follows the standard Hebrew text.)

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The God who tells the waves "no farther" stepped into a fishing boat. When a storm filled it with water, Jesus "rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, 'Peace, be still!' And the wind ceased and there was a great calm" (Mark 4:39). The disciples asked, "Who can this be, that even the wind and the sea obey Him!" (v. 41). The Psalms had already answered: "When its waves rise, You still them." Mark never names Psalm 89, but the authority Jesus exercises in the boat is the authority Israel's Scriptures give to the LORD alone.

Jonah's waves also point toward Christ. Jesus called His coming three days in the grave "the sign of the prophet Jonah" (Matthew 12:39–40). Jonah passed under God's waves and was brought up again. Christ went into death itself and rose.

The heaps follow the same pattern. The stones over Achan marked the Valley of Achor, which means trouble (Joshua 7:26). Through Hosea, God later promised to give His people "the Valley of Achor as a door of hope" (Hosea 2:15). The place of judgment was to become an entrance into restored love. In Christ, judgment has fallen on Another, and the New Testament gives stones a new picture. Peter describes believers "as living stones" being "built up a spiritual house" (1 Peter 2:5). Peter is not commenting on these heaps, but the contrast is fair to draw: a loose pile of stones covered the rebel, and God builds living stones into His house.

7. Spirit & Life

The psalmist's word "Your" matters for believers today. When troubles roll over us one after another, it is tempting to see only the storm. Psalm 42 and Jonah 2 give us words to say "Your waves." That is not resignation. It means the sea we are drowning in belongs to the One we can pray to. The God who disciplines His children has also set the sand where the waves must stop.

Genesis 31 shows that relationships can be settled before God. Jacob and Laban could not fully trust each other, yet they could set a boundary, eat together, and leave the watching to the LORD. Many hard family situations need that same kind of peace, kept honest because God is the witness.

Isaiah 48:18 shows what God wants for His people. Obedience is meant to produce a steady righteousness that keeps coming like the tide. The Lord who calms the destructive waves also wants to give His people righteousness like the waves.

Song of Solomon 4:12 offers a picture of the inner life. A sealed spring stays fresh because it is guarded. As an application, a heart kept for the Lord alone stays fresh, and life can flow from it to others as well.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Psalm 107:25, 29. God "commands and raises the stormy wind, Which lifts up the waves of the sea." Four verses later, He calms the storm so that its waves are still. The same God both raises the storm and ends it.

Jeremiah 31:35. God, "Who disturbs the sea, And its waves roar," points to His rule over creation as the guarantee of His promise to Israel.

Isaiah 51:15. "I am the LORD your God, Who divided the sea whose waves roared." This recalls the Exodus to comfort exiles who needed God to make a way for them again.

Ezekiel 26:3. God brings many nations against proud Tyre the way the sea drives its waves against the shore.

Zechariah 10:11. When God brings His scattered people home, He "shall pass through the sea with affliction, And strike the waves of the sea."

Job 8:17. Bildad describes the roots of the godless wrapping around a heap of stones. The plant looks secure but is soon torn up.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H5067 (nēd), heap of water. This is a distinct word used for the waters standing "like a heap" at the Red Sea (Exodus 15:8). It shows water held still by God's power, while gal shows waves in motion.
