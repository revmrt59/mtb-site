FOLDING, RODS - (gâlîyl, Strong's H1550)

Summary

The Hebrew word gâlîyl describes something that turns or is round. Scripture uses it only four times, in three verses, and each use is a small craftsman's detail. In Solomon's temple it describes the folding, turning panels of the great cypress doors (1 Kings 6:34). In the Persian palace of King Ahasuerus it names the silver rods that held up his linen curtains (Esther 1:6). In the Song of Solomon a bride uses it to praise her beloved: "His hands are rods of gold Set with beryl" (Song of Songs 5:14).

These small uses lead to large biblical subjects. The turning doors show a God whose house could be entered, but only through the way He designed. The silver rods hold up the showpiece of an earthly king whose feast ended with his queen sent away. The golden rods hold up no decoration at all. They are the beloved's own hands, and they are praised as the finest thing in the room. Read together, the three verses move from the doorway of God's house, past the glitter of a passing empire, to the strength and worth of a person. For Christian readers, that movement points to Jesus Christ, who is both the way into God's presence and the true treasure of His people.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gâlîyl is a Hebrew noun related to the verb gālal, "to roll." The connection fits the uses, since each object either turns on a pivot or has a rounded, cylinder-like shape. Even so, context decides what the word means in each verse, and the root is only a helpful background clue.

All three verses describe expensive objects made by skilled workers. One stood in Israel's temple, one in a Persian royal court, and one is a love poem's picture of a man's hands. The setting is always craftsmanship and costly display.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has two closely related senses:

1. A turning or folding part of a door, describing panels that swing or fold on a pivot. In 1 Kings 6:34, each of the temple's two doors had two panels that formed a "folding door."

2. A round rod, cylinder, or ring, often made of precious metal. In Esther 1:6, curtains hang "on silver rods," and in Song of Songs 5:14 the beloved's hands are "rods of gold." Older translations such as the KJV render it "rings" in both verses. The NKJV's "rods" and the older "rings" express the same basic idea of a rounded form.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning shifts with the setting. In 1 Kings the word describes how something works: the doors turn and fold. In Esther it describes what supports a display, the metal bars that hold up the curtains. In the Song of Solomon the word becomes a metaphor. The poet borrows the picture of a polished metal rod, the kind a reader might picture in a palace, and applies it to living hands.

In every verse, the word appears in the plural and in a list of fine materials such as cypress, silver, marble, gold, beryl, and ivory. The material matters. The palace rods are silver, while the beloved's hands are gold set with jewels.

4. Theological Significance

These verses do not give gâlîyl a fixed symbolic meaning. Together, though, they raise questions about access to God and about where true glory lies.

The temple doors show that God chose to live among His people in a house with a real entrance. That entrance was built with care, decorated, and overlaid with gold. Meeting God was neither casual nor impossible. Doors keep people out, and doors also let people in. Solomon's turning panels did both.

The rods in Esther and in the Song show two kinds of glory. One is the borrowed glory of a king's possessions. The other is the glory of a person, praised for who he is. Scripture consistently treats the second as greater. Wealth can decorate a court, but a person can be loved.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Kings 6:34 — the doors of the temple. After describing the olive-wood doors of the inner sanctuary (6:31–32), the writer turns to the entrance of the temple's main hall: "the two doors were of cypress wood; two panels comprised one folding door, and two panels comprised the other folding door." Because each door had two folding panels, these heavy doors could swing open without needing an enormous arc. Verse 35 adds that they were carved with cherubim, palm trees, and open flowers and overlaid with gold. Even the hinge work of God's house received skilled attention. The entrance was designed to be opened, and opening it meant stepping into the presence of the Holy One. Ezekiel's vision of a future temple describes the same arrangement, with "two folding panels" for each door (Ezekiel 41:24). Ezekiel uses different Hebrew wording there, but his vision shows that the pattern of doors that open into God's house continued in Israel's hope.

Esther 1:6 — the rods of an empire. Ahasuerus displayed "the riches of his glorious kingdom" for 180 days and then held a seven-day banquet (Esther 1:4–5). The writer lingers over the setting, which included white and blue linen hung "on silver rods and marble pillars," along with couches of gold and silver. The silver rods are only supports, hardware that keeps the curtains in place. The irony arrives quickly. At the height of the feast the king summons Queen Vashti to show off her beauty, she refuses, and she is removed as queen (1:10–19). All that silver held up a celebration that ended in a broken marriage. The king could display his treasure, but he could not win his wife's willing heart.

Song of Songs 5:14 — the hands of the beloved. When the daughters of Jerusalem ask what makes her beloved special (5:9), the bride answers with a portrait that moves from head to legs (5:10–16). She says, "His hands are rods of gold Set with beryl." The poet may picture rounded fingers with gem-like nails, or golden rings, but the point is clear. His hands are strong, precious, and finely made. Here the costly metal belongs to the man himself. His hands do not hold up a display, because they are the thing worth admiring. The portrait ends, "Yes, he is altogether lovely" (5:16).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The temple doors belong to a long biblical story about entering God's presence. Jesus said, "I am the door. If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved, and will go in and out and find pasture" (John 10:9). Hebrews says believers have "boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way which He consecrated for us, through the veil, that is, His flesh" (Hebrews 10:19–20). The carved cypress doors were a real way into God's house. Christ is the way they pointed toward. In Him, a person enters God's presence and is received.

The contrast between Esther and the Song also points toward Christ. Christians have long read the Song's bridegroom as a picture of Christ and His people. Scripture does not state that reading, but it rests on the biblical pattern of God as husband and Christ as bridegroom (Ephesians 5:25–32). Ahasuerus used his glory to show off and sent away a bride who would not perform for him. The Bridegroom gave Himself for His bride. The hands that Thomas was invited to touch still bore the marks of the nails (John 20:27). Hebrews 1:3 adds that the Son is "upholding all things by the word of His power." Hebrews speaks of His word rather than His hands, but it confirms what the Song's picture suggests. His strength holds things together.

7. Spirit & Life

The folding doors of 1 Kings 6 encourage reverence without fear. God built His house with an entrance and decorated it with gold. He wants His people to come in. Through Christ, believers come boldly, and they come into the presence of the Holy God.

Esther 1 warns about wealth that promises glory and cannot deliver it. Ahasuerus had every resource, and his story still begins with a lonely king and an empty throne beside him. Many lives are built like that court, with impressive supports holding up a display. The question is what the display is for, and whether anyone at the center is truly loved.

The bride in the Song shows another way to live. Her answer to "What is your beloved?" is a description of him, not of his possessions. A believer can learn to answer the same way: Christ Himself is the treasure. The bride praises her beloved's hands as golden, and believers can look at Christ's hands knowing what they have done. They were wounded for their sake, and they hold them still.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This word occurs only in the three verses developed in Section 5. In 1 Kings 6:34 the word appears twice, once for each folding door, so the second occurrence simply repeats the description of the first door's panels.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H1556 (gālal), "to roll":** This verb is the lexical root from which gâlîyl is derived. The idea of rolling fits the turning doors and rounded rods described by H1550.
