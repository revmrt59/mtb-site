GILEAD - (Gilʻâd, Strong's H1568)

Summary

Gilead is the high, rugged, well-watered country east of the Jordan River. It was known for rich pasture, thick forest and the healing balm that traders carried to Egypt. The same name also belongs to a man, Gilead the son of Machir and grandson of Manasseh, whose descendants became the Gileadite clans. It is used for the people who lived in that land as well. The meaning of the name is not certain. Genesis 31 links the region with Galeed, the heap of witness that Jacob and Laban raised as a boundary marker. That link is a wordplay on a related name rather than a proven derivation. The name itself most likely describes a rugged or rocky country, which fits the terrain well.

Gilead's story runs from Jacob's flight from Laban to the prophets' promises of a regathered flock. Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh asked to settle there because it was good cattle country. Moses granted the request only after the men of those tribes promised to fight beside their brothers west of the Jordan. From then on Gilead was fully part of Israel, yet always on the edge, exposed to Ammon, Aram and finally Assyria. It produced Jephthah and Elijah. It sheltered Saul's house and later David during Absalom's revolt. Its land was fought over at Ramoth Gilead, and it was among the first Israelite territory carried into exile. Jeremiah turned its most famous product into a piercing question: "Is there no balm in Gilead, Is there no physician there?" (Jeremiah 8:22).

That question goes to the heart of what Gilead teaches. Its natural wealth could not heal a people sick with sin, and the healing Israel needed had to come from the LORD Himself. The prophets do not leave Gilead in ruins. Micah prays for the divine Shepherd to let His flock "feed in Bashan and Gilead, As in days of old" (Micah 7:14). Zechariah hears God promise to bring His scattered people home "into the land of Gilead" (Zechariah 10:10). The New Testament never names Gilead, but those promises point toward the Shepherd and Physician whom the Gospels reveal in Jesus Christ.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The region. In most of its occurrences, Gilead names the hill country east of the Jordan River. Bashan lay to the north, and Moab and Ammon lay to the south and east. The Bible sometimes uses "Gilead" narrowly for the central highlands. At other times it uses the name broadly for nearly all of Israel's land east of the Jordan. Deuteronomy 3:12–16 divides Gilead between Reuben and Gad in the south and the half-tribe of Manasseh in the north. The region's boundaries shifted over the centuries as Ammonite and Aramean power rose and fell. The borders described in Joshua show what was granted, and Israel did not always hold all of it.

The people. "Gilead" can also mean the people who lived in the region, much as "Israel" can mean either a land or a nation. Deborah's song says Gilead "stayed beyond the Jordan" (Judges 5:17). In that verse the name stands for the tribes who held back from the battle. Individuals are identified as "the Gileadite": Jephthah (Judges 11:1), Barzillai (2 Samuel 17:27, a related term), and Elijah, "of the inhabitants of Gilead" (1 Kings 17:1).

Persons named Gilead. Three different men bear the name, and they should not be merged:

  • Gilead son of Machir, grandson of Manasseh (Numbers 26:29–30). He was the ancestor of several Manassite clans, including the line of Zelophehad, whose daughters received an inheritance (Numbers 27:1; 36:1; Joshua 17:1–6). Machir is repeatedly called "the father of Gilead" (1 Chronicles 2:21–23; 7:14, 17). In these genealogies the man's name and the land's name run together. Machir's descendants took the region, and Moses gave Gilead to Machir (Numbers 32:39–40).

  • Gilead the father of Jephthah (Judges 11:1–2). This Gilead lived in the period of the judges. He had sons by his wife, and those sons drove out Jephthah, the son of a harlot. Because the clan and the region share the same name, this man may be named after them, but the text treats him as a particular father in a particular family.

  • Gilead a Gadite, named in the genealogy of Abihail (1 Chronicles 5:14).

Compound and related names. Several place names are built with "Gilead." Jabesh Gilead is the town tied to Saul's story. Ramoth Gilead is the city of refuge and the frequent battleground of the Aramean wars. Galeed (H1567, heap of witness) is the name Jacob gave to the boundary heap in Genesis 31:47–48. These places deserve their own discussion. They appear here because their stories happened in Gilead and help explain it, not because they are additional meanings of the word.

The name's meaning. The name's meaning is uncertain. The most likely sense is a rugged or rocky region, which suits Gilead's steep, broken highlands. Genesis 31 adds a biblical wordplay. When Jacob and Laban piled up stones as a witness between them, Jacob called the heap Galeed, heap of witness (Genesis 31:46–48). The sound of Galeed echoes Gilead, and the scene gives the region a memorable association with a boundary witnessed before God. That association is a wordplay. It is not proof of where the name came from. The narrator already calls the area "the mountains of Gilead" before the heap is built (Genesis 31:21), so Galeed is better read as a fitting echo than as the origin of the name.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Beginnings: a boundary and a caravan

Gilead enters Scripture during a tense family break. Jacob slipped away from Laban: "He arose and crossed the river, and headed toward the mountains of Gilead" (Genesis 31:21). Laban caught up with him there (Genesis 31:23, 25). God had already warned Laban in a dream not to harm Jacob (Genesis 31:24). The two men ended their quarrel with a sworn agreement, a stone heap and a pillar. Neither would cross that line to harm the other, and the God of Abraham and Nahor would judge between them (Genesis 31:44–53). Gilead's first role, then, is a boundary witnessed before God.

Six chapters later, a caravan of Ishmaelites comes "from Gilead" carrying spices, balm and myrrh down to Egypt (Genesis 37:25). Joseph's brothers sold him to these traders. Gilead's healing resin travelled to Egypt in the same caravan as the young man God would use to preserve many lives (Genesis 50:20). The Bible makes nothing explicit of the pairing, but it is the first mention of the balm that Jeremiah will later make famous.

Conquest and a conditional settlement

After Israel defeated Sihon and Og, the land east of the Jordan lay open (Deuteronomy 2:36; 3:10). Reuben and Gad "saw the land of Jazer and the land of Gilead, that indeed the region was a place for livestock" (Numbers 32:1), and they asked to settle there rather than cross the Jordan. Moses heard the request as a repeat of Kadesh Barnea, where an earlier generation lost heart and refused the land (Numbers 32:6–15). The tribes answered with a pledge. They would build sheepfolds and cities for their families, and their armed men would cross over and fight until their brothers had received their inheritance. Moses accepted on that condition and warned that if they failed, their sin would find them out (Numbers 32:16–32). Their little ones stayed behind in the cities of Gilead (Numbers 32:26). Machir's clan took the northern part (Numbers 32:39–40).

Before he died, Moses climbed Mount Nebo. There the LORD showed him the whole land, beginning with "all the land of Gilead as far as Dan" (Deuteronomy 34:1). Ramoth in Gilead became a city of refuge (Deuteronomy 4:43; Joshua 20:8) and a Levitical city (Joshua 21:38). The daughters of Zelophehad, descendants of Gilead, received their inheritance among their father's brothers, because God had ruled that their claim was just (Numbers 27:1–7; Joshua 17:3–6).

The altar by the Jordan

The eastern tribes kept their word. When the wars ended, Joshua sent them home to Gilead with a blessing (Joshua 22:1–9). On the way they built a large altar by the Jordan. The western tribes assumed this was rebellion and gathered for war (Joshua 22:11–12). Phinehas and the leaders went to the land of Gilead to confront them (Joshua 22:13–15). The answer they received is one of the most moving moments in the book. The eastern tribes feared that someday the river would become a spiritual border. They worried that later generations west of the Jordan would tell their children, "You have no part in the LORD" (Joshua 22:24–25). They meant the altar as a witness that they too worshiped the LORD at His one sanctuary, not as a rival place of sacrifice (Joshua 22:26–29). Phinehas and the leaders returned satisfied (Joshua 22:32–33).

Judges: a frontier under pressure

Gilead's exposed position soon showed. In Deborah's day, Gilead stayed beyond the Jordan while other tribes risked their lives (Judges 5:17). Jair judged Israel and his sons held towns in Gilead (Judges 10:3–4). Then the Ammonites oppressed the Israelites in Gilead for eighteen years (Judges 10:8). When Ammon camped in Gilead, Gilead's leaders asked who would lead the fight (Judges 10:17–18).

Their answer was a man they had thrown out. Jephthah, the son of Gilead by a harlot, had been driven away by his half-brothers (Judges 11:1–3). Now the elders of Gilead came asking him to be their head. The elders called the LORD as witness, and Jephthah spoke all his words before the LORD at Mizpah (Judges 11:5–11). "The Spirit of the LORD came upon Jephthah," and he passed through Gilead to defeat Ammon (Judges 11:29–33). His victory came wrapped in tragedy through his rash vow (Judges 11:30–40). Then came civil war. The Ephraimites mocked the Gileadites as "fugitives of Ephraim," and Gilead's men held the fords of the Jordan. Anyone who said "Sibboleth" instead of "Shibboleth" was killed (Judges 12:4–6). The river that the eastern tribes had feared might divide Israel now marked a line of slaughter between brothers. Jephthah judged six years and was buried in a city of Gilead (Judges 12:7). Even so, Hebrews 11:32 names him among those who acted in faith.

At the end of Judges, Jabesh Gilead failed to join Israel's assembly at Mizpah and was nearly wiped out. Four hundred young women of Jabesh Gilead were given as wives to the surviving Benjamites (Judges 20:1; 21:8–14). The episode reveals how dark that period was. It also gave Jabesh Gilead family ties to Benjamin, the tribe of Saul.

Saul, David and the loyalty of Gilead

Jabesh Gilead appears again at the start of Saul's reign. Nahash the Ammonite besieged Jabesh Gilead and offered terms of surrender that included gouging out every right eye. Saul rallied Israel and rescued the city (1 Samuel 11:1–11). The men of Jabesh never forgot it. When the Philistines hung Saul's body on the wall of Beth Shan, the valiant men of Jabesh Gilead walked all night to take it down and bury him (1 Samuel 31:11–13). David blessed them for their kindness to their lord (2 Samuel 2:4–6) and later gave Saul's bones an honorable burial (2 Samuel 21:12–14).

After Saul's death, Abner made Ishbosheth king over Gilead and the northern tribes (2 Samuel 2:8–9). Years later David himself fled across the Jordan from Absalom. Absalom and Israel camped in the land of Gilead (2 Samuel 17:26). In that same eastern country, Shobi, Machir of Lo Debar and Barzillai the Gileadite brought beds, food and supplies to the king and his weary people, saying the people were hungry, tired and thirsty in the wilderness (2 Samuel 17:27–29, paraphrased). The battle that ended Absalom's revolt was fought in a forest where "the woods devoured more people that day than the sword devoured" (2 Samuel 18:8).

Gilead remained part of the kingdom's settled order. David's census passed through it (2 Samuel 24:6). Levites served the king's affairs at Jazer of Gilead (1 Chronicles 26:31), and the half-tribe of Manasseh in Gilead had its own officer (1 Chronicles 27:21). Solomon's district governors administered the region (1 Kings 4:13, 19).

Elijah of Gilead

During the reign of Ahab, a man from Gilead steps into the story without introduction: "And Elijah the Tishbite, of the inhabitants of Gilead, said to Ahab, 'As the LORD God of Israel lives, before whom I stand, there shall not be dew nor rain these years, except at my word'" (1 Kings 17:1). Scripture tells us nothing of Elijah's family or training. It gives only his home region and the God before whom he stood. That plain description sets him against Ahab's palace, where Baal worship had the queen's backing.

Ramoth Gilead and the Aramean wars

Ramoth Gilead became the flashpoint between Israel and Aram. Ahab persuaded Jehoshaphat of Judah to help him retake it (1 Kings 22:3–4; 2 Chronicles 18:2–3). Four hundred prophets promised victory. Micaiah alone spoke the truth, and Ahab died there in disguise, struck by an arrow drawn at random (1 Kings 22:29–37; 2 Chronicles 18:5–34). Later, Ahab's son Joram, who followed his brother Ahaziah (1 Kings 22:40, 51), was wounded at Ramoth Gilead (2 Kings 8:28; 2 Chronicles 22:5). There Elisha's young messenger anointed Jehu king and commissioned him to judge the house of Ahab (2 Kings 9:1–14).

Gilead paid dearly for being on the frontier. In Jehu's time "the LORD began to cut off parts of Israel," and Hazael of Aram struck all the land of Gilead (2 Kings 10:32–33). Amos says God would punish Damascus "Because they have threshed Gilead with implements of iron" (Amos 1:3). He condemns Ammon for ripping open pregnant women in Gilead to enlarge its borders (Amos 1:13).

Exile

The end came under Assyria: "Tiglath-Pileser king of Assyria came and took Ijon, Abel Beth Maachah, Janoah, Kedesh, Hazor, Gilead, and Galilee, all the land of Naphtali; and he carried them captive to Assyria" (2 Kings 15:29). Chronicles explains the reason for this early exile. The eastern tribes had once cried out to God in battle, and He answered them because they trusted in Him (1 Chronicles 5:18–22). Later they were unfaithful and played the harlot after the gods of the land, and God stirred up the king of Assyria against them (1 Chronicles 5:25–26).

The prophets: indictment, lament and hope

Hosea names Gilead's sin bluntly: "Gilead is a city of evildoers And defiled with blood" (Hosea 6:8). He also pairs it with worthless worship (Hosea 12:11). Whether "city" here means one town, perhaps Ramoth Gilead, or the whole region pictured as one corrupt community is uncertain. Either way, Hosea's charge is moral and spiritual, not geographical.

Jeremiah uses Gilead in three ways. First, he asks, "Is there no balm in Gilead, Is there no physician there? Why then is there no recovery For the health of the daughter of my people?" (Jeremiah 8:22). Second, he tells Egypt to go up to Gilead for balm, but in vain, because its wound will not heal (Jeremiah 46:11). Third, he uses Gilead to picture both glory and loss: God tells the royal house of Judah that it is like Gilead to Him, yet He will make it a wilderness (Jeremiah 22:6). Beyond judgment, Jeremiah promises that Israel will return to its pasture and be satisfied on Mount Ephraim and in Gilead (Jeremiah 50:19).

Obadiah foresees Benjamin possessing Gilead (Obadiah 19). Micah prays, "Let them feed in Bashan and Gilead, As in days of old" (Micah 7:14). Zechariah hears God promise to gather His people from Egypt and Assyria: "I will bring them into the land of Gilead and Lebanon, Until no more room is found for them" (Zechariah 10:10). Ezekiel's vision of the restored land draws its eastern border along the Jordan, between Gilead and the land of Israel (Ezekiel 47:18). In that vision, the tribal allotments lie west of the river, which differs from the historical settlement.

Gilead also appears in love poetry. The bride's hair is "like a flock of goats, Going down from Mount Gilead" (Song of Solomon 4:1; compare 6:5). Anyone who had watched dark goats streaming down a hillside would understand. The comparison draws on Gilead's reputation as rich grazing country (Numbers 32:1).

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states plainly that Gilead belonged to Israel's inheritance and to God. Moses gave it to Reuben, Gad and Manasseh (Numbers 32:33; Deuteronomy 3:12–16). In the Psalms, God Himself claims Gilead and Manasseh as His own while He promises victory over Israel's enemies (Psalm 60:7; 108:8). The land on the far side of the river was not second-class territory. God claimed it by name.

Scripture also shows Gilead living with a tension it never fully resolved. The river did not cut the eastern tribes off from the LORD, but it made separation easy. Their altar in Joshua 22 shows how keenly they felt that danger. Deborah's rebuke shows them holding back (Judges 5:17). The Shibboleth killings show brothers turning on one another at the Jordan fords (Judges 12:4–6). Chronicles records that their unfaithfulness brought an early exile (1 Chronicles 5:25–26). A reasonable conclusion, though Scripture never states it as a rule, is that the land that seemed most immediately suited to their flocks also left them most exposed. Distance from the center of worship made faithfulness harder, not impossible. Scripture does not condemn the settlement itself. Moses approved it on terms the tribes kept, and Joshua blessed them for keeping them (Joshua 22:1–6). The later failure came from turning to other gods, not from living east of the Jordan.

Gilead also shows how God protects and uses the margins of His people. The LORD raised a deliverer from Gilead's rejected son (Judges 11:29). He sent His sharpest prophetic word to Ahab through a man from Gilead (1 Kings 17:1). He provided for David in Gilead when Jerusalem had turned against him (2 Samuel 17:27–29). He preserved Saul's honor through Jabesh Gilead's loyalty (1 Samuel 31:11–13). Time and again, a region far from the capital became a place where God's purposes moved forward.

Finally, Gilead shows God's justice and His restoring mercy toward one region. He judged the nations that crushed Gilead (Amos 1:3, 13). He also judged Gilead for its own bloodshed and false worship (Hosea 6:8; 12:11). Yet the prophets do not end with Gilead lost. They name it as a place of homecoming (Jeremiah 50:19; Micah 7:14; Zechariah 10:10). The region that suffered early in the exile is also named in the promises of restoration.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

The New Testament never mentions Gilead by name, so the connections to Christ come through the larger themes Gilead carries in the Old Testament. Some of these connections rest on explicit New Testament teaching. Others are fitting resonances that readers should hold more loosely.

The Shepherd who brings the flock home. Micah's prayer, "Shepherd Your people with Your staff, The flock of Your heritage... Let them feed in Bashan and Gilead, As in days of old" (Micah 7:14), belongs to a strong line of prophetic hope. In that hope God Himself shepherds His scattered people (Ezekiel 34:11–16), and Zechariah's promise of regathering echoes it (Zechariah 10:8–10). Jesus claims that role directly when He calls Himself the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep and gathers other sheep into one flock (John 10:11, 16). The New Testament does not say "Gilead" when it speaks of the Good Shepherd. But the hope Micah attached to Gilead's pastures, rest and plenty under God's own care, is the hope Christians see fulfilled in Jesus, the good shepherd.

The Physician Gilead could not supply. Jeremiah's question exposes a painful gap. Gilead had balm, and Israel presumably had physicians, yet the daughter of God's people was not healed (Jeremiah 8:22). The sickness was sin, and natural remedies could not reach it. Elsewhere Jeremiah himself points to the only cure when he prays, "Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed" (Jeremiah 17:14). In the Gospels, Jesus describes His mission in a physician's terms. He says the healthy do not need a doctor, but the sick do, and He came to call sinners to repentance (Mark 2:17; Luke 5:31–32, paraphrased). The New Testament does not identify Jesus as the "balm of Gilead." Still, Christian readers have long heard Jeremiah's unanswered question answered in Christ, and the connection follows naturally from the Bible's own language of sin as sickness and God as healer.

Elijah of Gilead and the forerunner. Here the New Testament speaks explicitly. Malachi promised that God would send Elijah the prophet before the day of the LORD (Malachi 4:5). Jesus said John the Baptist was the Elijah who was to come (Matthew 11:14; 17:10–13). John came in the spirit and power of Elijah to prepare a people for the Lord (Luke 1:17). The prophet who appeared without warning from Gilead to confront Ahab became the pattern for the man who prepared Israel for Christ. John ministered at the Jordan, and John's Gospel twice locates his work "beyond the Jordan" (John 1:28; 10:40). That was the eastern side, though the Gospel does not call it Gilead. When Jesus later withdrew there, many came to Him and believed (John 10:40–42). The geographical echo is a resonance rather than a stated fulfillment, but it fits the pattern of God's work moving forward on the edge of things.

The rejected king sustained across the Jordan. David crossed into Gilead rejected by his own son and much of his own people. There he was fed and sheltered by loyal friends (2 Samuel 17:22–29). The New Testament does not point to this episode by name. Even so, it fits a pattern the whole Bible traces: God's anointed king rejected by those who should have received him, from David to the Son of David (John 1:11). As a canonical comparison rather than a stated type, David's flight gives a fitting picture of the rejected Son of David.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Gilead's story warns against letting distance from God's presence become a habit of the heart. The eastern tribes saw that danger clearly at the beginning. Their altar was a protest against the idea that a river could separate them from the LORD (Joshua 22:24–27). Generations later their descendants drifted into the very separation their fathers had feared (1 Chronicles 5:25–26). Most believers do not fall away in one dramatic act. They settle where life seems comfortable, attend to the flocks and fields, and slowly stop crossing over to worship. Gilead's story can call us to treat our connection to God's people and God's presence as something to guard deliberately, not something we can assume.

Jeremiah's question searches every generation: "Is there no balm in Gilead, Is there no physician there?" (Jeremiah 8:22). Judah had resources, religion and a famous local remedy, and still it was dying. We, too, can reach for good things to treat a wound only God can heal: success, comfort, busyness, even religious activity. The balm was real, but it was the wrong medicine for sin. The answer is not to despise ordinary help. It is to bring the deepest sickness to the Physician who forgives sins and makes the sinner whole.

Gilead also shows that God is not confined to the centers of power. He raised Jephthah from among the outcasts and put His Spirit on him (Judges 11:1–3, 29). He sent Elijah from Gilead's hill country to speak before a king who had built an altar for Baal in Samaria (1 Kings 16:32; 17:1). He fed David in the wilderness through an old man named Barzillai (2 Samuel 17:27–29; 19:31–39). People who feel they live at the edge of things, far from influence or recognition, can take heart. God's word and God's provision often arrive from places the capital overlooks.

The men of Jabesh Gilead give a quieter lesson. Saul had rescued them once (1 Samuel 11:1–11). Years later, when Saul had fallen and his body hung in disgrace, they risked their lives to honor him (1 Samuel 31:11–13). David blessed them for showing that kindness to their lord (2 Samuel 2:5–6). Gratitude that remembers and acts, long after the favor was received, is a form of faithfulness that God notices.

Finally, Gilead's last word in the prophets is hope. The land that was threshed with iron and carried away early is named in God's promise of homecoming: "I will bring them into the land of Gilead and Lebanon, Until no more room is found for them" (Zechariah 10:10). No one who has been scattered by sin or by suffering is beyond the reach of the Shepherd who promises to bring His flock home and feed them "As in days of old" (Micah 7:14).

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 31:21–25, 44–53 — Gilead's first appearance. Jacob and Laban make a sworn agreement there, and the heap named Galeed supplies the biblical wordplay on the name.

  • Genesis 37:25 — The Ishmaelite caravan from Gilead carries balm to Egypt along with Joseph. This is the first mention of Gilead's famous resin.

  • Numbers 32:1–42 — Reuben and Gad request Gilead for its pasture, and Moses grants it on condition that their soldiers fight alongside Israel. Machir's clan takes the north.

  • Numbers 26:29; Joshua 17:1–6 — Gilead the son of Machir and his descendants, including Zelophehad's daughters.

  • Deuteronomy 3:12–16; 34:1 — The division of Gilead among the eastern tribes, and Moses' view of the land from Nebo.

  • Joshua 22:9–34 — The altar by the Jordan, built to witness that the eastern tribes shared in the LORD's worship.

  • Judges 5:17; 10:17–12:7 — Gilead holds back in Deborah's day, then Jephthah delivers Gilead from Ammon, followed by the Shibboleth killings.

  • 1 Samuel 11:1–11; 31:11–13; 2 Samuel 2:4–6 — Saul rescues Jabesh Gilead, and the men of Jabesh show loyal kindness to him at his death.

  • 2 Samuel 17:22–29 — David finds refuge and provision in Gilead during Absalom's revolt.

  • 1 Kings 17:1 — Elijah, "of the inhabitants of Gilead," confronts Ahab.

  • 1 Kings 22:1–37; 2 Kings 9:1–14 — The battles at Ramoth Gilead, Ahab's death, and the anointing of Jehu.

  • 2 Kings 10:32–33; 15:29; 1 Chronicles 5:18–26 — Aramean and Assyrian conquest, and Chronicles' explanation for the early exile of the eastern tribes.

  • Psalm 60:7; 108:8 — God claims Gilead as His own.

  • Song of Solomon 4:1; 6:5 — Gilead's flocks as a picture of beauty.

  • Jeremiah 8:22; 46:11 — The balm of Gilead cannot heal God's people or Egypt, which points to the need for God's own healing.

  • Hosea 6:8; 12:11; Amos 1:3, 13 — Gilead's own guilt, and the nations judged for crushing it.

  • Jeremiah 50:19; Obadiah 19; Micah 7:14; Zechariah 10:10 — Promises of restoration, with Gilead as a pasture of homecoming.

  • John 10:11, 16; Matthew 11:14; 17:10–13 — Jesus as the Good Shepherd who gathers the flock, and John the Baptist as the Elijah who was to come.
