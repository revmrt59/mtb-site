LOVE, LOVED, LOVERS, FRIEND - (ʼâhab, Strong's H0157)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʼâhab is the Old Testament's main word for "to love." One word covers the whole range of love: a father's tenderness for his son, a husband's longing for his wife, the bond between two friends, a king's loyalty to an ally, and the love God commands from His people with "all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength" (Deuteronomy 6:5). It can also describe a person's attraction to sleep, money, wisdom, violence, or the law of God. Whatever a person loves shows where his heart has settled.

Some of the most important uses of ʼâhab tell how God loves. He sets His love on Israel because He chooses to, not because they deserve it. He loves justice and righteousness, and He loves the stranger. Through the prophet Hosea, He keeps loving a people who have run after other "lovers." In return, God asks for love that holds both loyalty and delight. Deuteronomy commands that love, Psalm 119 sings it, and God promises to make it possible by changing the heart. In Jesus Christ these lines meet. He is the beloved Son, the faithful Israel, and the One who loves the unfaithful back to Himself.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼâhab is a common verb: the word occurs 211 times in 197 verses of the Hebrew Bible. Many uses cluster in Deuteronomy, Psalms, Proverbs, Hosea, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. In the stories of Genesis and the historical books, it describes love within families and friendships. In Deuteronomy, it describes the relationship between God and Israel. In the Psalms and Proverbs, it reveals what a person truly values. In the prophets, it exposes Israel's betrayal and declares God's refusal to give her up.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

In the ancient world, "love" could also be the language of loyalty between rulers and allies. Scripture uses it this way when Hiram king of Tyre is said to have "always loved David" (1 Kings 5:1). This background helps explain why Deuteronomy can command love. The command asks for undivided loyalty, and the rest of Scripture shows that God also wants the heart behind it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʼâhab means to love: to be strongly attached to someone or something, with affection, desire, or loyal commitment. Context shows which side of love is in view. The main senses are:

1. Love between people, whether family affection, friendship, or romantic and married love, as when God speaks of "your only son Isaac, whom you love" (Genesis 22:2), or when the bride seeks "the one I love" (Song of Songs 3:1).

2. Loyal devotion within a committed relationship, such as Israel's love for the LORD (Deuteronomy 6:5) or one king's faithfulness to another (1 Kings 5:1).

3. Delight in or preference for a thing or way of life, such as righteousness, violence, wisdom, sleep, or money (Psalm 11:5; Proverbs 20:13; Ecclesiastes 5:10).

4. In a form that works like a noun, "one who loves": a "friend" (2 Chronicles 20:7) or, in the prophets, "lovers" who draw Israel away from God (Hosea 2:5).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Who loves, and what is loved, determines the force of the word. When God is the one who loves, ʼâhab describes His choosing, faithful commitment to His people. When a person loves a thing, the word works like a diagnosis of his heart. "Do not love sleep, lest you come to poverty" (Proverbs 20:13) uses the same verb as "you shall love the LORD your God."

Love often stands side by side with hate, and each sharpens the other. "The wicked and the one who loves violence His soul hates" (Psalm 11:5). Amos tells Israel to hate evil and love good (Amos 5:15). The contrast shows that biblical love takes sides.

The noun-like form "one who loves" changes with its setting. It names a trusted "friend," as when Abraham is called God's friend (2 Chronicles 20:7; Isaiah 41:8). In Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Hosea, the same form becomes "lovers," the false gods and foreign powers Israel pursued. The difference between a friend and a lover in these passages lies in whether the love is faithful.

The word also appears as a command. Elsewhere love seems to arise on its own, as in Genesis 29:18, where Jacob loved Rachel. Deuteronomy shows that love can also be required, because it involves the will and not only the feelings.

4. Theological Significance

God's love comes first. Deuteronomy explains that the LORD did not set His love on Israel because they were many. They were the fewest of all peoples. He loved them because He loved them, and because He kept the oath He swore to their fathers (Deuteronomy 7:7–8). In the same chapter, Moses promises, "He will love you and bless you" (7:13). God's love therefore rests on His own character and promise, not on Israel's impressiveness. Isaiah carries the same note when God tells His people that they are precious in His sight and that He has loved them (Isaiah 43:4). Jeremiah hears God say that He has loved them with an everlasting love (Jeremiah 31:3). Even when the people of Malachi's day cynically ask, "In what way have You loved us?", God answers by pointing to His choosing of Jacob: "Yet Jacob I have loved" (Malachi 1:2).

This love is holy. "He loves righteousness and justice" (Psalm 33:5), and "the LORD loves justice, and does not forsake His saints" (Psalm 37:28). Isaiah 61:8 sets God's love and hatred side by side: "I, the LORD, love justice; I hate robbery for burnt offering." Because God loves what is right, He opposes what oppresses and corrupts. His love is no indulgent mood that overlooks evil.

God's love also sets the pattern for His people's love. Deuteronomy 10:18 says that the LORD loves the stranger and provides him food and clothing. The next verse follows directly: "Therefore love the stranger, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 10:19). Leviticus 19 gives the familiar command to love your neighbor as yourself (Leviticus 19:18). Later in the same chapter, it extends the same words to the foreigner living in the land (Leviticus 19:34). Israel was to love outsiders because God does, and because they remembered what it was like to be outsiders themselves.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 6:5 and 30:6: love that only God can give. The Shema calls Israel to "love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength." Throughout Deuteronomy this love is joined to action: to fear Him, walk in His ways, serve Him, and keep His commandments (10:12; 11:1, 13, 22). It is the complete loyalty of a people who belong to one God. Deuteronomy does not pretend Israel can produce this by effort. Near the end of the book, God promises that He Himself will circumcise their hearts and the hearts of their descendants so that they will love Him with all their heart and soul, and live (30:6). The command of chapter 6 becomes the promise of chapter 30. What God requires, He undertakes to create.

1 Samuel 18: the love of Jonathan. "The soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul" (18:1). Jonathan immediately made a covenant with David. He gave David his robe, his armor, his sword, his bow, and his belt (18:3–4). Jonathan was the king's son and heir. In handing over the symbols of his position, he gave David what would have been his own future. His love was a willing surrender of rank for the sake of the one God had chosen. David later lamented Saul and Jonathan as beloved and pleasant in their lives (2 Samuel 1:23).

Psalm 119: love for God's word. No passage gathers ʼâhab more densely. The psalmist writes, "I will delight myself in Your commandments, which I love" (Psalm 119:47). He exclaims how much he loves God's law, which is his meditation all day (Psalm 119:97). He loves God's commandments more than fine gold (Psalm 119:127), and he loves God's law while hating falsehood (Psalm 119:163). This is the language of affection, not grudging duty. The psalmist did not treat God's instruction as a cold rulebook. He loved it as the voice of the God he loved, and he found that those who love God's law have great peace and nothing to make them stumble (Psalm 119:165). Psalm 119 shows what Deuteronomy 30:6 looks like when it comes true in a single life.

Hosea: love that will not let go. Hosea's marriage became a picture of God's heart. Hosea 2 is full of "lovers." Israel, like an unfaithful wife, credits her bread, wool, and oil to the lovers she chases (2:5). The prophets use the same word for false gods and foreign alliances (Jeremiah 22:20; Ezekiel 16:33–37; 23:5). These passages show that God experiences idolatry as personal betrayal, not as a broken technicality. Then God tells Hosea, "Go again, love a woman who is loved by a lover and is committing adultery, just like the love of the LORD for the children of Israel, who look to other gods and love the raisin cakes of the pagans" (Hosea 3:1). The verb appears three times in that one verse: in the command to Hosea to love, in the woman's being loved, and in Israel's love of the raisin cakes. The LORD's own love in the comparison uses the related noun ʼahăbâh (H0160). The LORD's faithful love is the model, and the betrayed husband is told to keep loving. Hosea then buys his wife back at a price (3:2). The book ends with God's promise to heal His people's turning away and to love them freely, as a gift they could never earn (14:4).

The Song of Songs helps explain why this imagery carries such force. Its bride seeks "the one I love" through the night streets (3:1–2) and refuses to rest until she finds him. The prophets take that same exclusive, searching love and show it broken by Israel and kept by God.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The first use of ʼâhab in Scripture is a father's love for "your only son Isaac, whom you love" (Genesis 22:2), spoken as God asks Abraham to offer him on Moriah. Abraham's love for Isaac is a human love, but the scene points beyond itself to the Father who did not spare His own beloved Son.

Hosea 11:1 recalls that when Israel was a child, God loved him and called His son out of Egypt. Matthew applies this verse to Jesus (Matthew 2:15). Where Israel failed to love God with a whole heart, Jesus is the obedient Son who did love Him fully, all the way to the cross. Psalm 45:7 says of the king, "You love righteousness and hate wickedness," and Hebrews 1:9 applies the verse to the Son. When Jesus was asked for the greatest commandment, He joined Deuteronomy 6:5 to Leviticus 19:18: "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Mark 12:31). He then tied love to obedience in the same way Deuteronomy does: "If you love Me, keep My commandments" (John 14:15).

Hosea's buying back of his unfaithful wife does not state a fulfillment, but it closely resembles the gospel. Christ pays the price to redeem a people who chased other lovers, and He loves them freely in order to present them as His bride. The promise of new hearts in Deuteronomy 30:6 finds its answer in the new covenant, where God writes His law on the heart.

7. Spirit & Life

ʼâhab removes a false choice many believers feel: whether love for God is a decision or a feeling. Deuteronomy commands love as loyalty, and Psalm 119 overflows with it as delight. Scripture holds the two together. Obedience without affection is not the whole of what God asks, and affection that refuses obedience is not love at all.

Because the same verb is used for loving God and for loving sleep, money, or violence, it gives each of us an honest question to ask: What does my soul love? Proverbs teaches that misplaced love leads toward ruin, while wisdom promises, "I love those who love me, and those who seek me diligently will find me" (Proverbs 8:17). What we love draws us toward it, so the direction of our love matters.

The deepest comfort in this word lies in the order of things. God loved first, loved the unimpressive, and kept loving the unfaithful. His people do not have to manufacture a perfect heart before coming to Him, because He promised to change the heart Himself. A believer who finds his love for God cold can bring that very coldness to the God who circumcises hearts. He can ask God to make His word as sweet to him as it was to the psalmist.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 29:30–32 — Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah. Leah named her firstborn in hope that her husband would now love her. These verses show how painful love's absence can be, and God saw Leah when Jacob did not.

  • 2 Samuel 13:1, 15 — Amnon claimed to love Tamar, but after he violated her, his hatred became greater than the love he had felt. Desire that uses another person is a counterfeit of love.

  • 1 Kings 11:1–2 — Solomon loved many foreign women, who turned his heart toward other gods. Even the wisest king was led astray by misdirected love.

  • Proverbs 3:12 — The LORD corrects the one He loves, as a father corrects the son he delights in. Hebrews 12:6 draws on this verse to explain the Father's discipline of His children.

  • Proverbs 17:17 — A friend loves at all times. Faithfulness under strain is the mark of true friendship.

  • Psalm 116:1 — The psalmist says he loves the LORD because God heard his voice and his pleas. His love for God is a response to God's mercy.
