GOING DOWN - (gâlash, Strong's H1570)

Summary

The Hebrew verb gâlash means to go down or to step down. The verb occurs 2 times in 2 verses of the Hebrew Bible, both in the Song of Solomon (4:1; 6:5) and both times in the same line of love poetry. The bridegroom tells his bride, "Your hair is like a flock of goats, going down from Mount Gilead" (Song of Solomon 4:1; compare 6:5). He pictures a dark flock pouring down a hillside, so that from a distance it ripples like a moving stream. In his eyes, her beauty is alive and full of motion.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

Because the word is so rare, where the two uses fall matters a great deal. The bridegroom first speaks the line in the joy of what is usually read as their wedding day (Song of Solomon 4). He speaks it again in Song of Solomon 6, after a night when the bride hesitated to open the door, he withdrew, and she searched the city for him in pain (Song of Solomon 5). When they meet again, he does not offer her a reduced or cautious affection. He repeats his earlier praise almost word for word and confesses that her eyes have "overcome" him. Read in light of Scripture's larger picture of Christ and His bride, this small verb carries a large comfort: the Lord's delight in His people outlasts their failures.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gâlash is a Hebrew verb found only in the Song of Solomon, a collection of love poems between a bridegroom and his bride. Some older interpreters suggested a meaning closer to sit or recline, based on possible relatives in other languages. The poem itself favors downward movement, and the NKJV translates both occurrences "going down."

The setting is pastoral. Gilead lay east of the Jordan and was known as good grazing country. When the tribes of Reuben and Gad saw it, they recognized it as "a place for livestock" (Numbers 32:1). Goats in that land were commonly dark-haired. An Israelite reader would easily imagine a black flock winding down a green slope.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Gâlash describes movement downward: stepping, streaming, or flowing down a slope. In both of its uses it describes goats making their way down a hillside. The verb carries no theological meaning of its own. Its spiritual weight comes from the poem in which it appears and from the moment in the story when the bridegroom speaks the line.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Both uses compare the bride's hair to a descending flock. The comparison depends on what the goats are doing. A flock standing still on a hill would suggest only dark color. A flock in motion suggests flowing, abundant, living hair that moves as she moves. The NKJV's "going down" keeps that sense of motion.

The two lines are nearly identical. Song of Solomon 4:1 says "from Mount Gilead," and Song of Solomon 6:5 shortens it to "from Gilead." The near-exact repetition looks deliberate. Repeated lines in poetry invite the reader to compare the two settings, and the difference between the settings is where this word's significance lies.

4. Theological Significance

The Song of Solomon celebrates real love between a husband and a wife. Scripture also uses marriage to show how God loves His people, and the New Testament applies that picture to Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:25–32; Revelation 19:7). With that warrant, the Song's portrait of the bridegroom's delight shows us something true about the Lord's heart.

Two truths stand out. The first is that the bridegroom sees his beloved as fully alive. He does not describe her like a statue. He uses the movement of a flock, and in the same passage he compares her teeth to sheep coming up from the washing (Song of Solomon 4:2). Every image he chooses is full of life and abundance. The second truth is that his praise holds steady. What he says on the wedding day, he says again after the rupture between them. His valuation of her does not rise and fall with her most recent response.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 4:1 — Praise on the wedding day. The bridegroom opens with a double "Behold, you are fair, my love! Behold, you are fair!" and then moves from her eyes to her hair, her teeth, her lips, and her neck (4:1–5). Gâlash belongs to this unhurried, admiring look. The flock streaming off Gilead tells us that he is not listing features from a distance. He is captivated by a living person whose beauty moves. This is love at its fullest and least troubled.

Song of Solomon 5:2–8 — The night of hesitation. Only a short time later in the poem, the bridegroom stands at the door, wet with dew, saying, "Open for me, my sister, my love, my dove, my perfect one" (5:2). The bride answers with excuses: "I have taken off my robe; how can I put it on again? I have washed my feet; how can I defile them?" (5:3). Her heart yearns for him, but by the time she opens, "my beloved had turned away and was gone" (5:6). She searches and calls, receives no answer, and is struck by the watchmen, who take her veil (5:7). She ends the scene "lovesick" (5:8). This passage does not contain gâlash, but it is what gives the second occurrence its force. Notice also that he called her "my perfect one" just before she answered him with excuses.

Song of Solomon 6:4–5 — Praise after the reunion. When the bridegroom speaks again, the reader might expect a reproach, or at least some restraint. Instead he says, "O my love, you are as beautiful as Tirzah, lovely as Jerusalem, awesome as an army with banners!" (6:4). Then comes the confession: "Turn your eyes away from me, for they have overcome me." Immediately he returns to the old line, "Your hair is like a flock of goats going down from Gilead" (6:5). He goes on to repeat his earlier praise of her teeth and temples as well (compare 6:6–7 with 4:2–3).

The distinction between the two chapters is the heart of this word study. In chapter 4 the praise comes after nothing has gone wrong. In chapter 6 the same praise comes after something has gone badly wrong. The bridegroom reaches back for the words of the wedding day. He is not merely tolerating her return. He is overwhelmed by her. The one who had withdrawn is the one who now asks her to look away because her gaze is more than he can bear.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Scripture repeatedly portrays God as a husband whose people wander and whom He woos back. Through Hosea, the Lord promises to "allure" unfaithful Israel and "speak comfort to her" (Hosea 2:14). Zephaniah describes the restored people of God with a God who does more than accept them: "He will rejoice over you with gladness, He will quiet you with His love, He will rejoice over you with singing" (Zephaniah 3:17). The bridegroom of Song of Solomon 6 fits this pattern. His joy at the reunion is as full as his joy at the wedding.

The pattern comes to its fullest expression in Jesus. Paul calls marriage a picture of "Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32), and Revelation looks toward "the marriage of the Lamb," for which His wife has made herself ready (Revelation 19:7). The Song nowhere names Christ, so its connection to Him is a canonical resonance rather than a stated fulfillment. Even so, the resonance is strong. After Peter's denials, the risen Jesus did not set him aside. He restored him and entrusted His sheep to him: "Feed My sheep" (John 21:15–17). Then He spoke again the words with which He had first called Peter by the Sea of Galilee: "Follow Me" (Matthew 4:19; John 21:19). As in the Song, a welcome after failure comes with a renewed call, and the Lord's first words to His disciple are given back undiminished.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers carry a quiet fear that God's view of them was highest at the beginning and has been declining ever since. Song of Solomon 5 describes a familiar failure. The bride does not commit a scandalous sin. She is comfortable, already in bed, and slow to rise when her beloved knocks. Her excuses about her robe and her washed feet sound like ours when Christ calls and we prefer to stay where we are. The loss that follows is real. She looks for him and cannot find him. Hesitation toward the Lord has consequences.

Gâlash, though, closes that story with assurance. The bridegroom's second speech borrows its lines about her hair, teeth and temples from the first. His delight in his bride did not shrink while she was hesitating, and he does not ask her to earn back a smaller share of it. For the believer who has drifted and is returning, this answers the question of what kind of welcome to expect. Christ does not forgive reluctantly. He rejoices, and He speaks over His people the same words of love He spoke at first.

The image of the flock itself also teaches something. A single goat on a hillside is unremarkable, while a whole flock flowing down Gilead is a sight worth naming. This is an analogy, not the poem's stated point, but it fits Paul's picture of the church as one body with many members (1 Corinthians 12:12). The beauty the Bridegroom sees in His people is the living movement of many lives following the Shepherd together.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Gâlash occurs only in Song of Solomon 4:1 and 6:5, and both verses are discussed above. Readers who want to see the repetition for themselves should read Song of Solomon 4:1–3 alongside Song of Solomon 6:5–7, where the bridegroom repeats his praise of the bride's hair, teeth, and temples after their reunion.
