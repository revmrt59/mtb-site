GOMER - (Gômer, Strong's H1586)

Summary

Gomer is the name of two distinct figures in the Old Testament, and they must be kept apart. The first is Gomer the son of Japheth and grandson of Noah. He heads the list of Japheth's sons in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:2–3; 1 Chronicles 1:5–6), and his name later stands for the people descended from him. Ezekiel names "Gomer and all its troops" among the nations that join Gog's final assault on restored Israel (Ezekiel 38:6). The second is Gomer the daughter of Diblaim, the woman the prophet Hosea married at God's command (Hosea 1:2–3). The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is often linked to the Hebrew verb gamar, "to complete" or "to come to an end," but scholars disagree on whether that root explains the name, and Scripture itself never comments on its meaning.

Hosea's Gomer carries the greater pastoral weight. Through her unfaithfulness and Hosea's costly love, God showed Israel what its worship of Baal looked like from heaven's side: a wife running after lovers and thanking them for gifts her husband had provided. The book does not end with her betrayal. Hosea is told to "go again, love a woman" (Hosea 3:1), and he buys her back. That act became a living picture of the LORD's love for a faithless people, and Paul and Peter later took up the names of her children, "Not My People" and "No Mercy," to describe what God has done in Christ for people who were once far from Him (Romans 9:25–26; 1 Peter 2:10). Gomer the nation, meanwhile, shows that even the most distant peoples on the edge of the biblical map stand under the rule of the God of Israel.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Gomer is the name of Japheth's son and Hosea's wife. They are distinct, and nothing in Scripture connects them.

Gomer, son of Japheth, and his descendants. Gomer is the first son of Japheth listed in Genesis 10:2, followed by Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, Meshech, and Tiras. His own sons are Ashkenaz, Riphath, and Togarmah (Genesis 10:3). The Chronicler repeats this record, though in 1 Chronicles 1:6 the Hebrew text spells the second son's name Diphath, probably reflecting the close resemblance of two Hebrew letters. In Ezekiel 38:6 the name refers to a people rather than to the individual ancestor. Many scholars identify this people with the Gimirrai of Assyrian records, known to Greek writers as the Cimmerians. These were horse-riding nomads who moved from north of the Caucasus into Asia Minor during the eighth and seventh centuries BC. The identification is widely held but comes from outside the Bible, which simply places Gomer among the northern peoples.

Gomer, daughter of Diblaim. This Gomer lived in the northern kingdom of Israel in the eighth century BC, during the reign of Jeroboam II (Hosea 1:1). She was Hosea's wife and the mother of Jezreel, Lo-Ruhamah, and Lo-Ammi.

The meaning of the name. The name is commonly associated with the Hebrew verb gamar, which means "to complete," "to bring to an end," or in some contexts "to fail." Whether that root actually lies behind the name is debated, so the meaning should be treated as uncertain. For the people of Genesis 10 and Ezekiel 38, the Hebrew name may simply represent a foreign people's name rather than a Hebrew word. For Hosea's wife, some interpreters hear a pun, as if her name suggested the "completion" of Israel's unfaithfulness. That is a possible wordplay, not something Hosea states. Her father's name, Diblaim, is probably related to the Hebrew word for a pressed cake of figs. This is also a proposal rather than a settled meaning.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Gomer the Wife of Hosea

Hosea began his ministry as Israel was enjoying outward prosperity and inward collapse. Baal worship had spread through the land, and the people treated the LORD as one god among many. Into that setting God gave His prophet a command that still startles readers: "Go, take yourself a wife of harlotry and children of harlotry, for the land has committed great harlotry by departing from the LORD" (Hosea 1:2). Hosea obeyed: "So he went and took Gomer the daughter of Diblaim, and she conceived and bore him a son" (Hosea 1:3).

That son received the name Jezreel, because God would soon judge the house of Jehu for the bloodshed at Jezreel and bring the kingdom of Israel to an end (Hosea 1:4–5). Gomer then bore a daughter named Lo-Ruhamah, meaning "No Mercy," because God would no longer have mercy on Israel (Hosea 1:6). A third child, a son, was named Lo-Ammi, "Not My People," because Israel had ceased to live as God's people (Hosea 1:8–9). Many readers notice that only the first birth is described as "to him." With the later two, the text says only that she conceived and bore. That may hint that Hosea was not their father, though the text does not say so directly.

Even in this first chapter, God promised that judgment would not have the last word. In the very place where Israel was told "You are not My people," they would be called sons of the living God (Hosea 1:10).

Hosea 2 widens the view. The prophet's household and the nation now blend together as God brings charges against "your mother," the unfaithful wife who is Israel. She says she will go after her lovers, who she believes give her bread, water, wool, linen, oil, and drink (Hosea 2:5). God's answer is the heart of the chapter: she did not know that He was the one who gave her grain, new wine, and oil, and multiplied her silver and gold, which she then used for Baal (Hosea 2:8). So God will take back His gifts and block her path, until she realizes she was better off with her first husband (Hosea 2:6–7, 9). Then, surprisingly, He will allure her, bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her heart (Hosea 2:14). He will betroth her to Himself forever in righteousness, justice, lovingkindness, mercy, and faithfulness, and she will know the LORD (Hosea 2:19–20). The names of the children are reversed: God will have mercy on "No Mercy" and will say to "Not My People," "You are My people" (Hosea 2:23).

Hosea 3 returns to the prophet's own life. "Go again, love a woman who is loved by a lover and is committing adultery, just like the love of the LORD for the children of Israel, who look to other gods and love the raisin cakes of the pagans" (Hosea 3:1). The woman is not named, but the word "again" and the flow of the book lead most interpreters to conclude that she is Gomer. Hosea writes, "So I bought her for myself for fifteen shekels of silver, and one and one-half homers of barley" (Hosea 3:2). The text does not explain from whom he bought her or what kind of bondage she was in. Then he set the terms of her restoration: "You shall stay with me many days; you shall not play the harlot, nor shall you have a man—so, too, will I be toward you" (Hosea 3:3). This waiting period pictures Israel's coming years without king, sacrifice, or idol, after which the people would return and seek the LORD their God and David their king (Hosea 3:4–5).

Gomer the Son of Japheth and His People

In the Table of Nations, Gomer appears as the first-listed son of Japheth, whose descendants spread into the coastlands and distant regions (Genesis 10:2–5). Through his sons, the line of Gomer reaches peoples who appear elsewhere in Scripture under their own names, which are indexed separately from Gomer. Ashkenaz appears among the kingdoms summoned against Babylon (Jeremiah 51:27), and Togarmah traded horses and mules with Tyre (Ezekiel 27:14). 1 Chronicles 1:5–6 repeats the genealogy as part of the Chronicler's sweep from Adam to Israel.

Centuries later, Gomer reappears as a people in Ezekiel's prophecy against Gog of the land of Magog. God declares that He will turn Gog around, put hooks into his jaws, and lead out a great army (Ezekiel 38:4). Persia, Ethiopia, and Libya are named, and then "Gomer and all its troops; the house of Togarmah from the far north and all its troops—many people are with you" (Ezekiel 38:5–6). This horde comes against a land that has been brought back from the sword and is dwelling safely (Ezekiel 38:8). God Himself destroys it on the mountains of Israel (Ezekiel 38:18–23; 39:1–6).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Hosea's marriage was a prophetic sign that God commanded. Scripture states its meaning directly: the land had departed from the LORD (Hosea 1:2), and Hosea's love for the adulterous woman was to be "just like the love of the LORD for the children of Israel" (Hosea 3:1). Gomer's story makes Israel's idolatry personal. The nation had entered a relationship with the LORD at Sinai that was as binding and intimate as marriage. Worshiping Baal was therefore not a minor religious preference but a betrayal of the One who loved them. Hosea 2:8 shows how that betrayal worked: Israel enjoyed God's harvests and credited them to Baal.

The text also shows that God's love was stronger than Israel's unfaithfulness. Judgment was real; the northern kingdom fell to Assyria within a generation. Yet the names of judgment were already being turned into names of promise (Hosea 1:10; 2:23). Hosea's purchase of his wife displayed what God intended to do for His people.

Some details invite careful inference rather than certainty. Many interpreters note that fifteen shekels plus the barley may have amounted to about thirty shekels, the value Exodus 21:32 assigns to a slave. If so, Hosea paid a slave's price for his own wife. The comparison is suggestive, but Hosea does not make it. Interpreters also debate whether Gomer was already immoral when Hosea married her, or whether "wife of harlotry" describes what she would become. The text allows either reading. In both cases, God knew what He was getting into with Israel and chose to love her anyway.

Gomer the nation serves a different purpose. Genesis 10 places all peoples, even those at the far edges of Israel's world, within one human family under one Creator. Ezekiel 38 shows that same God summoning distant nations and judging them so that the nations may know Him (Ezekiel 38:16, 23). Gomer is not given a personal story. Its role is to show that no army, however far away or however large, lies beyond the reach of the LORD's rule.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest connection comes from the New Testament itself. Paul quotes Hosea to explain how God has called a people "not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles" (Romans 9:24): "I will call them My people, who were not My people, and her beloved, who was not beloved" and "it shall come to pass in the place where it was said to them, 'You are not My people,' there they shall be called sons of the living God" (Romans 9:25–26). Peter writes to believers in the same terms: they "once were not a people but are now the people of God, who had not obtained mercy but now have obtained mercy" (1 Peter 2:10). The names first given to Gomer's children have become, in Christ, a description of every believer's former condition and present standing.

Beyond these direct quotations, the wider canon develops Hosea's picture in a well-supported way. The New Testament speaks of Christ as a Husband who gives Himself for His bride to make her holy (Ephesians 5:25–27; 2 Corinthians 11:2; Revelation 19:7). It describes believers as "bought at a price" (1 Corinthians 6:20) and redeemed not with silver or gold but with the precious blood of Christ (1 Peter 1:18–19). Scripture never calls Gomer a type of the church. Still, the pattern is unmistakable. Hosea paid silver and barley for an unfaithful wife, and Christ paid with His own life for a people who had wandered from God. Hosea 3:5 also looks ahead to the day when Israel will seek "David their king," a promise fulfilled in the Son of David.

The other Gomer belongs to a different line of biblical development. Revelation 20:8 speaks of Satan deceiving the nations at the four corners of the earth, "Gog and Magog, to gather them together to battle, whose number is as the sand of the sea." John does not name Gomer. Even so, by borrowing Ezekiel's names he evokes Ezekiel's pattern of hostile nations, a confederacy that included Gomer, as a picture of the final, worldwide rebellion against God, a rebellion that ends in its swift defeat.

5. Spirit & Life

Hosea 2:8 exposes an idolatry that ordinary believers fall into easily. Israel did not reject the good things of life. It received grain, wine, oil, silver, and gold, and then thanked the wrong giver. A heart can do the same today. It can credit a paycheck, a career, a relationship, or its own cleverness for what God has given, and then spend those gifts on what dishonors Him. The cure begins where God wanted Gomer's story to begin, with knowing who the Giver is.

God's response to that betrayal is also instructive. He removed Israel's false supports and led her into the wilderness, but His purpose was to speak to her heart there (Hosea 2:14). Loss can be the means by which God clears away rivals so that His voice can be heard again. That is severe mercy, and it is still mercy.

Most of all, Gomer's story tells us what God is like. Hosea 3:1 does not ask the prophet to love a woman who had reformed. He was told to love her while she was still loved by another man. God's love for Israel went out before Israel turned back, and God's love in Christ reached us "while we were still sinners" (Romans 5:8). Hosea also set terms for his wife's return: she would stay with him and belong to no one else (Hosea 3:3). Redemption brings a person home, and it also brings that person into a faithful, exclusive belonging to the One who paid for them.

The nation Gomer adds a sober note. Ezekiel's army was vast and came from the edges of the known world, yet God moved it like a man leading an animal with a hook (Ezekiel 38:4). No coalition of enemies, ancient or final, can overturn what God has promised to His people.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:2–3; 1 Chronicles 1:5–6: Gomer as the son of Japheth and father of Ashkenaz, Riphath (Diphath), and Togarmah.

  • Ezekiel 38:4–6, 16: Gomer's people among the nations God leads against restored Israel and then judges so that He may be known.

  • Hosea 1:2–3: God's command to Hosea and his marriage to Gomer, daughter of Diblaim.

  • Hosea 1:4–10: The prophetic names of Gomer's children and the first promise of their reversal.

  • Hosea 2:5–8: Israel credits Baal with the LORD's gifts.

  • Hosea 2:14–23: God leads His unfaithful people into the wilderness, betroths them to Himself, and renames them.

  • Hosea 3:1–5: Hosea buys back the adulterous woman, picturing the LORD's love and Israel's future return to "David their king."

  • Romans 9:24–26; 1 Peter 2:10: The New Testament applies Hosea's reversed names to those God calls in Christ, including Gentiles.

  • Revelation 20:8: Ezekiel's Gog and Magog imagery takes up the final rebellion of the nations.
