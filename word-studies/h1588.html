GARDEN - (gan, Strong's H1588)

Summary

The Hebrew word gan means garden: a piece of ground that someone has planted, watered and protected. Gardens in the ancient world were usually walled or hedged, which set them apart from open fields and wild country. The word can describe something as ordinary as a vegetable plot or a king's private grounds. Its most important use is the first one. God Himself planted the garden in Eden, placed the man there to tend and keep it, and walked in the garden where the people He had made lived (Genesis 3:8).

Because Eden was the place where God and humanity lived together, the garden becomes one of Scripture's great pictures of life with Him. Its loss marks the cost of sin, and the prophets describe judgment as gardens turning back into wilderness. They also describe hope as wilderness becoming "like the garden of the LORD" (Isaiah 51:3). The Song of Solomon calls the bride "a garden enclosed," a picture of devotion kept for the one she loves. Isaiah promises that people who live by God's ways will be "like a watered garden." The story then reaches Jesus, who was arrested in a garden and buried in a garden tomb, and who rose there. It ends with the river and tree of life in God's city.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gan is a common Hebrew noun. Lexicons place it in a word family linked with surrounding or protecting. The related verb appears when God promises, "I will defend this city" (Isaiah 37:35). The word's own uses fit the idea of a protected place: a gan often has boundaries, a water supply and someone responsible for it.

The word appears in four kinds of setting:

  • the Eden narrative in Genesis 2–3;

  • royal and city gardens in the history books;

  • love poetry in the Song of Solomon;

  • the prophets, who use it as a picture of either judgment or restoration.

A closely related feminine form, gannah, is a separate dictionary entry. Isaiah uses that word for the gardens where Israel practiced idolatry.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A cultivated, enclosed plot of ground, such as a vegetable garden or a royal park. Moses describes Egypt's fields as watered "as a vegetable garden" (Deuteronomy 11:10), and Zedekiah escaped Jerusalem "by the king's garden" (2 Kings 25:4).

2. The garden God planted in Eden, also called "the garden of the LORD" or "the garden of God" (Genesis 2:8; 13:10; Ezekiel 28:13).

3. A picture of a person or a people who are fruitful, cared for or set apart. The bride is called "a garden enclosed" (Song of Solomon 4:12), and the restored people "shall be like a watered garden" (Isaiah 58:11).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The words attached to gan show which kind of garden is meant. When the garden is described as "of the LORD," "of God" or "of Eden," it refers to God's own planting. Phrases such as "the king's garden" or "the garden of his own house" point to human ownership. Comparisons using the word "like" carry the prophetic uses: a land becomes "like the Garden of Eden," and a soul becomes "like a well-watered garden." In these comparisons Eden works as a measuring line. Both blessing and loss are judged by how near a place comes to the garden God planted, or how far it falls from it.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture presents God as the first Gardener. He plants Eden, supplies its river and gives its trees their glory. In Ezekiel's parable of the great cedar, God says of the tree, "I made it beautiful" (Ezekiel 31:9). That same chapter warns Pharaoh that the cedar grew proud and was cut down. Ezekiel 28:13 likewise places the king of Tyre "in Eden, the garden of God," covered with precious stones, before his fall. The highest gifts of God's garden became the occasion of the creature's pride. Gifts from God's hand do not secure the heart that receives them.

The garden is also tied to God's presence, so its destruction stands for judgment. Lamentations 2:6 says the Lord "has done violence to His tabernacle, as if it were a garden." That single comparison joins the garden and the sanctuary. Joel describes the invading army by its effect on the land: "The land is like the Garden of Eden before them, And behind them a desolate wilderness" (Joel 2:3). In judgment, what God planted is undone.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Eden as the first sanctuary (Genesis 2–3). The noun occurs 13 times in 12 verses of these two chapters; Genesis 3:8 contains two of them. The garden is set apart from the land around it. God plants it, and a river waters it (2:8, 10). He places the man there "to tend and keep it" (2:15). The two Hebrew verbs in that phrase, meaning to work or serve and to keep or guard, appear together again in Numbers 3:7–8. There they describe the Levites' service at the tabernacle. Adam's work in the garden therefore had a priestly side: he served and guarded a holy place.

Several other details point the same way:

  • God walks in the garden (3:8). He later speaks of walking among Israel in the tabernacle (Leviticus 26:12).

  • After the fall, cherubim guard the garden's eastern entrance (3:24). Cherubim were later woven into the tabernacle veil, and the tabernacle faced east.

Eden was the first place where heaven and earth met. When Adam and Eve hid "among the trees of the garden" (3:8), they were hiding inside the very place made for fellowship with God. Their expulsion (3:23–24) set the pattern for the rest of Scripture: humanity outside the garden, longing to return to God's presence.

The enclosed garden of love (Song of Solomon 4:12–5:1). "A garden enclosed Is my sister, my spouse, A spring shut up, A fountain sealed" (Song of Solomon 4:12). This is a direct metaphor: the bride is the garden. The walls and the sealed spring stand for her purity and her devotion kept for one beloved. In Song of Solomon 4:16 the bride, as the verse is usually read, asks the winds to blow on the garden so that its fragrance spreads. In the same verse she stops calling it her garden and calls it his, inviting him to come and eat its fruit. He answers that he has come to his garden (5:1). Love moves from a guarded enclosure to a garden freely opened to the rightful beloved. Read alongside Scripture's wider picture of God and His people as bridegroom and bride, the passage can illustrate how much God delights in hearts kept for Him.

The king's garden (2 Kings 21; 25; Jeremiah 39; 52). Jerusalem's royal gardens show the failure of human kings. Manasseh, who "seduced them to do more evil than the nations" (2 Kings 21:9), "was buried in the garden of his own house, in the garden of Uzza" (2 Kings 21:18), and his son Amon was buried there too (2 Kings 21:26). Earlier kings were often recorded as buried in the City of David. When Babylon broke through the walls, the soldiers fled "by way of the gate between two walls, which was by the king's garden" (2 Kings 25:4). Zedekiah was captured anyway (Jeremiah 39:4–5). The king's garden became a place of burial and an escape route. No human king could keep a garden that would save his people.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets refused to treat Eden as only a lost past. Isaiah promises, "He will make her wilderness like Eden, And her desert like the garden of the LORD; Joy and gladness will be found in it" (Isaiah 51:3). Ezekiel imagines people passing the restored land and saying, "This land that was desolate has become like the garden of Eden" (Ezekiel 36:35). In both passages God's restoration means bringing people back to the life He first intended, in His presence.

The Gospels write in Greek and use a different word for garden, kēpos. Even so, the setting John records carries this hope forward. Jesus went over the Kidron "where there was a garden" (John 18:1). There He was arrested, having already submitted to the Father's will (Matthew 26:39), while the first Adam had grasped at his own will in Eden. "In the place where He was crucified there was a garden, and in the garden a new tomb" (John 19:41). On resurrection morning Mary Magdalene, "supposing Him to be the gardener" (John 20:15), spoke more truly than she knew. John does not draw out the parallel with Eden, but the setting invites it: in a garden the risen Last Adam stands where the first Adam failed.

The Bible's closing vision brings the garden into the city. Revelation 22 shows "a pure river of water of life" flowing from the throne, and on its banks "the tree of life" (22:1–2). There "there shall be no more curse," and "His servants shall serve Him. They shall see His face" (22:3–4). Read beside Genesis 2:15, Adam's calling to serve is restored, and people who once hid among the trees will see God face to face.

7. Spirit & Life

A garden lives or dies by its water. Isaiah 58:11 promises those who loose the bonds of the oppressed and share bread with the hungry that God "will guide you continually, And satisfy your soul in drought... You shall be like a watered garden, And like a spring of water, whose waters do not fail." The drought does not disappear. The difference is that the soul has a source the drought cannot reach. Jeremiah uses the same picture for the returning exiles: "Their souls shall be like a well-watered garden, And they shall sorrow no more at all" (Jeremiah 31:12). Jesus describes the water He gives as "a fountain of water springing up into everlasting life" (John 4:14), and John explains that the "rivers of living water" Jesus promised were spoken "concerning the Spirit" (John 7:38–39).

Deuteronomy 11:10 sharpens the point. In Egypt, Israel "watered it by foot, as a vegetable garden." The phrase probably refers to opening irrigation channels by foot or working water devices. The Promised Land, by contrast, drinks rain from heaven (11:11). Moses uses the contrast to explain how Israel's life in the land will work. As an illustration, the contrast fits the believer's life well. A heart kept green by endless effort lives like Egypt. A heart that looks up to God for rain lives in His inheritance.

The garden also calls for guarding. Adam was told to keep the garden, and the bride's garden was enclosed. Proverbs 4:23 tells us to keep our hearts with all diligence. In the Song's picture, the walls exist so that the beloved may come in and find fruit there (Song of Solomon 4:16–5:1).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 13:10 – Lot sees the Jordan plain as watered like the garden of the LORD and chooses it. The plain led toward Sodom, a warning that what looks like Eden may not be.

  • 1 Kings 21:2 – Ahab wants Naboth's vineyard for a vegetable garden. The vineyard he wanted for his garden was finally taken through Naboth's murder (21:15–16).

  • Nehemiah 3:15 – The wall rebuilt "by the King's Garden" shows the returned exiles restoring Jerusalem's boundaries piece by piece.

  • Song of Solomon 6:2 – The beloved has gone to his garden to feed his flock in the gardens. The bride knows where he can be found.

  • Song of Solomon 8:13 – The Song closes by addressing the bride as one who dwells in the gardens, with a voice, usually taken as her beloved's, asking to hear her.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1598 (ganan, defend) – This verb is a lexical relative. It describes God shielding Jerusalem (Isaiah 37:35).

  • H1593 (gannah, garden) – This is the closely related feminine form. Isaiah uses it for the idolatrous gardens Israel had chosen, gardens that would bring shame (Isaiah 1:29). Those gardens stand in contrast to the garden God planted.

  • G2779 (kēpos, garden) – This is the New Testament Greek word for garden, a thematic parallel rather than a lexical relative. John uses it for the garden where Jesus was arrested and the garden that held His tomb (John 18:1; 19:41).
