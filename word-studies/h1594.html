GARDEN - (ginnâh, Strong's H1594)

Summary

The Hebrew word ginnâh means "garden," a cultivated and usually enclosed plot of trees, vines, and plants. This form occurs 4 times in 4 verses of the Hebrew Bible (three in Esther 1:5; 7:7–8 and one in Song of Solomon 6:11), and that rarity makes each use stand out. Three uses come from the book of Esther, where the word describes the garden of King Ahasuerus's palace in Shushan. One comes from the Song of Solomon, where a lover goes down to "the garden of nuts" to see whether the vine has budded.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

Within Esther, the palace garden frames the whole story. The book opens with the king feasting there to display his glory. Near its end, the king goes out in fury to the palace garden on the day Haman's plot against the Jews is exposed, and the man who planned their destruction is swiftly condemned. God is never named in Esther, yet the story shows Him at work even in the private grounds of the most powerful king of that day. The Song of Solomon uses the same word for a different kind of garden: a place of watchful, loving attention, where someone looks eagerly for the first signs of new life. Set side by side, the four uses can picture two kinds of garden. The royal garden can picture self-protective glory, and God overrules what happens there. The Song's garden suggests cherished intimacy, where fruit is looked for and welcomed.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Ginnâh is the feminine form of the much more common Hebrew word for garden, gan (H1588). Genesis uses gan for the Garden of Eden, and the Song of Solomon uses it again and again. Ginnâh appears only in Esther and the Song of Solomon. In both books it names a carefully cultivated space.

In the ancient Near East, royal gardens were famous. Persian kings in particular built enclosed parks with water, shade trees, and pavilions. These parks were status symbols that showed wealth, order, and control. Esther's palace garden belongs to that world. The garden in the Song of Solomon is agricultural instead, a grove with vines and pomegranates set in a valley.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ginnâh means a garden: ground set apart, planted, and tended. The word itself describes a physical place and carries no symbolic meaning of its own. Its four uses fall into two settings:

1. A royal palace garden, the enclosed grounds attached to a king's residence (Esther 1:5; 7:7–8).

2. A cultivated orchard or grove, planted with fruit-bearing trees and vines (Song of Solomon 6:11).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Esther, the word always appears in connection with the king's palace: "the court of the garden of the king's palace" (1:5) and "the palace garden" (7:7, 8). Because it belongs to the king, the garden is a space of royal privilege, opened to guests when the king made his feast (1:5).

In Song of Solomon 6:11, the garden is defined by what grows there: "the garden of nuts." The nut tree is probably the walnut, though the exact identification is uncertain. The verbs around the word matter more than the tree. The speaker "went down" in order "to see," and that purpose is stated twice. This is a garden someone visits to inspect it with hope.

4. Theological Significance

Two truths come out of these passages. First, no human enclosure is closed to God. Ahasuerus ruled "one hundred and twenty-seven provinces, from India to Ethiopia" (Esther 1:1), and his palace garden was a place of royal privilege within that empire. Yet the story that unfolds there shows what Proverbs 21:1 states plainly: "The king's heart is in the hand of the LORD, like the rivers of water; He turns it wherever He wishes." God is never named in Esther, and the book still shows Him at work through the king's own feasts, moods, and decisions.

Second, the Song presents its garden as a place of attentive care. Someone goes down to a garden expecting to find life there. Song 6:11 pictures love that watches for the first buds, and Jesus later uses a related image when He calls His Father "the vinedresser" who looks for fruit on every branch (John 15:1–2).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Esther 1:5 — The garden of glory. The king had already spent 180 days showing "the riches of his glorious kingdom and the splendor of his excellent majesty" (1:4). He then threw a seven-day feast "for all the people who were present in Shushan the citadel, from great to small, in the court of the garden of the king's palace." Verses 6–7 describe the setting in detail: linen curtains on silver rods, marble pillars, couches of gold and silver, and wine in golden vessels, "each vessel being different from the other." Everything in the garden announced Persian power. This same feast also set the story in motion. On its seventh day Queen Vashti refused to appear, and she was removed (1:10–19), which opened the way for Esther to become queen. The garden of human display was already serving purposes the king could not see.

Esther 7:7–8 — The garden of reversal. At Esther's second banquet, she exposes Haman's plot to destroy her people. "Then the king arose in his wrath from the banquet of wine and went into the palace garden" (7:7). The garden that once hosted his celebration now receives a furious king who has just learned that his trusted official tricked him into ordering a massacre. While the king is outside, Haman stays behind to plead with Queen Esther for his life (7:7), and he falls across the couch where she is. "When the king returned from the palace garden," he saw Haman there and said, "Will he also assault the queen while I am in the house?" (7:8). Haman's face was covered at once. At the king's command he was then hanged on the gallows he had prepared for Mordecai (7:9–10).

The garden therefore brackets the crisis of the book. The place where the king had celebrated his glory becomes the place where events slip out of the king's control and justice falls on the enemy of God's people. The timing of the king's step into the garden and his return to the room is the kind of detail through which Esther, without naming God, points to His hidden hand.

Song of Solomon 6:11 — The garden of watchful hope. "I went down to the garden of nuts to see the verdure of the valley, to see whether the vine had budded and the pomegranates had bloomed." Interpreters differ on whether the bride or the groom is speaking here. In either reading, the scene is one of loving inspection. Earlier in the same chapter the beloved "has gone to his garden, to the beds of spices" (6:2), followed by the confession, "I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine" (6:3). The garden belongs to the relationship. The speaker goes down, watches for buds and blossoms, and looks with longing for signs of coming fruit.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The four uses of ginnâh fit into a larger story the Bible tells through gardens. Humanity's story begins in a garden, where Adam and his wife "hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God among the trees of the garden" (Genesis 3:8). On the night of His betrayal, Jesus "went out with His disciples over the Brook Kidron, where there was a garden" (John 18:1). In Gethsemane He accepted the cup of suffering, and His obedience there stands against Adam's disobedience in Eden (compare Romans 5:19).

Esther's palace garden is not a picture of Christ, and Ahasuerus is no figure of God. His anger was the volatile anger of a proud man. Still, the scene echoes the pattern. Around a garden, the fate of an enemy is sealed and the rescue of a condemned people begins. As a comparison, what Esther shows on a small scale under a pagan king, the gospel shows fully in Christ. God turns the place of crisis into the place of deliverance.

The Song's garden points in a different direction, toward the love that looks for fruit. Jesus says, "I am the true vine, and My Father is the vinedresser" (John 15:1), and He promises that "he who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit" (John 15:5). The watchful visitor of Song 6:11, looking to see whether the vine has budded, gives a fitting picture of that patient, attentive care.

7. Spirit & Life

As an illustration, the two gardens can be set against each other. Neither passage describes the garden's walls, but the royal garden can picture self-protective glory: a private space kept for the king's display and his chosen guests. The garden of the Song suggests cherished intimacy, a place kept because what grows inside it is precious and belongs to love. The difference raises a fair question for any believer: what are my walls for? People build enclosures of comfort, achievement, and self-protection and assume nothing can reach them there. Esther shows that God walks through such walls whenever He chooses. For those who belong to Him, that is a comfort. No palace, office, or court is sealed against the Lord who keeps His people.

The Song of Solomon adds a gentler picture. Read alongside John 15, the one who goes down to the garden "to see whether the vine had budded" can illustrate how God watches over what He has planted, noticing the first buds before the harvest comes. A believer who feels that very little has grown can take heart from this. The vinedresser of John 15 prunes "that it may bear more fruit" (John 15:2), so His attention is the care of someone who expects growth. The same picture can also encourage believers to watch over one another's growth in the same way, looking hopefully for what God has begun.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Ginnâh occurs only four times, and all four have been discussed above: Esther 1:5, Esther 7:7, Esther 7:8, and Song of Solomon 6:11. The two verses in Esther 7 are worth reading together. The king goes into the palace garden in 7:7 and returns from it in 7:8. That absence is the hinge on which Haman's downfall turns.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1588 (gan), garden — a lexical relative. Gan is the common masculine form of the same word, used for the Garden of Eden (Genesis 3:8) and for the beloved's garden in Song of Solomon 6:2.

  • G2779 (kēpos), garden — a thematic connection. This Greek word comes from the New Testament garden imagery, including the garden across the Kidron (John 18:1). It shares the image of the garden but is not a lexical relative of ginnâh.
