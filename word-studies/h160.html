LOVE - (ʼahăbâh, Strong's H0160)

Summary

The Hebrew noun ʼahăbâh means "love." It names love itself: the bond between two people, the devotion one holds for another, and the force that drives a person toward the one they love. In the Old Testament it covers a wide range. It describes Jacob's love for Rachel, the friendship of David and Jonathan, the passion of the Song of Solomon, and the wise peacemaking praised in Proverbs. Most importantly, it describes the LORD's love for His people. That love is everlasting, it takes the first step, and in Zephaniah's words it is able to "quiet" a restless people.

The word itself does not guarantee that a love is good. The same noun describes Amnon's craving for Tamar, which turned to hatred within minutes. Whether ʼahăbâh is good or harmful depends on who loves and what is loved. When God is the One who loves, the word opens a window into His heart. He loves Israel before she deserves it. He pursues her when she is unfaithful. He carries her through affliction. His love proves "as strong as death." These passages prepare the way for the New Testament announcement that God loved us first and sent His Son.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼahăbâh is a feminine noun formed from the same family as the common Hebrew verb "to love" (ʼāhab, H0157). That verb is used far more often and carries the great commands to love God and neighbor. The noun focuses on love as a reality in its own right: something a person has, gives, keeps, or loses. It appears in narrative, poetry, wisdom, and prophecy. Its largest cluster is in the Song of Solomon, and its weightiest uses are in the prophets, where God speaks of His own love.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is simply "love," an affection and attachment that binds one person to another. Context shows two main directions:

1. Human love between people. This includes romantic and marital love (Genesis 29:20; Song of Solomon 8:6), deep friendship (1 Samuel 18:3), and a settled goodwill that keeps relationships whole (Proverbs 10:12).

2. God's love for His people, the affection behind His choosing, redeeming, and drawing Israel to Himself (Deuteronomy 7:8; Jeremiah 31:3).

Love can also be directed toward a quality rather than a person. In Micah 6:8 the object of love is mercy itself. There the Hebrew uses an infinitive of the related verb ʼāhab (H0157), which some older word lists file under this noun, so the verse serves as a close companion to this word rather than a clear instance of it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject and the object of love decide its character. Jacob's love for Rachel made seven years of labor seem like a few days (Genesis 29:20). The Song of Solomon twice uses a phrase that means "sick with love," which the NKJV renders "lovesick" (Song of Solomon 2:5; 5:8). The phrase describes love so strong that the body feels it.

Proverbs sets this love directly against hatred. "Hatred stirs up strife, But love covers all sins" (Proverbs 10:12). In Proverbs, love is not a mood. It is a way of handling offenses. The same book warns that love which never speaks up is not enough: open rebuke is better than love kept hidden (Proverbs 27:5). Love covers sin so that it does not become a weapon, but love does not pretend sin is absent.

Foreign rulers also speak of God's love for Israel. The Queen of Sheba blesses the LORD "because the LORD has loved Israel forever" (1 Kings 10:9), using the related verb ʼāhab (H0157), which older word lists file under this noun. Hiram says much the same with this noun itself (2 Chronicles 2:11). Even outsiders could see that Israel's king existed because God loved His people.

4. Theological Significance

When God is the subject, ʼahăbâh is the reason behind His actions, not a reward He gives in response to them. Moses tells Israel that the LORD brought them out of Egypt because He loves them and keeps His oath to their fathers (Deuteronomy 7:8). Israel did not win this love by being greater than other nations. God set His love on Israel first, and everything else followed from that.

God's love is also relational and responsive. The prophets describe it in the language of marriage. That means Israel's unfaithfulness wounds God, and persistent rebellion can bring real loss. This love is personal, never mechanical. It rejoices, grieves, pursues, and disciplines. The noun's moral neutrality in human hands sharpens this point. Human love can be twisted by the one who loves. God's love is trustworthy because the One who loves is righteous.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Jeremiah 31:3 and Zephaniah 3:17 — love that draws and quiets. Jeremiah speaks to an exiled people who had every reason to think God was finished with them: "Yes, I have loved you with an everlasting love; Therefore with lovingkindness I have drawn you." The word "therefore" carries the point. God's drawing of His people flows out of a love that already existed long before. Zephaniah adds an unusually tender picture: "He will rejoice over you with gladness, He will quiet you with His love, He will rejoice over you with singing." The Mighty One who saves is also the One who calms His people. He is like a parent hushing a frightened child, and He sings over the people He has restored.

Song of Solomon 8:6–7 — love stronger than death. At the climax of the Song, the bride asks to be set "as a seal upon your heart," because "love is as strong as death, Jealousy as cruel as the grave." Love is pictured as "a most vehement flame" that "many waters cannot quench." Love also cannot be bought: a man who offered all his wealth for it "would be utterly despised." These verses first describe the faithful, exclusive devotion of marriage. They also give Scripture a vocabulary for a love that floods cannot drown and money cannot purchase.

Jonathan and Amnon — love that gives and love that takes. "Jonathan and David made a covenant, because he loved him as his own soul" (1 Samuel 18:3). Jonathan, the heir to Saul's throne, immediately gave David his robe, armor, sword, and bow (1 Samuel 18:4). In doing so he handed over the symbols of his own claim to the throne to the man God had chosen. He later renewed that bond (1 Samuel 20:17). After Jonathan's death, David said Jonathan's love for him surpassed the love of women (2 Samuel 1:26).

Amnon shows the opposite. After he violated his half-sister Tamar, the narrator says his hatred for her became greater than the love he had felt (2 Samuel 13:15). The same noun names both men's "love." Jonathan's love gave up a crown. Amnon's love took what it wanted and then despised what it had taken. Desire that serves only itself, once satisfied, can turn into disgust.

Jeremiah 2:2 and Hosea 3:1; 9:15 — betrothal love and its betrayal. God remembers "The love of your betrothal, When you went after Me in the wilderness, In a land not sown" (Jeremiah 2:2). Israel's early devotion is pictured as a bride following her husband into a barren place. Hosea shows that bride being unfaithful. God commands the prophet to go again and love an adulterous wife, just as the LORD loves Israel while she turns to other gods (Hosea 3:1). Yet in Hosea 9:15, speaking of the wickedness at Gilgal, God declares He will love them no more. Both statements are true. God's love goes after the unfaithful. Hardened rebellion still forfeits the closeness and protection that love had given. Hosea holds both truths, and so must we.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah 63:9 brings the Old Testament portrait close to the gospel: "In all their affliction He was afflicted, And the Angel of His Presence saved them; In His love and in His pity He redeemed them; And He bore them and carried them All the days of old." God's love is shown in sharing His people's suffering and carrying them.

The New Testament shows that love in full. "In this is love, not that we loved God, but that He loved us and sent His Son" (1 John 4:10). The initiating love of Deuteronomy 7:8 and Jeremiah 31:3 becomes visible at the cross: "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). Paul speaks of God's "great love with which He loved us, even when we were dead in trespasses" (Ephesians 2:4–5).

Song of Solomon 8 is never directly quoted as a prophecy of Christ. Still, its imagery finds a fitting echo in Him. At the cross, love met death and proved stronger. Paul's confidence that neither death nor life "shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38–39) is the confidence of a bride whose seal cannot be broken.

7. Spirit & Life

Zephaniah 3:17 speaks to the anxious believer. God's love does not only rescue from the outside. It calms the inside. The One who has every right to rebuke chooses to rejoice and sing. Many Christians accept that God saves them but doubt that He delights in them. Zephaniah says both.

Jonathan teaches what love costs among God's people. He gave his robe and sword to the man who would take his place. That fits Paul's call to "esteem others better than himself" (Philippians 2:3) and Jesus' words, "Greater love has no one than this, than to lay down one's life for his friends" (John 15:13). Amnon stands as a warning beside Jonathan. Strong feeling is no proof of love. Ask what the love is willing to give up.

Proverbs makes love practical in daily friendship. "He who covers a transgression seeks love, But he who repeats a matter separates friends" (Proverbs 17:9). Peter carries the same wisdom into the church: love covers a multitude of sins (1 Peter 4:8). Repeating someone's failure divides friends. Love absorbs the offense and refuses to spread it, while still telling the truth when rebuke is needed.

Micah 6:8, using the related verb, goes deeper still. God requires His people to love mercy, not merely to perform it. Duty can show kindness through clenched teeth. Micah asks for a heart that treasures mercy the way God does. Jesus' warning to Ephesus, "you have left your first love" (Revelation 2:4), echoes Jeremiah's memory of the bride's early devotion. The remedy in both places is to return to the One who loved first.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 29:20 — Jacob's seven years of service for Rachel seemed only a few days because of his love for her. Love changed how long the labor felt.

  • Song of Solomon 2:4 — The banner over the bride is love. Love is pictured as the flag that marks whom she belongs to.

  • Song of Solomon 2:7; 3:5; 8:4 — The repeated charge not to stir up or awaken love until it pleases teaches patience and restraint with love's power.

  • Psalm 109:4–5 — David's enemies repay his love with accusation and hatred, a pattern fulfilled in the rejection of the Son of David.

  • Proverbs 15:17 — A simple meal of vegetables where love is present is better than a rich feast where hatred is present.

  • Ecclesiastes 9:1, 6 — The Preacher notes that no one can tell love or hatred from outward circumstances, and that the dead no longer love or hate under the sun.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H0157 (*ʼāhab*), "to love" — The verb from the same word family as ʼahăbâh. It carries the commands to love God and neighbor. Its infinitive forms in Micah 6:8 and 1 Kings 10:9 are listed under ʼahăbâh in some older word lists.

  • **H2617 (chesed), "lovingkindness, mercy"** — A distinct word paired with ʼahăbâh in Scripture. In Jeremiah 31:3 God's everlasting love leads Him to draw His people "with lovingkindness," and in Micah 6:8 His people are called to love mercy.
