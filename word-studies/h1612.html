VINE - (gephen, Strong's H1612)

Summary

The Hebrew word gephen means vine. It almost always refers to the grapevine: the living plant with its roots, branches, and clusters, as distinct from the vineyard where it grows. In Israel the vine was one of the clearest signs of God's good gifts. It produced the wine that gladdened a household, it marked a family settled on its own inheritance, and its loss to drought or locusts meant that joy itself had dried up.

Scripture also takes this plant and makes it a picture of God's people. God brought "a vine out of Egypt" and planted it in the land (Psalm 80:8). He tended it and looked for good fruit, yet Israel became "the degenerate plant of an alien vine" (Jeremiah 2:21). Ezekiel adds a hard truth: vine wood is useless for building, so a vine that bears no fruit is good only for the fire. Out of this history comes the prophets' hope that every person will one day sit safely under his own vine. That hope leads to Jesus, who said, "I am the true vine" (John 15:1). He is the faithful Israel who bears the fruit the nation never did, and those who abide in Him share His life.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Gephen is an ordinary Hebrew noun for the grapevine. It names the plant itself. The related idea of a vineyard, a field planted with vines, uses a different word (kerem). The distinction matters, because some famous vineyard passages, such as Isaiah 5, use the vineyard word rather than gephen.

The vine appears in nearly every kind of biblical writing. In the Law it is one of the treasures of the Promised Land, a land of wheat, barley, vines, fig trees, and pomegranates (Deuteronomy 8:8). It shows up in dreams, fables, and royal records. In poetry it portrays a wife and the awakening of love. Above all, it belongs to the prophets, where more than half of its uses appear. A vine needed years of patient labor before it produced, so a flourishing vine meant a settled and peaceful people. That is why the prophets could speak of a whole nation through this one plant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Gephen has one basic meaning, the grapevine, which Scripture uses in two ways:

1. A literal grapevine, valued for its grapes and wine. Joseph hears about one in the cupbearer's dream, where a vine buds, blossoms, and ripens clusters of grapes (Genesis 40:9–10).

2. A figure drawn from the vine's growth and fruit to describe a person or people, such as Israel (Psalm 80:8), a royal house (Ezekiel 17:6), or a wife (Psalm 128:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The vine often marks the health of the land. When God struck Egypt, hail destroyed their vines (Psalm 78:47; 105:33). When judgment falls on Israel or the nations, the vine languishes. Isaiah pictures the vine of Moab, once spreading to the sea, now wept over (Isaiah 16:8–9). Joel connects the ruined crop directly to the human heart: "The vine has dried up... Surely joy has withered away from the sons of men" (Joel 1:12). Joel calls the invaded land "My land" and the devastated plant "My vine" (Joel 1:6–7), most naturally the LORD's own claim, a reminder that the land and its fruit belong to Him.

The vine is often paired with the fig tree. Together the two describe ordinary prosperity. Under a phrase about each person sitting under his own vine and fig tree, they also describe settled peace.

When the vine stands for a people, the questions change. The concern becomes who planted it, where its roots reach for water, and what kind of fruit it produces.

4. Theological Significance

The vine passages teach that God's grace comes before any fruit. Israel did not plant itself. God brought it out of Egypt, cleared away the nations, and set it in prepared ground. The nation's existence was His work from start to finish.

That same grace makes God's expectation reasonable. A vinedresser who has done everything for his vine rightly looks for grapes. When the fruit came out bitter, the fault lay with the vine and not the gardener. The prophets never hint that God's care was lacking. Their grief is that so much care produced so little.

The vine also reveals God's judgment and His mercy. Judgment strips the vine bare. Jeremiah describes God's people being gleaned "thoroughly... as a vine," with the grape-gatherer putting his hand back into the branches (Jeremiah 6:9). Yet the last word in the prophets belongs to restoration. God promises that the vine will again yield its strength (Joel 2:22), give its fruit (Zechariah 8:12), and no longer fail in the field (Malachi 3:11). The God who plants also replants.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 80, Jeremiah 2, and Hosea 10: the planted vine that turned. Psalm 80 tells Israel's history as the story of a vine: "You have brought a vine out of Egypt; You have cast out the nations, and planted it" (Psalm 80:8). The psalm goes on to describe the vine filling the land and reaching to the sea, then mourns that its walls are broken down. The psalmist pleads with God to look down from heaven and visit this vine (Psalm 80:14).

Jeremiah gives God's side of the story: "Yet I had planted you a noble vine, a seed of highest quality. How then have you turned before Me Into the degenerate plant of an alien vine?" (Jeremiah 2:21). God planted the best stock, and the plant became something foreign to itself. Hosea names how this happened: "Israel empties his vine; He brings forth fruit for himself. According to the multitude of his fruit He has increased the altars" (Hosea 10:1). The more God blessed the vine, the more altars Israel built to other gods. Moses' song had already warned of a vine like the vine of Sodom, bearing grapes of gall and bitter clusters (Deuteronomy 32:32). A people planted to show God's goodness could end up resembling the most notorious city of corruption.

Ezekiel 15 and 17: a vine is only good for fruit. God asks Ezekiel how the wood of the vine compares with the trees of the forest. Nobody can make anything from it, not even a peg to hang a pot on. Once it has been through the fire it is worth even less. God then applies the picture: as He gives vine wood to the fire for fuel, He will give up the inhabitants of Jerusalem (Ezekiel 15:2–6). A vine has no value as lumber. Its whole worth lies in its fruit. Jerusalem had no strength of its own to fall back on once it stopped bearing what God planted it to bear.

Two chapters later, Ezekiel describes the royal house of Judah as a spreading vine planted by water. Instead of staying where it was planted, the vine bent its roots toward another eagle, meaning Egypt (Ezekiel 17:6–8). The direction of a vine's roots shows where it really looks for life. Judah's king turned toward a foreign power instead of trusting the word God had spoken.

The vine and the fig tree: peace that only God gives. In Solomon's days, Judah and Israel lived safely, each man under his vine and fig tree (1 Kings 4:25). The phrase pictures a family eating from its own inheritance with no fear of raiders. Centuries later, the Assyrian field commander stood outside besieged Jerusalem and offered that same picture as a bribe: "Make peace with me by a present and come out to me; and every one of you eat from his own vine and every one from his own fig tree" (2 Kings 18:31). He promised God's blessing on the condition that the people surrender to a pagan king. In his very next words he admitted that he would then deport them to another land. Micah and Zechariah set God's own promise against offers like his. In the coming day of the Lord's reign, everyone will sit under his vine and fig tree and no one will make them afraid (Micah 4:4; Zechariah 3:10).

Genesis 49:11: the king of overflowing abundance. Jacob's blessing on Judah speaks of a ruler to whom the obedience of the peoples belongs. Of him Jacob says: "Binding his donkey to the vine, And his donkey's colt to the choice vine, He washed his garments in wine, And his clothes in the blood of grapes" (Genesis 49:11). A farmer would not normally tie a donkey to a choice vine, because the animal would strip it. Under this king, vines are so plentiful that the best of them can serve as a hitching post, and wine is common enough to wash clothes in.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The vine's story in the Old Testament ends with a question. God planted a noble vine, and it turned degenerate. Psalm 80, after mourning the ruined vine, prays for "the man of Your right hand," the son of man God made strong for Himself (Psalm 80:17). The psalm places the vine's restoration in the hands of one person.

Jesus answers that hope directly: "I am the true vine, and My Father is the vinedresser" (John 15:1). The Greek word there is different, but the image is the one the prophets used. By calling Himself the true vine, Jesus presents Himself as the faithful Israel. He is the planting that does not turn, the one who lives wholly from the Father and bears the fruit God always sought. Jacob pictures Judah's ruler with his donkey and its colt tied to the vine. Zechariah 9:9 later shows the King coming "Lowly and riding on a donkey, A colt, the foal of a donkey," and Jesus fulfilled that picture when He rode into Jerusalem (Matthew 21:4–5). Jacob's ruler is pictured with clothes washed in "the blood of grapes." Readers have long heard in that phrase an echo of the cup Jesus gave His disciples, the fruit of the vine that He called His blood, which He promised to drink new in His Father's kingdom (Matthew 26:27–29). The Genesis verse does not state that meaning, but the connection fits the way Scripture moves.

The vine image also carries a warning at the close of the Bible. Revelation shows an angel gathering "the clusters of the vine of the earth," which is thrown "into the great winepress of the wrath of God" (Revelation 14:18–19). Jesus warned that a branch that does not abide in Him is gathered and thrown into the fire (John 15:6), the same end Ezekiel described for fruitless vine wood. The peace under one's own vine that Micah foresaw belongs to the kingdom of the true King.

7. Spirit & Life

The vine passages expose a temptation every believer feels: wanting to be the sturdy wood. We would rather be valued for strength, usefulness, and impressive height. Ezekiel 15 removes that option. A vine cannot be a beam, and a branch cannot be a pillar. Jesus makes a related point in His own words: "As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in Me" (John 15:4). The Christian life does not grow by self-reliance. It flourishes when we depend on Christ as our source, and for those who confess their weakness that is good news.

Hosea's phrase "He brings forth fruit for himself" is a searching warning. Israel was not barren. It was productive, and that very productivity fed its idolatry. Fruitfulness can be turned to serve our own reputation and our own altars. The question is never only whether something is growing, but for whom the fruit is borne.

The commander's offer in 2 Kings 18 still sounds today. The world offers the very things God promises, such as security, provision, and a place of one's own, if we will only bow to another master. Accepting God's gifts on the world's terms would have meant exile, not rest, as the commander himself admitted (2 Kings 18:32).

The vine also teaches about joy. The Nazirite, during his vow, ate nothing produced by the grapevine (Numbers 6:4). The vine was not evil. Its fruit was a good, God-given gladness, and the Nazirite set it aside as part of separating himself to the LORD (Numbers 6:2), a sign that his deepest satisfaction rested in Him. Habakkuk reached the same place by a harder road. Even if there should be no fruit on the vines and the fields should yield no food, he would rejoice in the LORD (Habakkuk 3:17–18). Where Joel saw joy wither with the vine, Habakkuk found a joy the locusts could not touch.

Finally, the vine brings God's goodness into ordinary home life. "Your wife shall be like a fruitful vine In the very heart of your house" (Psalm 128:3). The God who judges degenerate vines also delights to make homes fruitful and glad.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 40:9–10 — The cupbearer's dream of a vine with three branches that ripened into grapes. Joseph explains that God gives the interpretation, and the vine's quick ripening points to the cupbearer's restoration.

  • Judges 9:12–13 — In Jotham's fable the vine refuses to leave its new wine, which cheers God and men, just to rule over the trees. The vine prefers its God-given purpose to political power.

  • Song of Solomon 2:13 — "The vines with the tender grapes Give a good smell. Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away!" The blossoming vine announces spring and the awakening of love.

  • Song of Solomon 7:8 — "Let now your breasts be like clusters of the vine." Married love is celebrated without embarrassment through the image of ripe fruit.

  • Jeremiah 8:13 — God finds no grapes on the vine and no figs on the fig tree, a picture of a people with nothing to offer Him.

  • Haggai 2:19 — While the vine had not yet yielded, God promised the returned exiles, "From this day I will bless you."

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3754 (kerem, vineyard) — A distinct Hebrew word for the planted field rather than the plant. It is closely related in imagery, especially in Isaiah's Song of the Vineyard (Isaiah 5:1–7).

  • H3196 (yayin, wine) — The vine's product, joined with the vine in Genesis 49:11. This is an image-family connection, not a word-family relative.

  • G288 (ampelos, vine) — The Greek word Jesus uses in "I am the true vine" (John 15:1) and the word for "the vine of the earth" in Revelation 14:18–19. It is a cross-language counterpart that carries the Old Testament image forward.
