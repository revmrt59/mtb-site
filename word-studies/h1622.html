GIRGASHITE, GIRGASHITES - (Girgâshîy, Strong's H1622)

Summary

Girgâshîy is the Hebrew name for the Girgashites, one of the peoples descended from Canaan who lived in the land God promised to Abraham. The name appears only seven times, and never in a story of its own. No Girgashite king, city, or individual is ever named. The Girgashites appear only in lists: in the family tree of the nations, in God's promise to Abram, in the conquest under Moses and Joshua, and in a prayer of thanksgiving prayed by the exiles who returned from Babylon.

The lists matter because they trace God keeping His word across many centuries. The Girgashites are named when God promises the land in Genesis 15:21. They are named when Israel faces nations "greater and mightier than you" (Deuteronomy 7:1). They are named when Joshua tells the people that God's victory will show "the living God is among you" (Joshua 3:10). Centuries later Nehemiah 9:8 names them once more and concludes, "You have performed Your words, For You are righteous." A forgotten people becomes lasting evidence that God does exactly what He says.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Girgâshîy is a Hebrew ethnic name. It identifies a people group in the same way "Hittite" or "Jebusite" does. Scripture places their origin in the Table of Nations, where they appear among the descendants of Canaan, the son of Ham: "the Jebusite, the Amorite, and the Girgashite" (Genesis 10:16). The Chronicler repeats the same line word for word (1 Chronicles 1:14).

Beyond this genealogy, the Bible places the Girgashites among the inhabitants of Canaan, the land promised to Abraham, but it does not tell us their precise territory, their towns, or what they were like. Their customs and history are not described, and it is wiser to admit that than to fill the gap with guesses. Every use of the word falls within the long story of Israel and the land of Canaan, from God's promise to Abraham through the conquest and finally to Israel's memory of these events after the exile.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one meaning: a member of the Girgashite people, or the people as a whole. The NKJV renders it "Girgashite" twice and "Girgashites" five times. The name has no hidden symbolic sense, and building a lesson on its sound or supposed root would go beyond the evidence. Its significance comes from the passages where God names this people.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The seven occurrences fall into four settings:

  • Genealogy (Genesis 10:16; 1 Chronicles 1:14). The Girgashites are a real family line among the nations of the earth.

  • Promise (Genesis 15:21). They are among the present inhabitants of the land God pledges to Abram's descendants.

  • Conquest (Deuteronomy 7:1; Joshua 3:10; 24:11). They are listed among the peoples God will drive out, and then among those He did drive out.

  • Remembrance (Nehemiah 9:8). Israel names them in a prayer that praises God's faithfulness.

The lists themselves vary. Genesis 15 names ten peoples. Deuteronomy 7:1 and Joshua 3:10 give the familiar "seven nations." Nehemiah 9:8 names six. Several lists elsewhere leave the Girgashites out altogether; Exodus 3:8, for example, has six names without them. These lists summarize the land's inhabitants. They are not official censuses, and the variation should not trouble anyone. The Girgashites are the least conspicuous member of the group, which makes their presence at each major turning point all the more telling.

4. Theological Significance

The genealogy in Genesis 10 shows that the nations Israel faced were real families descended from Noah's grandson Canaan. They were not mythical monsters or symbols of evil drawn on a map. God's promises and judgments took place in actual history, among peoples with names and ancestors. The Bible's account of redemption is grounded in real geography and real generations. The Chronicler repeated this genealogy long after the conquest so that Israel would still know who had once held the land and how it came to be theirs.

The Girgashites also show God's justice and patience. In the same chapter where the land is promised, God tells Abram that his descendants must wait four generations, because "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Genesis 15:16, paraphrased). The dispossession of Canaan was not an arbitrary land grab. God waited for centuries before He judged the nations of Canaan. Deuteronomy 7 then commands Israel to make no marriages and no treaties with these nations, because their gods would turn Israel's sons "away from following" the Lord (Deuteronomy 7:4, paraphrased). God's patience with entrenched wickedness is long, but it has a limit, and His people cannot hold on to Him while also holding on to the idols He judges.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 15:21 and Nehemiah 9:8: promise and performance. In Genesis 15, God makes a covenant with Abram while Abram is in a deep sleep and God alone passes between the divided animals (15:12, 17). God then defines the gift: the land "from the river of Egypt to the great river, the River Euphrates" (15:18, paraphrased), now occupied by ten named peoples ending with "the Amorites, the Canaanites, the Girgashites, and the Jebusites" (15:21). Abram receives a settled land held by established nations, and God names the inhabitants who must one day give way. That specificity makes the promise testable.

Many centuries later, the returned exiles gather in Jerusalem to confess their sins and recount their history (Nehemiah 9). Their prayer looks back to Abraham: God "found his heart faithful," made a covenant "To give the land of the Canaanites, The Hittites, the Amorites, The Perizzites, the Jebusites, And the Girgashites," and then the prayer gives its verdict: "You have performed Your words, For You are righteous" (Nehemiah 9:8). The people praying had come through judgment and exile. They stood in a ruined city under foreign rule, yet they could point to the land under their feet as proof that God had kept His word. In this prayer God's righteousness means His reliability. He said He would give the land of the Girgashites, and He did.

Deuteronomy 7:1, Joshua 3:10, and Joshua 24:11: victory that belongs to God. Moses did not flatter Israel. He called the seven nations, the Girgashites among them, "greater and mightier than you" (Deuteronomy 7:1). He then told the people that God's choice of them had nothing to do with their size; they were "the least of all peoples" (7:7, paraphrased), and God acted because He loved them and kept His oath to their fathers (7:8).

At the edge of the Jordan, Joshua turned the coming conquest into a sign: "By this you shall know that the living God is among you, and that He will without fail drive out" the seven nations (Joshua 3:10). The miracle at the river would come first, and the victories would follow. Israel's success in Canaan was meant to show that God Himself was present among them.

At the end of his life, Joshua let God speak in the first person. The men of Jericho "fought against you," along with the seven nations, "But I delivered them into your hand" (Joshua 24:11). A few verses later God adds that it was "not with your sword or with your bow" (24:12, paraphrased). God sent Israel against peoples it could not defeat, so that no one could mistake whose victory it was.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Girgashites belong to the story of the promise God made to Abraham, and that promise reaches its goal in Christ. The New Testament says Abraham was promised that he would be "heir of the world" (Romans 4:13, paraphrased), and Hebrews says the patriarchs were looking for a better, heavenly country (Hebrews 11:13–16, paraphrased). Canaan was a real gift, faithfully given, and it also pointed beyond itself to the inheritance God's people receive in Christ.

The conquest also points forward. Joshua, whose name is the Hebrew form of "Jesus," led Israel into rest, but Hebrews notes that his rest was not the final one (Hebrews 4:8–9, paraphrased). Christ leads His people into the fuller rest. Paul's statement that all God's promises are "Yes" in Christ (2 Corinthians 1:20, paraphrased) is the same confession Nehemiah made, now spoken on a larger scale. Nehemiah could say, "You have performed Your words," because of a land. The church says it because of an empty tomb.

7. Spirit & Life

Most readers pass quickly over lists of nations, but these lists teach something about God. When He made a promise, He named the peoples involved. When He gave the victory, He named the peoples He had overcome. God does not deal in vague generalities. He knows the actual obstacles His people face, by name, and His promises address the real circumstances of their lives.

The phrase "greater and mightier than you" deserves careful attention. God did not call Israel to a fight it could win in its own strength. Believers often feel overmatched by temptation, by grief, or by habits that have held ground for years. Deuteronomy does not tell God's people to pretend those enemies are small. It tells them that the living God is among them, and that the victory, when it comes, will plainly be His.

The Girgashites also warn against compromise. Israel was not to make treaties with the gods of Canaan. Believers today are not called to make war on nations, but they are called to refuse any accommodation with whatever would draw their hearts away from the Lord.

Finally, Nehemiah 9 gives believers a pattern for prayer. The exiles told God's history back to Him, naming even the Girgashites, and found their confidence renewed. Remembering in detail what God has already done is one of the surest ways to trust Him with what He has not yet done.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All seven occurrences have been discussed above. Genesis 10:16 and its parallel in 1 Chronicles 1:14 ("the Jebusite, the Amorite, and the Girgashite") establish the people's descent from Canaan. Genesis 15:21, Deuteronomy 7:1, Joshua 3:10, Joshua 24:11, and Nehemiah 9:8 trace the Girgashites through promise, conquest, and remembrance.
