RAIN, SHOWERS - (geshem, Strong's H1653)

Summary

The Hebrew word geshem means rain, often the substantial, soaking rain of the land's winter season. Israel's harvests depended on it, and Scripture insists that this rain comes from the LORD alone. Elijah's drought and the downpour after Mount Carmel show that the LORD, not Baal, governs the sky. Isaiah gives the word its most painful irony: the rain God sends nourishes a tree, and a man carves an idol from its wood. Under the law of Moses, rain "in its season" heads the blessings promised for faithfulness. The prophets later warn that God can withhold it, and Zechariah foresees nations that refuse to worship the King receiving none.

The same rain can also become a flood of judgment, as in Noah's day and against the whitewashed walls of Ezekiel's false prophets. The prophets then lift the word higher still. Isaiah compares God's word to rain that does not go back to heaven until it has made the earth fruitful. Hosea pictures God Himself coming to His returning people like the rain. Geshem teaches that every good harvest, physical or spiritual, is a gift from above, and that the Giver longs to be known for Himself.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Geshem is a Hebrew noun for rain. In the land of Israel, the rains came in the cooler months, and a failed rainy season meant hunger. Canaanite religion credited Baal, a storm god, with sending rain. That background helps explain why rain in Scripture so often becomes a question of worship. The NKJV usually translates geshem as "rain," twice as "showers" (Ezekiel 34:26), and once as "rainy" (Ezekiel 1:28). The word appears in the law, in history, in wisdom literature, and especially in the prophets.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Geshem means rain. It often describes substantial rain. Ezra 10:9 and 1 Kings 18:45 call it "heavy rain," and Job 37:6 speaks of "the heavy rain of His strength." Hebrew has a more general word for rain, matar, and separate terms for the early and latter seasonal rains. Scripture sometimes places these words side by side, so geshem should not be pressed into a rigid technical category. Its senses are close together:

1. Literal rainfall from the sky, especially the substantial rain of the wet season (1 Kings 18:45; Ezra 10:9).

2. Rain as a figure for something else in a comparison: God's word (Isaiah 55:10), God's coming to His people (Hosea 6:3), or a flood of judgment (Ezekiel 13:11).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most often geshem is the rain God gives or withholds. Its presence brings life, and its absence brings drought. Jeremiah 14:4 describes ground cracked because there was no rain. Jeremiah 5:24 grieves that the people never said in their hearts that they should fear the LORD who gives rain in its season. The same downpour can also be destructive. In Genesis 7:12 the rain falls forty days and forty nights, and Genesis 8:2 records that it was finally restrained.

Wisdom writers use rain to describe human life. Proverbs 25:14 compares a man who boasts of a gift he never gives to clouds and wind that bring no rain. Ecclesiastes 11:3 states a plain fact of nature: "If the clouds are full of rain, They empty themselves upon the earth." Not every rainfall in Scripture carries a symbol. The wet season could also stop ordinary life. In Ezra 10:13 the assembly admits it is the season of heavy rain and the people cannot stand outside.

4. Theological Significance

Rain in Scripture belongs to God. Job 37:6 pictures Him speaking to the weather: "He says to the snow, 'Fall on the earth'; Likewise to the gentle rain and the heavy rain of His strength." Rain obeys His word, so its coming and going become a form of communication. In Leviticus 26:4, the first blessing promised for obedience is rain in its season. The land's fruitfulness is tied to Israel's faithfulness to God.

That is why, in these passages, the withholding of rain is discipline rather than mere bad weather. In Amos 4:7, God speaks of rain falling on one city while another withers. These are aimed acts meant to bring His people back, yet the refrain of Amos 4 is that they did not return. Zechariah 14:17 carries this pattern into the future kingdom: "whichever of the families of the earth do not come up to Jerusalem to worship the King, the LORD of hosts, on them there will be no rain." In Zechariah's vision, rain is a gift that belongs with worship, and those who refuse to worship the King do not receive it.

The same rain that nourishes can also judge. Psalm 105:32 recalls Egypt: "He gave them hail for rain." Ezekiel 38:22 includes flooding rain among God's weapons against Gog. In these judgment passages, one God and one sky bring two outcomes, depending on how people stand before Him. This does not make every rainfall or drought a verdict on the people it touches; Jesus says the Father "sends rain on the just and on the unjust" (Matthew 5:45).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Elijah and the rain (1 Kings 17–18). The drought Elijah announces strikes at the heart of Baal worship. If Baal sends rain, let him send it. Instead "the brook dried up, because there had been no rain in the land" (1 Kings 17:7). Meanwhile God feeds a widow with flour and oil that will last "until the day the LORD sends rain on the earth" (1 Kings 17:14). On Carmel, Baal's prophets cry out from morning till noon, "but there was no voice; no one answered" (1 Kings 18:26). Fire falls at Elijah's prayer. Then, with no cloud yet visible, Elijah tells Ahab, "there is the sound of abundance of rain" (1 Kings 18:41). After the seventh look, a cloud "as small as a man's hand" rises from the sea (1 Kings 18:44), and soon "there was a heavy rain" (1 Kings 18:45). The drought and the downpour together answer Elijah's challenge: "If the LORD is God, follow Him" (1 Kings 18:21).

Rain that grows an idol (Isaiah 44:14). Isaiah describes a man who plants a tree, and the rain makes it grow. He burns part of the wood to bake bread and warm himself. He then carves the rest into a god and bows down to it (Isaiah 44:15–17). God's rain grew the wood, and the man used that gift to worship something else. Idolatry often works this way. It takes what God has given and treats the gift as though it were God.

Rain that does not return empty (Isaiah 55:10). "For as the rain comes down, and the snow from heaven, And do not return there, But water the earth, And make it bring forth and bud, That it may give seed to the sower And bread to the eater." Verse 11 completes the comparison: God's word will not return to Him empty but will accomplish what He sends it to do. Isaiah's point is that the rain does not go back up without doing its work. It waters the earth and makes it fruitful, and the result is bread for today and seed for next year. God's word falls on dry hearts the same way, and its fruit comes from its own power.

Flooding rain and whitewashed walls (Ezekiel 13:11, 13). False prophets were telling Israel "peace" when there was no peace. Ezekiel compares their message to a flimsy wall covered with whitewash. God promises flooding rain, hail, and stormy wind that will bring the wall down and leave its foundation bare. In this passage the downpour does more than punish. It shows what the wall really was. Smooth assurances that ignore sin cannot survive the day God's truth arrives.

God coming like the rain (Hosea 6:3). Hosea 6:1–3 voices a call to return to the LORD, confident that God, who has torn, will heal (Hosea 6:1–2). It urges, "Let us pursue the knowledge of the LORD," and says, "He will come to us like the rain, Like the latter and former rain to the earth" (Hosea 6:3). God's reply in Hosea 6:4 questions how lasting Israel's faithfulness is, but the picture of His coming remains. Here the image changes in an important way. The words do not say only that God will send rain; they say God Himself will come like the rain. The greatest gift He offers His people is His own presence. Psalm 68:9 sounds the same note: God sent plentiful rain to restore His inheritance when it was weary.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Rain's story moves from provision to presence. God sends rain, commands it, likens His word to it, and finally promises to come like it Himself. The New Testament takes up these threads. James 5:17–18 points to Elijah, who prayed and the heavens gave rain, as an example of a righteous person's prayer. Jesus speaks of rain falling on two houses (Matthew 7:24–27). Only the house built on His words stands. That parable reads like Ezekiel's warning about whitewashed walls, now applied to every hearer.

Isaiah 55 finds its fullest expression in Christ, though Isaiah does not name Him there. John calls Jesus the Word who came down and dwelt among us (John 1:14). He did not go back to the Father empty-handed. He gave Himself fully, and like a seed falling into the ground (John 12:24), His death bears much fruit. Hosea's promise that God would come like the rain, set beside the words about being raised up "on the third day" (Hosea 6:2), anticipates the day God came to His people in the Son. Isaiah 4:6 uses a different Hebrew word for rain, but it pictures God sheltering His people "from storm and rain." Isaiah pictures those who belong to Him kept safe through the storm of judgment.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 44 holds up a mirror. Every good thing in our lives has been watered by God's rain: health, skill, income, relationships. The question is whether we bow to the Giver or to what His gifts have grown. Jeremiah 5:24 describes the sin as a failure to fear the LORD. The people enjoyed the rain and never stopped to fear the One who sent it. Gratitude that traces each gift back to God is a guard against idolatry.

Isaiah 55:10 frees believers from trying to produce spiritual life by effort alone. Ground does not make itself fruitful; it receives the rain. We open our lives to God's word by reading, hearing, and trusting it, and the word brings its own power to bear fruit. Some seasons feel like Ezra's assembly, sitting in the cold rain "trembling because of this matter and because of heavy rain" (Ezra 10:9). Conviction can be heavy, but it was the beginning of restoration for Ezra's people. In the Song, the beloved calls his bride to come away with the words, "For lo, the winter is past, The rain is over and gone" (Song of Solomon 2:10–11). God sets limits on hard seasons.

Elijah teaches patient, expectant prayer. He heard "the sound of abundance of rain" before any cloud appeared, and he kept praying through six reports of nothing. Hosea shows what that prayer seeks most: God Himself. The heart that presses on to know the LORD will find that He comes like rain on dry ground.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Kings 3:17 – Elisha tells three thirsty armies that they will see neither wind nor rain, yet the valley will fill with water. God is not limited to the means He usually uses.

  • Ezekiel 1:28 – Ezekiel describes the brightness of God's glory as like a rainbow in a cloud on a rainy day. The vision of glory recalls the bow God set in the clouds after the flood.

  • Ezekiel 34:26 – God promises that His restored flock under His shepherd will receive showers in their season, showers that bring blessing.

  • Joel 2:23 – The children of Zion are called to rejoice because the LORD gives them rain, the former and the latter. Restoration after judgment includes the return of the rain.

  • Zechariah 10:1 – The people are told to ask the LORD for rain in its season. He makes the storm clouds and gives showers. Rain is something to pray for, not something to assume.

  • Ecclesiastes 12:2 – In the closing picture of old age, the clouds return after the rain. The image describes the sorrows of later years that come one after another.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H4306 (matar) – A distinct, more general Hebrew word for rain. It is paired with geshem in Job 37:6 and Zechariah 10:1.

  • H3138 (yoreh) and H4456 (malqosh) – The former (early) and latter rains of the agricultural year. Jeremiah 5:24 names them beside geshem: the LORD "gives rain, both the former and the latter, in its season." Hosea 6:3 uses the same picture of "the latter and former rain" beside geshem, though there the word for the former rain is a related verb form (H3384) rather than this noun.
