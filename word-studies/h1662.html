GATH-HEPHER - (Gath-ha-Chêpher, Strong's H1662)

Summary

Gath-hepher was a small town in the tribal territory of Zebulun in lower Galilee. Its name most likely means "winepress of digging" or "winepress of the well," combining the common Hebrew word gath, "winepress," with a term related to digging. Scripture names the town only twice. Joshua lists it on Zebulun's boundary, and Second Kings identifies it as the hometown of "Jonah the son of Amittai, the prophet." That second detail gives this farming village a lasting place in the Bible's story. From Gath-hepher came the prophet who announced God's mercy to a struggling Israel under Jeroboam II and who was also sent, unwillingly, to call the Gentile city of Nineveh to repentance.

Gath-hepher also carries weight in the Gospels, even though they never name it. When the Pharisees dismissed Jesus by saying "no prophet has arisen out of Galilee" (John 7:52), they overlooked a prophet whose hometown their own Scriptures recorded in Galilee. Isaiah had promised that the land of Zebulun would see "a great light," and Matthew applies that promise to Jesus' Galilean ministry. Jesus Himself pointed to "the sign of the prophet Jonah" as the sign of His death and resurrection. Gath-hepher shows how God raises His messengers from places people are quick to dismiss.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Gath-hepher is a single place, a town on the eastern side of Zebulun's tribal inheritance in what later became lower Galilee. It should not be confused with Gath of the Philistines (H1661) or Gath-rimmon (H1667). Each is a separately indexed name, and all three share the ordinary word for "winepress." In Joshua 19:13 the name appears with a Hebrew directional ending, which is why some translations render it "Gittah-hepher."

The first part of the name, gath, plainly means "winepress." The second part, hepher, is generally connected with the verb meaning "to dig" or "to search out." That gives the probable meaning "winepress of digging" or "winepress of the well (or pit)." The name points to a rural, agricultural setting where grapes were pressed. Scripture draws no wordplay or spiritual meaning from the name itself.

The town is traditionally identified with the area of the village el-Meshed, a few miles northeast of Nazareth. The identification is widely accepted, though not beyond question.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Settlement under Joshua. Gath-hepher first appears as a landmark tracing Zebulun's border: "And from there it passed along on the east of Gath Hepher, toward Eth Kazin, and extended to Rimmon, which borders on Neah" (Joshua 19:13). This border note places the town firmly within Israel's northern tribes, in the region that Isaiah would later call "Galilee of the Gentiles."

The prophet Jonah. Centuries later, in the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel, the town reappears as a prophet's home. Jeroboam "restored the territory of Israel from the entrance of Hamath to the Sea of the Arabah, according to the word of the LORD God of Israel, which He had spoken through His servant Jonah the son of Amittai, the prophet who was from Gath Hepher" (2 Kings 14:25). The surrounding verses explain why God gave this success. Jeroboam did evil in the LORD's sight, yet the LORD saw Israel's bitter affliction, saw that no one was left to help, and chose to save them through Jeroboam (2 Kings 14:24, 26–27). Jonah's word from Gath-hepher was therefore a message of undeserved mercy to his own people.

The same "Jonah the son of Amittai" received the call to go to Nineveh (Jonah 1:1). His book never mentions his hometown, but Second Kings fixes him as a real man from a real Galilean village, not a figure of legend.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states three facts plainly. Gath-hepher belonged to Zebulun, Jonah came from there, and God spoke through Jonah to restore Israel's borders. From these facts we can draw a few careful conclusions.

First, God's word was active among the northern tribes even during a time of national unfaithfulness. He did not confine His prophetic voice to Jerusalem or to priestly centers. He called a man from a small winepress town.

Second, the prophet from Gath-hepher became the bearer of God's mercy in two directions. Toward Israel, he announced rescue for a nation that had not earned it. Toward Nineveh, he carried a warning that led the city to repent and receive mercy (Jonah 3:5–10). Jonah's own complaint reveals what he knew about the God who sent him. He fled because he knew the LORD was gracious, merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in lovingkindness (Jonah 4:2). Scripture does not record how Jonah felt about the rescue he announced for Israel, but it does record his anger when the same mercy reached Gentiles (Jonah 4:1). Through him, God showed that His compassion reaches beyond Israel's borders.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

An explicit connection: the light in Zebulun. Isaiah promised that the land "of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali… Galilee of the Gentiles" would not remain in gloom: "The people who walked in darkness Have seen a great light" (Isaiah 9:1–2). Matthew explicitly applies this prophecy to Jesus' ministry in Galilee (Matthew 4:13–16). Gath-hepher lay within that very land of Zebulun.

An explicit connection: the sign of Jonah. Jesus said that no sign would be given to "an evil and adulterous generation" except "the sign of the prophet Jonah." As Jonah was three days and three nights in the great fish, so the Son of Man would be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth. The men of Nineveh, who repented at Jonah's preaching, would rise in judgment against that generation, because "a greater than Jonah is here" (Matthew 12:39–41). Jesus Himself made Jonah a pointer to His death, resurrection, and the repentance of the nations.

A telling irony: "no prophet… out of Galilee." When Nicodemus urged a fair hearing for Jesus, the Pharisees answered, "Are you also from Galilee? Search and look, for no prophet has arisen out of Galilee" (John 7:52). They told Nicodemus to search the Scriptures, yet 2 Kings 14:25 names a prophet from Gath-hepher in Galilee. Their contempt for Galileans (see John 7:47–49) led them to a claim their own Bible contradicted.

A geographical resonance. Gath-hepher lies only a few miles from Nazareth, where Jesus grew up. Scripture never pairs the two towns, so the closeness should not be pressed into a hidden prophecy. Still, it is striking that the prophet Jesus named as His sign came from the same hill country where Jesus spent His boyhood. The Galilee that religious leaders dismissed had already produced Jonah, and it now produced the One greater than Jonah.

5. Spirit & Life

Gath-hepher tells us something about how God works. He raised a prophet from a farming village on a tribal border, and later He raised up His Son in an unremarkable town in the same region. God is not impressed by the places people respect, and He is not hindered by the places they look down on.

The Pharisees' words in John 7 are a sober warning to anyone who knows the Bible well. They were confident and learned, yet prejudice led them to say what Scripture did not say, and to reject Christ while doing it. Knowing God's Word requires humility as well as study. Our assumptions about who God can use must stay open to correction by the text itself.

Jonah's own story probes the heart. He carried God's word of rescue to his own nation, yet when the same mercy reached his enemies, he was angry enough to ask God to take his life (Jonah 4:1–3). The God who saved undeserving Israel through Jonah's word, and then spared repentant Nineveh, is the same God who sent Jesus. He calls us both to receive mercy we have not earned and to rejoice when that mercy reaches people we would never have chosen.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Joshua 19:13 places Gath-hepher on the boundary of Zebulun's inheritance.

  • 2 Kings 14:23–27 identifies Jonah as "the prophet who was from Gath Hepher" and records his message of mercy to Israel under Jeroboam II.

  • Jonah 1:1–2; 3:5–10; 4:2 tell of the same prophet's mission to Nineveh and his witness to God's compassion.

  • Isaiah 9:1–2 promises light to the land of Zebulun and "Galilee of the Gentiles."

  • Matthew 4:13–16 applies Isaiah's promise to Jesus' Galilean ministry.

  • Matthew 12:39–41 records Jesus naming "the sign of the prophet Jonah" as the sign of His death and resurrection.

  • John 7:52 contains the Pharisees' claim that no prophet arises from Galilee, a claim contradicted by 2 Kings 14:25.
