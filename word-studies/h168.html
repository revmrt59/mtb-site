TENT, TABERNACLE - (ʼôhel, Strong's H0168)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼôhel means "tent." It is the ordinary portable home of shepherds and wanderers: woven goat hair stretched over poles, held down by cords and stakes, and taken down when the flocks moved on. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob lived in tents. Soldiers slept in them. A family's tent stood for its whole household and its future. When God told Moses how to build His dwelling among Israel, He also chose a tent. The NKJV usually calls this sacred tent the "tabernacle" or "tabernacle of meeting," and that is why one Hebrew word appears in English Bibles as both "tent" and "tabernacle."

That choice says a great deal about God. He did not wait until Israel was settled to live among them. He pitched His tent in the middle of their camp in the wilderness, moved when they moved, and spoke with Moses there. The prophets and psalmists drew on the same picture. God stretches out the heavens like a tent. The tents of the wicked collapse. God's people long to stay in His tent, and one day Zion's tent will be enlarged and never taken down. John says the Word "dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and his Greek verb is related to the word for tent. Paul says that God's people themselves are now "being built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:22). In Revelation the story ends with "the tabernacle of God is with men" (Revelation 21:3).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼôhel is the common Hebrew word for a tent. In the ancient Near East, tents were usually made of dark goat-hair cloth. The bride in the Song of Solomon compares herself to "the tents of Kedar" (Song of Solomon 1:5), the black tents of desert herdsmen. A tent could be set up quickly, expanded by adding cloth, and packed up for travel. It offered shelter without any claim to permanence.

The word appears most often in Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers, where it names God's sanctuary. Many of its occurrences, especially in the phrase "tabernacle of meeting," are translated "tabernacle" in the NKJV. Readers should know that the NKJV also uses "tabernacle" for a different Hebrew word, mishkan (H4908, dwelling place). Exodus 26:7 shows the two side by side. God tells Moses to make "curtains of goats' hair, to be a tent over the tabernacle." In that verse, "tent" is ʼôhel, the outer covering of goat hair and skins, and "tabernacle" is mishkan, the dwelling beneath it. God's house was covered with the same kind of goat-hair cloth used for ordinary family tents.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A tent as a physical dwelling, the movable home of nomads, herdsmen, and soldiers. Abram "pitched his tent with Bethel on the west and Ai on the east" (Genesis 12:8).

2. A home or household. The word extends to the people and the life sheltered by the tent, and sometimes the NKJV simply translates it "home" (Judges 19:9) or "dwelling" (Psalm 91:10).

3. God's sacred tent, the sanctuary where He dwelt among Israel. This tent is most often called the "tabernacle of meeting" (Exodus 40:34; Leviticus 1:1) and sometimes "the tent of the Testimony" (Numbers 9:15).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Genesis the tent marks the life of the patriarchs. The NKJV describes Jacob as "a mild man, dwelling in tents" (Genesis 25:27). The tent is also where private things happen. Noah "became uncovered in his tent" (Genesis 9:21), and in Genesis 18 Sarah listens at the tent door while the LORD speaks of the son she will bear.

In the Law, the phrase "tabernacle of meeting" (literally "tent of meeting") explains the tent's purpose. God told Moses that at its door "I will meet you to speak with you" (Exodus 29:42, paraphrased). The tent was the place where God met with His people and spoke to them.

Because a tent represented a household, its fate stood for a family's fate. Bildad warns that "the light is dark in his tent" when the wicked man falls (Job 18:6, paraphrased). Proverbs promises that the tent of the upright will flourish (Proverbs 14:11). Later, when Israel lived in houses, the old word survived in a political cry. "Every man to his tents, O Israel!" (2 Samuel 20:1) was a call to go home and abandon the king.

4. Theological Significance

God's decision to live in a tent is the foundation of this word's theology. Israel was a people on the move, and God made His dwelling a tent like theirs. When the work was finished, "the cloud covered the tabernacle of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exodus 40:34). From then on, the cloud over the tent decided when Israel camped and when it marched (Numbers 9:15–23). God traveled with His people, at the pace of a herding nation.

His nearness did not shrink His holiness. Moses "was not able to enter the tabernacle of meeting, because the cloud rested above it" (Exodus 40:35). The God who chose humble cloth was still a consuming glory, and the priestly laws that follow in Leviticus begin with Him speaking "from the tabernacle of meeting" (Leviticus 1:1) to teach a sinful people how to approach Him.

The same image shows His greatness on the largest scale. Isaiah describes God as the One "who stretches out the heavens like a curtain, and spreads them out like a tent to dwell in" (Isaiah 40:22). Psalm 19:4 says that in the heavens "He has set a tabernacle for the sun." The whole sky is no harder for Him than a tent is for a shepherd, and the people below are "like grasshoppers."

The fragility of a tent also serves as a warning. A tent that God does not uphold falls in a moment. Jeremiah grieves, "suddenly my tents are plundered" (Jeremiah 4:20, paraphrased). Lamentations 2:4 says God poured out His fury like fire on the tent of the daughter of Zion. The image applies to Israel as well as to its enemies. Security never belonged to the tent itself.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 12:8; 13:3–4; 18:1 — Tent and altar. Near Bethel, Abram pitched his tent, and there he "built an altar to the LORD and called on the name of the LORD" (Genesis 12:8). When he came back from Egypt, he returned "to the place where his tent had been at the beginning… to the place of the altar" (Genesis 13:3–4). The tent showed that Abram was a temporary resident, and the altar showed whom he belonged to. Abram owned no land, yet later, at Mamre, the LORD came to him at his tent: He "appeared to him… as he was sitting in the tent door" (Genesis 18:1). Hebrews explains what the tents meant: "By faith he dwelt in the land of promise as in a foreign country, dwelling in tents with Isaac and Jacob" (Hebrews 11:9), because he was waiting for a city God would build (11:10). The patriarchs' tents were an act of faith, a refusal to treat the present land as their final home.

Exodus 33:7–11 — The tent outside the camp. After the golden calf, Moses pitched a tent far outside the camp and called it the tabernacle of meeting. Anyone who sought the LORD went out to it. When Moses entered, the pillar of cloud came down at the door, and every Israelite stood at the door of his own tent and worshiped. The same word, ʼôhel, names both God's tent and theirs, and the two face each other across the distance that sin had created. Then comes the heart of the passage: the LORD spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend (33:11, paraphrased). Before any gold or altar was made for the finished tabernacle, God's tent was a place of conversation and friendship. Even after Moses returned to the camp, young Joshua stayed at the tent and did not leave. This passage shows what God's dwelling was meant to be: a place where He is known.

Jeremiah 10:20; Isaiah 54:2; 33:20 — The tent torn down and the tent enlarged. Jeremiah gives voice to a ruined Jerusalem. Her tent is plundered, her cords are broken, her children are gone, and no one is left to set up her curtains (Jeremiah 10:20, paraphrased). Isaiah 54 uses almost the same vocabulary to picture the reverse: "Enlarge the place of your tent, and let them stretch out the curtains of your dwellings; do not spare; lengthen your cords, and strengthen your stakes" (Isaiah 54:2). The verse before tells the barren woman to sing, because the children of the desolate will be more than the children of the married woman (Isaiah 54:1). A tent can grow, so the image fits a family about to overflow. Isaiah 33:20 adds what no ordinary tent could claim: Jerusalem will be "a tabernacle that will not be taken down; not one of its stakes will ever be removed, nor will any of its cords be broken." God's restored people combine the reach of a tent with a permanence no tent ever had.

Psalms 15, 27, and 61 — Longing for God's tent. David asks who may stay as a guest in the LORD's tabernacle, and Psalm 15 answers with a portrait of integrity: truthful speech, honest dealings, and a refusal to slander. In trouble David is confident that God will hide him in the secret place of His tabernacle and that he will offer sacrifices of joy there (Psalm 27:5–6). He resolves to abide in God's tabernacle forever and take refuge under the shelter of His wings (Psalm 61:4). The tent on Zion became, in David's prayers, a picture of living in God's presence. Guests in that tent received protection, and they were expected to share the character of their Host.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The tent thread runs from Abraham's door at Mamre to the new creation. John's words "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory" (John 1:14) echo Exodus 40. The glory that once filled the tent of meeting now appears in the Son, who took on a human body and lived among His people. He came to them in their own condition, as God once did in the wilderness.

Isaiah 16:5 looks to a King: "In mercy the throne will be established; and One will sit on it in truth, in the tabernacle of David, judging and seeking justice and hastening righteousness." The verse describes the future house of David as a tent, and it ties that rule to mercy, truth, and justice. Christians rightly see this as pointing to Jesus, the Son of David, whose reign is marked by mercy, truth and justice rather than oppression.

Between the Incarnation and the final day that Revelation describes, God has not left His people without His dwelling presence. Paul tells believers from the nations that they are "no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19). With "Jesus Christ Himself being the chief cornerstone," they "are being built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:20, 22). Paul asks the Corinthian church, "Do you not know that you are the temple of God and that the Spirit of God dwells in you?" (1 Corinthians 3:16). Paul uses Greek words for a building and a temple here, not the Hebrew word for tent, but he is carrying forward the same purpose the tent of meeting served: God living among His people and meeting with them. In Exodus 33 the people stood at their own tent doors and watched while Moses went in to speak with God (Exodus 33:8–10). Through Christ, believers no longer stand at a distance and watch. Joined together, they are the place where God lives by His Spirit, so that worship, prayer and the life of the church are lived in His presence.

Paul applies the barren woman's song in Isaiah 54 to the children of the Jerusalem above (Galatians 4:27). Read in that light, the enlarging of the tent pictures the gospel going out to the nations and bringing in more children than the old boundaries could hold. The story ends with the promise these tents foreshadowed: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3). The God who traveled with Israel in a tent will at last live with His people without end.

7. Spirit & Life

Abraham's pattern still applies. A believer lives in the world the way Abraham lived in Canaan, present and active but not settled, because "the place" that matters most is the altar. The tent and the altar stood together. A light hold on this world and a firm hold on God belong together.

Our own bodies are tents. Hezekiah, writing after he had been sick and near death, said, "My life span is gone, taken from me like a shepherd's tent" (Isaiah 38:12). Paul faced the same fact with hope: "if our earthly house, this tent, is destroyed, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens" (2 Corinthians 5:1). The weakness of the tent need not end in fear, because God has prepared a home beyond it.

Scripture also asks which tent we will live in. When Korah rebelled, Moses told Israel to depart from the tents of these wicked men before the ground opened (Numbers 16:26). The psalmist would rather be a doorkeeper in God's house than dwell in the tents of wickedness (Psalm 84:10). Where we live, and with whom we stand, has lasting consequences.

Exodus 33 holds out the deepest invitation. The tent stood outside the camp, and those who sought the LORD had to walk out to it. Hebrews 13:13 calls believers to go out to Jesus "outside the camp." The resonance is fitting. God's friendship is open to anyone willing to leave the crowd's comfort and go where He has promised to meet them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 24:67 — "Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah's tent… So Isaac was comforted after his mother's death." When Rebekah enters Sarah's tent, the promised family line continues into the next generation.

  • Joshua 7:21–23 — Achan hid the stolen treasure "in the earth in the midst of my tent." The tent walls hid nothing from God, and one man's private sin brought defeat on the whole camp.

  • Judges 4:21; 5:24 — Jael killed the oppressor Sisera with a tent peg, and Deborah and Barak sing, "Blessed is she among women in tents." God used an ordinary household tool to deliver His people.

  • 1 Kings 12:16 — "To your tents, O Israel!… So Israel departed to their tents." With this cry the northern tribes broke away from David's house and divided the kingdom.

  • Exodus 26:11 — Moses was to "couple the tent together, that it may be one." The fifty bronze clasps joined many curtains into a single dwelling for God.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H4908 (mishkan; dwelling, tabernacle) — A distinct Hebrew word that is often paired with ʼôhel in the tabernacle passages. In Exodus 26:7 the goat-hair tent covers the dwelling.

  • H5521 (sukkah; booth, tabernacle) — A different word for a temporary shelter. Amos 9:11 uses it for "the tabernacle of David, which has fallen down." That verse is a thematic parallel to Isaiah 16:5, but it uses a different Hebrew word.

  • G4633 (skēnē; tent, tabernacle) — The New Testament Greek word that carries the same image, as in "the tabernacle of God is with men" (Revelation 21:3).
