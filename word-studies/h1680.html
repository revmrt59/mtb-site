GENTLY, GLIDE - (dâbab, Strong's H1680)

Summary

The Hebrew verb dâbab means to glide, to move slowly and smoothly, or to flow gently. It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 7:9. There the beloved's mouth is compared to "the best wine," a wine that "goes down smoothly for my beloved, moving gently the lips of sleepers." Because the word appears only in this verse, its meaning comes entirely from this verse: a smooth, unforced motion, the opposite of something abrupt or harsh.

The picture is small, but it says a great deal. The finest wine is also the gentlest. It flows over the lips of people at rest and stirs them without jolting them awake. Scripture uses this image for the sweetness of married love and the affectionate words that pass between lovers. Elsewhere the Bible describes the Lord awakening His people in the same way, through a voice that knocks, a word spoken to the weary, and a love whose strength does not need to be loud.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dâbab is a Hebrew verb found only once in the Old Testament. A word with a single use can be understood only through its one context, so building theories from its supposed root is unreliable. The setting is love poetry. In Song of Solomon 7 the bridegroom praises his bride from her feet in sandals (7:1) up to her head, "like Mount Carmel" (7:5). He ends with her breath and her mouth (7:8–9). The language throughout is full of the senses: fruit, fragrance, wine, and touch.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Dâbab describes smooth, gentle movement, a gliding or slow flowing. The NKJV translates it "moving gently." The KJV renders the line "causing the lips of those that are asleep to speak." That translation connects the word to the idea of stirring lips into murmuring. Both renderings describe the same scene. Something flows over sleeping lips so softly that the lips begin to move. One definition covers the word: gentle, gliding motion.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In its single use, dâbab describes how the wine moves. The verse builds in two steps. First the wine "goes down smoothly," which describes how easily it is received. Then it is "moving gently the lips of sleepers," which describes what it does to those it reaches. The second phrase adds tenderness to the first. The wine is easy to drink, and it also stirs people at rest without startling them.

One detail of the passage helps the reader. The phrase "for my beloved" is the bride's usual way of speaking about the bridegroom. The voice therefore seems to change in the middle of the verse. He begins praising her mouth, and she finishes his sentence, saying that this wine flows for him. The gentle gliding belongs to love that is shared by both.

Some modern versions follow ancient translations and read "lips and teeth" in place of "lips of sleepers." The NKJV keeps the traditional Hebrew wording, and this study follows it.

4. Theological Significance

This verse teaches something about love that runs through the whole Bible. Real strength and real gentleness belong together. The wine in Song 7:9 is "the best wine," the finest available, and it moves "gently." The bridegroom does not have to choose between strong love and tender love. In a fallen world people often equate power with force, volume, and pressure. This verse presents the deepest love as rich in quality and gentle in the way it is given.

The passage also shows that God honors the delight of married love. The Song's frank praise of the body and its pleasure in mutual affection are part of Scripture. Wisdom literature agrees elsewhere, urging a husband to rejoice in the wife of his youth (Proverbs 5:15–19). The intimacy God designed is meant to be sweet, received with ease, and enjoyed by both husband and wife.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 7:6–9: the best wine that glides. The bridegroom's praise reaches its height in a cry of wonder: "How fair and how pleasant you are, O love, with your delights!" (7:6). He compares her to a palm tree and her breath to apples (7:7–8). Then he names the roof of her mouth. In Hebrew poetry the palate is the place of both taste and speech. When he likens it to "the best wine," he is praising the taste of her kisses and the sweetness of her words together. Then comes dâbab. This wine does not burn or overwhelm. It glides, and it gently moves "the lips of sleepers." Her love is strong enough to rouse someone and tender enough to do it softly.

Song of Solomon 5:2 and 5:16: the gentle voice and the sweet mouth. The Song has already prepared the reader for this picture. In 5:2 the bride says, "I sleep, but my heart is awake; it is the voice of my beloved!" He does not break down the door. He knocks and calls her tender names: "my sister, my love, my dove, my perfect one." Later she describes him: "His mouth is most sweet" (5:16). Taken together, these verses show that the lovers awaken each other through affection. The mouth that speaks and kisses is sweet, and the voice that rouses the sleeper is gentle. Song 7:9 gathers both ideas into one image of wine flowing over sleeping lips.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Paul reaches back to the first marriage when he quotes "the two shall become one flesh" and then adds, "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32). The New Testament does not quote Song 7:9 directly. Still, Paul's teaching invites readers to see human married love as a reflection of Christ's love for His people. When the church reads the Song with that in mind, it sees in the gently flowing wine something of how Christ loves His bride.

That love also awakens. In Isaiah 50:4 the Servant of the Lord says, "The Lord GOD has given Me the tongue of the learned, that I should know how to speak a word in season to him who is weary. He awakens Me morning by morning." The Servant is first awakened by the Father, and then He speaks a fitting word to tired people. The Gospels show Jesus as that Servant. He did not break a bruised reed. He called the weary to come to Him, and He spoke to the broken in ways that brought them back to life.

Paul applies the same picture to the gospel: "Awake, you who sleep, arise from the dead, and Christ will give you light" (Ephesians 5:14). The call is urgent, and its result is light, not terror. Read alongside these passages, the wine that moves the lips of sleepers becomes an image of Christ's love reaching people who are spiritually dull. His love wakes them, and they begin to speak and respond to Him. The Song does not state this fulfillment directly. It is a fitting connection that the wider Bible supports.

7. Spirit & Life

This one word speaks into the way we talk to the people we love. The bride's palate is like the best wine, and her words glide. Proverbs 16:24 says the same thing in wisdom form: "Pleasant words are like a honeycomb, sweetness to the soul and health to the bones." Husbands and wives can wound each other with words that grate and accuse, or they can refresh each other with speech that flows easily and builds up. Gentle words take nothing away from honesty. They help honest words be received.

The verse also speaks to believers who feel spiritually asleep, numb in prayer, or slow to praise. The Lord does not usually rouse His people by crushing them. Song 5:2 shows Him knocking and calling His bride by tender names. Isaiah 50:4 shows Him speaking a word in season to the weary. The "best wine" of His love flows over sleeping lips until they begin to move. When your heart is dull, return to His promises and let His Word work quietly. Often the first sign of renewal is a murmured "thank You," which is a sleeper's lips beginning to stir.

The verse also corrects how we think about strength. The finest wine is the one that moves gently. Christians who want to influence others, whether as parents, pastors, or friends, can learn from this that the deepest love rarely needs to be forceful.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Dâbab occurs only in Song of Solomon 7:9, which has been discussed above. Other verses help explain its meaning, although none of them contains this Hebrew word:

  • Song of Solomon 5:16: "His mouth is most sweet." Here the bride praises the bridegroom's mouth, just as he praises hers in 7:9.

  • Proverbs 16:24: Pleasant words are compared to honey, which carries the theme of sweet, life-giving speech into wisdom teaching.

  • Proverbs 23:31: This verse warns against wine that sparkles in the cup and goes down with ease (paraphrased). It is a sober counterpoint. Ordinary wine's smoothness can deceive, while the "best wine" of faithful love in Song 7:9 refreshes without harm.
