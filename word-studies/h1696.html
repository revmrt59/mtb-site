SPEAK, SPOKE, PROMISED - (dâbar, Strong's H1696)

Summary

The Hebrew verb dâbar is the Old Testament's main word for speaking. It describes ordinary conversation, a king's instructions, a prophet's message, and above all the voice of God. "The LORD spoke to Moses" opens chapter after chapter of the Law. The same verb is often translated "promised" when God commits Himself to a future act, and "pronounced" when He declares judgment. In Scripture, when God speaks, He is binding Himself to act.

The word shows that the God of the Bible makes Himself known by speaking. He spoke to Moses "face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (Exodus 33:11). He kept every word He promised to Israel. God's speech sets the living God apart from idols that have mouths but cannot speak. The Old Testament ends waiting for a Prophet who would speak everything God commanded, and that expectation reaches its fulfillment in Jesus. Because God speaks truthfully and faithfully, the word also calls His people to speak the same way.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dâbar appears in nearly every book of the Old Testament. It clusters heavily in Numbers, Exodus, Deuteronomy, and Leviticus, where it introduces God's instructions. It is also frequent in Jeremiah and Ezekiel, which carry the prophetic message of exile and return. It is closely related to the common Hebrew noun for "word" or "matter." In 1 Kings 8:56, Solomon says that "not one word" of God's promise has failed, and the noun and the verb stand side by side in that verse.

The verb often works alongside the ordinary Hebrew verb for "say." That pairing produces the familiar pattern "the LORD spoke to Moses, saying." In that pattern, dâbar names the act of speaking to someone, and the following verb introduces the words that were spoken.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, dâbar means to speak: to put a message into words and address it to someone. Context determines the nuance.

1. To speak, talk, or converse. This sense covers ordinary speech between people, as when Joseph "spoke kindly" to his frightened brothers (Genesis 50:21).

2. To declare, command, or promise. Here the speaker binds himself or directs others by what he says. This sense explains NKJV renderings such as "promised" (Deuteronomy 1:11; 1 Kings 8:56), "pronounced" (Jeremiah 11:17), and "directed" (1 Kings 12:12).

3. To subdue. In two psalms the NKJV renders the word "subdues" or "subdue" (Psalm 18:47; 47:3). Many lexicons treat these verses as a separate, rare Hebrew root that happens to share the same spelling.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The speaker makes the largest difference in how the word should be understood. When people speak, the verb describes negotiations, counsel, quarrels, and courtship. When God speaks, the same verb introduces law, revelation, promise, and judgment. When God speaks of the future, translators often render the verb "promised." The verb itself has not changed. A speech from God about the future is a pledge He intends to keep.

The hearer and the setting also matter. At Sinai, God's speaking terrified the people, and they begged Moses to speak to them instead (Exodus 20:19). In the tabernacle, God promised to meet with Moses and speak with him from above the mercy seat (Exodus 25:22; Numbers 7:89). In the Psalms and Proverbs, the verb measures human character: people who speak truth in the heart (Psalm 15:2), and people who speak peace while evil is in their hearts (Psalm 28:3).

4. Theological Significance

Biblical faith begins with a God who speaks. People did not reach Him by searching or speculating. He addressed them. "And God spoke to Moses and said to him: 'I am the LORD'" (Exodus 6:2). Leviticus opens the same way (Leviticus 1:1), and the prophets received their calling the same way (Jeremiah 1:7; Ezekiel 2:2). Each time, God starts the relationship by speaking.

Idols cannot do this, and that difference separates the living God from every false god. "They have mouths, but they do not speak" (Psalm 115:5; repeated in 135:16). Jeremiah describes idols as upright like a palm tree, yet unable to speak and needing to be carried (Jeremiah 10:5). On Mount Carmel, Baal's prophets cried out from morning until evening, "But there was no voice; no one answered" (1 Kings 18:26, 29). A god who cannot speak cannot command his worshipers, comfort them, or make promises to them.

God's speech also accomplishes what it announces. Balaam was hired to curse Israel, but he was forced to confess this truth: "Has He said, and will He not do? Or has He spoken, and will He not make it good?" (Numbers 23:19). Ezekiel repeats the idea often. The LORD has spoken, and He will do it (Ezekiel 22:14; 24:14). God warns that none of His words will be delayed any longer (Ezekiel 12:25, 28). The same certainty that brought judgment on Jerusalem also stands behind restoration. God says He will rebuild the ruined places, and He will put His Spirit into the dry bones of His people, because He has spoken it (Ezekiel 36:36; 37:14).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

2 Samuel 7 and 1 Kings 8: Praying and praising on the basis of what God has spoken. After God promised David a lasting house, David's prayer rests entirely on that promise. He asks God to establish forever "the word which You have spoken" concerning his house, and to "do as You have said" (2 Samuel 7:25). He then confesses that God's words are true and that He has "promised this goodness" to His servant (7:28). David's boldness in prayer did not come from his own merit. It came from what God had already spoken. A generation later, Solomon stood at the dedication of the temple and testified that God had "fulfilled His word which He spoke" (1 Kings 8:20). He then summed up all of Israel's history from Egypt to that day: "There has not failed one word of all His good promise, which He promised through His servant Moses" (8:56). The prayer of the father and the praise of the son belong together. David asked God to keep what He had spoken, and Solomon testified that God had kept it.

Exodus 33 and Numbers 12: Speaking face to face. The God who thundered at Sinai also spoke with closeness. "So the LORD spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (Exodus 33:11). When Miriam and Aaron challenged Moses, God defended him by describing how He spoke with him: "I speak with him face to face, Even plainly, and not in dark sayings" (Numbers 12:8). After those conversations, Moses' face shone because he had been talking with God (Exodus 34:29). Through this verb, Scripture shows that God's greatest desire in speaking was to have real communion with His servant. Giving orders from a distance was never the whole of it.

Deuteronomy 18 and Numbers 22: The prophet's narrow authority. A prophet in Israel had no message of his own. God promised to raise up a prophet like Moses, to put His words in that prophet's mouth, and that this prophet would speak everything God commanded (Deuteronomy 18:18). Anyone who presumed to speak a word God had not commanded was to die (18:20). Even Balaam, a compromised pagan seer, admitted this boundary: "The word that God puts in my mouth, that I must speak" (Numbers 22:38). The messenger could not edit, soften, or add to the message.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Deuteronomy 18 left Israel waiting. Prophets came and went, and some were faithful. Still, none of them fully matched the promise of a Prophet like Moses who would speak everything God commanded. Jesus claimed that role directly: "For I have not spoken on My own authority; but the Father who sent Me gave Me a command, what I should say and what I should speak" (John 12:49). His words echo the terms of Deuteronomy 18. He was given a command, and He spoke it.

Hebrews gathers the whole Old Testament history of God's speaking into one sentence. God "spoke in time past to the fathers by the prophets," and He "has in these last days spoken to us by His Son" (Hebrews 1:1–2). Hebrews is written in Greek and uses a different verb, but it describes the same pattern this Hebrew word traces. The God who started a relationship by speaking at the burning bush and at Sinai now speaks finally in a Son. The Son delivers God's message, and John's Gospel also calls Him the Word made flesh (John 1:1, 14). The promises God spoke to David reach their goal in David's greater Son. The certainty Solomon praised in 1 Kings 8:56 becomes the believer's confidence in Christ, who is God's "Yes" to every promise (2 Corinthians 1:20).

7. Spirit & Life

Because God speaks, the first duty of His people is to listen. Young Samuel learned to answer the voice in the night by asking the LORD to speak, because His servant was listening (1 Samuel 3:9–10). That kind of prayer begins by giving God the first word. Many believers come to Scripture already sure of what they want to hear. Samuel's response teaches us to come expecting God to speak and to let Him set the agenda.

God's speaking is also tender. Through Isaiah, God told His messengers to speak comfort to Jerusalem (Isaiah 40:2). Through Hosea, He said He would lead His unfaithful people into the wilderness and speak comfort to them (Hosea 2:14). The God who pronounces judgment also speaks to win back the people He loves. For someone who has wandered, this matters greatly. His word comes with correction, but He means it to bring the wanderer home.

What God has spoken is also the ground of our prayers. David prayed, "do as You have said" (2 Samuel 7:25). That is still a faithful way to pray. Believers stand on promises God has already made, and they can be confident because He has not let one word of those promises fail.

Finally, people made in the image of a speaking God are responsible for their own words. The person who may dwell on God's holy hill is the one who speaks the truth in his heart (Psalm 15:2). In Malachi's day many people spoke harshly against God (Malachi 3:13). Those who feared the LORD spoke to one another instead, and the LORD listened, heard them, and had their names written in a book of remembrance (3:16). God pays attention to how His people talk with one another. Ordinary conversation among those who fear Him is something He hears and remembers.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 50:21 — Joseph comforts his brothers and speaks kindly to them. In this verse, human speech carries forgiveness.

  • Joshua 21:45 — Joshua records that not one of the good things the LORD had spoken to Israel failed, and all came to pass. The verse anticipates Solomon's later testimony in 1 Kings 8:56.

  • Judges 12:6 — The men of Ephraim could not pronounce "Shibboleth" correctly, and their speech gave away who they were. The verb here describes pronouncing a single word.

  • Jeremiah 20:9 — Jeremiah resolved not to speak in the LORD's name any longer, but the word became like a burning fire shut up in his bones. A true prophet could not keep silent.

  • Psalm 116:10 — The psalmist believed, and therefore he spoke. Paul quotes this verse to describe his own preaching (2 Corinthians 4:13).
