HONEY - (dᵉbash, Strong's H1706)

Summary

The Hebrew word dᵉbash means honey. It can refer to honey taken from a wild hive or to a thick, sweet syrup boiled down from fruit such as dates or grapes. In the ancient world honey was the richest sweetness people knew, so it came to stand for delight, abundance and life at its best. Its most familiar use is in God's promise of "a land flowing with milk and honey." That phrase runs from the burning bush to the prophets and tells Israel that the God who rescued them from Egypt was bringing them into fullness.

Scripture then carries honey further. David and the psalmist say God's words are sweeter than honey, and Ezekiel finds a scroll full of mourning sweet as honey in his mouth. Honey is also kept off the Lord's altar of fire, it becomes Israel's survival food in Isaiah's prophecy of judgment, and in Ezekiel Jerusalem offers it to idols. Proverbs warns that too much honey sickens. Taken together, these uses teach a believer to receive God's good gifts gladly, to keep them in proportion, and to find in God's own voice a sweetness greater than anything the land could give.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dᵉbash was an everyday word in a land of wild bees and date palms. Some passages clearly mean bee honey: Samson finds bees and honey inside a lion's carcass (Judges 14:8), and Saul's soldiers walk through a forest where honey lies on the ground (1 Samuel 14:25–26). Other passages may point to fruit syrup. When Hezekiah's people bring the firstfruits of grain, new wine, oil and honey (2 Chronicles 31:5), the honey sits among farm produce, which suggests it was probably a fruit syrup such as date syrup. Often the exact kind cannot be determined, and the message does not depend on it. The word appears in promises about the land, in family and royal gifts, in battle stories, in wisdom sayings, in love poetry and in prophecy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Bee honey, whether found wild in the comb or gathered from hives, as in Judges 14:8–9.

2. A thick, sweet syrup made from fruit, especially dates, which was counted with the land's produce, probably as in 2 Chronicles 31:5.

In both senses the word names something sweet, precious and nourishing. Its figurative uses grow out of these qualities rather than adding a separate dictionary meaning.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The land promise. The most frequent setting is the phrase "flowing with milk and honey" (Exodus 3:8; Numbers 13:27; Deuteronomy 6:3; Jeremiah 11:5). Milk came from the flocks every day, while honey was a treat. Together they describe a land that supplies both what people need and what they enjoy, so that nothing is lacking.

Food and gift. Honey was valuable enough to give. Jeroboam's wife carries "a jar of honey" to the prophet (1 Kings 14:3), and Jacob includes some honey among the gifts for Egypt's ruler (Genesis 43:11).

Comparison for words. Poets compare God's judgments to honey (Psalm 19:10; 119:103). Proverbs compares pleasant words to a honeycomb that sweetens the soul and heals the bones (Proverbs 16:24), and a father tells his son that wisdom will be to his soul what honey is to his mouth (Proverbs 24:13–14).

Limits. The same book also warns the reader. If you find honey, eat only what you need, or you will be sick of it (Proverbs 25:16). It is not good to eat much honey (Proverbs 25:27).

Love. In the Song of Solomon the bridegroom says, "Your lips, O my spouse, Drip as the honeycomb; Honey and milk are under your tongue" (Song of Solomon 4:11). Later he says, "I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey" (Song of Solomon 5:1). The pairing of milk and honey echoes the language of the promised land. The bridegroom delights in his bride, and Scripture calls married love good and sweet without embarrassment.

4. Theological Significance

Honey shows a God who promises and then delivers. He first mentions the land of milk and honey to Moses while Israel is still enslaved (Exodus 3:8). That means the sweetness was promised before there was any sign of rescue. Ezekiel later recalls the promise in God's own voice: He brought them "into a land that I had searched out for them, 'flowing with milk and honey,' the glory of all lands" (Ezekiel 20:6). The words "searched out" show that God had chosen this place for His people with care. Joshua 5:6 adds a sober note. The generation that did not obey the voice of the LORD died in the wilderness without seeing the land.

God's provision is not limited by circumstances. The Song of Moses celebrates the abundance God gave Israel in the land. He made them "draw honey from the rock, And oil from the flinty rock," alongside the produce of the fields, curds and milk, the rams of Bashan, the choicest wheat and wine (Deuteronomy 32:13–14). The Song praises God for providing sweetness even from rocky country. Psalm 81:16 repeats the image with grief: "with honey from the rock I would have satisfied you," if only Israel had listened. God was willing to give, but Israel turned away from His voice and missed the blessing.

Honey also reveals that God's holiness sets limits even on good things. The best sweetness of the land had a place in Israel's life, but it was not permitted to be burned on God's altar.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 19 and 1 Samuel 14: honey that brightens the eyes. Saul had bound his weary army with an oath against eating, and his men fought hungry. Jonathan had not heard the oath. He dipped his staff into a honeycomb, ate, and was restored at once. The Hebrew literally says his eyes grew bright (1 Samuel 14:27–29). David uses the same pair of ideas in Psalm 19. "The commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes" (Psalm 19:8), and God's judgments are "Sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb" (Psalm 19:10). Honey gave a tired soldier new strength and clear sight. God's Word does the same for the soul, and it does so far more deeply. David also places the Word above gold. It is more valuable than wealth and more delightful than the sweetest food.

Ezekiel 2–3: a bitter scroll that tasted sweet. The scroll handed to Ezekiel was written with lamentations, mourning and woe (Ezekiel 2:10). Yet when he ate it at God's command, it was like honey in his mouth (Ezekiel 3:3). The message was severe, but it was God's own word, true and pure, and receiving it was sweet to the prophet. Psalm 119:103 says, "How sweet are Your words to my taste," and Ezekiel shows that this sweetness remains even when God's words bring warning. John's experience in Revelation 10:9–10 matches Ezekiel's: the little book was "as sweet as honey in my mouth," but it made his stomach bitter. Taking in God's word is joy, and carrying its judgment can still bring pain.

Leviticus 2:11–12: no honey on the fire. Israel could bring honey as firstfruits, but no leaven and no honey could be burned in any offering made by fire to the Lord. This prohibition is striking because honey was the very sweetness of the promised land (Leviticus 20:24). The text does not give a reason. Many interpreters connect honey with leaven because both cause fermentation. That suggests God did not want what spoils and sours placed on His altar. At the least, the law shows that the altar belonged to God's own terms. Even the best sweetness Israel could offer was not allowed to change what He had appointed for drawing near to Him.

Isaiah 7: honey in a ruined land. "Curds and honey He shall eat, that He may know to refuse the evil and choose the good" (Isaiah 7:15). The context explains this diet. Invasion would leave the fields so empty of people that the few survivors would live on curds from a young cow and two sheep and on honey, with briars where vineyards once grew (Isaiah 7:21–25). The usual sign of plenty here becomes the food of a stripped land. Even so, those who remained were fed. God's judgment fell, and He still kept a remnant alive. The child named Immanuel would eat the same food, sharing the hardship of His people.

Ezekiel 16: God's honey set before idols. God describes Jerusalem as an abandoned infant whom He rescued, raised and adorned. He fed her fine flour, honey and oil (Ezekiel 16:13). Then comes His charge: the food He gave, the flour, oil and honey, she placed before idols as sweet incense (Ezekiel 16:19). The sin was not a lack of blessing. Jerusalem took God's own gifts and used them to honor false gods. Few passages show so plainly how grace can be turned against the Giver.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Israel tasted the promised land before entering it. In the wilderness, the manna tasted like wafers made with honey (Exodus 16:31). The same sweetness that marked their destination came to them each morning in the desert. Jesus calls Himself the true bread from heaven (John 6), and Paul calls the Spirit "the guarantee of our inheritance" (Ephesians 1:14). Through the Spirit, believers taste now what they will one day possess in full.

The honey from the rock in Deuteronomy 32:13 belongs to poetry about the land's abundance. It is a different scene from the wilderness rock that gave Israel water, of which Paul writes that Israel "drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them, and that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4). Even so, the two pictures sit well together. Sweetness drawn even from rocky country offers a fitting thematic picture of Christ as the Rock who sustains His people and brings life out of what seemed barren.

Samson's riddle offers a striking parallel to the gospel. "Out of the eater came something to eat, and out of the strong came something sweet" (see Judges 14:14). Scripture does not call this a picture of Christ, so the connection should be held loosely. Still, it fits the shape of the gospel. Death was the great eater, and from Christ's death and resurrection came the sweetest food of all. Isaiah's Immanuel ate the honey of a ruined land, and when Jesus came He entered fully into His people's need. John the Baptist, who prepared His way, "ate locusts and wild honey" (Mark 1:6). John lived on what God provided in the wilderness, apart from the comforts of Jerusalem, as he called the nation to repent.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 19 raises a searching question: is God's Word actually sweeter to you than the best things He gives? The land of milk and honey was God's gift, and David still ranked God's voice above it. Earthly honey has limits. Proverbs says too much of it brings sickness, and even good pleasures turn sour when they are made the center of life. Scripture never places that limit on God's words. A believer can enjoy food, friendship and married love as true gifts from God, while still receiving from God Himself the satisfaction those gifts cannot give.

Jonathan's brightened eyes offer hope to weary Christians. Saul's harsh rule had left his men faint, and a taste of honey revived Jonathan. Rules that depend on human effort often leave people exhausted. God's Word, received with trust, restores strength and clarifies vision. Ezekiel shows that this sweetness does not require easy news. A believer can receive a hard word from God and still find it good, because it is His word.

Ezekiel 16 calls for honest self-examination. Every gift, ability and comfort a believer has came from God. The danger is not only losing these gifts but offering them to something other than God. The safest response is to return thanks to the One who searched out the land and filled it with honey for His people.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 16:13–14. Dathan and Abiram call Egypt "a land flowing with milk and honey" and accuse Moses of failing to deliver one. Unbelief can turn God's promise upside down and make slavery seem like blessing.

  • Genesis 43:11. During a famine, Jacob sends a little honey from Canaan as a gift to Egypt's ruler. Even this small gift came from a land God would one day give his descendants.

  • 2 Samuel 17:29. Friends bring honey and other food to David and his weary people during Absalom's revolt. God supplied the fleeing king through the kindness of others.

  • Deuteronomy 8:8. Honey appears in Moses' list of the land's produce. That chapter warns Israel not to forget the Lord once they are full.

  • Jeremiah 41:8. Ten men save their lives by telling Ishmael about hidden stores of wheat, barley, oil and honey. The scene shows how highly such stores were valued in a time of collapse.

  • Job 20:17. Zophar says the wicked will never enjoy streams of honey and cream. Here the land's abundance is denied to those who reject God.
