FISH - (dâgâh, Strong's H1710)

Summary

The Hebrew word dâgâh simply means "fish," usually referring to fish as a group: the living creatures of seas and rivers. It has no hidden spiritual definition. Yet Scripture uses this plain word at turning points in its story. Fish appear when God creates the world and gives humanity rule over "the fish of the sea" (Genesis 1:26, 28). They die when the Nile turns to blood (Exodus 7:18, 21). Israel remembers them with longing in the wilderness (Numbers 11:5). They swarm in a once-dead sea healed by the river flowing from God's temple (Ezekiel 47:9–10). And Jonah prays to the Lord "from the fish's belly" (Jonah 2:1).

Followed through these passages, the fish become a visible measure of what God is doing. When He judges, the fish die and the waters stink. When His presence flows, life teems where nothing could live before. Their story points to Jesus Christ, who commands the fish of the sea with a word. He was in the heart of the earth three days as Jonah was in the fish. Life flows from Him to the dead places of the world.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dâgâh is a feminine Hebrew noun closely related to the masculine dâg, which also means "fish." The two forms carry the same meaning and can appear side by side in a single story. Jonah 1:17 uses dâg for the "great fish," and Jonah 2:1 uses dâgâh when Jonah prays from the fish's belly. A full biblical theology of fish would need both words. This study follows the fifteen uses of dâgâh, which appear in creation, the plagues on Egypt, the wilderness, the warnings of Moses, the Psalms, and the prophets.

In the ancient world the depths of the sea were mysterious and frightening, often linked with chaos. Israel's neighbors sometimes worshiped creatures and powers of the deep. That background helps explain why Scripture insists so firmly that the fish belong to God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Dâgâh means fish, usually fish collectively rather than one particular kind or a single animal. Jonah 2:1 is the exception, where it refers to the one great fish that swallowed the prophet. The word has one sense. Its theological weight comes from what God does with the waters and their creatures.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses are literal. In Genesis and Exodus the word names real creatures in the sea and the Nile. In Numbers 11:5 it is food, remembered by hungry people. In Deuteronomy 4:18 fish are a possible model for a carved idol.

The prophets add figurative use. In Ezekiel 29:4–5 "the fish of your rivers" clinging to Pharaoh's scales stand for the people bound to him. That figurative identification belongs to the prophecy and should not be read back into the literal fish of Genesis, Exodus, or Numbers. In Ezekiel 47 the fish are part of a vision, real creatures in a picture of restored life. The setting changes what the fish signify: provision, judgment, temptation, dependence, or abundance.

4. Theological Significance

The first use of dâgâh places the fish under human rule. When God says, "Let Us make man in Our image," the first creatures He names under human dominion are the fish of the sea (Genesis 1:26). Humans cannot live in the depths, and most of the sea lies beyond their sight. Even so, God's grant of authority reaches all the way down. Nothing in creation sits outside the order He established.

Moses guards that order in Deuteronomy 4:18. He forbids Israel to carve the likeness of any fish in the water beneath the earth. The fish was made to be ruled by God's image-bearers. Bowing to it would turn creation upside down. The Creator alone is to be worshiped, however mysterious the deep may seem.

God's power over the fish also shows up in judgment. When the Nile became blood, the fish died and the river stank, and the Egyptians could not drink from it (Exodus 7:18, 21). Psalm 105:29 compresses the event into a single line: "He turned their waters into blood, And killed their fish." Egypt's great river and its abundance were helpless before the Lord. Isaiah 50:2 recalls that same power when God asks whether His hand is too short to redeem. He can dry up the sea until its fish rot for lack of water. The God who can empty the waters can surely rescue His people.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 11:5 — The fish of Egypt. In the wilderness the people complain that they remember the fish they ate in Egypt at no cost, along with cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions, and garlic. They now have only manna. Their memory is strikingly selective. They may not have paid for that food, but the life that came with it was never free. Egypt had taken their labor, their dignity, and once even their infant sons. Hardship had distorted their memory until slavery looked like a banquet. Worse, the longing for Egypt's fish set itself against the bread God was sending from heaven every morning.

Ezekiel 29:4–5 — The fish clinging to Pharaoh. Ezekiel pictures Pharaoh as a great monster lying in his rivers. Pharaoh boasts that the Nile is his own, made by himself (29:3). God answers that He will put hooks in his jaws and pull him out. The fish of his rivers will stick to his scales. Then He will leave him in the wilderness, he and all his fish, as food for beasts and birds. Here the fish clearly stand for those who depend on Pharaoh: his people and the allies who trusted his strength. When the proud ruler falls, those clinging to him go down with him. Pride brings down whoever has staked their life on the proud.

Ezekiel 47:9–10 — Fish in the healed sea. In Ezekiel's temple vision, water flows from the sanctuary, grows into a river, and runs down into the Dead Sea. That is the saltiest, most lifeless water in the land. Wherever the river goes, things live. Ezekiel sees a very great multitude of fish. Fishermen stand along its shore, spreading their nets from En Gedi to En Eglaim. The fish are of many kinds, like those of the Great Sea. The proof that the sea is healed is not described as a change in color or taste. It is the fish. Fish depend completely on waters they cannot change, so their abundance is the visible sign of what God has done to the sea. The river comes from God's dwelling place, and where it flows, death gives way to abundant life.

Jonah 2:1 — Prayer from the fish's belly. "Now the LORD had prepared a great fish to swallow Jonah" (Jonah 1:17). Jonah was running from God, and the fish looks at first like judgment. But it kept him from drowning. Inside it, the prophet who had refused to speak for God finally speaks to Him: "Then Jonah prayed to the LORD his God from the fish's belly" (2:1). God appointed this creature from the dark sea to discipline His servant and to save him. It became a place of prayer, where Jonah had nothing left to rely on but the Lord.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Psalm 8 echoes Genesis when it marvels that God has put under man's feet "the fish of the sea That pass through the paths of the seas" (Psalm 8:8). Hebrews 2:8–9 admits the gap honestly: "we do not yet see all things put under him. But we see Jesus." The Gospels show what that rule looks like in a man. After a night of empty nets, Jesus tells Simon, "Launch out into the deep." The nets fill until they break, and Peter falls at His knees, crying, "Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord!" (Luke 5:4–8). Jesus also sends Peter to find a coin in a fish's mouth (Matthew 17:27). The dominion Adam failed to exercise, Jesus exercises with a word.

Jesus Himself names Jonah's three days in the fish as the sign of His own three days in the heart of the earth (Matthew 12:40). The fish that held Jonah in darkness and released him alive becomes a picture of death unable to hold the Son of God.

Ezekiel's river runs on into the New Testament. Jesus promises that rivers of living water will flow from the one who believes in Him, speaking of the Spirit (John 7:38–39). John sees "a pure river of water of life… proceeding from the throne of God and of the Lamb," with leaves "for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:1–2). Ezekiel's healed sea full of every kind of fish fits naturally with Jesus calling fishermen to become fishers of men (Matthew 4:19). It also fits His kingdom parable of a net gathering fish of every kind (Matthew 13:47), though in that parable the catch is then sorted and the bad thrown away, a picture of the separation at the end of the age (13:48–50). The New Testament does not cite Ezekiel 47 directly here, so this is a resonance between the passages rather than a stated fulfillment. Still, the picture is the same: life from God's presence and a great gathering from many peoples.

7. Spirit & Life

Numbers 11 exposes something every believer has felt. When obedience becomes hard, the old life suddenly looks generous. Memory edits out the chains and keeps the fish. Egypt's provision always came with Egypt's bondage, and the world's offers still do. Faith learns to remember honestly and to receive today's manna with thanks instead of grieving for a menu that came with slavery.

Read together, the waters of the Nile and the waters of Ezekiel 47 present a sober choice. Pharaoh's river, the source of his boasting, turned to blood and stank with dead fish. God's river turned a sea of death into a fishing ground. Whatever a person trusts as their Nile, God can dry it up. The life that comes from His presence reaches places that seemed beyond hope, including hearts as salty and barren as the Dead Sea.

Jonah's fish speaks to anyone who feels swallowed by circumstances they brought on themselves. The darkest place Jonah ever entered was something God had prepared, and it was where Jonah finally prayed. God's discipline holds His children so that He can bring them back.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:28 — God blesses the first humans and repeats their charge to rule over the fish of the sea. Their dominion is given as a blessing, not seized.

  • Exodus 7:18 — Moses announces beforehand that the fish in the river will die. The plague comes as God's spoken word, not as chance.

  • Exodus 7:21 — The fish died, the river stank, and Egypt could not drink. Judgment reached Egypt's daily water supply.

  • Psalm 105:29 — Israel's worship remembers the plague as God's own act on their behalf.

  • Isaiah 50:2 — Rotting fish in a dried-up sea answer the doubt that God's hand is too short to redeem.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings
