BANNERS, CHIEF - (dâgal, Strong's H1713)

Summary

The Hebrew verb dâgal means to lift up a banner, or to stand out the way a raised flag stands out above a crowd. It appears only four times in the Bible, all in poetry. In Psalm 20 God's people promise to "set up our banners" in His name. In the Song of Solomon the bride calls her beloved "Chief among ten thousand." Twice, in turn, she is praised as "awesome as an army with banners." Every use deals with being seen: openly declaring whom you belong to, or standing out in a way no one can miss.

These few verses carry real weight. Psalm 20 shows that raising a banner to God openly declares that victory comes from the Lord and not from human strength. The Song of Solomon moves from the beloved, who stands out from every rival, to the bride, who herself becomes a bannered host. A related noun describes Israel's tribes camped under their banners around God's tent in the wilderness. Read together, these passages describe a people who are seen in the world because they are gathered around the One who is supreme.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dâgal belongs to the same word family as the Hebrew noun degel. That noun is the word for the tribal "standards" in Numbers 1–2, where each tribe of Israel camped under its own flag. Armies in the ancient world used standards to mark rallying points and to identify who belonged to which company. The verb takes that public, identifying function and applies it to people. It appears only in Psalms and the Song of Solomon, so every use is poetic. All four uses describe something seen, recognized, and admired.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To raise a banner. This is the literal action of setting up a flag or standard, as in "we will set up our banners" (Psalm 20:5).

2. To be conspicuous or distinguished. This sense describes a person who stands out the way a raised banner stands out, rendered "Chief" in Song of Solomon 5:10.

3. To be arrayed under banners. This sense describes a company drawn up beneath its standards, rendered "an army with banners" in Song of Solomon 6:4 and 6:10.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's form changes its effect in noticeable ways. In Psalm 20:5 it is something people resolve to do: "we will set up our banners." The worshipers deliberately commit themselves in public. In Song of Solomon 5:10 the form describes a quality the beloved has rather than an action he performs. Literally he is "bannered," marked out as plainly as a standard above a crowd. The NKJV captures this with "Chief." In Song of Solomon 6:4 and 6:10 the form describes a whole company already drawn up under its standards. The word moves from an act of allegiance, to one person's distinction, to a gathered people's appearance. Interpreters debate the exact image in Song 6, and some read "bannered ones" as the stars of the night sky. Even so, the sense of an awe-inspiring host arrayed in order remains clear.

4. Theological Significance

Across its uses, dâgal shows that belonging to God is meant to be visible. Banners exist to be seen and to show identity. When God's people raise them "in the name of our God," they let everyone know where their hope rests.

The word also shows how worship and identity are related. The Song of Solomon calls the beloved "Chief" before it calls the bride "an army with banners." Her bannered majesty follows her praise of his. God's people are not made conspicuous by promoting themselves. They become noticeable when they are gathered around the One who is supreme, and their glory comes from His.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 20:5 — banners raised in God's name. Psalm 20 is a prayer for the king on the eve of battle. The people pray, and then they declare: "We will rejoice in your salvation, And in the name of our God we will set up our banners!" The banners are raised before the battle is fought, so they announce trust before there is any victory to see. Two verses later the psalm explains what kind of trust this is. Some trust in chariots and horses, but God's people will remember the name of the LORD their God (Psalm 20:7, paraphrased). Raising a banner could easily have been a soldier's boast in his own army. Here the banner bears God's name, and so it gives up any claim to human strength. Lifting that banner says openly that the outcome of the battle belongs to the Lord.

Song of Solomon 5:10 — the beloved who stands out. The bride's friends have asked what makes her beloved better than any other (5:9). She answers: "My beloved is white and ruddy, Chief among ten thousand." "Ten thousand" stands for a vast crowd. Her beloved does not simply rank highest among good men. He is "bannered," as easy to find as a standard lifted over a crowded field. Her praise in the following verses is detailed and personal, and it begins with this claim that he is unmistakable.

Song of Solomon 6:4, 10 — the bride as a bannered host. In chapter 6 the beloved speaks of her: "O my love, you are as beautiful as Tirzah, Lovely as Jerusalem, Awesome as an army with banners!" Tirzah and Jerusalem were royal cities. To this tender beauty he adds an image of military majesty, which is unexpected in a love poem. Then in 6:10 others ask, "Who is she who looks forth as the morning, Fair as the moon, Clear as the sun, Awesome as an army with banners?" Her beauty is radiant, but it is also formidable. It commands respect rather than mere admiration.

The family noun adds further depth here. In Numbers 2 God commanded each tribe to camp by its own standard around the tabernacle, the Tent of Meeting. That camp was an ordered people arranged around the dwelling place of God, with His presence at the center. Its banners showed who each tribe was and where it belonged in relation to Him. An "army with banners" brings that picture to mind. The bride's majesty is the majesty of a people rightly ordered around the One they love. The Song itself uses the same noun earlier, when the bride says of her beloved that his banner over her was love (Song 2:4). The banner that first covered her becomes the banner she is arrayed under.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Psalm 20 prays for God's anointed king. The Hebrew word for "anointed" in verse 6 is the root of the title Messiah, so the banners in the psalm are raised in anticipation of the anointed King's victory. That pattern reaches its fullness in Jesus, the Christ. His people rejoice in a salvation He has won and that they did not earn.

Christians have long read the Song of Solomon as a picture of Christ and His people. The New Testament supports that reading in principle when it calls the church Christ's bride (Ephesians 5:25–32). In that light, "Chief among ten thousand" speaks of Christ's uniqueness. Jesus said, "And I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all peoples to Myself" (John 12:32). He is the One lifted up whom people are drawn toward, much as people gather to a raised standard. The church, gathered around Him the way Israel camped around the tabernacle, becomes an "army with banners." Her glory is borrowed from the One at her center.

7. Spirit & Life

The order of these passages matters. The bride praises her beloved as "bannered" before she is ever called bannered herself. A believer does not become spiritually formidable by working at an impressive identity. Our attention belongs on Jesus, who stands out above every rival. When He holds that place, His people take on a dignity and strength they could never produce themselves.

Psalm 20 turns this into a practical choice. Every believer meets situations where it is tempting to trust "chariots": money, influence, skill, or careful planning. Raising a banner in the name of our God means openly naming where your confidence rests, even before the outcome is known. That public trust is itself worship.

Song 6 also corrects a thin picture of the church. The people Christ loves are tender and beautiful, but they are not fragile. A church gathered in order around His presence carries an "awesome" quality that the world notices and darkness cannot dismiss. The two images belong together: her beauty is the source of her strength.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All four occurrences of dâgal are discussed above: Psalm 20:5 and Song of Solomon 5:10, 6:4, and 6:10. Because the word is so rare, no additional uses remain to survey.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H1714 (degel), lexical relative:** the noun "standard" or "banner," from the same word family. It is used for the tribal standards arranged around the tabernacle in Numbers 2 and for the bride's words about her beloved's banner over her in Song of Solomon 2:4.

  • Thematic connection: In Exodus 17:15 Moses names an altar "The-LORD-Is-My-Banner." This verse uses a different Hebrew word for banner, but it develops the same thought found in Psalm 20, that God Himself is His people's rallying point and the source of their victory.
