STANDARD, BANNER - (degel, Strong's H1714)

Summary

The Hebrew word degel means a standard: a flag or banner raised over a division of people so that everyone could see where they belonged and whom they followed. Almost every use comes from Numbers, where God arranged the twelve tribes of Israel in the wilderness. Each tribe camped "by his own standard." Four great camps faced the tabernacle from four sides, and when the cloud lifted, those camps set out in an order God Himself appointed. The standard turned a crowd of freed slaves into an ordered people. It gave every family a place, pointed them toward the presence of God, and kept them together on the march.

The word appears only once outside Numbers, and that use is unexpected. In the Song of Solomon the bride says of the king, "He brought me to the banqueting house, And his banner over me was love" (Song 2:4). An army's emblem becomes the sign raised over a beloved bride. For believers, the two settings belong together. God's people are a pilgrim host, ordered and led through a hostile world behind the One from the tribe of Judah. The banner that finally stands over them, claiming them and gathering them, is the love of their King.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Degel is a Hebrew noun for a military or divisional standard. Many scholars connect it with an Akkadian word related to seeing. That connection fits what the word describes, an object raised high so that people can look at it and gather around it.

The setting is almost entirely Israel's wilderness camp. Numbers 1, 2, and 10 use the word thirteen times as Moses records how the nation was to camp and march. The fourteenth use, in Song of Solomon 2:4, carries the word into love poetry.

Another Hebrew word, nes (H5251), is also translated "banner." It usually describes a signal pole set up on a height to rally people from far away or to sound an alarm. Moses named his altar "The-LORD-Is-My-Banner" with that word after the victory over Amalek (Exodus 17:15). Degel is the more local, organizing standard. It marks where a company of tribes belongs within the camp and in the line of march.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A standard or banner that marks and gathers a tribal division of Israel's camp. In Numbers 2 each of the four great camps, made up of three tribes, has its own degel. In phrases such as "by their standards" the word can point to the division gathered under the flag as well as to the flag itself (Numbers 2:34).

2. A banner used figuratively, a visible sign raised over someone that declares a claim and a relationship: "his banner over me was love" (Song 2:4).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Numbers 1–2 the standard governs rest. God tells Moses that Israel "shall pitch their tents, everyone by his own camp, everyone by his own standard, according to their armies" (Numbers 1:52). Judah, Reuben, Ephraim, and Dan each lead a camp on the east, south, west, and north (Numbers 2:3, 10, 18, 25). The Levites camp in the middle around the tabernacle.

In Numbers 10 the same standards govern movement. The camps set out one after another in the order already assigned. Numbers 2:17 ties the two together: Israel was to move out the same way it camped, each one in his place by the standards.

Song 2:4 keeps the basic idea of a raised, visible sign over a person, but the setting is a banquet, and what the sign declares is love.

4. Theological Significance

The standards show that God forms His people into an ordered community and gives each member a place. Israel did not sort itself out. God assigned every position, down to which side of the tabernacle each camp would face. Paul states the principle behind it: "For God is not the author of confusion but of peace" (1 Corinthians 14:33). Order in God's camp comes from His own character.

The arrangement also centered everything on God's presence. The four camps did not face one another. They faced the tabernacle, and they pitched "some distance" from it, with the Levites between them and the holy tent (Numbers 2:2). The standards therefore expressed two things at once. God lived at the center of His people, and He was holy, so His people approached Him in reverence and in the way He set.

The standards also gave Israel an identity. The phrase "according to their armies" (Numbers 1:52) recalls the night of the Exodus, when "all the armies of the LORD went out from the land of Egypt" (Exodus 12:41). Only months earlier these people had been brick-makers under Pharaoh. Under the standards they became the LORD's host, ready for a dangerous wilderness and the conquest ahead.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 1:52 and 2:2: camping by one's own standard. The command is personal: "everyone by his own standard." No Israelite was left to wander between camps or choose a spot by preference. Numbers 2:2 adds that each man camped "beside the emblems of his father's house." Those emblems are a different Hebrew word, the markers of the families inside each tribe. So every Israelite knew his place at several levels: his family, his tribe, his camp, and the tabernacle at the center of the whole nation. When an Israelite pitched his tent under his standard, he was obeying God. He accepted the place God had given him among God's people.

Numbers 10:14–25: the standards in motion. Numbers 9 sets the scene. The cloud stood over the tabernacle, and "at the command of the LORD they remained encamped, and at the command of the LORD they journeyed" (Numbers 9:23). Whether the cloud stayed two days, a month, or a year, Israel waited (Numbers 9:22). When the cloud lifted, the standards turned God's direction into an orderly march. "The standard of the camp of the children of Judah set out first according to their armies" (Numbers 10:14). The Gershonites and Merarites followed with the tabernacle structure. Reuben came next (10:18), then the Kohathites carrying the holy things, then Ephraim (10:22). Dan marched last as "the rear guard of all the camps" (10:25). The cloud told Israel when to move, and the standards told each person where to walk. The holy things travelled near the middle of the column, so God's presence was still at the center of His people on the road.

Song of Solomon 2:4: the banner of love. This is the word's most striking use. The bride says, "He brought me to the banqueting house, And his banner over me was love." In Numbers a degel told a soldier where he belonged and whose command he was under. The bride uses that same word for what the king has raised over her. His public claim on her, the sign everyone can see that she is his, is love. The setting adds to the force of the image. She is not in a war camp but at a feast he prepared and brought her into. A banner of this kind spoke of celebration and possession together. It said she belonged to him, and it was a matter of joy. She did not raise the banner herself. He brought her in, and the banner over her is his.

The Song later describes the bride as "Awesome as an army with banners!" (Song 6:4; also 6:10). The Hebrew there is a related form from the same root. The sight of an ordered army with its standards raised was one of the most stirring sights of the ancient world, and the poet compares the bride's beauty to that sight. The language of the camp and the language of love meet in this book.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The standard of Judah led the way. Judah camped on the east, "toward the rising of the sun" (Numbers 2:3), on the side of the tabernacle's entrance, and Judah's standard "set out first" (Numbers 10:14). Its leader, Nahshon the son of Amminadab, appears in the family line of David and in Matthew's genealogy of Jesus (Ruth 4:20; Matthew 1:4). Numbers does not draw out this connection. Still, it fits the promise that "the scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Genesis 49:10), and it points ahead to the moment in Revelation when the elder announces, "Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has prevailed" (Revelation 5:5). God's people still march behind the King from Judah.

The Song of Solomon is first of all a love poem between a bride and her groom. Ephesians 5 gives good reason to hear in it the greater love it reflects. "Christ also loved the church and gave Himself for her" in order to "present her to Himself a glorious church" (Ephesians 5:25, 27). The banner over the church is the love Christ proved at the cross. The same people who march as His army are also His bride, brought into His house and claimed in front of everyone.

7. Spirit & Life

A believer's place among God's people is given, not invented. Every Israelite had a standard to camp by, and none was told to find a place wherever he could. Belonging to Christ means belonging to His people, in fellowship and with a part to play. The standards also kept everyone facing the tabernacle. Our sense of who we are depends most of all on whether we are turned toward God's presence. Tribe, background, or church tradition come second.

Numbers 9–10 shows that following God involves both waiting and walking. Israel stayed put while the cloud stayed, even for a year, and moved as soon as it lifted. The church needs both kinds of obedience. Believers do not march alone or rush ahead. We move together, each in our place, behind the One God has set at the front.

Song 2:4 shows what stands over all of this. Christians do face real warfare (Ephesians 6:10–12), and the wilderness is real. Yet the banner over the believer is not finally a record of our discipline or strength. It is the love of the King who brought us to His table. Israel sang, "In the name of our God we will set up our banners!" (Psalm 20:5). We can lift Christ's name and His love the same way. His love tells us where we belong, and it marks us as His in the sight of everyone.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 2:10 — Reuben's standard leads the camp on the south side. This was the firstborn's tribe, placed second behind Judah.

  • Numbers 2:17 — The tabernacle and the Levites move in the middle of the camps, each tribe "by their standards." God's dwelling stays at the center even on the march.

  • Numbers 2:31 — Dan's camp, the largest after Judah's, breaks camp last "with their standards." That placement fits Dan's role as rear guard in Numbers 10:25.

  • Numbers 2:34 — Israel did "according to all that the LORD commanded Moses," camping and setting out by their standards. The whole arrangement ends in a note of obedience.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H5251 (nes)** — A distinct word also translated "banner" or "standard," usually a signal pole raised on a height to rally people from far away (Exodus 17:15). It is a thematic relative of degel rather than a word from the same family.

  • Related form from the same root — A related Hebrew form describes an army "with banners" in Song 6:4, 10, and the act of setting up banners in Psalm 20:5. It extends the image of raised standards into praise and poetry.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Numbers 2 arranges Israel as four camps on four sides around the dwelling of God. Ezekiel and Revelation describe four living creatures around God's throne, with the faces of a lion, an ox, a man, and an eagle (Ezekiel 1:10; Revelation 4:7). Later Jewish tradition assigned these four faces to Israel's four standards. Scripture never says what the standards looked like, so that detail should not be taught as fact. The parallel in arrangement is still worth noticing. In both scenes, four companies are gathered around the glory of God, and the camp on earth reflects in a small way the order around His throne in heaven.
