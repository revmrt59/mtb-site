DODANIM - (Dôdânîym, Strong's H1721)

Summary

Dodanim is the name of a people descended from Javan, the son of Japheth and grandson of Noah. It appears only in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:4) and in its later restatement in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 1:7). The meaning of the name is not established. Most Hebrew scholars regard "Dodanim" as a variant spelling of "Rodanim," because the Hebrew letters d (ד) and r (ר) look almost alike and were easily confused in copying. The NKJV reads "Rodanim" in 1 Chronicles 1:7, and the ancient Greek translation renders the name "Rhodians." Both point to the people of the island of Rhodes and the nearby Aegean coastlands. This identification is probable, though not certain.

The Dodanim never take part in any biblical story, but their place in the record still matters. Genesis lists them among the families from whom "the coastland peoples of the Gentiles were separated into their lands" (Genesis 10:5). The list comes just before God calls Abram and promises that in him all the families of the earth will be blessed (Genesis 12:3). Distant island peoples whom Israel would barely have known are named in God's record of the nations. Later Scripture announces that the coastlands will wait for God's Servant, a promise the New Testament applies to Jesus.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Dodanim is a people group, not an individual. The Hebrew ending -im is plural, so the name describes the descendants of an ancestor rather than a single son. Genesis 10:4 lists them as the fourth of Javan's sons: "The sons of Javan were Elishah, Tarshish, Kittim, and Dodanim." Javan is a son of Japheth (Genesis 10:2), and his name is generally connected with the Ionian Greeks. Kittim is commonly identified with Cyprus. Placing Dodanim beside these names points toward the islands and coasts of the northeastern Mediterranean.

The meaning of the name is not established. The main question about this name concerns its spelling. In the square Hebrew script, daleth (ד) and resh (ר) differ by only a small stroke, so Dodanim (דֹּדָנִים) and Rodanim (רֹדָנִים) could easily be confused in copying. The NKJV reflects the "r" reading in 1 Chronicles 1:7: "The sons of Javan were Elishah, Tarshishah, Kittim, and Rodanim." The Samaritan Pentateuch reportedly reads Rodanim in Genesis 10:4 as well. The Septuagint, the Greek translation made by Jews in Alexandria before Christ, renders the name "Rhodians." For these reasons, most interpreters take the original form to be Rodanim, meaning the people of Rhodes and nearby islands.

Some historical geographers who keep the "d" reading have proposed the Dardanians of Asia Minor or the Danunians of Cilicia. The context of the "coastland peoples" in Genesis 10:5 and the early Greek witness favor Rhodes. Rodanim is the spelling variant treated together with Dodanim under this same entry (H1721), so it is best understood as a form of the same name rather than a different people.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Dodanim have no recorded deeds. They appear in Genesis 10, where Noah's descendants spread across the earth after the flood. Japheth's line extends north and west toward Asia Minor and the Mediterranean, and Genesis 10:5 summarizes Javan's branch this way: "From these the coastland peoples of the Gentiles were separated into their lands, everyone according to his language, according to their families, into their nations." Centuries later, the Chronicler opened his history of Israel with the same table of nations (1 Chronicles 1:7). That choice placed Israel's story within the whole human family before narrowing to Abraham, David, and the returning exiles.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states that the Dodanim belong to Javan's line and to the coastland peoples (Genesis 10:4–5). Two further points follow from where the list sits in Scripture. First, Genesis records the nations before it records the call of Abram (Genesis 12:1–3). When God promises blessing to "all the families of the earth," the reader has just seen many of those families named, including this remote seafaring people. Israel's calling was God's way of reaching them, not a reason to forget them.

Second, the Chronicler's repetition of the list after the exile reminded a small, recovering nation that the God who chose them also governs the boundaries of every people. Paul makes a related point in Acts 14:16, where he speaks of God, "who in bygone generations allowed all nations to walk in their own ways," and yet "did not leave Himself without witness" (Acts 14:17). The Dodanim lived among the nations outside Israel's covenant community, and Scripture records nothing of what they knew of God, but they were never outside His notice.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Dodanim is not named in prophecy, but the "coastlands" it represents become an important theme in later Scripture. Isaiah announces that the coastlands will wait for the law of God's Servant (Isaiah 42:4). Matthew explicitly applies that Servant passage to Jesus, concluding that in His name the Gentiles will trust (Matthew 12:18–21). The distant maritime nations first named in Genesis 10 thus become, through Isaiah and Matthew, part of the people Christ came to gather.

The New Testament also brings Rhodes itself into view. On his final voyage toward Jerusalem, Luke writes, "we came to Cos, the following day to Rhodes, and from there to Patara" (Acts 21:1). Luke records no preaching on Rhodes, so this verse should not be presented as the conversion of the Rodanim. Still, it is fitting that the apostle to the Gentiles sailed past the very coastlands Genesis named, carrying a gospel already spreading through the Aegean world. This connection is a thematic resonance, not an explicit fulfillment. It traces how Abraham's promise moved outward toward the families listed before his call.

5. Spirit & Life

A name with no story can seem like dead weight in a genealogy. Yet God had Moses write down a seafaring people at the far edge of Israel's world, and He had the Chronicler copy it again centuries later. Scripture does not tell us what the Dodanim knew of the Lord, yet they had a place in His record. Nations and people who seem distant to us are not distant to Him. The gospel's reach to "the coastlands" was planned from the beginning, and that should give us confidence when we pray for and witness to people far removed from the church.

The small spelling question behind this name also deserves a calm word. God gave His Word through real writers and preserved it through real copyists. A d and an r that look alike in Hebrew do not change what this passage teaches: God sees, names, and remembers the nations.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:4 – The primary occurrence; Dodanim among the sons of Javan.

  • Genesis 10:5 – Places Javan's descendants among "the coastland peoples of the Gentiles."

  • 1 Chronicles 1:7 – The Chronicler's restatement, read "Rodanim" in the NKJV, supporting the Rhodes identification.

  • Genesis 12:3 – God's promise of blessing to all the families of the earth, set right after the Table of Nations.

  • Isaiah 42:4; Matthew 12:18–21 – The coastlands waiting for the Servant, applied by Matthew to Jesus.

  • Acts 14:16–17 – God's patience with the nations and His continued witness among them.

  • Acts 21:1 – Paul's voyage past Rhodes, the probable homeland of this people.
