BELOVED, LOVE, UNCLE - (dôwd, Strong's H1730)

Summary

The Hebrew word dôwd names someone dear and close. Most often it is the bride's name for the man she loves in the Song of Solomon: "my beloved." In the plural it means love itself, especially love expressed in affection and intimacy: "For your love is better than wine" (Song of Solomon 1:2). The same word also names an uncle or near male relative. In Israel's law, that relative could step in to buy back a family member who had been forced by poverty to sell himself (Leviticus 25:47–49).

The prophets used the same word for God's relationship with His people. Isaiah sings of "my Well-beloved" and the vineyard He planted with such care (Isaiah 5:1). Ezekiel remembers the "time of love" when the Lord took Jerusalem as His own, and then uses the same word for her unfaithfulness. In the Song, the bride's confidence grows. At first she says, "My beloved is mine," and in the end she rests in the knowledge that she belongs to him and that his desire is toward her. Both strands of the word, the beloved who delights and the kinsman who redeems, point to Jesus Christ. He is the Father's beloved Son, the Bridegroom of His people, and the Redeemer who restores what they lost.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dôwd is a Hebrew word that appears only in the Old Testament. It occurs 61 times in 53 verses of the Hebrew Bible across its beloved, love and male-relative senses; 39 of the occurrences are in the Song of Solomon, so the word's main home is love poetry. The kinship sense appears in law and history (Leviticus, Numbers, 1 Samuel, 2 Kings, 1 Chronicles, Esther), in Jeremiah's land purchase, and once in Amos. Isaiah, Ezekiel, and Proverbs use the romantic sense.

Two related Hebrew words are worth knowing. The name David is commonly connected with this word family and is usually understood to mean beloved. The word for mandrakes, a plant associated with love and fertility (Genesis 30:14–16), sounds very much like dôwd. The Song of Solomon plays on that similarity in sound. These connections add color, but each occurrence takes its meaning from its own context.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Beloved, loved one. This is the person someone loves, most often the bridegroom in the Song: "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (Song of Solomon 2:16). Isaiah applies the same title to the Lord (Isaiah 5:1).

2. Love, lovemaking. In the plural, the word means the affection and intimacy shared between lovers: "your love is better than wine" (Song of Solomon 1:2). The same form can describe corrupt love (Proverbs 7:18).

3. Uncle, close relative. This is a father's brother or another near male relative, as with Saul's uncle (1 Samuel 10:14) or Jeremiah's uncle Shallum (Jeremiah 32:7). Sometimes the relationship carries a duty to redeem (Leviticus 25:49).

The passages keep these senses separate, and the surrounding words make clear which one is meant.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Song, the word most often comes with "my": my beloved. It is the language of a relationship that someone claims and that claims her in return. The daughters of Jerusalem ask the bride what makes her beloved better than any other (Song of Solomon 5:9). She answers with a long description of him and finishes by calling him both her beloved and her friend (Song of Solomon 5:10–16).

The plural, translated "love," refers to love expressed between people. The word itself is morally neutral. The relationship around it decides whether the love is faithful or false. The groom praises the bride's love as better than wine (Song of Solomon 4:10). The adulteress in Proverbs uses the same word as bait: "Come, let us take our fill of love until morning" (Proverbs 7:18).

The kinship sense is often simple identification. For example, Abner's father Ner was Saul's uncle (1 Samuel 14:50–51). In other places, the word carries legal weight, because an uncle was among the relatives who had the right to redeem.

4. Theological Significance

The prophets' use of this word is telling. God could have described His tie to Israel only as that of a king and his subjects. Instead, Isaiah and Ezekiel used the same vocabulary of love that fills the Song. Their choice shows that God wants His people to delight in Him and know Him personally. Obedience matters, but it is not the whole of what He seeks.

A love that gives this much also expects fruit, and it grieves when the fruit does not come. That is why the same word appears in both promise and judgment.

Amos shows what remains when that love is refused. In the coming devastation, "a relative of the dead" comes to carry the bodies out of a house. He calls to someone still inside, "Are there any more with you?" The answer is "None." Then comes the command, "Hold your tongue! For we dare not mention the name of the LORD" (Amos 6:10). In the law, a relative like this was among those who could buy back what was lost. In Amos, under judgment, all he can do is carry out the dead, and no one dares even to speak God's name. Family ties cannot rescue anyone from what only God can redeem.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The bride's three declarations (Song of Solomon 2:16; 6:3; 7:10). The bride's words about her beloved change as the Song moves forward, and the change is worth tracing.

  • Early on she says, "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (Song of Solomon 2:16). Her claim on him comes first.

  • After a night when he came and she was slow to open the door, he was gone. She searched the city for him (Song of Solomon 5:2–8), and then her words change. In Song of Solomon 6:3 she reverses the order: first she says that she belongs to her beloved, and only then that he is hers.

  • Finally, in Song of Solomon 7:10, her claim on him drops away entirely. She says that she is her beloved's and that his desire is toward her. She now rests in his desire for her.

The Song celebrates real marital love, and that should be honored. Read alongside the prophets' use of the same word, though, the progression gives a fitting picture of growing faith. Many believers begin by rejoicing that Christ is theirs. Over time they learn to rest in the deeper and steadier truth that they are His and that He wants them.

The Beloved's vineyard (Isaiah 5:1–7). Isaiah opens like a love song: "Now let me sing to my Well-beloved / A song of my Beloved regarding His vineyard." Then he lists everything the Beloved did for that vineyard. "He dug it up and cleared out its stones, / And planted it with the choicest vine," He built a tower, and He made a winepress. Yet "it brought forth wild grapes." The Lord Himself then asks, "What more could have been done to My vineyard / That I have not done in it?" The opening words of tenderness make the ending more painful: "He looked for justice, but behold, oppression." The song is the grief of a lover whose care has been betrayed.

The time of love and the bed of love (Ezekiel 16:8; 23:17). Ezekiel remembers that God found Jerusalem abandoned and helpless. "Your time was the time of love; so I spread My wing over you and covered your nakedness... and you became Mine." Here the love comes entirely from God's side. Jerusalem had nothing to offer Him. Later, Ezekiel uses the same word for the "bed of love" that Jerusalem shared with the Babylonians (Ezekiel 23:17). The capacity for devotion God awakened in His people did not disappear when they turned from Him. They gave it to foreign nations and their idols, and it left them defiled (Ezekiel 23:17, 30).

The uncle's field (Jeremiah 32:7–12; Leviticus 25:49). The law named an uncle among the relatives who could redeem a family member who had sold himself into service (Leviticus 25:49). In Jeremiah's day, Babylon was besieging Jerusalem, and Jeremiah himself was confined in the court of the prison. Then the LORD told him that his cousin would come with a request: "Hanamel the son of Shallum your uncle will come to you, saying, 'Buy my field which is in Anathoth, for the right of redemption is yours to buy it'" (Jeremiah 32:7). Hanamel came just as the LORD had said (Jeremiah 32:8), and Jeremiah weighed out "seventeen shekels of silver," signed and sealed the deed, and gave it to Baruch before witnesses (Jeremiah 32:9–12). By any ordinary measure, buying land in a country about to fall was foolish. God made the purchase a promise that houses, fields, and vineyards would again be bought in the land (Jeremiah 32:15). The family's right of redemption became a sign of hope that the nation itself would be restored.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus told a parable that deliberately echoes Isaiah's song. A man planted a vineyard, "set a hedge around it, dug a place for the wine vat and built a tower" (Mark 12:1). Mark writes in Greek, so the Hebrew word itself does not appear there. The image, however, is Isaiah's. The owner sends servants, and the tenants beat them or kill them. "Therefore still having one son, his beloved, he also sent him to them last" (Mark 12:6). The tenants killed him and cast him out of the vineyard. Isaiah grieved that the vineyard yielded wild grapes. Jesus reveals how far the rejection would go: the vineyard would kill the Son.

The name David adds another thread to the story. The prophets promised that God would set over His people "My servant David" as their shepherd (Ezekiel 34:23), and that in the latter days Israel would seek "David their king" (Hosea 3:5). At the Jordan the Father declared, "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:17). The Greek word for "beloved" there is a different word, so this is a thematic echo, not a fulfillment the text states outright. Still, it is a fitting echo: the promised son of David arrives as the Father's Beloved.

The two senses of dôwd also come together in Christ. Like the redeeming relative of Leviticus 25 and Jeremiah 32, He became our kin by sharing our flesh, and He buys back an inheritance we could never recover on our own. Like the beloved of the Song, He seeks His people for a marriage. Paul calls the union of husband and wife a great mystery that speaks of Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:32).

7. Spirit & Life

The bride's journey corrects a common anxiety. Many Christians measure their security by how firmly they feel they hold on to Christ. The Song moves the weight to the other side. Her final confidence rests on his desire for her rather than on the strength of her own hold on him. Even her failure in chapter 5, when she was slow to rise and open the door, became the occasion for a deeper search and a fuller surrender.

The Song also ties love to fruitfulness. In the vineyards the bride says, "There I will give you my love," and in the next breath, "The mandrakes give off a fragrance" (Song of Solomon 7:12–13). In Hebrew, "love" and "mandrakes" sound alike, and mandrakes were linked with fruitfulness. The poet's wordplay pictures what the rest of Scripture affirms: love for the true Beloved bears fruit and fragrance. Isaiah's wild grapes show what grows when a people's heart is given elsewhere.

Ezekiel's account of Jerusalem suggests a sober warning. The longing for intimacy in every human heart will be spent somewhere. If it is not given to God, it will be given to something that cannot keep it safe.

The Song itself ends in longing, with the bride calling her beloved to make haste (Song of Solomon 8:14). The church still lives in that posture of waiting, loved and belonging to Him, while it watches for His coming.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 10:4. After the deaths of Nadab and Abihu, Moses calls the sons of Uzziel, Aaron's uncle, to carry the bodies out of the camp. Kinsmen bearing the dead under God's judgment offers a grim parallel to the scene in Amos 6:10.

  • Numbers 36:11. The daughters of Zelophehad married the sons of their father's brothers. These marriages kept the family inheritance within their tribe.

  • 1 Samuel 10:14–16. Saul's uncle presses him about what Samuel said. Saul tells him only about the donkeys, "but about the matter of the kingdom, he did not tell him what Samuel had said."

  • Esther 2:7, 15. Mordecai raised Esther, "his uncle's daughter," as his own. The book never names God, but through this family bond the young woman who would help preserve God's people was already in place.

  • 2 Kings 24:17. The king of Babylon made Mattaniah, Jehoiachin's uncle, king and renamed him Zedekiah. Zedekiah was the last king of Judah before Jerusalem fell.
