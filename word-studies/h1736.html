MANDRAKES - (dûwday, Strong's H1736)

Summary

The Hebrew word dûwday names the mandrake, a low Mediterranean plant with fragrant yellow fruit and a thick, forked root. People in the ancient world prized it as a love charm and a help for childbearing, and its name sounds like the Hebrew word for love. It occurs 6 times in 4 verses of the Hebrew Bible, all naming mandrakes and found in two places. In Genesis 30, Rachel and Leah bargain over them. In Song of Solomon 7:13, their fragrance fills the air of a bride's invitation to her husband. Jeremiah 24:1 uses a related, separately indexed word (H1731), which older numbering listed with this one, for something different: two baskets of figs set before the temple.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

These passages carry real weight. In Genesis, the mandrakes expose a household where love has been turned into trade. Rachel trades a night with her husband for plants she apparently hopes will make her fruitful. Leah conceives instead, and Rachel's womb stays closed until God Himself remembers her. In the Song, the same plant belongs to love that is secure and freely given. In Jeremiah, ordinary baskets, named with the related word, hold a message of judgment and a promise that God will give His exiled people "a heart to know Me." In every setting, the object matters less than the God who gives life and changes hearts.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The mandrake (Mandragora officinarum) grows close to the ground in Israel and around the Mediterranean. Its small yellow-orange fruit ripens in late spring, which fits Reuben's finding them "in the days of wheat harvest" (Genesis 30:14). Its root can look strangely like a human figure, and the plant has narcotic properties. Together, these traits made it a common folk remedy for barrenness and a reputed stimulant to desire. That reputation likely explains why Rachel wanted Reuben's find so badly.

The Hebrew name dûwday resembles dôd, the word for love or beloved, and many readers have called mandrakes "love-apples." The resemblance shows what people associated with the plant. It does not prove the plant had any power.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Dûwday names the mandrake. A separately indexed basket word has also been associated with this entry in older numbering:

1. Mandrake, the plant and its fragrant fruit. It is used in Genesis 30:14–16 and Song of Solomon 7:13.

Related word: basket, a container for produce, in Jeremiah 24:1, for the "two baskets of figs." Older Strong's numbering listed this use under H1736, but current STEPBible data tags it as the separately indexed dûd (H1731, pot or basket), so it is not counted among this word's occurrences. Lexicons connect it with dûd and do not treat it as a development of the plant's meaning.

The word itself does not mean fertility, romance, or judgment. It names a plant; the related basket word names a container. Scripture uses these objects within larger stories.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Genesis, the mandrakes are something to own and trade. Leah says "my son's mandrakes" twice (30:15–16). Rachel asks for "some of your son's mandrakes" (30:14) and then sets a price for them. In the Song, nobody owns the mandrakes. They simply "give off a fragrance" in a spring landscape of budding vines and blooming pomegranates (7:12–13). In Jeremiah, the baskets play no part in the message. God interprets the figs, and the baskets only hold them.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these passages show that God is the giver of life. People in the ancient world looked to the mandrake to produce children, and Genesis shows it doing nothing. Jacob had already said so to Rachel: "Am I in the place of God, who has withheld from you the fruit of the womb?" (Genesis 30:2). The psalmist states the same truth: "children are a heritage from the LORD" (Psalm 127:3).

The passages also show God's grace at work in damaged relationships. God does not wait for Jacob's household to become healthy before He acts in it. He saw that Leah was "unloved" and opened her womb (Genesis 29:31). Later He remembered Rachel. From this bitter rivalry came the tribes of Israel.

Finally, Jeremiah's baskets show that God distinguishes between people and that He changes hearts. He sets the exiles apart from those who remained in Jerusalem or fled to Egypt, calling the exiles good figs and the others bad (24:5, 8), and promises to make the good figs into a people who know Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 30:14–18: Love Turned into Trade

The mandrake scene follows Rachel's desperate cry, "Give me children, or else I die!" (30:1). When Reuben brings mandrakes to his mother, Rachel asks for some, apparently seeing a remedy she can get her hands on. Leah's answer reveals years of hurt: "Is it a small matter that you have taken away my husband? Would you take away my son's mandrakes also?" (30:15). Rachel then names a price: "he will lie with you tonight for your son's mandrakes." That evening, as Jacob comes in from the field, Leah goes out to meet him with words that should make any reader wince: "I have surely hired you with my son's mandrakes" (30:16). In this exchange, people are treated like goods for trade. Jacob is rented out, the marriage bed is bought, and a child's gift becomes currency.

Then comes the irony. The narrator writes, "God listened to Leah, and she conceived" (30:17). The woman who gave the mandrakes away conceives. The woman who received them remains barren while Leah bears two more sons and a daughter. Only later does the text say that God remembered Rachel, listened to her, and opened her womb (30:22). The narrative gives the mandrakes no credit at all. Rachel's son came from God's remembering, not from a root dug out of a wheat field.

Leah's naming of her fifth son sharpens the point. She calls him Issachar, a name that sounds like the Hebrew for wages, and says, "God has given me my wages" (30:18). The same idea of hiring that Leah used in verse 16 now appears in the child's name. Yet the "wages" came from God, who had listened.

Song of Solomon 7:10–13: Love Freely Given

The Song uses the same plant in a completely different setting. The bride begins with security: "I am my beloved's, and his desire is toward me" (7:10). She invites him out to the fields and vineyards and promises, "There I will give you my love" (7:12). Then, "The mandrakes give off a fragrance," with "pleasant fruits, all manner, new and old, which I have laid up for you" (7:13). In Hebrew, the words for "my love" and "mandrakes" sound alike and stand side by side. The poet seems to be playing on the sound deliberately.

Genesis showed the mandrake claimed, bargained over, and bought. The Song shows it giving off fragrance freely in an open field. Love in the Song is offered, and nobody needs to purchase it. The bride has stored up her gifts for her husband, and nothing in the scene is anxious or transactional.

Related Word H1731 — Jeremiah 24:1–7: Ordinary Baskets, Weighty Promise

The basket word here is the related form H1731, which older numbering listed with this word. After Nebuchadnezzar carried Jeconiah and the leaders of Judah to Babylon, God showed Jeremiah "two baskets of figs set before the temple of the LORD" (24:1). One basket held "very good figs," and the other held figs "so bad" they could not be eaten (24:2). The surprise lies in which group God calls good. The good figs are the exiles, the people already carried away, and God says He sent them out "for their own good" (24:5). He promises, "I will set My eyes on them for good... I will build them and not pull them down" (24:6). The promise reaches its height in verse 7: "I will give them a heart to know Me... for they shall return to Me with their whole heart." The baskets are ordinary objects, yet they stand before God's house holding His verdict and His promise.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Genesis 30 is part of a larger story about God bringing the promised line into being in ways nobody could control. Leah, the unloved wife, bore Judah (Genesis 29:35), and from Judah came David and, in time, Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:2–3). The Messiah's family tree runs through the woman who gave the mandrakes away.

Rachel's story echoes later in Scripture. Jeremiah pictures Rachel weeping for her lost children (Jeremiah 31:15), and Matthew applies those words to the grief in Bethlehem at Jesus' birth (Matthew 2:18). The woman who once cried "Give me children" becomes a picture of Israel's sorrow, and that sorrow meets its answer in Christ.

Jeremiah's promise of "a heart to know Me" (24:7) grows in the same book into the promise of a new covenant, with the law written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31–34). Jesus announced that covenant in His blood (Luke 22:20). What Jeremiah's vision promised, the gospel accomplishes.

The Song's secure, freely given love also resonates with Paul's teaching that marriage reflects Christ's self-giving love for the church (Ephesians 5:25–32). The Song does not state this as prophecy. Even so, the contrast between bargained love and freely given love fits the gospel well.

7. Spirit & Life

Rachel's mandrakes are familiar to anyone who has faced a painful lack, whether barrenness, loneliness, or unfruitful work. Most of us have something we reach for, a remedy we can hold and a deal we hope will finally work. Genesis does not mock Rachel's pain. It simply shows that her remedy did nothing, and that her answer came when "God remembered" and "listened." That word "listened" suggests she had brought her grief to God, since no plant could open her womb.

Leah's story speaks to people who feel unloved. She bought one night of her husband's presence, yet God had seen that she was unloved and had looked on her affliction long before (Genesis 29:31–33). His attention to her was never for sale.

The Song shows what love becomes when it is secure. A bride who knows "I am my beloved's" has no need to bargain. She gives freely. Believers who rest in Christ's love can stop trying to purchase God's favor and simply offer Him what they have "laid up" for Him.

Jeremiah adds one more encouragement. Some of God's "good figs" are people in exile, carried away from everything familiar. God can be at work for good in the very place that looks like loss.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All six occurrences counted by STEPBible appear in the passages discussed above: five in Genesis 30:14–16 and one in Song of Solomon 7:13. Jeremiah 24:1, discussed in Section 5, uses the related basket word H1731.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1730 (dôd, love, beloved): a related word of similar sound and probable relation. It appears beside "mandrakes" in the bride's "There I will give you my love" (Song of Solomon 7:12–13).

  • H1731 (dûd, pot, basket): a related word usually linked with the "baskets" sense in Jeremiah 24:1.
