ALOES - (ʼăhâlîym, Strong's H0174)

Summary

The Hebrew word translated "aloes" names one of the most costly fragrances of the ancient world. This is not the soothing aloe vera plant found on kitchen windowsills. It is an aromatic wood imported from India and Southeast Asia, widely identified as agarwood or "aloeswood." The word occurs 4 times in 4 verses of the Hebrew Bible, always in poetry or prophecy, and each time it marks something rare, precious and delightful.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

Those four uses tell a connected story. Balaam sees Israel's desert camp "like aloes planted by the LORD" (Numbers 24:6). A royal psalm, which Hebrews applies to the Son of God, describes the King's garments scented with myrrh and aloes (Psalm 45:8). The Song of Solomon pictures the bride as a garden growing myrrh and aloes (Song of Songs 4:14). Proverbs warns that the adulteress perfumes her bed with the same spices (Proverbs 7:17). In the New Testament, Nicodemus brings myrrh and aloes to bury Jesus (John 19:39). Together these passages show a King whose fragrance belongs to His people, and a counterfeit that borrows that fragrance to lead people toward death.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Aloeswood came to Israel by long trade routes from the forests of India and Southeast Asia. Ordinary families could not afford it. Like cinnamon and cassia, it belonged to kings, wealthy households and lavish celebrations. Most interpreters identify the Old Testament word with agarwood from trees of the Aquilaria family. The identification is widely held, though ancient plant names cannot always be matched with certainty to modern species.

One physical detail about agarwood matters for the rest of this study. The healthy wood of the tree is pale and has little scent. The dark, fragrant resin forms when the heartwood is wounded and invaded by a mold, so the tree's value comes from its injury. Myrrh, the spice most often paired with aloes in Scripture, is also a resin that flows from cuts in the bark of its tree.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one meaning: aloes, an aromatic wood prized for its fragrance. It can describe the living tree, as in Numbers 24:6, or the processed scent used on garments and furnishings, as in Psalm 45:8. These are the same thing at two stages, the growing tree and the finished perfume, so they are not separate senses.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Every use of the word is poetic, and aloes never appear alone. In Numbers they stand among valleys, gardens and cedars as images of a flourishing people. In the other three passages they are listed with other luxury spices, and myrrh is present each time. The setting decides the tone. The same scent signals royal joy in a palace, love in a protected garden, and danger in the house of the adulteress. The fragrance itself stays the same, and the place where it is found reveals whether it is being used rightly.

4. Theological Significance

Aloes show how God values what He has made His own. When He looks at a camp of tents in the desert, He sees a garden of costly trees. Israel's worth comes from "the LORD" who planted them, and their surroundings and achievements do not set it.

The word also shows that God is not opposed to beauty and delight. Scripture gives the finest fragrances to the King and His bride. Because beauty belongs to Him, sin's power to imitate it becomes all the more dangerous. Proverbs never condemns the spices themselves. It condemns their theft.

The steady pairing with myrrh is worth noticing. Both scents come from trees that have been cut or wounded. Scripture does not explain this botany, but the image fits a pattern the Bible teaches openly: God brings His richest fragrance out of costly suffering.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 24:5–6 — Tents seen as aloes. Balaam was hired to curse Israel, but God turned his words into blessing. Looking at the camp, he cries, "How lovely are your tents, O Jacob! Your dwellings, O Israel!" Then he adds, "Like aloes planted by the LORD, Like cedars beside the waters." Aloeswood grows in moist tropical forests and could never take root in the dry land of the wilderness. God was describing a people whose life and value He had produced in a place where such things should not exist.

The Hebrew adds a striking detail. The word for "tents" (ʼōhālîm) and the word for "aloes" (ʼăhālîm) look almost identical in written Hebrew and sound alike. The likeness is very likely deliberate wordplay. The plain, dusty tents of a wandering people become, in God's eyes, costly fragrant trees. Some scholars have questioned whether "aloes" is the right reading here, but the phrase "planted by the LORD" fits trees, and the wordplay strengthens the reading.

Psalm 45:8 — The King's garments. This royal wedding psalm addresses a King whose throne is forever (Psalm 45:6). It says, "All Your garments are scented with myrrh and aloes and cassia, Out of the ivory palaces, by which they have made You glad." The verse before speaks of His righteousness and His anointing "with the oil of gladness." His fragrance flows from that joy, and the scent clings to everything He wears. This King is just, and He is also deeply attractive. His people find in Him something to love as well as obey.

Song of Songs 4:14 — The bride's garden. The bridegroom describes his bride as a garden closed off for him alone (Song of Songs 4:12). It is filled with "frankincense, Myrrh and aloes, With all the chief spices." The bride grows the same spices that scent the King's robes in Psalm 45. What clothes the King is cultivated in the one He loves. To the bridegroom she has become a garden of his own fragrance.

Proverbs 7:17 — The stolen fragrance. The adulteress tells the naive young man, "I have perfumed my bed With myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon." These are the spices of the palace and the bridal garden. She has taken the language of royal love and spread it over a trap. The chapter ends by calling her house the road to the grave, leading down to the chambers of death (Proverbs 7:27). The contrast in setting tells the story. In Psalm 45 the fragrance comes from ivory palaces, and in the Song it grows in a guarded garden. In Proverbs it lies on the bed of a stranger whose house leads down to death. Temptation seldom smells like destruction. It usually smells like something precious.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hebrews 1:8–9 quotes Psalm 45 and applies it to the Son: "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever." The King whose garments carry the scent of aloes is Jesus Christ. That application is stated plainly in the New Testament, not left as a guess.

John's Gospel completes the picture. After the crucifixion, "Nicodemus, who at first came to Jesus by night, also came, bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about a hundred pounds" (John 19:39). John uses the Greek word for aloes. That is an enormous quantity, the kind of honor given at a royal burial. The King of Psalm 45 goes to His tomb wrapped in the same spices that scented His robes of joy. Even in death He is buried as a King.

The physical nature of these spices deepens the connection. Agarwood becomes fragrant only after its heart is wounded, and myrrh flows from cut bark. Nicodemus wrapped the body of the One who "was wounded for our transgressions" and "bruised for our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:5). The Bible never draws this botanical parallel itself, so it is best held as a fitting illustration rather than a stated prophecy. Still, it matches what the gospel says outright: the joy of Christ's reign and the wounds of His cross cannot be separated. The anointed King of Psalm 45 is the pierced Savior of John 19, and the fragrance of salvation reaches the world through His suffering.

7. Spirit & Life

The movement from Psalm 45 to Song of Songs 4 shows something about life with Christ. The King wears the fragrance, and the bride grows it. Paul says it directly: "we are to God the fragrance of Christ" (2 Corinthians 2:15). A believer does not produce this scent alone. It comes from belonging to Christ and staying close to Him, the way a garden takes on the character of what is planted in it. Because the fragrance is myrrh and aloes, it often comes through the believer's own wounds. Times of loss and discipline that God uses to make us more like Christ are often the times when others sense Him most clearly in us.

Numbers 24 speaks to anyone who feels their life is mostly desert. God called a tent camp "aloes planted by the LORD." This kind of fragrance is not native to the wilderness or to fallen human nature. God brings it from outside and keeps it alive where it should not survive. You may not see beauty in your circumstances, but the Lord who planted you does.

Proverbs 7 calls for discernment. The enemy rarely offers something that smells like ruin. He offers love, beauty, closeness and worth, the very things God created us to enjoy in Him. The question is not whether an offer smells sweet, but where it leads: to the King's palace and the guarded garden, or down the road to the chambers of death.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

As noted in the Summary, the Hebrew word occurs four times: Numbers 24:6, Psalm 45:8, Proverbs 7:17 and Song of Songs 4:14. Each of these is developed in Section 5, so no further occurrences remain. John 19:39, discussed in Section 6, uses the Greek word for aloes and completes the Old Testament picture.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Myrrh (H4753): a canonical pairing, not a word relative. Myrrh appears with aloes in Psalm 45:8, Proverbs 7:17 and Song of Songs 4:14, and again in John 19:39. Both are fragrant resins drawn from wounded trees.

  • Aloes, Greek aloē (G0250): the New Testament counterpart of this word, used for the burial spices of Jesus in John 19:39.

  • Tents (Numbers 24:5): a related image rather than a related word. The Hebrew word for tents, ʼohel (H0168), closely resembles the word for aloes, creating the wordplay discussed in Section 5.
