DUMAH - (Dûwmâh, Strong's H1746)

Summary

Dumah is a name used for more than one subject in the Old Testament. In Genesis 25:14 and 1 Chronicles 1:30, Dumah is one of the twelve sons of Ishmael and the ancestor of an Arabian tribe. In Joshua 15:52, Dumah is a small town in the hill country of Judah. In Isaiah 21:11, "the burden against Dumah" opens a brief and mysterious oracle addressed to someone calling "out of Seir," the mountain country of Edom. The name is spelled exactly like the Hebrew word for "silence," a word associated in sound and sense with the idea "to be silent" or "to be still," though its precise derivation is debated. Whether that sense explains why the Ishmaelite clan or the Judean town received the name cannot be known. In Isaiah, though, the sound of the word is almost certainly part of the message.

The genealogy records God keeping His word. He promised Abraham that Ishmael would father twelve princes, and Dumah stands in that list as one of them. The town of Dumah is one small piece of the land God gave to Judah. The oracle in Isaiah carries the most spiritual weight. A voice keeps asking the watchman, "what of the night?" The watchman answers, "The morning comes, and also the night," and then urges the questioner, "If you will inquire, inquire; Return! Come back!" A name that sounds like the silence of the grave becomes the setting for an invitation to inquire again.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Dumah names a man and a town, and its meaning in Isaiah's oracle is debated:

  • Dumah the son of Ishmael (Genesis 25:14; 1 Chronicles 1:30). He is listed sixth among Ishmael's twelve sons, after Mishma and before Massa. He was the ancestor of an Arabian clan that bore his name. Many scholars link this clan with the northern Arabian oasis later known as Dumat al-Jandal. That identification is a reasonable proposal, but Scripture does not state it.

  • Dumah the town in Judah (Joshua 15:52). It appears in a district of the hill country that also includes Hebron (Joshua 15:52–54). The site is commonly identified with modern Deir ed-Domeh, southwest of Hebron. No connection to the Ishmaelite clan is recorded beyond the shared name.

  • Dumah in Isaiah's oracle (Isaiah 21:11). Because the caller speaks "out of Seir," most interpreters read Dumah here as a deliberate play on Edom. The two Hebrew words sound alike, and the altered form turns Edom into "Silence." The ancient Greek translation is reported to render the heading as "Idumea," meaning Edom. Isaiah's very next oracle, however, addresses Arabia and names Dedan, Tema and Kedar (Isaiah 21:13–17). Tema and Kedar are Dumah's own brothers in Genesis 25. For that reason the Arabian Dumah cannot be ruled out, and the prophet may have intended both associations.

The name is spelled like the common Hebrew word for "silence" (H1745), which is connected in sound and sense with the verb damam, "to be silent, still, or dumb," though Strong derives the noun from an unused root meaning "to be dumb." The Psalms use that noun for the stillness of death: "My soul would soon have settled in silence" (Psalm 94:17); "The dead do not praise the LORD, Nor any who go down into silence" (Psalm 115:17). The link between the name and the word for silence is clear; a direct derivation of the name from damam is possible but not established. What remains uncertain is whether "silence" meant anything in particular when Ishmael's son or the Judean town was named. In Isaiah 21, however, the wordplay is hard to miss.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The son of Ishmael. When Hagar's son was passed over as the heir of the promise, God did not forget him. He told Abraham that Ishmael would father twelve princes and become a great nation (Genesis 17:20). Genesis 25:12–16 then names those twelve, "Mishma, Dumah, Massa," among them, and calls them "twelve princes according to their nations." First Chronicles 1:29–31 repeats the list as part of its record of all the families descended from Abraham. Scripture tells us nothing more about Dumah himself. His place in the list is the whole of his biblical story.

The town in Judah. Joshua 15 records the cities given to Judah when the land was divided. Dumah appears there among the hill-country towns near Hebron. It is one of dozens of names in the list, each a sign that the land promised to Abraham's descendants was actually distributed, town by town.

The burden against Dumah. Isaiah 21:11–12 is only two verses long. "The burden against Dumah. He calls to me out of Seir, 'Watchman, what of the night? Watchman, what of the night?'" The repeated question may suggest someone straining through a long darkness, wanting to know how much of it is left, though the text does not describe the caller's feelings. The answer does not tell him what he hopes to hear: "The morning comes, and also the night. If you will inquire, inquire; Return! Come back!" Relief is coming, but so is more darkness. The watchman does not close the conversation. He tells the questioner to keep asking and to come back.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God promised Ishmael twelve princes and then recorded those twelve by name. Dumah's appearance in that list shows that God's word to Abraham about Ishmael was fulfilled down to the individual names. God's care reached a family outside the line of promise as well as the line that ran through Isaac.

The town in Judah shows the same faithfulness at a smaller scale. God's promise of land was not left vague. It was carried out in specific places like Dumah.

Isaiah's oracle sits among his "burdens" against the nations (Isaiah 13–23), where God declares His rule over powers beyond Israel. If Dumah stands for Edom, the name pronounces judgment. Edom, the nation descended from Jacob's brother Esau, became a persistent enemy of Israel (see Obadiah 10–14), and the prophets repeatedly announced its downfall (Isaiah 34:5–6). Recasting "Edom" as "Silence" points toward the stillness of the grave that awaits proud hostility against God's people. That reading rests on a strong contextual inference, not an explicit statement. Even so, the oracle is not pure sentence. The watchman's final words leave room to ask again. The Hebrew "return" may simply mean "come back with your question." It is still significant that God's prophet answers a voice from Seir at all and tells it to keep inquiring.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No New Testament passage mentions Dumah. The connections here are thematic, not explicit fulfillment. The image of a watchman reading the night runs through Scripture (Isaiah 62:6; Ezekiel 33:1–9; Habakkuk 2:1). Night and morning together describe God's coming day, which brings light for some and darkness for others. Amos warned those who longed for it, "It will be darkness, and not light" (Amos 5:18).

Paul takes up the same picture for believers: "The night is far spent, the day is at hand. Therefore let us cast off the works of darkness, and let us put on the armor of light" (Romans 13:12). Isaiah's watchman was answering a question about Edom's night, not predicting Christ, but Paul's words give the same images a gospel meaning: in Christ the day has begun to dawn, and its full light is near. For those who belong to Him it is a day of light. For those who refuse Him, the night is not over. The watchman's "Return! Come back!" sounds a note the gospel shares.

5. Spirit & Life

The genealogy shows that God remembers what He has said. Years separate the promise about Ishmael in Genesis 17 from the list in Genesis 25, yet twelve princes appear, just as God had promised, and the names make the fulfillment concrete. A God who keeps His word to Ishmael can be trusted with every promise He has made in Christ.

Isaiah's oracle searches the heart. The voice from Seir seems to have wanted to know how long the trouble would last. The watchman's answer invites another inquiry, and believers can rightly hear in it a call to keep seeking God. That is an application rather than the oracle's stated demand. People still ask "what of the night?" when their world is shaken, and they often want only a forecast. God's answer is that troubles may lift and return, and the one safe place is in turning to Him. The name Dumah sounds like silence, but even there God did not stay silent. He said, "Inquire... Come back."

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:20 – God's promise that Ishmael would father twelve princes.

  • Genesis 25:13–16 – Dumah named among Ishmael's sons, fulfilling that promise.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:29–31 – The genealogy repeated in Israel's record of Abraham's descendants.

  • Joshua 15:52 – Dumah the town in Judah's hill country.

  • Isaiah 21:11–12 – The burden against Dumah and the watchman's answer.

  • Isaiah 21:13–17 – The adjoining oracle against Arabia naming Tema and Kedar, relevant to identifying Dumah.

  • Psalm 94:17; 115:17 – The related word "silence" used for death.

  • Amos 5:18; Romans 13:12 – Night and day as images of God's coming day.
