DISHON - (Dîyshôwn, Strong's H1787)

Summary

Dishon is the name of two men in the family of Seir the Horite, the people who lived in the mountains of Seir before Esau's descendants took the land that became Edom. The first Dishon was a son of Seir and one of the Horite chiefs (Genesis 36:21, 30; 1 Chronicles 1:38). The second was a son of Anah and the brother of Aholibamah, who became one of Esau's wives (Genesis 36:25; 1 Chronicles 1:41). The name is generally understood to mean "leaper" or to name a leaping wild animal such as an antelope or mountain goat. That meaning is probable rather than certain, and it fits a wider habit of animal names in this Horite family.

Neither Dishon has a story beyond the genealogy, but the record they belong to matters. Genesis carefully lists the Horite chiefs who ruled Seir, and Deuteronomy later explains that Esau's descendants "dispossessed them and destroyed them from before them, and dwelt in their place" (Deuteronomy 2:12). Dishon's name preserves a people who held real power and real land, and then lost both when God gave Seir to Esau. Their story stands as a sober witness that the Lord rules over the nations and assigns their boundaries, even among peoples outside Israel.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Scripture names two individuals called Dishon, and both belong to the Horite family of Seir. The first is the fifth of Seir's seven listed sons: Lotan, Shobal, Zibeon, Anah, Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan (Genesis 36:20–21). Genesis names him among the "chiefs of the Horites" (Genesis 36:30). The second Dishon is a son of Anah and the brother of Aholibamah (Genesis 36:25; 1 Chronicles 1:41). Because Genesis names one Anah as a son of Seir (36:20) and another as a son of Zibeon (36:24), this younger Dishon was either a nephew or a grandnephew of the first. The two men should not be merged. Dishon must also be kept separate from the elder Dishon's brother Dishan (H1789), a different chief whose sons were Uz and Aran (Genesis 36:28). English spelling alone does not settle which man is meant, because the Hebrew and the NKJV do not always match: in Genesis 36:26 the Hebrew has the Dishan form where the NKJV reads Dishon, and in 1 Chronicles 1:42 the Hebrew has the Dishon form where the NKJV reads Dishan. The family relationships in each list, not the English spelling, identify the person. This study follows the NKJV and the supplied list of occurrences.

The Hebrew name Dîyshôwn is usually connected with a leaping animal, so the meaning "leaper" or "antelope" is likely. A closely related Hebrew word, indexed separately as H1788, names a clean wild animal in Deuteronomy 14:5. English versions render that animal differently, so its exact species is not certain. The animal sense also matches other names in this genealogy, such as Zibeon and Ajah, which are commonly linked with animals. This is a probable etymology. The Bible does not comment on the name or build any wordplay on it.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Seir was the rugged highland south of the Dead Sea. Genesis calls Seir's sons the Horites "who inhabited the land" (Genesis 36:20). The elder Dishon was one of the clan heads in that land, and Genesis 36:29–30 repeats his title, "Chief Dishon," in a formal roll of Horite leaders.

Four sons are credited to a Dishon: Hemdan, Eshban, Ithran, and Cheran (Genesis 36:26). Chronicles spells the first name Hamran (1 Chronicles 1:41). It is unclear which Dishon fathered them. In Chronicles the list follows directly after "The son of Anah was Dishon," which points to the younger man. Yet both Genesis and Chronicles move through the sons of Seir in order, and the sons of Dishon appear in the elder Dishon's place in that order, just before the sons of Ezer and Dishan. The text leaves the question open.

The younger Dishon's sister Aholibamah married Esau (Genesis 36:2, 25). That marriage tied the Horite clans to Esau's household. In the end the Horites did not survive as an independent people. Deuteronomy 2:12 and 2:22 record that Esau's descendants displaced them, and verse 22 says the Lord Himself destroyed the Horites before them. The Chronicler later copied the same names into the opening genealogies of 1 Chronicles 1.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that the Lord gave Mount Seir to Esau as his possession (Deuteronomy 2:5). The careful list of Horite chiefs in Genesis 36 shows that Seir already belonged to an established people with recognized leaders, and Dishon was one of them. Esau's line did not settle in empty land. God removed one people and planted another.

Moses draws the lesson himself. The Horites were displaced "just as Israel did to the land of their possession which the LORD gave them" (Deuteronomy 2:12). God's dealings with Seir served as a precedent for Israel. He had done this before, and He would do it again for Israel.

Genesis 36:31 adds that Edom had kings "before any king reigned over" Israel. The Horite chiefs had already established leadership in Seir. These records contrast neighboring powers with Israel's later monarchy; they do not date every Edomite king before Israel received Canaan. It is a fair implication, though the text does not say so directly, that early worldly strength is no measure of God's purposes.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No direct connection to Christ is established for Dishon. His place in 1 Chronicles 1 belongs to the Chronicler's opening survey of the nations, which places Israel's story, and the line that leads to David, within a world God knows by name.

5. Spirit & Life

Scripture never records that Dishon knew or worshiped the Lord. Yet God did not leave this family as a nameless crowd. Their chiefs and sons are written down, and their removal is attributed to the Lord's own hand (Deuteronomy 2:22). The God of Israel rules the borders of every nation, not only His own people's. A chief's title and a mountain stronghold did not make Dishon's clan permanent. What lasts is what God gives and keeps.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:20–21, 29–30 — Dishon son of Seir, a chief of the Horites in Seir.

  • Genesis 36:24–25 — Dishon son of Anah, brother of Aholibamah.

  • Genesis 36:26; 1 Chronicles 1:41 — The four sons of Dishon, with Hemdan spelled Hamran in Chronicles.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:38 — The Chronicler's record of Seir's sons, including Dishon.

  • Deuteronomy 2:12, 22 — The Horites displaced by Esau's descendants, presented as a parallel to Israel's possession of its land. These verses do not name Dishon.
