LEAP, LEAPING - (dâlag, Strong's H1801)

Summary

The Hebrew verb dâlag means "to leap" or "to spring." It describes a vigorous bound over something that stands in the way, such as a wall, a mountain range, or a doorway. The word appears only five times in the Old Testament, and each use is poetic or prophetic. Leaping carries meaning in these passages because something blocks the way. In each case the leap shows what the barrier could not do.

The positive uses trace a striking progression. David says that by his God he can "leap over a wall," crossing the barrier that should have stopped him. In the Song of Solomon the beloved comes "leaping upon the mountains," and distance cannot keep him from the one he loves. In Isaiah's vision of God coming to save, "the lame shall leap like a deer," and broken bodies are made whole. Jesus pointed to these very signs as evidence of who He is. One use stands apart. Zephaniah condemns those who "leap over the threshold" in service of idolatry or violence. That warning shows that energy and eagerness are not holy in themselves. What matters is where the leap comes from and where it goes.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dâlag is a Hebrew verb of quick, springing movement. It belongs to vivid poetry rather than ordinary narrative. It never appears in a plain account of someone jumping. Every use pictures energy overcoming an obstacle. Two of the five uses are the same line in David's song of deliverance, which Scripture preserves twice (2 Samuel 22 and Psalm 18). The others come from a love song (Song of Solomon), a prophecy of restoration (Isaiah), and a prophecy of judgment (Zephaniah). Because the word is so rare, each occurrence carries real weight.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Dâlag means to leap, spring, or bound. The idea is forceful forward movement that clears something in its path: a wall, a mountain, a threshold, or the stillness that lameness imposes. The verb has one basic sense. Its different uses come from what is leaped over and who is leaping, not from different meanings of the word.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines what the leap shows. With David, the obstacle is a fortified wall, so the leap shows victory over enemies. With the beloved in the Song, the obstacle is distance, so the leap shows eager love. With the lame in Isaiah, the obstacle is the body's own brokenness, so the leap shows healing and joy. In Zephaniah the doorway is the barrier, and the leap belongs to people bent on idolatry or plunder.

Two uses draw a comparison with animals of the hills. Isaiah says the lame will leap "like a deer," and the Song soon compares the beloved to a gazelle or young stag (Song 2:9, paraphrased). These comparisons point to effortless grace and swiftness, the movement of a creature at home on hard terrain.

4. Theological Significance

In every positive use, the barrier is real and the strength to cross it comes from God or is given by God. David does not boast of athletic skill. He says "by my God." The lame do not train themselves into leaping. They leap because God has come. Scripture uses this small word to show God as the One who removes limits that people cannot remove themselves. These limits include enemy fortifications, the distance between the lover and the loved, and the disabilities of a fallen world.

Zephaniah adds a necessary balance. God does not bless energy simply because it is energetic. The same vigorous leap can serve idolatry and violence, and God judges it. The direction and source of zeal matter to Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

David's wall (2 Samuel 22:30; Psalm 18:29). "For by You I can run against a troop; By my God I can leap over a wall." David sang this after the Lord delivered him from Saul and all his enemies. A city wall was built precisely so that no one could get over it. David pictures himself charging a band of raiders and clearing a defensive wall. He credits both achievements to God, and the repeated "by You... by my God" places the strength outside himself. The same psalm goes on to say that God makes his feet like the feet of deer and sets him on high places (Psalm 18:33, paraphrased). David's leap is the sure-footedness of a man whom God has equipped for the fight.

The beloved on the mountains (Song of Solomon 2:8). "The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes Leaping upon the mountains, Skipping upon the hills." The bride hears him before she sees him. The mountains that separate them do not slow him down. He crosses them like a gazelle. This picture shows love that is eager and joyful, and it does not wait for the obstacles to clear. Many Christian readers have heard in this song an echo of God's love for His people. That reading is a canonical resonance and not a stated interpretation in the text. Still, the leaping lover fits the way Scripture shows God moving toward His people with delight.

The lame leaping (Isaiah 35:6). Isaiah 35 pictures the desert blossoming and announces to fearful hearts that God Himself will come to save them (Isaiah 35:4, paraphrased). Then the signs follow: "Then the lame shall leap like a deer, And the tongue of the dumb sing. For waters shall burst forth in the wilderness, And streams in the desert." The promise goes far beyond a return to ordinary function. Isaiah could have said the lame would walk. Instead, the people least able to move become the most exuberant movers in the scene, and the silent tongue does more than speak; it sings. Healing in people and renewal in the land happen together. The waters bursting into the wilderness show that God's salvation restores His whole creation.

The threshold leapers (Zephaniah 1:9). "In the same day I will punish All those who leap over the threshold, Who fill their masters' houses with violence and deceit." This is the word's only negative use, and its exact background is debated. One view connects it with the Philistine custom that began when Dagon's idol fell before the ark. According to 1 Samuel 5:5, "neither the priests of Dagon nor any who come into Dagon's house tread on the threshold." On that view, Judah had adopted a pagan superstition. Another view sees servants or raiders bounding into homes to seize goods for their masters, which fits the "violence and deceit" of the next line. On either reading, the leap is eager movement in the service of falsehood or theft, and God promises to punish it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah's promise does not stay in the future. When John the Baptist sent from prison to ask whether Jesus was the Coming One, Jesus answered by pointing to Isaiah's signs: "The blind see and the lame walk; the lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear; the dead are raised up and the poor have the gospel preached to them" (Matthew 11:5). His answer was clear. The God whom Isaiah said would come to save had arrived in Him.

The fulfillment becomes almost literal in Acts 3. Peter and John healed, in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, a man who had been lame from birth. "So he, leaping up, stood and walked and entered the temple with them—walking, leaping, and praising God" (Acts 3:8). Luke writes in Greek and uses a different word, but the picture is Isaiah's. The man does more than walk. He leaps, and his first steps carry him into the temple in praise. The restoration Isaiah foresaw had broken into the present through the risen Christ. What happened at the temple gate offered a preview of the full renewal still to come.

David's song points in the same direction. The king who leaped walls by God's strength anticipated the greater Son of David. Christ broke through the strongest barrier of all when He rose from death.

7. Spirit & Life

These passages teach believers to measure the obstacle honestly and then to look to the right source of strength. David did not pretend the wall was low. He said, "By my God." Many walls in a believer's life are built to stop people: entrenched sin, fear, opposition, and old wounds. The psalm does not promise that they will disappear. It promises a God who equips His people to get over them.

The Song shows that God's movement toward His people is eager. A believer who pictures God as distant or reluctant has misread Him. The voice of the beloved comes over the mountains with joy.

Isaiah and Acts show what kind of life God restores. The healed man did not limp cautiously into the temple. He leaped and praised. Grace aims at more than barely getting by. It aims at the freedom of a deer on the hills. Zephaniah keeps that freedom honest. A person can bound enthusiastically toward the wrong house. Spiritual vitality is measured by its source and direction, and true vitality moves toward God's presence with praise.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All five occurrences of dâlag are treated in Section 5. Two of them are the same line preserved in both 2 Samuel 22:30 and Psalm 18:29. Psalm 18:29 places David's testimony in Israel's book of worship, so that every worshiper who sings it can say, "By my God I can leap over a wall."
