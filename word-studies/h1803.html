POOR, POOREST, HAIR, LOOM - (dallâh, Strong's H1803)

Summary

The Hebrew noun dallâh first pictures something hanging loose: the threads that dangle from a weaver's loom, or long hair falling from the head. The same word also describes what is brought low, such as weak and gaunt animals, and especially "the poorest of the land." When Babylon emptied Jerusalem of its leaders, soldiers, and craftsmen, many of these poor were left behind.

The word occurs 8 times in 8 verses of the Hebrew Bible, yet it covers a remarkable range. Hezekiah uses it to describe his life as a thread about to be cut from the loom (Isaiah 38:12). The exile accounts use it for the helpless people who were left in Judah to work its vineyards and fields. The Song of Solomon uses it for the beloved's hair, which is so lovely that a king is held captive. Together these passages teach that human strength is far more fragile than it looks. They also show that God does not discard the weak and dangling threads of life. He preserves them, gives them a place in the land, and can see beauty in what hangs low.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dallâh is a Hebrew noun belonging to a small family of words. That family includes the verb dâlal, to hang down, be brought low, and the much more common adjective dal, weak, poor. The word appears in very different settings: Pharaoh's dream in Egypt, the historical record of Jerusalem's fall, a king's prayer after a deadly illness, and a love poem. Its physical picture of something hanging is plain in the poetic passages. Its social meaning of "the poor" appears in the narratives of the exile. Lexicons commonly connect the two senses through the idea of hanging low or being weak. That is a reasonable link, but Scripture itself never spells out how one sense grew from the other.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Something hanging or dangling. In Isaiah 38:12 it refers to the loose threads of a weaver's work on the loom, where NKJV translates "loom." In Song of Solomon 7:5 it refers to hair falling from the head.

2. Weakness or depletion. In Genesis 41:19 it describes cows that are "poor and very ugly and gaunt," meaning thin animals that have lost their strength.

3. The poor as a group. In 2 Kings and Jeremiah, it refers to the lowest class of people in Judah, whom NKJV calls "the poor" or "the poorest of the land."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines which sense is meant. In poetry the word keeps its concrete picture of threads or hair. In historical narrative it names a group of people. Five of those eight occurrences come in the closely parallel accounts of Judah's fall (2 Kings 24:14; 25:12; Jeremiah 40:7; 52:15, 16). The repetition carries weight. Most of these passages name the poor as the people who were left behind. Jeremiah 52 records both groups: Nebuzaradan carried away "some of the poor people" (Jeremiah 52:15) and left "some of the poor of the land as vinedressers and farmers" (Jeremiah 52:16).

Genesis 41:19 stands in between. There the word describes the body of an animal, not its economic status, and it shows how naturally the word moves from hanging low to lacking strength.

4. Theological Significance

The uses of dallâh reveal two truths about God's ways with people. The first is that human security is fragile. Kings, armies, and craftsmen cannot hold themselves together when God brings judgment. A king's own life can be cut off as easily as a weaver trims a thread. The second is that God watches over the weak. When Jerusalem fell, God preserved many of the people the empire passed over. Hannah's song states the same pattern in plain words: the Lord raises the poor from the dust (1 Samuel 2:8, which uses the related word dal). In Scripture, God's judgment brings down the proud, and His mercy reaches those who have nothing to boast in.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The poorest of the land (2 Kings 24:14; 25:12). Jehoiachin's deportation is described by listing what Babylon removed: "all the captains and all the mighty men of valor, ten thousand captives, and all the craftsmen and smiths." The verse then ends, "None remained except the poorest people of the land" (2 Kings 24:14). Babylon took everything that made Judah strong, including its leaders, its soldiers, and its skilled workers. These were the people and skills a proud nation relied on, and judgment carried them all away. After the final destruction, "the captain of the guard left some of the poor of the land as vinedressers and farmers" (2 Kings 25:12). Using the related word dal, Jeremiah adds that Nebuzaradan left "the poor people, who had nothing, and gave them vineyards and fields at the same time" (Jeremiah 39:10).

The irony is striking. People who had never owned a vineyard suddenly worked the vineyards of the Promised Land, while the powerful sat in captivity. Babylon left them to work the land, but behind Babylon's decision stood God's purpose. The loose threads of a nation that had been torn apart became the people who stayed in the land.

Cut from the loom (Isaiah 38:12). Hezekiah had been told he would die. In his song after recovering, he writes, "I have cut off my life like a weaver. He cuts me off from the loom." A weaver who finished a piece of cloth cut it free from the threads that held it on the loom. Hezekiah saw his whole life rolled up like finished cloth, held by threads that God could cut at any moment. A king who commanded an army had no more grip on his own life than a dangling thread. Job's complaint that his days are "swifter than a weaver's shuttle" (Job 7:6) uses a similar picture. The fear Hezekiah later described in this song had driven him to weep and pray (Isaiah 38:2–3), and God answered by adding fifteen years to his life (Isaiah 38:5).

Hair like purple (Song of Solomon 7:5). In this verse the same word describes beauty: "the hair of your head is like purple; A king is held captive by your tresses." Purple dye was costly and associated with royalty. The beloved's hair, falling loose like the threads of fine cloth, captures a king. In every other use, what hangs low is weak. Here it holds a king spellbound. The beloved is not weak, but the contrast is still worth noticing. The quality that elsewhere marks vulnerability becomes, in the eyes of love, what draws the king.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The poor who were left in Judah anticipate a pattern that runs throughout Scripture. God works through a humble remnant, and the low are lifted up. Jesus brought that pattern to its fullest expression when He blessed the poor in spirit and promised that the meek will inherit the earth (Matthew 5:3–5). Those words echo landless people receiving vineyards from the hand of their conqueror. Jesus speaks this blessing as a direct promise. The link to the exile is a resonance between the two passages, not a stated fulfillment.

Christ did not merely pity the poor; He entered their condition. "Though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor, that you through His poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9). Hezekiah's fragile life was extended for fifteen years. Christ allowed His own life to be cut off and then rose with a life that cannot be cut short. Through Him, people who are hanging by a thread receive an inheritance that no empire can take away.

7. Spirit & Life

Hezekiah's prayer teaches what Psalm 39:4 asks God to teach: "That I may know how frail I am." Believers often live as if they were held together by their own strength. Then illness, loss, or failure reveals how thin the thread really is. Hezekiah's discovery did not end in despair. It drove him to God, and that is the right response when our frailty is exposed. Admitting that we hang by a thread is the beginning of honest prayer.

The exile accounts add a second lesson. The people who had nothing were the ones who were given fields. In God's hands, those who have nothing can receive what the strong have lost. God is not embarrassed by people who are depleted, and He does not overlook them. Seen through the Song of Solomon, even what seems weak and dangling can be precious to the One who loves us. A believer who feels like a loose thread is neither forgotten nor without worth. Such a believer is held by grace and, in the King's eyes, can be lovely.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 41:19 – Pharaoh's dream shows cows "poor and very ugly and gaunt." This first use describes physical depletion and pictures the famine that would consume Egypt's abundance.

  • Jeremiah 40:7 – The captains hear that Gedaliah has been made governor over "the poorest of the land who had not been carried away captive to Babylon." The poor are now a community that needs leadership and protection.

  • Jeremiah 52:15–16 – Jeremiah's closing chapter repeats the account. Some of the poor were carried away along with the rest of the people, and others were left to tend vineyards and fields. Even the poor were not entirely spared from the exile, which makes the preservation of those left behind all the more a matter of mercy.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relatives: H1809 (dâlal), meaning to hang down, be feeble, be brought low. In Psalm 116:6 the psalmist says he was brought low and the Lord saved him. H1800 (dal) is the common word for weak or poor, as in 1 Samuel 2:8, where God raises the poor from the dust.

  • Thematic connections: Job 7:6 and Isaiah 38:12 both use weaving to picture how brief and fragile life is. James 4:14 makes the same point with a different image, comparing life to "a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away."
