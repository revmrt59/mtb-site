DOOR, DOORS, GATES - (deleth, Strong's H1817)

Summary

The Hebrew word deleth names the door itself: the wooden or metal leaf that swings shut across an opening. It hangs in the doorway of a house, on the great gates of a walled city, and on the gold-covered entrances of Solomon's temple. Because it means the movable panel, it can also describe the folding leaves of a temple door, the "lid" of an offering chest, and the "columns" of a scroll. Poets use it for the limits of creation, as when God says He shut in the sea "with doors" and opened "the doors of heaven" to send manna. David also uses it for the mouth when he prays, "Keep watch over the door of my lips."

The door is the place where someone decides what comes in and what stays out, and Scripture puts some of its most searching moments there. A servant who loved his master had his ear pierced against the household door. A widow shut her door and watched God fill her jars with oil. King Ahaz shut the temple doors, and Hezekiah's revival began when he opened them again. Through Malachi, God said He would rather see those doors shut than see worship offered without reverence. The word teaches that God holds authority over every opening and closing, from the sea to the sanctuary. It also prepares the way for Jesus, who calls Himself the door and stands at the door and knocks.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Deleth is a common Hebrew noun found from Genesis to Malachi. It refers to the panel that closes an entrance. The opening itself is usually called by another word, pethach, and the whole gate complex of a city by sha'ar. Genesis 19:6 shows the difference in a single line: "So Lot went out to them through the doorway, shut the door behind him." Lot passes through the opening and then swings the deleth closed behind him.

The word appears in three main settings. Narratives use it for the doors of homes and the gates of cities. The temple accounts in 1 Kings 6–7, 2 Chronicles 3–4, and Ezekiel 41 describe richly carved sanctuary doors. Poetry and prophecy extend the image to the sea, the sky, the womb, and the mouth. Nehemiah uses the word more than any other book, because hanging Jerusalem's doors was the last step in rebuilding the city wall.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A door or gate leaf: the movable barrier that closes a house, a room, a city, or a sanctuary (Gen 19:6; Deut 3:5; 1 Kgs 6:31).

2. A hinged or folding panel that works like a door: the folding leaves of a temple door (Ezek 41:24), the lid of Jehoiada's collection chest (2 Kgs 12:9), and the sections or "columns" of the scroll King Jehoiakim cut apart (Jer 36:23).

3. A figurative door: a barrier God sets around natural forces (Job 38:8; Ps 78:23), or the entrance of the womb (Job 3:10) or the lips (Ps 141:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The verbs that go with the word carry much of its meaning. A door is shut, opened, bolted, hung, broken, or carried away, and each action tells the reader who holds power in the scene. In Judges 3 Ehud locks the doors of Eglon's chamber and escapes while the guards wait outside. In Judges 16 Samson tears the doors of Gaza's gate out of the ground. Everything turns on who controls the door.

The same door can mean safety or rejection, depending on who shuts it and why. At Sodom, the mob tried to break Lot's door down. The angels pulled Lot inside, shut the door, and struck the men outside with blindness (Gen 19:9–10). The shut door protected those inside and kept out those who meant them harm. In 2 Samuel 13, the door becomes a weapon. After Amnon violated his half-sister Tamar, he ordered his servant, "Put this woman out, away from me, and bolt the door behind her" (2 Sam 13:17). Verse 18 repeats the action and notes the royal robe she was wearing. A king's daughter was locked out by the man who had wronged her. Scripture does not soften the scene. Whoever has power over a door can use it to shelter people or to shut them out.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture's door language makes a clear claim: God governs every boundary. When He answers Job from the whirlwind, He asks, "Or who shut in the sea with doors, When it burst forth and issued from the womb" (Job 38:8), and adds, "When I fixed My limit for it, And set bars and doors" (Job 38:10). In the ancient world the sea stood for chaos and danger, yet God hangs doors on it the way a builder hangs them on a house. Psalm 78:23 shows the same authority used in the opposite direction: "Yet He had commanded the clouds above, And opened the doors of heaven." He opened those doors to rain down manna on a complaining people. God shuts the doors that hold back destruction and opens the doors that release blessing, and both acts belong to Him alone.

That authority reaches into human history. Through Isaiah, God promised Cyrus, the Persian king He had appointed to free His people, that He would open the double doors before him so the gates would not be shut. He would even break the gates of bronze in pieces (Isa 45:1–2). The strongest doors in Babylon could not stay closed against God's plan to bring His people home. No human fortification is stronger than God's purpose.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The temple doors: access, apostasy, and true worship (2 Chr 28–29; Ezek 41; Mal 1:10). Across Israel's history, the condition of the temple doors shows the condition of Israel's worship. Ahaz cut up the temple's sacred articles, "shut up the doors of the house of the LORD," and built altars on every corner in Jerusalem (2 Chr 28:24). Hezekiah's reform began in the first month of his reign, when he opened the doors of the house of the LORD and repaired them (2 Chr 29:3). He then confessed that their fathers had shut the doors, put out the lamps, and stopped burning incense and offering burnt offerings (2 Chr 29:7). Revival began at the door, with a king reopening the way to God.

The doors were also full of meaning in themselves. Solomon's olivewood doors were carved with cherubim, palm trees, and open flowers and covered with gold (1 Kgs 6:32). In Ezekiel's vision, the doors of the temple carry cherubim and palm trees just like its walls (Ezek 41:25). Cherubim had guarded the way back into Eden (Gen 3:24). Carved on the sanctuary doors among trees and blossoms, they suggest a garden entrance where God again lets His people draw near, though only by the way He provides through sacrifice.

Malachi turns this history around. The priests of his day were offering blind and sick animals, and God said He wished someone among them would shut the doors so that no one would kindle fire on His altar in vain (Mal 1:10). Ahaz's shut doors were rebellion. Malachi's shut doors would have been a mercy, because careless worship offends God more than no worship at all. An open door does not prove that worship is true. God looks for people who come through His doors honoring Him.

The door of the willing servant (Ex 21:6; Deut 15:17). A Hebrew servant could go free after six years. If he said he loved his master and his family and did not want to leave, his master brought him to the door and pierced his ear with an awl against it, and he served for life. The door he could have walked through to freedom became the place where he bound himself to the house. The mark was on his ear, the organ of hearing and obeying. The law built a permanent sign for one thing: service chosen out of love.

The shut door of faith (2 Kgs 4; Isa 26:20). Elisha told the widow to borrow empty vessels and then "shut the door behind you and your sons" (2 Kgs 4:4). The oil kept flowing until the last jar was full (vv. 5–6). At Shunem, Elisha shut the door on himself and the dead boy and prayed (2 Kgs 4:33). In both stories the miracle happens behind a closed door, away from onlookers and alone with God. Isaiah extends the image to the whole nation: "Come, my people, enter your chambers, And shut your doors behind you; Hide yourself, as it were, for a little moment, Until the indignation is past" (Isa 26:20). The closed door is where God's people meet Him in trust, and it is also where He shelters them while judgment passes, much as the Passover door once sheltered Israel.

Hanging the doors in Nehemiah (Neh 3; 6–7). As each section of the wall was rebuilt, the builders "hung its doors" (Neh 3:1, 3, 6). Sanballat and his allies pressed hardest once the wall had no gaps but the doors were not yet hung (Neh 6:1). A wall with empty gateways cannot protect a city, and Nehemiah's enemies knew it. Once the doors were in place, Nehemiah appointed gatekeepers, singers, and Levites, and he ordered that the gates not be opened until the sun was hot (Neh 7:1–3). Hanging the doors turned a construction site into a guarded, worshiping city. Later Nehemiah shut those same doors to stop trade on the Sabbath (Neh 13:19), using them to protect what was holy.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Each line of the door theme points ahead to Jesus. The servant at the door with his pierced ear foreshadows the Son, who took the form of a servant (Phil 2:7) and of whom Isaiah wrote that the Lord opened His ear and He was not rebellious (Isa 50:5). Christ's service to the Father was chosen out of love and has no end. The Exodus law does not name Him, but the likeness is clear and fitting.

The broken gates point to His victory. Samson rose at midnight, "took hold of the doors of the gate of the city and the two gateposts, pulled them up, bar and all," and carried them to a hilltop (Judg 16:3). The city that thought it had trapped him lost its gate. Psalm 107:16 praises God because "He has broken the gates of bronze." These scenes look forward to the risen Christ, who holds the keys of Death and Hades (Rev 1:18). The gates that held His people as prisoners cannot hold them now.

Psalm 24 uses a different Hebrew word for "doors," but the image completes this story: "Lift up your heads, O you gates! And be lifted up, you everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in" (Ps 24:7). The temple doors carved with cherubim find their meaning in the One who said He is the door, through whom anyone who enters will be saved (John 10:9). In the end the King stands at a different door: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Rev 3:20). The Lord who hangs doors on the sea waits at the doors of human hearts.

7. Spirit & Life

David knew his mouth was a door that needed a guard: "Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; Keep watch over the door of my lips" (Ps 141:3). Nehemiah posted gatekeepers because enemies come through unguarded doors, and David asks God to be the gatekeeper of his speech. The prayer admits that our own watchfulness is not enough. Words can wound as surely as the bolted door wounded Tamar, so we ask the Lord to stand at that gate for us.

Wisdom speaks of the blessed person who listens to her, watching daily at her gates, where deleth stands behind the English "gates" (Prov 8:34). Notice what this disciple does. He does not force the door or wander away from it. He comes back every day and waits for it to open. Jesus said the same thing in His own words: "blessed are those who hear the word of God and keep it!" (Luke 11:28).

Some closed doors are gifts. The widow found God's supply behind a shut door, and Jesus told His disciples to shut the door and pray to the Father in secret (Matt 6:6). When God closes something around us, He may be making room to meet with us alone. And whenever we enter His presence, Malachi asks us to come with reverence, because God cares how we come through His door as much as whether we come.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 3:10 — "Because it did not shut up the doors of my mother's womb, Nor hide sorrow from my eyes." In his grief, Job pictures birth itself as a door that was opened.

  • Song of Solomon 8:9 — "If she is a wall, We will build upon her A battlement of silver; And if she is a door, We will enclose her With boards of cedar." A door that opens easily needs protecting, and her brothers mean to guard her purity.

  • Judges 11:31 — Jephthah vowed to offer whatever came out of the doors of his house to meet him. His rash promise ended in grief when his daughter came through that door first.

  • Nehemiah 6:10 — Shemaiah urged Nehemiah to hide inside the temple and shut its doors. Nehemiah refused to save himself by fear and disobedience.

  • Zechariah 11:1 — "Open your doors, O Lebanon, That fire may devour your cedars." When God commands a nation's defenses to open, judgment comes through them.

  • Jeremiah 36:23 — As each few columns of Jeremiah's scroll were read, King Jehoiakim cut them off and burned them. He tried to shut out God's word one section at a time.
