LIKE, LIKEN, THINK - (dâmâh, Strong's H1819)

Summary

The Hebrew verb dâmâh means to be like or to liken, that is, to set one thing beside another and see a resemblance. In the NKJV it is translated "like," "liken," "likened," "compared," "alike," and "symbols." The same verb also describes the inner act of thinking something through, intending it, or plotting it. It appears as "thought," "intended," "plotted," "think," and "mean." Lovers use it to praise each other in the Song of Solomon. Sufferers use it to describe their sorrow in the Psalms. The prophets use it as a weapon against idolatry and pride.

The word's greatest use comes in a question Isaiah keeps asking: to whom will you liken God? The answer is no one. Every idol and every boast fails here, whether it is the king who says "I will be like the Most High" or the sinner who assumes God is "altogether like you." The same incomparable God still chooses to make Himself known through prophetic pictures. When God "thinks" a thing, it comes to pass. Those Old Testament questions find their answer in Jesus Christ, "the image of the invisible God."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dâmâh belongs mostly to the poetic and prophetic books. Isaiah, the Psalms, the Song of Solomon, and Ezekiel together hold most of its uses. Most of these are comparisons. Some are tender, like the bride's beloved who is like a gazelle (Song of Solomon 2:9). Some are desolate, like the psalmist who is like a pelican of the wilderness (Psalm 102:6). Some are theological, like the "liken" questions in Isaiah.

In the narrative books (Numbers, Judges, 2 Samuel, and Esther), the word usually concerns what someone has in mind to do. Hebrew dictionaries list both senses under this verb. The connection between them is plausible, because comparing and planning are both acts of the mind that hold a picture up to reality. That link should not be pressed beyond what the uses themselves show.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Dâmâh has two principal senses:

1. To be like, resemble, or compare. Someone or something is likened to another, or someone asks what a thing can be compared with. Example: "Who among the sons of the mighty can be likened to the LORD?" (Psalm 89:6).

2. To think, intend, or plan. This sense describes forming a purpose or idea in the mind, sometimes a hostile plan. Example: the men of Gibeah "intended to kill me" (Judges 20:5).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word carries no moral weight of its own. Its effect depends on who is comparing or planning, and what they compare or plan.

  • Poetic comparison. The Song of Solomon uses it freely for the beauty of the beloved (Song of Solomon 1:9; 7:7). Lament uses it for frailty. "Man is like a breath," says Psalm 144:4. The comparison helps the reader feel what plain statement cannot.

  • Moral and prophetic comparison. Isaiah admits that without a surviving remnant, Judah would have been made like Gomorrah (Isaiah 1:9). The comparison is a verdict.

  • Rhetorical questions. Many uses come inside questions such as "To whom will you liken...?" (Isaiah 40:18, 25; 46:5) or "Whom are you like?" (Ezekiel 31:2). In Isaiah the question about God's equal does not wait for an answer, because it forces the hearer to realize there is none. Ezekiel's question to Pharaoh works differently: God answers it Himself with the story of the Assyrian cedar (Ezekiel 31:3–18).

  • Intention. The word describes Saul's plan to destroy the Gibeonites, which the NKJV renders "plotted" (2 Samuel 21:5). Mordecai uses it to warn Esther: "Do not think in your heart that you will escape in the king's palace" (Esther 4:13). Here the word names a mental picture that reality is about to overturn.

4. Theological Significance

Running through these passages is one conviction: God has no equal. The heavens, the angelic "sons of the mighty" (Psalm 89:6), the nations, and their idols supply nothing that can stand beside Him as His equal. God made humanity in His image and likeness (Genesis 1:26–27), and He even speaks to His people through created pictures, but no creature or idol can equal Him or fully represent His nature. This is part of what His holiness means in these passages. He is set apart from everything He made.

Sin, then, can be described as false likening, and it runs in two directions. Isaiah 14:14 puts a boast in the mouth of the king of Babylon: "I will ascend above the heights of the clouds, I will be like the Most High." Christians have long heard behind that taunt the original rebellion of Satan, and the pattern fits Eden's temptation, "you will be like God" (Genesis 3:5). The creature tries to raise itself to God's level. Psalm 50:21 shows the opposite error. God speaks to people who recited His words while living in theft and slander: "These things you have done, and I kept silent; You thought that I was altogether like you." Here the sinner drags God down to his own level. God's patience was mistaken for approval. Both errors deny God's incomparability, and both end in rebuke.

The second sense of the word adds something just as important. Human intentions are often frustrated or shown to be blind. Gibeah's plan, Saul's plot, and Esther's imagined safety are examples. God's intentions are different. In Numbers 33:56, God warns Israel that if they spare the Canaanites, "I will do to you as I thought to do to them." What God has in mind is a certainty.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 40:18, 25; 46:5 — "To whom will you liken Me?" Isaiah speaks to a people who will face exile surrounded by the gods of Babylon. Before he asks the question in Isaiah 40:18, he has already described the LORD measuring the waters in the hollow of His hand and weighing the mountains on scales. He counts the nations as a drop in a bucket (Isaiah 40:12–17). Then he asks to whom anyone could liken God. He follows the question with a craftsman who overlays an idol with gold and a poor man who picks a tree that will not rot (Isaiah 40:19–20). The comparison is absurd. Something a man carries cannot be compared to the One who carries the stars. In Isaiah 40:25 God Himself asks the question, "says the Holy One." In Isaiah 46:5 He piles up the terms: liken, make equal, compare, that we should be "alike." Isaiah 46 then shows Babylon's gods loaded onto weary animals as they go into captivity (Isaiah 46:1–2). Israel's God says instead that He has carried His people from birth and will carry them to old age (Isaiah 46:3–4). Idols need to be carried. The true God carries His people. Paul describes the same exchange in Romans 1:23: people "changed the glory of the incorruptible God into an image made like corruptible man."

Isaiah 14:24 — "As I have thought." In the same chapter as the king of Babylon's boast, the LORD swears an oath concerning Assyria: "Surely, as I have thought, so it shall come to pass, And as I have purposed, so it shall stand." The arrogant king imagined himself climbing above the clouds. God's thought decides what happens. The contrast sharpens in Isaiah 10:7, where the Assyrian king "does not mean so." Assyria thinks it is conquering for its own glory and does not know it is a rod in God's hand. Human beings intend things. God's intention governs history, including the plans of those who oppose Him (Isaiah 14:26–27).

Ezekiel 31:2, 8, 18 — "Whom are you like in your greatness?" God has Ezekiel put this question to Pharaoh. Then He answers it with a story. Assyria had once been a cedar so tall that no tree in the garden of God was like it. It was cut down because its heart was lifted up in its height (Ezekiel 31:3–14). The chapter ends by turning the question back on Egypt: to which of the trees of Eden will you be likened (Ezekiel 31:18)? In the lament that follows, Pharaoh is likened to a young lion and a monster in the seas (Ezekiel 32:2). That lament announces his fall. Pride that sets itself beyond comparison invites God to show who truly is.

Psalm 48:9 — Thinking on lovingkindness. "We have thought, O God, on Your lovingkindness, In the midst of Your temple." The psalm has just described kings who assembled against Zion, saw it, and fled in terror (Psalm 48:4–7). Then the worshippers say, "As we have heard, So we have seen" (Psalm 48:8). Standing in the temple, they deliberately fix their minds on God's steadfast love. The same verb that elsewhere describes plotting here describes worship. The mind forms its picture of God from what He has actually done.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah's question seems to close every door. No creature can equal God, and no idol can represent Him. Yet the New Testament announces that God has provided the perfect likeness Himself. "He is the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15). The Son is "the brightness of His glory and the express image of His person" (Hebrews 1:3). This does not overturn Isaiah. No idol, angel, or empire could ever equal God or show Him fully. Human beings were made in His image, but the Son alone reveals Him perfectly, because He shares God's nature. The forbidden comparison is answered by God's own self-revelation in His Son.

The story moves in this direction through Hosea 12:10. There God says He gave "symbols" through the prophets, and the word is dâmâh again. The God who cannot be compared still uses comparisons with His people. He gives pictures of a shepherd, a husband, a refiner, and a cedar. Hebrews 1:1 gathers these up: "God, who at various times and in various ways spoke in time past to the fathers by the prophets." Then He spoke by His Son. The prophetic likenesses served for a time. Christ is the likeness itself.

The Son also reverses the king of Babylon's boast. Isaiah 14 shows a creature grasping at being "like the Most High." Philippians 2:6–8 shows the One who was truly equal with God refusing to grasp. He humbled Himself to death on a cross. Isaiah 14:24 shows that God's purpose stands, and in Christ it stands as salvation. After purging our sins, the Son "sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" (Hebrews 1:3). The throne that rebellion tried to seize was given to the obedient Son. Those who belong to Him are destined to be conformed to His image (Romans 8:29).

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 50:21 contains one of the most searching sentences in Scripture: "You thought that I was altogether like you." Every person carries a mental picture of God. Unless Scripture corrects that picture, it tends to drift toward a God who is as tolerant of our sins as we are. God's silence in that psalm was patience. The people mistook it for agreement. A believer who has gone a long time without discipline should not conclude that God has stopped caring about sin.

Psalm 48:9 offers the remedy, and it uses the same verb. The worshippers did not wait for a right view of God to arrive by itself. They deliberately thought on His lovingkindness, and they did so with fresh evidence of His protection before them. Believers can practice the same habit. When God answers prayer or keeps His people through danger, they can stop and fix their minds on His faithful love until their picture of Him is formed by His deeds rather than by their moods.

Isaiah 14:24 brings steady comfort. Mordecai warned Esther that the palace would not save her. Human security is often imagined. What God has "thought" "shall come to pass," and in Isaiah 14 His purpose is to lift the Assyrian yoke from His people (Isaiah 14:25). The believer's confidence rests on God's purpose, which no Assyrian plan, Pharaoh's pride, or private fear can overturn.

Finally, Isaiah's question, "To whom then will you liken God?" (Isaiah 40:18), now has a face. A believer who wants to know what God is like looks to Jesus: His compassion at a widow's funeral, His holiness in the temple, His love on the cross. In Him the incomparable God has made Himself known.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 89:6 — "Who among the sons of the mighty can be likened to the LORD?" The psalmist makes this claim about God's faithfulness before he wrestles with the apparent collapse of David's throne.

  • Psalm 102:6 — The afflicted psalmist says he is like a pelican of the wilderness. He uses a lonely creature in a desolate place to picture his isolation as he brings his grief to God.

  • Song of Solomon 2:9 — The beloved is like a gazelle or a young stag. The image captures the eager swiftness of love as he comes to the beloved.

  • Lamentations 2:13 — The poet searches for something to liken to the ruin of Jerusalem and cannot find a comparison large enough for her wound.

  • 2 Samuel 21:5 — The Gibeonites remember Saul as the man who plotted against them. That buried injustice had to be addressed before the famine was lifted.
