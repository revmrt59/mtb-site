DINHABAH - (Dinhâbâh, Strong's H1838)

Summary

Dinhabah was the royal city of Bela the son of Beor, the first king of Edom named in Scripture. The meaning of the name is uncertain, and no meaning has been established. The Bible mentions Dinhabah only twice, in the list of Edom's kings (Genesis 36:32) and again in the Chronicler's genealogies (1 Chronicles 1:43). Its location is unknown.

Two mentions may seem like very little, but the setting gives them weight. Moses introduces the list with a pointed remark: these kings reigned in Edom "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). God had already promised Jacob that kings would come from him (Genesis 35:11). Yet Esau's descendants had a king and a capital first, long before Israel had a king of its own. Dinhabah stands at the head of that early, visible success. After Bela's notice the list moves on to rulers associated with other places, and Dinhabah never appears in the story again.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Dinhabah is a city in Edom, the territory around Mount Seir southeast of the Dead Sea, where Esau's descendants settled. Scripture identifies it only as the city of Bela son of Beor, Edom's first listed king. No other person or place in the Bible bears this name. Proposed sites, including a place called Danaba and a location near Tafilah, have no archaeological confirmation.

The etymology is obscure. Proposed meanings rest on uncertain comparisons with other languages. The text itself contains no wordplay on the name, so it is wiser to say plainly that its meaning is unknown.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

After Jacob and Esau separated, Esau settled in Seir (Genesis 36:6–8), and his descendants became a nation with chiefs and then kings. Genesis 36:31–39 lists eight kings. The first entry reads: "Bela the son of Beor reigned in Edom, and the name of his city was Dinhabah" (Genesis 36:32).

The list never shows a son succeeding his father. Bela is followed by Jobab son of Zerah of Bozrah (36:33), then Husham of the land of the Temanites (36:34), then Hadad son of Bedad, whose city was Avith (36:35). The list names several cities and regions, though not a seat for every king, and this variety suggests, without proving, a kingship that was not passed down within one family. Dinhabah is recorded only as Bela's city.

Centuries later, after Israel's own monarchy had risen and fallen, the Chronicler copied the same record (1 Chronicles 1:43). He opens Israel's story by placing it among the nations, and Edom's forgotten capital keeps its line in his account.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states that Edom had kings before Israel did (Genesis 36:31). The rest of this section draws implications from that fact. Isaac had told Esau he would live by the sword and eventually break his brother's yoke (Genesis 27:39–40). Edom's early kingdom fits that word, but it stands apart from the promise of blessing given to Jacob (Genesis 25:23; 28:13–14).

The contrast becomes sharper later in Scripture. Israel waited a long time for a king. When God finally promised David a lasting house and throne (2 Samuel 7:12–16), the promise centered on a royal house rather than a capital. Edom's list, by comparison, names rulers from varied places and no lasting royal house. Dinhabah is a small example of a recurring biblical pattern: those outside the line of promise are often the first to build cities and gain political power (Genesis 4:17; 10:8–12).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No later Scripture mentions Dinhabah. The kingdom that began there, however, became a lasting rival of Israel. Balaam foresaw a "Star" from Jacob who would take possession of Edom (Numbers 24:17–18). Amos spoke of the rebuilt tabernacle of David possessing the remnant of Edom (Amos 9:11–12), and James applied that prophecy to the gathering of the Gentiles through Christ (Acts 15:16–17).

There is also a thematic contrast, though Scripture does not draw it explicitly. Edom's list moves from ruler to ruler and place to place. The angel told Mary that God would give Jesus "the throne of His father David" and that His kingdom would have no end (Luke 1:32–33).

5. Spirit & Life

Genesis 36 records that Edom had kings before Israel did. Read alongside God's promise of kings to Jacob, the contrast invites trust in God's timing rather than envy of earlier political success.

Dinhabah's brief history makes the same point. A king's city is named, the list moves on, and the city disappears from the record after that one notice. God's kingdom grew slowly, but it was built on His promise. That is why it endures.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:31–32 – Names Dinhabah as Bela's city and notes that Edom had kings before Israel.

  • Genesis 36:33–39 – Lists later kings with varied places of origin or rule.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:43 – The Chronicler's repetition of the record after the exile.

  • Genesis 35:11; 27:39–40 – God's promise of kings to Jacob and Isaac's word to Esau, which together frame the list.

  • 2 Samuel 7:12–16 – The lasting Davidic house and throne, in contrast to Edom's changing rulers.

  • Numbers 24:17–18; Amos 9:11–12; Acts 15:16–17 – Edom's later place in prophecy and its fulfillment in Christ's kingdom.
