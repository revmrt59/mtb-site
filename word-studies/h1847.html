KNOWLEDGE - (daʻath, Strong's H1847)

Summary

Daʻath is the Old Testament's main word for knowledge. It can mean awareness of what one is doing, the practical know-how of a skilled craftsman, or the deep moral and spiritual understanding that comes from walking with God. Scripture never treats it as neutral information. Proverbs locates its beginning in the fear of the LORD. The first humans reached for the knowledge of good and evil on their own terms, and death followed. In Job, men who speak about God's ways "without knowledge" are rebuked for it.

The prophets give the word its sharpest edge. Through Hosea, God says He desires the knowledge of Him more than burnt offerings. Through Jeremiah, He says that defending the poor and needy is what it means to know Him. Isaiah then gives the word its highest use: "By His knowledge My righteous Servant shall justify many, For He shall bear their iniquities" (Isaiah 53:11). The knowledge Israel failed to live out is perfectly lived by the Servant, and His knowledge brings others into righteousness.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Daʻath is a noun related to the ordinary Hebrew verb "to know." In Hebrew usage, knowing often involves experience, relationship, and response as well as mental awareness, and this noun follows that pattern.

The word gathers heavily in the wisdom books. Proverbs contains nearly half its uses, and Job and Ecclesiastes add many more. It also appears in the tabernacle and temple accounts, in the laws for the cities of refuge, and in prophetic indictments and promises from Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hosea, and Malachi.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Daʻath means knowledge: what a person perceives, understands, and can act upon. Context brings out three main senses:

1. Conscious awareness or intent. In the refuge laws, someone who kills "without knowledge" acted without awareness or plan. The NKJV translates this as "unintentionally" (Deuteronomy 19:4).

2. Practical skill. This is the know-how to make and build. Huram "was filled with wisdom and understanding and skill in working with all kinds of bronze work" (1 Kings 7:14).

3. Moral and spiritual understanding, including the knowledge of God. This is the discernment Proverbs urges readers to seek (Proverbs 2:10). It is also the knowledge of God whose absence Hosea mourns (Hosea 4:1).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Exodus, daʻath is a gift of the Spirit. God fills Bezalel with the Spirit of God in wisdom, understanding, and knowledge so that he can make the tabernacle and its furnishings (Exodus 31:3; 35:31). Huram brings the same kind of skill to Solomon's temple. In these passages, knowledge is something a person does with his hands, and God gives it for building the place where He dwells.

The phrase "without knowledge" is worth tracing. In the law it protects a person. Someone who killed a neighbor accidentally could flee to a city of refuge and live (Deuteronomy 4:42; Joshua 20:3, 5). God drew a firm line between hatred that plots harm and a tragic accident, and He provided a place of safety for the second. In Job the same phrase accuses: "Job speaks without knowledge, His words are without wisdom" (Job 34:35). Proverbs adds that it is not good for a soul to be without knowledge (Proverbs 19:2). Acting without knowledge can be forgiven, but speaking confidently without it is folly.

In Proverbs, daʻath usually appears alongside wisdom, understanding, and instruction. The wise store it up, while fools despise it (Proverbs 10:14; 1:22).

4. Theological Significance

God is the source of all true knowledge. The LORD gives knowledge and understanding from His mouth (Proverbs 2:6). He teaches knowledge to mankind (Psalm 94:10), and no one ever taught Him (Isaiah 40:14). By His knowledge the depths were broken up and the clouds drop dew (Proverbs 3:20).

That is why Genesis and Proverbs read as two sides of one story. In Eden, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil marked a limit (Genesis 2:9, 17). To eat from it was to claim the right to decide good and evil apart from God. Proverbs answers by calling the fear of the LORD the beginning of knowledge (Proverbs 1:7). It explains that those who hated knowledge did not choose the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:29), and it promises that whoever seeks wisdom will find the knowledge of God (Proverbs 2:5; 9:10). Job 28:28 says the same thing in other words: "Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom." Knowledge taken by force leads to death. Knowledge received in reverence leads to life.

Ecclesiastes shows the other limit. The Preacher gained great knowledge, yet he found that increasing knowledge increases sorrow (Ecclesiastes 1:16, 18). A man who labors with knowledge must still leave everything to someone who never worked for it (Ecclesiastes 2:21). Human knowledge cannot master death, and the book ends with the call to fear God and keep His commandments (Ecclesiastes 12:13).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Hosea 4:1, 6; 6:6 and Jeremiah 22:16. Hosea brings God's charge against a land that has no truth, no mercy, and no knowledge of God (4:1). The verses that follow list the results: swearing, lying, murder, stealing, and adultery. The lack of knowledge shows up in how people treat their neighbors. God then says that His people are destroyed for lack of knowledge, and because the priests rejected knowledge, He rejects them (4:6). In 6:6 He states what He wants: mercy instead of sacrifice, and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings. Israel kept the altar busy while it lost the relationship the altar was meant to serve.

Jeremiah makes the point even more concrete. He contrasts King Jehoiakim's luxury with his father Josiah, who judged the cause of the poor and needy, and the LORD asks whether this was not what it means to know Him (Jeremiah 22:15–16). Knowing God, in His own definition, includes doing what He does for the vulnerable.

Job 38:2 and 42:3. When God finally speaks from the whirlwind, His first question is, "Who is this who darkens counsel By words without knowledge?" (38:2). Job has argued about God's justice without seeing God's purposes. After God shows him the depths of creation, Job repeats the question back to Him and confesses: "Therefore I have uttered what I did not understand, Things too wonderful for me, which I did not know" (42:3). Job does not receive an explanation of his suffering. He receives a true sense of how great God is, and that is the beginning of real knowledge.

Isaiah 53:11. At the end of the Servant's suffering, the LORD says, "By His knowledge My righteous Servant shall justify many, For He shall bear their iniquities." The phrase can be read as the Servant's own knowledge, meaning His understanding of and obedience to the Father's will, or as the knowledge of Him that others receive. Either way, it is tied directly to His bearing of iniquities. Justification comes through this knowledge because the Servant who has it carries the sin of the many.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word follows the shape of the whole biblical story. Humanity first grasped at knowledge in Eden in order to stand apart from God. The wisdom books and the prophets then show the knowledge God actually desires: reverent, humble, and lived out in mercy and justice. Israel failed to live that way. Isaiah promised a Branch from Jesse on whom the Spirit would rest, including "the Spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the LORD" (Isaiah 11:2). The same Spirit who gave Bezalel knowledge to build the tabernacle would rest fully on the King who builds God's kingdom.

Jesus quoted Hosea 6:6 twice when religious leaders put ritual ahead of mercy (Matthew 9:13; 12:7). In Him, the knowledge of God that Hosea longed for became visible in a life of mercy. Isaiah 53:11 reaches the deepest point of the story. Adam grasped at knowledge to become like God and brought death. The Servant submitted His knowledge to the Father's will, even to bearing the iniquities of others, and so He justifies many. The New Testament shows this fulfilled in Jesus' death for sinners.

7. Spirit & Life

Hosea and Jeremiah let no one separate knowing God from the way we treat people. If someone has accurate beliefs but no mercy, God says that person lacks knowledge of Him. The evidence of knowing God includes defending the poor, telling the truth, and keeping faith with others, because that is what God Himself does.

Job teaches a different kind of knowledge, the kind that knows its limits. Some of the most honest words a believer can speak are Job's: "Things too wonderful for me, which I did not know." Admitting this does not end faith. It is often where faith grows deeper.

The refuge laws show God's heart toward people who caused harm without intending it. He sees the difference between malice and accident and gives a place of safety. We can trust Him to judge the heart rightly, and we should learn to do the same when we judge others.

The account of Bezalel gives dignity to ordinary skill. A craftsman's know-how can be the Spirit's gift, given so that God's dwelling place can be built.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 19:2 — Night after night the heavens declare knowledge. Creation itself teaches about its Maker.

  • Psalm 139:6 — David calls God's knowledge of him too wonderful and too high for him to attain.

  • Psalm 119:66 — The psalmist asks God to teach him good judgment and knowledge, because he believes God's commandments.

  • Isaiah 47:10 — Babylon's wisdom and knowledge led her astray and made her say, "I am, and there is no one else besides me." Knowledge can feed pride.

  • Malachi 2:7 — The priest's lips should keep knowledge, because he is the messenger of the LORD of hosts.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • (a) Lexical relative: H1844 (de'ah). This is a closely related noun for knowledge. It is used in the promise that the earth will be full of the knowledge of the LORD (Isaiah 11:9). Habakkuk 2:14 repeats the promise with the related verb yada (H3045), "to know."
