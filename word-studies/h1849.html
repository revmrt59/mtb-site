BEAT, DRIVE, KNOCK - (dâphaq, Strong's H1849)

Summary

The Hebrew verb dâphaq means to beat, strike, or push something forward by force. It appears only three times in the Old Testament, and each use shows a different kind of force. Jacob warns that driving his nursing flocks too hard for even one day would kill them (Genesis 33:13). The men of Gibeah pound on a door to drag out a guest and abuse him (Judges 19:22). In the Song of Solomon, the beloved knocks at the door of the woman he loves, asking her to open (Song of Solomon 5:2).

Because the word is so rare, the contrast among these three scenes stands out. Two of them take place at a closed door. In one, men try to force their way in and destroy the person inside. In the other, the one who loves her knocks, speaks, and waits for her to open. This contrast prepares us to hear Jesus say, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock" (Revelation 3:20). Christ has the power to break down any door. He comes to His people as the Good Shepherd who leads at a pace the weak can keep and as the Beloved who knocks and calls.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Dâphaq is a Hebrew verb for physical striking or pushing. It describes force applied to move a creature or to get through an obstacle. Its three uses come from three different parts of the Old Testament. Genesis gives a herdsman's scene, Judges records a violent night in a city, and the Song of Solomon is love poetry. Each time, the setting and the person acting decide whether the force is cruel, careless, or loving.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one basic meaning, "to strike or beat," which shows up in two practical applications:

1. To drive or push animals forward, possibly beyond their strength. Jacob uses it when warning that to "drive them hard one day" would kill the nursing flock (Genesis 33:13).

2. To beat or knock on a door. The men of Gibeah "beat on the door" (Judges 19:22), and the beloved "knocks" (Song of Solomon 5:2).

The word itself does not say whether the force is good or evil. That moral weight comes from who is striking and why.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Hebrew verbs come in several patterns, sometimes called "stems," that can change how an action is pictured. The two door scenes use different patterns.

In Judges 19:22, the verb takes a form that often describes repeated action. The picture is men pounding over and over, a crowd hammering at the house. In Song of Solomon 5:2, the verb uses the simple, ordinary form. The beloved is simply knocking. The grammar adds to what the stories already make plain. One scene is a siege against the door, and the other is a man knocking and asking to come in.

4. Theological Significance

These three verses show the difference between power that serves and power that destroys. All three involve force, and the Bible does not treat force as evil in itself. The question is whether it is used against the weak or on their behalf.

Scripture treats the doorway as a boundary of protection. Under the roof of a host, a guest was meant to be safe. At the first Passover, the blood on the doorposts marked the house the LORD would cover. When He saw it, He would "pass over the door and not allow the destroyer to come into your houses to strike you" (Exodus 12:23). God guards the door of His people.

Against that background, the men of Gibeah are doing more than damaging wood. They are attacking the boundary God gave for the protection of life. When the beloved knocks at a door and will not force it, he respects the same boundary from the other side.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 33:13 — The shepherd who will not overdrive. After years of fear, Jacob meets Esau, who has come with four hundred men (Genesis 33:1). Esau offers to travel together. Jacob declines: "My lord knows that the children are weak, and the flocks and herds which are nursing are with me. And if the men should drive them hard one day, all the flock will die." Jacob's reply may also have helped him keep a cautious distance from his brother. Even so, it reflects what twenty years of shepherding had taught him (Genesis 31:38–40). Esau's pace suited armed men. Jacob proposes instead to move slowly, at the pace of the livestock and the children (Genesis 33:14). A weak flock pushed beyond its strength does not grow stronger. It dies.

Judges 19:22 — Pounding to destroy. A Levite and his concubine find shelter with an old man in Gibeah. Then "certain men of the city, perverted men, surrounded the house and beat on the door," demanding the guest "that we may know him carnally!" The scene deliberately echoes Sodom. There, too, the men "surrounded the house" and "came near to break down the door" (Genesis 19:4, 9). At Sodom, however, God's messengers pulled Lot inside and struck the attackers with blindness (Genesis 19:10–11). At Gibeah no one rescues. The Levite pushes his concubine out to the mob. By morning she lies dead at the door with her hands on the threshold (Judges 19:25–27). This is what the beating on the door was after: entry to devour a life. Israel had sunk to the level of Sodom.

Song of Solomon 5:2 — The knock that speaks. The woman says, "I sleep, but my heart is awake; It is the voice of my beloved! He knocks, saying, 'Open for me, my sister, my love, My dove, my perfect one; For my head is covered with dew, My locks with the drops of the night.'" The knock and the voice come together. She hears him before she sees him, and his knock is a request: "Open for me." He addresses her with tenderness, and he has waited outside in the night until his hair is wet with dew. He does not force the door. She hesitates, and when she finally rises to open, he has gone, and she searches the streets for him (Song of Solomon 5:3–6). His restraint is real, and so is the loss when she delays.

Two knocks at the door. The same verb describes Gibeah's mob and the beloved's knock. The men of Gibeah pound to break in, seize, and destroy. The beloved knocks to awaken the one he loves, calls her by tender names, and leaves the door for her to open. Love and lust can make the same sound at a door, but they want opposite things. The mob wanted to take a life. The beloved wants to share his.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Both of the word's main images lead toward Jesus.

Jacob's care for the flock reflects the way God shepherds His people. The LORD "will gather the lambs with His arm, And carry them in His bosom, And gently lead those who are with young" (Isaiah 40:11). He sets His pace by what His people can bear: "For He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust" (Psalm 103:14). Jesus takes up this role as the Good Shepherd: "he goes before them; and the sheep follow him, for they know his voice" (John 10:4). A shepherd who goes ahead of the flock leads them. He does not drive them from behind.

The knock in the Song points forward to Christ's words to the lukewarm church at Laodicea: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20). Revelation 3:20 uses Greek, not dâphaq, so the connection comes from the shared image. Christians have long read the Song as a picture of Christ and His people. Whether or not one reads the whole book that way, the parallel is close. In both passages a knock comes with a voice and with a call to open, and the one knocking waits for a response. He offers shared life, sitting down to eat together. The Lord who could break any door stands outside a church that has grown cold and asks to be let in.

7. Spirit & Life

Jacob's words give a useful test of the pressures in our lives. The world, the flesh, and every system of earning God's favor push from behind with demands and deadlines. Pushed hard enough, the flock dies. Scripture describes God's children differently: "For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God" (Romans 8:14), and "if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the law" (Galatians 5:18). This comparison is an analogy rather than what Genesis 33 directly teaches, but it fits the pattern of the whole Bible. When you feel crushed under constant pressure that never lets you rest, it is worth asking whose voice is doing the pushing. The Shepherd may call you to costly obedience, but He goes ahead of you and knows how weak His lambs are.

Gibeah should also reassure people who have been hurt. The pounding at that door is the way of abusers and of the enemy who comes "to steal, and to kill, and to destroy" (John 10:10). Christ never comes to His people that way. Anyone who has known violation can be sure that God's approach is the opposite of the mob's.

The Song also carries a warning. The beloved does not stand outside indefinitely. The bride's sleepy excuses cost her his presence, and she had to search for him. When Christ's voice comes, through His Word, a conviction, or a call back to Him, the right response is to open quickly. He wants to come in and share a meal with you, and He leaves the door for you to open.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This word appears only three times, in Genesis 33:13, Judges 19:22, and Song of Solomon 5:2. All three are discussed in Section 5. Genesis 19:4–9 (the parallel scene at Sodom) and Revelation 3:20 (Christ's knock) use different words, but they help explain the two door scenes.
