UZAL - (ʼÛwzâl, Strong's H0187)

Summary

Uzal was a son of Joktan, a descendant of Shem through Eber, and his name appears in the two parallel lists of nations in Genesis 10:27 and 1 Chronicles 1:21. The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is often associated with the idea of "going" or "departing," but the evidence does not settle the question. Like his brothers Sheba, Ophir, and Havilah, Uzal stands at the head of a people who settled toward southern Arabia. Long-standing tradition connects the name with Sana'a in Yemen, which was anciently called Azal, though Scripture itself does not make that identification.

Uzal's place in the record matters because of where it sits. Eber had two sons. The line of promise ran through Peleg toward Abraham and eventually Christ, and Uzal belongs to Peleg's brother Joktan. God's Word does not drop that other branch of the family. It names Joktan's sons one by one, and the Chronicler copies the list again for Israel after the exile. The God who chose Abraham never stopped being the God who "made from one blood every nation of men" (Acts 17:26).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Uzal is the sixth of Joktan's thirteen sons listed in Genesis 10:26–29. The family line runs from Shem through Arphaxad, Salah, and Eber to Joktan (Genesis 10:22–25). That makes Peleg, the ancestor of Abraham, Uzal's uncle. Like most names in the Table of Nations, Uzal refers both to an individual ancestor and to the people descended from him. Joktan's descendants lived "from Mesha as you go toward Sephar, the mountain of the east" (Genesis 10:30). The exact locations of Mesha and Sephar are debated, but the names in this group point toward the Arabian Peninsula. Identifying Uzal with Sana'a is a traditional and widely repeated proposal, not a fact the Bible states.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. Some connect it with a Hebrew verb meaning "to go" or "to go away," which yields suggested meanings such as "wanderer" or "departure." That connection is possible, but it has not been demonstrated, and the name may come from an Arabian place name whose original sense is unknown. Scripture offers no wordplay on the name, so a theme of "wandering" should not be read into Uzal's life.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Bible tells no story about Uzal himself. Genesis 10 places him among the families that spread across the earth after the Flood, "according to their families, according to their languages, in their lands, according to their nations" (Genesis 10:31). First Chronicles 1:21 repeats the name word for word. The Chronicler opens Israel's history with Adam and the nations before narrowing to David's line, so Israel's story begins inside the story of the whole human family.

Ezekiel 27:19 may contain a later reference. In the lament over Tyre, the Hebrew includes a word that many modern translations read as "from Uzal," which would list Uzal among the distant traders who supplied Tyre with wrought iron, cassia, and cane. The NKJV reads the same word differently: "Dan and Javan paid for your wares, traversing back and forth." Because the reading is disputed and the verse is not indexed under H0187, it should be treated as a possible later mention, not a confirmed one. If the reading "from Uzal" is correct, the family named in Genesis had become a recognized trading people by the time of Tyre's commercial greatness, part of the wealthy world Ezekiel saw coming under God's judgment.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states only that Uzal was Joktan's son and part of the nations that filled the earth. It does not say Uzal's people rejected God or chased wealth, and that should not be claimed. The text does show a clear choice of lines: the promise moved through Peleg, not Joktan (Genesis 11:16–26). Yet God's choice of one line did not mean He forgot the other. Joktan's branch is named in full, and later prophets saw the far peoples of Arabia coming to Zion with their treasures. Isaiah 60:6 foresees that "all those from Sheba shall come; they shall bring gold and incense, and they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD." Scripture uses the name Sheba for more than one line, so Isaiah need not mean Uzal's brother specifically, but the direction is plain. The distant nations are headed for worship.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No passage connects Uzal directly with Christ. The connection is through the nations as a whole. God promised Abraham that all the families of the earth would be blessed in him (Genesis 12:3), and families like Uzal's are what that promise has in view. Paul told the Athenians that God set the times and boundaries of every nation "so that they should seek the Lord" (Acts 17:26–27). Revelation 7:9 pictures a multitude "of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues" before the Lamb. Revelation does not trace particular genealogies, but its picture is the broad hope that the nations descended from both Joktan and Peleg will be represented before the same throne.

5. Spirit & Life

It is easy to read past a name like Uzal, but God did not leave it out. He recorded the son of a brother whose line did not lead to Messiah, and then had the name written a second time centuries later. The world's peoples, including those far from Jerusalem, were in God's view from the beginning. That should affect how we think about people who seem remote from God's purposes. They are not remote to Him, and the gospel is meant to reach them.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:25–30 — Places Uzal among Joktan's sons, beside Peleg's line, and gives the general region of settlement.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:21 — Repeats the name in Israel's post-exilic genealogy of the nations.

  • Ezekiel 27:19 — Possible reference to Uzal as a trader with Tyre, depending on the reading of a disputed word. The NKJV does not read a place name here.

  • Acts 17:26 — God made every nation and set its times and boundaries.

  • Isaiah 60:6 — Arabian peoples bring gold and incense and praise the LORD.
