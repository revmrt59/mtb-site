GRASS, TENDER GRASS, HERB - (desheʼ, Strong's H1877)

Summary

Desheʼ is the Hebrew word for fresh, young grass: the soft green growth that covers the ground after rain. The NKJV usually translates it "grass" or "herb," sometimes "tender grass," and once "green" in the familiar line "He makes me to lie down in green pastures" (Psalm 23:2). This tender growth depends on rain and light. When they come it springs up quickly, and when they are withheld it fails just as quickly. That makes it one of Scripture's clearest pictures of life that depends entirely on what God sends.

The Bible uses this tender grass in several ways. It appears among the first living things God calls forth at creation. Moses compares his teaching to rain falling on it, and David compares a righteous king to grass springing up in sunshine after rain. The Shepherd of Psalm 23 leads His sheep into pastures of it. The wicked wither like it, and when God judges a land, it disappears. Isaiah reaches for it to promise that the bones of God's people "shall flourish like grass" (Isaiah 66:14). The same small plant can show God's provision, the brevity of rebellion, and the coming renewal of everything He has made.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Desheʼ belongs to the farming and herding world of ancient Israel. There the rains of late autumn and winter turned brown hillsides green almost overnight, and the summer sun could scorch the same ground just as fast. Shepherds watched for this new growth because it fed their flocks. Its fifteen occurrences fall across Genesis, Deuteronomy, the historical books, Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Isaiah, and Jeremiah. Most are in poetry, where the writers draw on this everyday sight to speak about God and people.

Hebrew had several words for vegetation. Desheʼ often stands beside ʿesev, the broader word for plants or herbs, and chatsir, another word for grass. Within those pairings, desheʼ is the word for the fresh green shoot.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Desheʼ means new, tender vegetation, especially young grass. Translations vary between "grass," "herb," "tender grass," and "green," but they all describe the same thing: the first green growth that rises from the ground. It does not mean a crop or a mature plant. Its distinctive quality is freshness, and with freshness comes vulnerability. One sense covers its uses.

In Jeremiah 50:11 the NKJV reads "a heifer threshing grain." The Hebrew wording there is difficult, and some translations render the line "a heifer at grass," which pictures a well-fed animal grazing on fresh pasture.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses desheʼ in three main ways. In the literal sense it names green growth that feeds animals or marks the healthy return of the seasons (Job 6:5; Proverbs 27:25). In the life-giving sense it is the growth that responds to rain, light, and God's care (Deuteronomy 32:2; 2 Samuel 23:4; Psalm 23:2). In the sense of withering, its fragility pictures how quickly the wicked fall or a land collapses under judgment (Psalm 37:2; Isaiah 15:6; Jeremiah 14:5).

The verb attached to the grass decides which picture is in view. Grass that is "springing out of the earth" speaks of blessing. Grass that "withers" speaks of judgment. Grass that will "flourish" speaks of renewal. In every case the poetry works by simile, and the writers say directly what they are comparing.

4. Theological Significance

The first thing desheʼ teaches about God is that He gives life by speaking. "Then God said, 'Let the earth bring forth grass'... and it was so" (Genesis 1:11). The earth produced what God commanded, and "God saw that it was good" (Genesis 1:12). The tender grass that covers the hills after rain still bears witness to the voice that first called it out of the ground.

Second, this word shows that life depends on God. Tender young grass depends on rain and light for its growth. Scripture applies that dependence to God's people. They live by His word, His appointed rule, and His shepherding care, and they wither without them.

Third, God is not neutral toward the world He made. He sends rain and makes the grass flourish, and He can also withhold rain and let it fail. When grass disappears in the prophets, people have refused God. When it springs up again, He has turned toward them in mercy.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Rain and light: Deuteronomy 32:2 and 2 Samuel 23:4. Moses opens his final song with a prayer: "Let my teaching drop as the rain, My speech distill as the dew, As raindrops on the tender herb." He wants God's words to reach Israel the way soft rain reaches young growth, gently and steadily, keeping it alive. A people cut off from God's word dries up the way new grass does in a rainless spring.

At the end of his life, David describes the king God desires: one who is just, "Ruling in the fear of God" (2 Samuel 23:3). Such a ruler "shall be like the light of the morning when the sun rises... Like the tender grass springing out of the earth, By clear shining after rain" (23:4). Rain is not enough by itself. Fresh growth also needs the sun that follows it. Moses supplies the rain of God's teaching, and David adds the sunlight of righteous rule, under which God's people come alive like a field after a storm. Psalm 72:6 uses the same picture for the king: "He shall come down like rain upon the grass before mowing." David knew his own house fell short of this ideal (2 Samuel 23:5), and his words reach toward a King still to come.

Pasture and withering: Psalm 23:2 and Psalm 37:2. In Psalm 23 the Shepherd "makes me to lie down in green pastures," literally pastures of desheʼ. A shepherd had to know where fresh grass grew, and in a dry land that knowledge kept a flock alive. Sheep that lie down in the middle of their food have stopped searching. The picture is one of complete provision and peace.

Psalm 37 uses the same word to describe the wicked: "For they shall soon be cut down like the grass, And wither as the green herb" (37:2). David is writing to people tempted to envy those who prosper through evil. Their success is real, but it is as short-lived as spring grass under a hot wind. The same green growth that feeds the Shepherd's sheep becomes, for those who defy God, a sign of how briefly they will last. Second Kings 19:26 (repeated in Isaiah 37:27) applies this to the nations crushed by Assyria. They were "as the grass of the field And the green herb," because the Lord, and not Sennacherib's own strength, had decided their fall.

Failure and flourishing: Jeremiah 14:5 and Isaiah 66:14. During a drought sent as judgment, Jeremiah gives a heartbreaking detail: "the deer also gave birth in the field, But left because there was no grass." When God withholds rain, the natural order breaks down, and even a mother's instinct gives way.

Isaiah 66:14 turns that picture around. God promises Jerusalem comfort like a mother's (66:13), and then: "your heart shall rejoice, And your bones shall flourish like grass." Bones were the frame of a person's strength, and dry bones were the last remains of death (compare Ezekiel 37:2, "indeed they were very dry"). Isaiah sets the driest image beside the freshest. God will make worn-out strength green again. The same verse adds, "The hand of the LORD shall be known to His servants, And His indignation to His enemies." The two uses of desheʼ, flourishing and withering, stand side by side as two outcomes of one coming day.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Across Scripture, desheʼ follows the shape of the whole biblical story. It appears at creation when God speaks it into being, it fails under judgment when His people turn away, and it returns in the prophets' promise of a renewed people. Jesus stands at the center of each stage. He is the Word through whom all things were made (John 1:3). He is the King of 2 Samuel 23, ruling in the fear of God, under whose reign His people grow like grass after rain. He calls Himself the Good Shepherd (John 10:11), the Shepherd of Psalm 23 who leads His sheep into pasture and lays down His life for them.

Isaiah's promise that bones will flourish like grass looks forward to the resurrection life Christ secured. The New Testament does not quote Isaiah 66:14 directly, but its hope runs in the same direction: the God who brings green life out of bare ground will raise the bodies of His people.

7. Spirit & Life

Tender grass is not ashamed of being dependent. Its tender growth depends on rain falling and light shining. Believers are made the same way. Spiritual life is received as God's word falls on us, as we live under Christ's rule, and as we stay in the pastures where the Shepherd has placed us. When a Christian feels dry, the remedy begins with returning to the rain of God's word, which "shall not return to Me void" (Isaiah 55:11).

Job 38:27 adds something tender. God asks Job who sends rain "to satisfy the desolate waste, And cause to spring forth the growth of tender grass," and the surrounding verse describes a land where no one lives (38:26). God waters grass that no farmer will cut and no person will ever see. His care is not limited to places that are useful or noticed. A believer in a hidden, unseen stretch of life is not overlooked. The God who greens the empty wilderness sees that place as well.

Psalm 37 also warns us. Prosperity that has no root in God is grass in a heat wave. Psalm 23 offers the alternative: to lie down where the Shepherd has led, and to let Him decide where the green grass is.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 6:5 — Job observes that a wild donkey does not bray when it has grass. Contentment follows provision, and Job's complaint shows that he has none.

  • Proverbs 27:25 — The new growth appears after the hay is removed. It belongs to a reliable rhythm God built into the seasons, which the wise farmer trusts and works with.

  • Isaiah 15:6 — In the oracle against Moab, "The grass fails, there is nothing green." The failure of fresh growth matches the collapse of a proud nation.

  • Isaiah 37:27 — Isaiah's record of the Assyrian crisis repeats 2 Kings 19:26, emphasizing that the Lord, not Assyria, controls the rise and fall of nations.

  • Jeremiah 50:11 — Babylon, the destroyer of God's heritage, has "grown fat like a heifer" (some translations "at grass"). It fed on plunder, and judgment is coming.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6212 (ʿesev), herb, plants — a distinct word frequently paired with desheʼ. In Genesis 1:11 desheʼ is "grass" and ʿesev is "the herb that yields seed." In Deuteronomy 32:2 desheʼ is the "tender herb" and ʿesev the "grass."

  • H2682 (chatsir), grass — a distinct word used beside desheʼ in Psalm 37:2 ("cut down like the grass") and in 2 Kings 19:26 ("the grass on the housetops"). It is also the word in Isaiah 40:6–8, "All flesh is grass," which 1 Peter 1:24 quotes.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Grass is not a fixed code in Scripture. The writers state what they are comparing, using "like" or "as": the righteous king is like springing grass (2 Samuel 23:4), the wicked wither as the green herb (Psalm 37:2), and restored bones flourish like grass (Isaiah 66:14). The point of comparison is always what the grass is doing.

Grass on the housetops. In 2 Kings 19:26, the green herb (desheʼ) stands next to "the grass on the housetops," which uses another word. Israelite roofs were flat and covered with packed earth. Seeds blew onto them, sprouted in the rains, and burned away because the soil was shallow and the sun was strong. The image fits the nations raised up in Assyria's path, and it is a sharp warning to anyone who flourishes high above the ground without roots: the height that makes them visible also exposes them to the heat.

Grass before the sun. In Genesis 1, grass appears on the third day, before the sun and moon are set in the sky on the fourth (Genesis 1:14–19). The first tender growth lived under the light God made on the first day, by His word. This is a reflection drawn from the sequence of the narrative, not a teaching the text spells out. It still offers a fitting picture, and it looks ahead to the new creation, where the city has no need of the sun because the Lord God gives it light (Revelation 22:5).
