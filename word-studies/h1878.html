MAKE FAT, PROSPER, ANOINT, ACCEPT - (dâshên, Strong's H1878)

Summary

The Hebrew verb dâshên means "to be fat" or "to make fat." In the ancient world, fatness meant richness, health, and the best portion of a thing. The same word can describe a prosperous life, a head soaked with fragrant oil, a sacrifice God receives with favor, and even the fat-soaked ashes cleared from the altar. The NKJV translates it many ways, including "accept," "anoint," "rich," "healthy," "prospered," "fat," "overflowing," and "saturated."

This small word, used only eleven times, covers a wide range of experience. In Psalm 23 the Lord is a Host who drenches His guest's head with oil while enemies look on. In Psalm 20 His people pray that He will "accept" a burnt offering. Proverbs promises this richness to the generous and to "he who trusts in the LORD." Deuteronomy warns that Israel would grow fat and then turn to other gods. Isaiah takes the language of the altar and applies it to the day of God's judgment. Taken together, these passages ask what richness is for, and whether we enjoy it with God or apart from Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

For people who farmed and kept flocks, fat was a sign that an animal or a household was thriving. The fat portions of a sacrifice belonged to the Lord, and the altar fire turned them into the greasy ashes that had to be cleared away each day. Dâshên lives in both of these worlds. It shows up in the instructions for the tabernacle altar, in Israel's worship songs, in the wisdom of Proverbs, in Moses' warning before Israel entered the land, and in Isaiah's oracle against Edom. Wherever it appears, it describes something rich and filled up, whether that fullness is good or terrible.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core idea is fatness, the condition of being rich, full, and saturated. Context determines which of several related uses is meant:

1. To clear away the fat ashes of the altar, removing what the fire has left of the sacrifice (Exodus 27:3; Numbers 4:13).

2. To regard an offering as rich, that is, to accept it with favor (Psalm 20:3).

3. To make rich, healthy, or prosperous, whether the head with oil (Psalm 23:5) or the soul and body with well-being (Proverbs 11:25; 15:30).

4. To grow fat or become saturated, sometimes to the point of complacency (Deuteronomy 31:20) or in a picture of slaughter (Isaiah 34:6–7).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb makes a large difference. When God is the actor, dâshên describes His favor: He accepts the offering, He anoints the head, and the trusting person "will be prospered" by Him. When the people themselves "grow fat," as in Deuteronomy 31:20, the word describes a fullness that has stopped looking toward the Giver. In Isaiah 34 the sword of the LORD is the thing "made overflowing with fatness," and the word becomes frightening.

Proverbs uses the word in a passive sense three times: the generous soul "will be made rich" (11:25), the diligent soul "shall be made rich" (13:4), and the one who trusts "will be prospered" (28:25). The wording leaves room for a Giver behind the gift, and Proverbs 28:25 names Him outright. Proverbs 15:30 uses the verb actively: "a good report makes the bones healthy."

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God is generous by nature. He does not hand out a bare minimum. The vocabulary of fatness is the vocabulary of abundance, and Scripture uses it freely for what God gives.

That abundance has a moral and relational shape. In Proverbs, richness comes to "the generous soul," while "he who waters will also be watered himself" (11:25). It belongs to "the diligent," while the lazy man "desires, and has nothing" (13:4). It comes to the one who trusts, set against the proud heart that "stirs up strife" (28:25). Grasping, laziness, and pride lead to emptiness. Open hands, faithful work, and reliance on the Lord lead to fullness. Proverbs 15:30 adds that this vitality reaches inward: "a good report makes the bones healthy."

The same word also shows that God's holiness is as complete as His kindness. The richness that marks His blessing also marks His judgment. Neither one is done halfway.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 20:3: an offering God receives. Before the king rides into battle, the people pray, "May He remember all your offerings, And accept your burnt sacrifice." The word for "accept" is dâshên. It is probably best understood as "regard as fat," treating the offering as rich and worthy. The tabernacle uses the same verb for removing the ashes from the altar (Exodus 27:3; Numbers 4:13), so the prayer is tied closely to the fire. A burnt offering that God accepted did not stay on the altar untouched. It was consumed. Scripture records moments when fire from the Lord fell and consumed an offering as a visible sign of His approval (Leviticus 9:24; 1 Kings 18:38). Israel prayed for a king whose offering would be received in that way, because a king whose sacrifice God accepted could go into battle with the people's confidence and God's favor.

Psalm 23:5: the Host who overflows. "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies; You anoint my head with oil; My cup runs over." David does not use mashach, the usual word for ceremonial anointing, the kind that set apart kings and priests. He says, in effect, "You make my head fat with oil." At this point the psalm moves from the pasture to the banquet hall, and the Shepherd becomes a Host. In that world, rich oil on a guest's head was a mark of honor and refreshment. David's enemies have not gone away; they are watching. God's answer to their presence is to set a table and pour more oil than a guest could need. The cup that "runs over" belongs to the same picture.

Deuteronomy 31:20: fat and forgetful. Just before Israel enters the land, God tells Moses what will happen: "they have eaten and filled themselves and grown fat, then they will turn to other gods and serve them; and they will provoke Me and break My covenant." The good land, the milk and honey, and the full table were all God's gift. The danger lay in the fatness itself once it was separated from dependence on Him. Moses' song in the next chapter makes the same point with different words: Jeshurun grew fat and kicked, then forsook the God who made him (Deuteronomy 32:15). Comfort can do what hardship never did.

Isaiah 34:6–7: the altar turned toward judgment. "The sword of the LORD is filled with blood, It is made overflowing with fatness... For the LORD has a sacrifice in Bozrah, And a great slaughter in the land of Edom." In verse 7 the dust is "saturated with fatness." Isaiah takes the language of the altar and applies it to the battlefield: lambs, goats, rams, and bulls appear, but the victims are the enemies of God. The word that described the accepted offering and the anointed guest now describes ground soaked by judgment. This passage stands in deliberate contrast to the altar of grace. At the altar, a substitute is consumed and the worshiper goes free. At Bozrah there is no substitute, and the rebellion itself is put on the altar. God's justice is as complete as His provision.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prayer of Psalm 20:3 finds its answer in Jesus. Paul describes Christ as having given Himself "an offering and a sacrifice to God" that was pleasing to Him (Ephesians 5:2, paraphrased), and Hebrews teaches that His single offering accomplished what the daily altar fires never could (Hebrews 10:10–14). The ashes cleared from the bronze altar every day testified that no sacrifice had yet finished the work. Christ's offering was received, and His resurrection is the Father's public sign that He accepted it.

Psalm 23 also points ahead. Jesus calls Himself the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11), and the Gospels repeatedly picture the kingdom as a feast to which He welcomes unlikely guests. The anointed head of Psalm 23 belongs to the guest of the Shepherd-King.

Isaiah 34 belongs to the larger prophetic picture of the Lord coming from Edom and Bozrah in garments stained by judgment (Isaiah 63:1–6), a picture Revelation takes up when it shows the returning Christ, whose robe is dipped in blood and who treads the winepress of God's wrath (Revelation 19:13–15). The story allows only two altars. Either the accepted Substitute is offered for us, or the sword of judgment falls on us.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 23:5 shows us God's temperament. He does not wait until our enemies are gone before He honors His people. He sets the table while they watch, and He pours the oil until it runs down. The believer who feels surrounded can expect more than survival from the Lord. He gives honor and refreshment in the very place of pressure.

Deuteronomy 31:20 belongs beside that verse. Days when the cup runs over are the days when the heart most easily drifts. God foresaw this before Israel ever tasted the land. Gratitude must be kept up deliberately, by remembering the Giver at the full table as well as in the wilderness.

Proverbs 11:25 offers a way to keep the blessing from going sour: "he who waters will also be watered himself." Fatness that flows outward stays healthy. When it is hoarded, it turns into the complacency Moses warned about. Proverbs 28:25 traces both outcomes to the heart's root: pride stirs up strife, but trust in the Lord brings richness. A life of open hands and quiet reliance on God is what Scripture means by prosperity.

Isaiah 34 keeps all of this sober. God's abundance is not soft. Because the Lord accepted the one Sacrifice, those who come to Him through Christ receive the anointed head and the overflowing cup in place of the sword.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Exodus 27:3: The bronze altar needed "pans to receive its ashes." The richness of sacrifice left a residue that had to be carried away, a daily reminder that the work of the altar was never finished.

Numbers 4:13: Before the altar was moved, Aaron and his sons were to "take away the ashes from the altar, and spread a purple cloth over it." Even the cleanup of worship was done with care and honor.

Proverbs 15:30: "A good report makes the bones healthy." Good news reaches all the way to a person's inner strength, giving a fitting picture of what the good news of Christ does for the soul.
