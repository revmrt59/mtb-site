WOE - (ʼôwy, Strong's H0188)

Summary

ʼôwy is a short Hebrew cry that names ruin and says whom it has reached. On some lips it is lament: Isaiah before the throne of God cries, "Woe is me, for I am undone!" On others it is panic, as when the Philistines learn that God has come into Israel's camp. From a prophet speaking for the Lord, it becomes a sentence of judgment: "Woe to the wicked!" Because the same cry can carry confession, terror, grief, or verdict, readers must notice who speaks and over whom. The word appears most often in the prophets, especially Jeremiah. Its most important moment is Isaiah 6, where a man faces the holiness of the King, whom John later identifies with Christ's glory. There the cry of ruin is answered by cleansing and then by a call to go.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

ʼôwy does not describe ruin from a distance. It declares that ruin has arrived or is about to arrive. The speaker usually names the one affected, such as "me," "us," "you," "them," or "the bloody city." He then usually gives the reason, often with "for" or "because." Isaiah says "Woe is me, for I am undone!" The Philistines say "Woe to us! For such a thing has never happened before." The Lord says of Israel, "Woe to them, for they have fled from Me!" (Hosea 7:13).

That pattern is the key to the word. ʼôwy is an exclamation, but it is rarely an empty one. It points straight at the cause of disaster, whether that cause is sin, the approach of God, an invading army, or a calling that has become almost too heavy to carry.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

A cry over one's own condition. Isaiah's "Woe is me" (Isaiah 6:5) comes when his eyes have "seen the King, The LORD of hosts." He confesses unclean lips, both his own and his people's. Jeremiah uses the same cry over the cost of his ministry: "Woe is me, my mother, That you have borne me, A man of strife" (Jeremiah 15:10). His scribe Baruch echoes it: "Woe is me now! For the LORD has added grief to my sorrow" (Jeremiah 45:3). The cry can also be communal. The daughter of Zion spreads her hands and gasps, "Woe is me now, for my soul is weary Because of murderers!" (Jeremiah 4:31). In Lamentations 5:16 the survivors of Jerusalem mourn the fallen crown and add a confession: woe to us, for we have sinned. In that verse the lament has become repentance.

The alarm of people facing defeat. In 1 Samuel 4:7–8 the Philistines hear Israel's shout when the ark arrives. Their response is "Woe to us!" followed by a frightened question: "Who will deliver us from the hand of these mighty gods?" Their theology is confused, yet their fear is not foolish. The cry did not foretell an Israelite victory. The Philistines fought on, defeated Israel, and captured the ark (4:10–11). In the next chapter, however, "the hand of the LORD was heavy" on Ashdod and then on the other Philistine cities where the ark was taken (5:6–12), showing that Israel's defeat had not diminished His power. In Jeremiah the same cry of being overrun falls on Judah itself, as the people say woe to us, for we are plundered (Jeremiah 4:13).

A prophetic sentence of judgment. Spoken for God, ʼôwy becomes a verdict. Isaiah says of brazen sinners, "Woe to their soul! For they have brought evil upon themselves" (Isaiah 3:9). Ezekiel doubles it: "'Woe, woe to you!' says the Lord GOD" (16:23). He also pronounces woe on Jerusalem as "the bloody city" (24:6, 9). Even here the word can carry grief. In Jeremiah 13:27 the cry of woe over Jerusalem is joined to a pleading question: will you still not be made clean? Judgment is announced, but the speaker has not stopped longing for the city's cleansing.

A name for misery. Proverbs 23:29 uses the word almost as a noun: "Who has woe? Who has sorrow?" The answer is the drunkard, the one who lingers over wine (23:30). Here woe is the bitter condition a foolish life produces.

3. Biblical Connections & Distinctions

Hebrew has another, more common interjection usually transliterated hôy. It is widely recognized as the opening word of formal prophetic woe series, such as the string of woes in Isaiah 5. The two words overlap in judgment settings, so readers should not force a sharp contrast between them. Still, ʼôwy is the word behind the most personal cries, especially "Woe is me." The English "woe" can also stand for other Hebrew terms. Psalm 120:5 reads "Woe is me" in the NKJV, but it is not among this word's occurrences.

One connection within this word's own uses is striking. Numbers 21:29 preserves an old taunt song: woe to you, Moab. Centuries later, Jeremiah 48:46 takes up that very cry against Moab again. An ancient woe over a proud nation had not expired. God brought it back into force through His prophet.

The strongest canonical link is explicit. After quoting Isaiah, John writes, "These things Isaiah said when he saw His glory and spoke of Him" (John 12:41). The King whose holiness drew out Isaiah's "Woe is me" is the glory John saw in Jesus. In the Gospels Jesus also pronounces woes on the scribes and Pharisees (Matthew 23), using the Greek ouai, which is a different word. In this He stands in the line of the prophets. Like Jeremiah, He joins woe to grief, for the same chapter ends with His lament over Jerusalem and His longing to gather her children (Matthew 23:37).

4. Spirit & Life

Isaiah's cry shows the right human response to God's holiness. He does not walk out of the temple feeling affirmed. He feels undone. Yet the woe is not the end of the scene. A seraph touches his lips with a coal from the altar and declares his iniquity taken away. Then the Lord asks whom He shall send (Isaiah 6:6–8). The man who said "Woe is me" becomes the man who says "Here am I! Send me." God does not leave the confessing sinner in his woe. He cleanses him and then sends him.

Hosea shows the opposite direction. "Woe to them, for they have fled from Me!" (7:13) names Israel's real disaster: running from the One who redeemed them. Two chapters later the woe falls when God departs from them (9:12). Whether the people flee God or God withdraws from them, the ruin is the same, because life apart from Him is the woe.

Jeremiah and Baruch show that faithful servants also cry this word. God did not scold Baruch for his "Woe is me." He told him not to seek great things for himself and promised to preserve his life wherever he went (Jeremiah 45:4–5). God listens to that honest grief, and He answers it with Himself rather than with ease.

5. Reading Guidance

Ask who is crying woe and over whom. A pronoun helps but does not settle everything. "Woe to us" is panic in 1 Samuel 4 but confession in Lamentations 5:16. "Woe to you" is a verdict in Ezekiel 16 but carries longing in Jeremiah 13:27. Let the reason that follows the cry tell you which it is.
