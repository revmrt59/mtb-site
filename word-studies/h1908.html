HADAD - (Hădad, Strong's H1908)

Summary

Hadad is the name of at least three different Edomite men in the Old Testament. Two were early kings of Edom, listed among the rulers who reigned in the land of Esau's descendants before Israel had a king (Genesis 36:31–39; 1 Chronicles 1:43–51). The third, often called "Hadad the Edomite," is the one whose story Scripture actually tells. He was a royal child who escaped Joab's slaughter of Edom's men, grew up honored in Pharaoh's court, and later returned to trouble Solomon (1 Kings 11:14–22, 25). The name most likely means "thunderer." It is the name of the storm god worshiped across Syria and Canaan, the god also known as Baal. That meaning is a probable derivation rather than a certain one, and it tells us about the religious world of Edom, not about the character of any of these men.

The weight of the name rests on one sentence: "Now the LORD raised up an adversary against Solomon, Hadad the Edomite" (1 Kings 11:14). Solomon had once told Hiram that God had given him rest, with no adversary troubling him (1 Kings 5:4). When Solomon turned his heart to other gods, the same God who had given that rest sent an adversary. Scripture records no worship of the LORD by Hadad. Even so, he became the kind of correction God had warned David his son would face, the "rod of men" (2 Samuel 7:14). His life also follows a pattern that runs from Moses to Jesus: a life is threatened by a king, followed by flight and a return after those who sought that life are dead. Hadad came back to bring discipline on David's house. Jesus, the greater Son of David, came back to save.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H1908 covers twelve occurrences in eleven verses. The name appears twice in 1 Kings 11:21. In 1 Kings 11:17 the Hebrew spells it Adad, a variant that older indexes list separately (H0111), which is why some concordances count thirteen occurrences; both spellings refer to the same Edomite. These occurrences belong to three separate men, and they should not be merged into one figure.

  • Hadad the Edomite (1 Kings 11:14, 17, 19, 21, 25) belonged to Edom's royal line. Scripture calls him "a descendant of the king in Edom" (11:14), but it does not name his father. He lived during the reigns of David and Solomon.

  • Hadad son of Bedad (Genesis 36:35–36; 1 Chronicles 1:46–47) was an early king of Edom whose city was Avith.

  • Hadad of Pai (1 Chronicles 1:50–51) was a later king in the same list. His city was Pai and his wife was Mehetabel. The parallel list in Genesis 36:39 calls him Hadar and his city Pau. In Hebrew script the letters d and r look very much alike, and most interpreters treat the two spellings as the same king. The Genesis form is indexed separately and is not counted among these occurrences.

The text never says how Hadad the Edomite was related to the earlier kings named Hadad. A family line is possible, but it is not stated.

The name probably comes from the name of the ancient Semitic storm god Hadad. It is tentatively explained as "thunderer." Hadad was the great weather and fertility god of the Arameans and Canaanites, and Baal was his usual title. Using his name for a king was a way of placing the king under that god's protection. That the name recurs among Edom's kings and royal line shows how much Edom shared in the religion of its neighbors, though how these men were related is not stated. Related names built on the same god belong to separate entries. These include Ben-Hadad, the dynastic name of Aramean kings (indexed separately as H1130), and Hadadezer king of Zobah (1 Kings 11:23). None of them should be counted as occurrences of this entry. The meaning of the name is the religious background of these men. It is not a key to their lives.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Hadad the Edomite

The story begins in David's wars. After David's victory in the Valley of Salt, Edom became subject to Israel, and the record adds that "the LORD preserved David wherever he went" (2 Samuel 8:13–14). First Kings supplies a harder detail. Joab "remained there with all Israel" for six months, "until he had cut down every male in Edom" (1 Kings 11:15–16). Within that slaughter, some of the king's servants rescued one boy. "Hadad was still a little child" (11:17). They fled south through Midian and Paran, picked up men along the way, and reached Egypt (11:18).

Pharaoh received the boy generously. He gave Hadad a house, food, and land. In time "Hadad found great favor in the sight of Pharaoh," so much that Pharaoh gave him the sister of Queen Tahpenes as his wife (11:19). Their son Genubath was weaned by the queen herself and "was in Pharaoh's household among the sons of Pharaoh" (11:20). The refugee had become family to the throne of Egypt.

Then news came from the north. "When Hadad heard in Egypt that David rested with his fathers, and that Joab the commander of the army was dead, Hadad said to Pharaoh, 'Let me depart, that I may go to my own country'" (11:21). Pharaoh could not understand it: "But what have you lacked with me, that suddenly you seek to go to your own country?" Hadad's answer was blunt: "Nothing, but do let me go anyway" (11:22). Egypt had given him everything except Edom. The Hebrew text does not describe his return in detail. It does say that Rezon troubled Israel "besides the trouble that Hadad caused" (11:25), which confirms that Hadad troubled Solomon. That he did so from Edom is a reasonable inference, but the account does not narrate his return or say when his attacks began.

The Early Kings of Edom

Hadad son of Bedad succeeded Husham of the land of the Temanites. Genesis remembers him as the king who struck Midian in the field of Moab, a brief glimpse of old border wars east of the Jordan. When he died, Samlah of Masrekah reigned (Genesis 36:35–36; 1 Chronicles 1:46–47). Hadad of Pai appears near the end of the list. His wife, Mehetabel, is named as "Mehetabel the daughter of Matred, the daughter of Mezahab" (1 Chronicles 1:50). After the note "Hadad died also," the list turns to the chiefs of Edom (1 Chronicles 1:50–51). These lists show that Esau's descendants had an organized kingdom before Israel did (Genesis 36:31). God had told Rebekah that two nations would come from the twins in her womb (Genesis 25:23), but He did not specify which would first have kings.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The text states plainly who sent Hadad: "the LORD raised up an adversary against Solomon" (1 Kings 11:14). The account follows directly after the LORD's anger at Solomon's idolatry and His announcement that the kingdom would be torn from Solomon's son (11:9–13). The Hebrew word for "adversary" here is satan. In this passage it means a human opponent, someone who stands in the way, not the devil. The word repeats what Solomon himself had said. In the days of his faithfulness he told Hiram that the LORD had given him rest on every side, so that there was neither adversary nor evil occurrence (1 Kings 5:4). Peace had been God's gift, and Solomon learned that the gift could be withdrawn.

Scripture also points back to God's promise to David. God said that if David's son committed iniquity, He would chasten him with the rod of men, yet His mercy would not depart from him as it had from Saul (2 Samuel 7:14–15). Hadad, along with Rezon (1 Kings 11:23–25) and Jeroboam (11:26–40), was that rod in human form. God's mercy also kept its limits. Solomon kept his throne, and one tribe was preserved for David's sake (11:12–13).

Some further points are reasonable inferences rather than things the text says outright. The narrator ties Hadad's existence to Joab's six months of killing in Edom. Scripture does not openly condemn that campaign, and David's victories are credited to the LORD (2 Samuel 8:14). Still, the account invites reflection, because the law had told Israel, "You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7). The child who escaped Joab's sword became the trouble of Joab's king's son. The placement of the account also matters. Hadad asked to leave Egypt after hearing that David and Joab had died, though Kings does not say how soon he left or when his attacks began, and the writer tells his story at the point of Solomon's decline. The writer is showing what these adversaries meant: their trouble was God's answer to a divided heart. Hadad's restlessness also echoes Isaac's word that Esau would one day break Jacob's yoke from his neck (Genesis 27:40). Edom did break free later, in the days of Jehoram (2 Kings 8:20–22).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hadad's life follows a pattern that Scripture traces through some of its most important figures. Moses fled a king who sought his life and found a home and a wife in Midian. He asked his father-in-law to let him go back, and he returned once the LORD told him that all the men who sought his life were dead (Exodus 2:15–21; 4:18–19). Hadad passed through Midian, found a wife in Pharaoh's house, and asked Pharaoh to let him go once David and Joab were dead. The resemblance is clear, though Scripture never draws it out. It is best read as a deliberate literary echo. The writer tells the story of an Edomite in the shape of Israel's own deliverance, except that his return brings trouble on Israel instead of rescue.

The same pattern reaches the Gospels. Matthew describes a king who ordered the killing of Bethlehem's boys, a royal child carried to Egypt, and an angel announcing that those who sought the young Child's life were dead (Matthew 2:13–21). Matthew's wording deliberately echoes Exodus 4:19, so Moses is the explicit link. Hadad's story stands alongside these accounts as a striking thematic parallel, not as a stated type of Christ. The contrast is what teaches. Hadad came out of Egypt as God's rod of discipline on David's son. Jesus came out of Egypt as David's true Son, the one in whom God's faithful love for David's house reaches its goal. He took the chastening on Himself so that His people could receive mercy.

5. Spirit & Life

Hadad's story shows that God governs the nations for the sake of His people. Solomon's troubles did not begin as foreign policy failures. They began in his heart, when his wives turned it after other gods (1 Kings 11:4). God answered by raising up Hadad, a man with every reason to remember what Edom had lost. The adversaries God permits can be His way of calling a wandering heart back to Himself. That is sobering, but it is also a mercy. God did not abandon Solomon's line to silence and slow decay. He disturbed it, as a father disciplines a son (2 Samuel 7:14; Hebrews 12:6).

The account also shows that the past is never simply over. Hadad escaped Joab's six months of killing in Edom as a little child, together with some of his father's Edomite servants (1 Kings 11:16–17), and he stayed in Egypt until David and Joab were dead. Hadad's words to Pharaoh, "Nothing, but do let me go anyway," show that comfort could not quiet a heart set on another country. His stated longing was for his own country. A desire for vindication is an understandable inference, but the text does not state it. Believers are called to a better country (Hebrews 11:16). Yet his restlessness raises a fair question for each of us: whether any amount of favor and provision could ever replace what our hearts were made for.

6. Key Scriptures

  • 1 Kings 11:14 – The LORD raises up Hadad as an adversary. This is the central theological statement about the name.

  • 1 Kings 11:15–22 – Hadad's escape from Joab's slaughter, his favor in Egypt, his family ties to Pharaoh, and his insistence on going home.

  • 1 Kings 11:25 – Confirms the continuing trouble Hadad caused Solomon.

  • 1 Kings 11:9–13 – The idolatry and judgment that explain why the adversaries came.

  • 1 Kings 5:4 – Solomon's earlier claim of rest with no adversary, which Hadad's story reverses.

  • 2 Samuel 7:14–15 – God's warning of "the rod of men" and His promise of lasting mercy to David's house.

  • 2 Samuel 8:13–14 – David's conquest of Edom, the background of Hadad's flight.

  • Genesis 36:35–36; 1 Chronicles 1:46–47 – Hadad son of Bedad, king of Edom.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:50–51 (compare Genesis 36:39) – Hadad of Pai, husband of Mehetabel.

  • Exodus 4:18–19; Matthew 2:13–21 – The flight-and-return pattern that Hadad's story echoes.
