HADORAM - (Hădôwrâm, Strong's H1913)

Summary

Hadoram is the name of three different people in the Old Testament, and they should not be confused. The first is a son of Joktan, listed among Shem's descendants in the Table of Nations, whose family settled in Arabia (Genesis 10:27; 1 Chronicles 1:21). The second is a prince of Hamath, the son of King Tou, sent with gifts of gold, silver, and bronze to congratulate David on his victory over Hadadezer (1 Chronicles 18:10). The third is the royal official over the labor force. Rehoboam sent him to the northern tribes after they rejected the house of David, and they stoned him to death (2 Chronicles 10:18). The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is commonly thought to be foreign, probably Aramean, and to mean "Hadad is exalted," after the storm god of the Arameans. That proposal fits the prince of Hamath best and is less secure for the other two.

Though the name appears only four times, two of these men stand at turning points in Israel's kingdom. The prince of Hamath arrives when David's reign is rising, and David dedicates the prince's gifts to the LORD. The official dies the day that reign's unity collapses, when the kingdom Solomon had burdened with a "heavy yoke" tears in two. Between these two scenes lies a sober lesson about kingship. When God's king is honored, the wealth of nations flows toward the LORD's house. When a king rules by pressure and pride, his own people revolt. Both scenes point beyond themselves to the true Son of David. The kings of the earth will one day bring their glory to Him, and He tells the weary, "My yoke is easy and My burden is light" (Matthew 11:30).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H1913 is one Hebrew name borne by three separate people.

1. Hadoram the Joktanite, a son of Joktan in the line of Shem, Arphaxad, and Eber (Genesis 10:21–27; 1 Chronicles 1:17–21). He represents a family or tribe that settled in Arabia.

2. Hadoram of Hamath, son of Tou (spelled Toi in 2 Samuel), king of Hamath, an Aramean kingdom in central Syria north of Damascus (1 Chronicles 18:9–10). In the parallel account he is called Joram (2 Samuel 8:10), which is a separately indexed name (H3141).

3. Hadoram the official over the labor force under Rehoboam (2 Chronicles 10:18). In the parallel account of the same event he appears as Adoram (H151; 1 Kings 12:18). An Adoram also served late in David's reign (2 Samuel 20:24), and Adoniram the son of Abda served under Solomon (H141; 1 Kings 4:6). These may be one man with a long career or successive officials with similar names; Scripture does not settle it. These are separately indexed names, and their occurrences are not counted among the four for H1913.

The meaning of the name is not certain. It is usually treated as non-Israelite, probably Aramean, and is commonly explained as "Hadad is exalted." Hadad was the storm god worshiped in Syria. That explanation fits naturally with a prince from Hamath. It cannot simply be assumed for a much earlier Arabian family line or for an official in Jerusalem. For the official, the alternate forms Adoram and Adoniram may reflect a different spelling tradition or a different derivation altogether. The name gives us a cultural clue, not a key to anyone's character.

The Hamath prince's alternate name is significant. Scripture records the same son of Tou both as Hadoram and as Joram, and Joram honors the LORD ("Yah is exalted"). Why the two forms differ is not explained. It may be a scribal variant, or the prince may have carried both names. The difference is worth noticing, but it should not be pressed into certainty.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Hadoram of Hamath. David's victory over Hadadezer king of Zobah changed the balance of power in Syria. Tou of Hamath had long been at war with Hadadezer, so he sent his son to David "to greet him and bless him, because he had fought against Hadadezer and defeated him (for Hadadezer had been at war with Tou); and Hadoram brought with him all kinds of articles of gold, silver, and bronze" (1 Chronicles 18:10). A neighboring royal house was acknowledging that David now held the upper hand. The Chronicler then records what David did with such gifts: "King David also dedicated these to the LORD, along with the silver and gold that he had brought from all these nations—from Edom, from Moab, from the people of Ammon, from the Philistines, and from Amalek" (1 Chronicles 18:11). Chronicles consistently links David's victories to the coming temple, and this treasure was set apart for the LORD rather than for the palace.

Hadoram the official. An official named Adoram was "in charge of revenue" late in David's reign (2 Samuel 20:24). Under Solomon, Adoniram the son of Abda was "over the labor force" (1 Kings 4:6), the conscripted workforce behind Solomon's great building projects (compare 1 Kings 5:13–14). Whether this is one long career or two men of a similar name in the same office, the role is clear. He was the face of royal forced labor.

After Solomon died, the northern tribes came to Rehoboam at Shechem and asked him to lighten the heavy yoke his father had laid on them. Rehoboam rejected the counsel of the older men and promised a heavier yoke instead (2 Chronicles 10:1–14). The people answered, "What share have we in David? We have no inheritance in the son of Jesse. Every man to your tents, O Israel!" (2 Chronicles 10:16). Rehoboam's next move was disastrous: "Then King Rehoboam sent Hadoram, who was in charge of revenue; but the children of Israel stoned him with stones, and he died. Therefore King Rehoboam mounted his chariot in haste to flee to Jerusalem" (2 Chronicles 10:18). Scripture does not say whether Hadoram was sent to negotiate, to enforce, or to restore order. In any case Rehoboam chose the one man the protesters most associated with their burden. The Chronicler closes the scene with lasting consequences: "So Israel has been in rebellion against the house of David to this day" (2 Chronicles 10:19).

Hadoram the Joktanite. Genesis lists him among the sons of Joktan: "Hadoram, Uzal, Diklah" (Genesis 10:27). Chronicles repeats the list word for word (1 Chronicles 1:21). Joktan was Peleg's brother (Genesis 10:25). Abraham would come through Peleg's line, while Joktan's sons spread into Arabia (Genesis 10:30). Scripture tells us nothing more about Hadoram himself, and his exact homeland cannot be pinned down.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The Joktanite Hadoram belongs to Genesis 10, which records the families of the earth spread out "after the flood" (Genesis 10:32). His line is not the chosen line, yet God names it. Directly afterward God calls Abraham so that "all the families of the earth" will be blessed (Genesis 12:3). Hadoram's family is among those the promise has in view.

Hadoram of Hamath shows David's kingdom at its height. Scripture states that David dedicated the tribute of the nations to the LORD (1 Chronicles 18:11). It follows from Chronicles' larger story that these gifts became part of the stockpile for the house of God that Solomon would build (1 Chronicles 22:14; 29:2–5), although the text does not trace Hadoram's particular articles into the temple. God moved a foreign prince to bring treasure that David then gave back to God.

The official's death marks the moment the united kingdom breaks. Scripture explicitly frames this as God's doing. Solomon's idolatry had brought the word that the kingdom would be torn away (1 Kings 11:9–13, 29–39), and the Chronicler says the turn of events came from God to fulfill the word spoken through Ahijah (2 Chronicles 10:15). Rehoboam acted freely and foolishly, and through those very choices God carried out His judgment. Hadoram's stoning was the first blood of a division that defines the rest of Old Testament history and the backdrop of nearly all the prophets.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Two patterns connect these scenes to Christ. They are canonical connections rather than explicit fulfillments of Hadoram himself.

The first is the gifts of the nations. A foreign prince bringing gold, silver, and bronze to David anticipates a theme Scripture develops at length: kings and nations bringing tribute to God's anointed King (Psalm 72:10–11). Revelation completes the picture in the new Jerusalem: "the kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into it" (Revelation 21:24). What happened in a small way at David's court will happen fully before the Lamb.

The second is the yoke. Scripture uses the same Hebrew vocabulary for Israel's forced labor in Egypt, where Pharaoh set "taskmasters" over them (Exodus 1:11). Samuel had warned that a king would take Israel's sons for his own work (1 Samuel 8:11–17), and Moses had warned that a king must not multiply horses or cause the people to return to Egypt to do so (Deuteronomy 17:16). Applying that warning to oppressive kingship is a thematic extension rather than the command's direct subject. Kings describes Solomon's Israelite workers as serving in rotating shifts (1 Kings 5:13–14), while permanent forced labor fell on the remaining non-Israelite peoples (1 Kings 9:20–22), so Israel's burden was not the same legal slavery it had known in Egypt. Still, by Rehoboam's day the people felt the yoke as heavy, and the echo of Egypt is hard to miss. Hadoram, the man over the labor force, was the visible sign of it. Against that background Jesus, the Son of David, speaks differently: "Take My yoke upon you and learn from Me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For My yoke is easy and My burden is light" (Matthew 11:29–30). The contrast is thematic rather than a stated prophecy, but it is real. Rehoboam threatened to add weight. Christ offers rest.

5. Spirit & Life

Rehoboam's choice to send Hadoram shows how pride blinds a leader. He had just refused to listen to people crying out under a burden. He then sent the man who administered that burden. Scripture does not say what he hoped Hadoram would accomplish, but the choice fits a king who had just answered the people's pain with threats. Scripture records the cost: a dead official, a fleeing king, and a divided nation. Anyone who leads a family, a church, or a workplace can fall into the same trap. Scripture's answer is the kind of King Jesus is, gentle and lowly, who carries burdens rather than piling them on.

The prince of Hamath points the other way. He came to bless David, and David turned the blessing toward the LORD. Whatever comes into our hands, whether victory, wealth, or the goodwill of others, finds its proper end when it is dedicated to God's house and God's glory.

The obscure Joktanite Hadoram is a quieter witness. His name survives only in a list, but God recorded him. The nations of Genesis 10 are not forgotten by the God who promised to bless all the families of the earth.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:25–30; 1 Chronicles 1:19–21 — Hadoram the son of Joktan among Shem's descendants spread into Arabia.

  • 1 Chronicles 18:9–11 — Hadoram of Hamath brings gifts to David, and David dedicates the nations' treasure to the LORD.

  • 2 Samuel 8:9–11 — Parallel account naming Tou's son Joram (separately indexed, H3141).

  • 2 Samuel 20:24; 1 Kings 4:6; 5:13–14 — Adoram/Adoniram over revenue and the labor force (separately indexed, H151/H141).

  • 2 Chronicles 10:1–19; 1 Kings 12:1–20 — Rehoboam's harsh answer, Hadoram's stoning, and the division of the kingdom.

  • 1 Kings 11:29–39; 2 Chronicles 10:15 — The division as God's judgment announced through Ahijah.

  • Exodus 1:11; Deuteronomy 17:16; 1 Samuel 8:11–17 — Background of forced labor and the warnings about kings.

  • Psalm 72:10–11; Revelation 21:24 — The nations bringing tribute to God's King.

  • Matthew 11:29–30 — Christ's gentle yoke in contrast to the heavy yoke of earthly kings.
