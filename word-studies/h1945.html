WOE - (hôwy, Strong's H1945)

Summary

Hôwy is the cry Israel raised over its dead, and the prophets carried that cry out of the graveyard and into the streets. Mourners said it beside a fresh tomb ("Alas, my brother!"). Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos, Habakkuk, and others spoke it over people who were still alive and prospering. When a prophet cries "Woe" over the greedy, the drunken, or the self-serving shepherd, he is announcing God's judgment in the language of a funeral. The same word can also work as a loud call for attention. In Isaiah 55:1 it opens God's invitation to the thirsty, and in Zechariah 2:6–7 it calls exiles to escape. To hear this word well, a reader has to ask who is saying it and over whom. The answer can be a grieving friend, a God who will not overlook evil, or a God calling sinners to come and drink freely.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hôwy is a cry, not a description. A speaker who says it does not explain sorrow or danger. He voices it aloud, usually over someone else. Its most basic setting was mourning. The packet records it in funeral laments addressed to the dead as "brother," "sister," "master," or "lord."

In the prophets the word usually leads into a description of the people in view. "Woe to those who…" is followed by what they do: they join house to house, they rise early to chase strong drink, they call evil good. The prophet is not wishing them ill or merely expressing pity. He is declaring, on God's behalf, that their way of life ends in ruin. He speaks as though the mourning has already begun.

A smaller group of uses has a different force. In these the cry gathers attention for an urgent message. The NKJV renders those uses "Ho!" or "Up!" Because one word serves as lament, sentence, and summons, the occasion and the speaker decide its force every time.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

A cry of mourning over the dead. After the old prophet of Bethel lured the man of God into disobedience and saw him killed, he buried him in his own tomb, "and they mourned over him, saying, 'Alas, my brother!'" (1 Kings 13:30). It is a grim scene, because the man who raises the lament helped bring about the death he grieves. Jeremiah shows that these words were customary. Of King Jehoiakim, the LORD says, "They shall not lament for him, Saying, 'Alas, my brother!' or 'Alas, my sister!' They shall not lament for him, Saying, 'Alas, master!' or 'Alas, his glory!'" (Jeremiah 22:18). In contrast, Zedekiah is told that the people will burn incense for him "and lament for you, saying, 'Alas, lord!'" (Jeremiah 34:5).

The prophetic woe. This is by far the most frequent use. Isaiah 5 strings six woes together (verses 8, 11, 18, 20, 21, 22) against land-grabbers, drunkards, scoffers, those who are wise in their own eyes, and, at verse 20, those who call evil good and good evil. Habakkuk 2 aims five woes at Babylon (verses 6, 9, 12, 15, 19) for plunder, violence, and idols that cannot speak. Isaiah 10:5 pronounces woe on Assyria, the very rod God had used to discipline His people. Instruments of judgment are not exempt from it. Amos 5:18 turns the word on worshipers who longed for the day of the LORD without realizing it would be darkness for them. Ezekiel 34:2 and Jeremiah 23:1 direct woe at Israel's shepherds, the leaders who fed themselves and scattered the flock.

A summons to hear and act. Isaiah 55:1 opens, "Ho! Everyone who thirsts, Come to the waters; And you who have no money, Come, buy and eat." In Zechariah 2:6 the LORD calls, "Up, up! Flee from the land of the north," and in the next verse He urges Zion to escape from Babylon. Here the cry does not mourn anyone. It interrupts people so they will take hold of a rescue God is offering.

3. Biblical Connections & Distinctions

English readers should know that "woe" in the Old Testament does not always translate hôwy. A related Hebrew interjection, 'ôy (listed as H188), is also rendered "woe." It commonly appears in the first person, as a speaker's own cry of distress. The two overlap and should not be forced into tidy categories. Still, the formal prophetic "Woe to…" addressed to others belongs largely to hôwy.

Jesus spoke in this prophetic tradition. The Gospels use a different, Greek word (ouai), so these are not occurrences of hôwy. Their force matches the prophets' woes closely: "But woe to you who are rich, For you have received your consolation. Woe to you who are full, For you shall hunger" (Luke 6:24–25). In Matthew 23 He pronounces repeated woes on scribes and Pharisees. Like Ezekiel facing the shepherds, He confronts leaders who burdened the people they should have served.

That shepherd thread runs deeper. Right after the woe of Ezekiel 34:2, God promises to search for His sheep Himself and to set one shepherd, His servant David, over them. After Jeremiah 23:1 comes the promise of a righteous Branch from David. Jesus' claim to be the Good Shepherd (John 10) takes up that promise. The woe clears away false shepherds so that the true Shepherd can come.

Isaiah 55:1 also points forward. At the Feast of Tabernacles, Jesus stood and cried out that anyone who thirsts should come to Him and drink (John 7:37). John uses different words, but the invitation is the same: free water offered to people who cannot pay.

4. Spirit & Life

Jeremiah 22 holds a sober discovery. At verse 13, the prophet pronounces hôwy on the king who builds his house by unrighteousness and works his neighbor without wages. Five verses later, God says no one will cry hôwy over that same king at his death. Jehoiakim received the prophet's woe while he lived and was denied the mourners' woe when he died. People who refuse to grieve over their sin may find in the end that no one grieves over them.

The funeral sound of the word also says something about how God judges. When a prophet speaks over living people in the language of lament, judgment is not delivered coldly. In Isaiah 1:24 the word ("Ah") comes from the LORD's own mouth as He resolves to deal with His adversaries. His justice is certain, but He does not take it up lightly.

Isaiah's book also holds both uses side by side. The cry that rang over Jerusalem's sins in chapter 5 rings again in chapter 55, this time over the thirsty and the penniless. The God who announces death over sin is the same God who calls the dying to come and drink without money and without price. The woe and the welcome come from one heart, and the gospel keeps them together.

5. Reading Guidance

When you meet "Woe," "Alas," "Ho," or "Up" in these passages, ask who is crying out and over whom. A mourner at a grave, a prophet at a city gate, and God calling the thirsty all use the same word, and each setting gives it a different weight. Do not soften a prophetic woe into mild regret. It is a death-lament spoken over the living.
