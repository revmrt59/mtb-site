HOMAM - (Hôwmâm, Strong's H1950)

Summary

Homam was a Horite, a son of Lotan and a grandson of Seir, from the people who lived in the mountains of Seir before Esau's descendants took the land. His name appears only once in the Bible, in the Chronicler's genealogy (1 Chronicles 1:39). Genesis records the same man as Hemam (Genesis 36:22), a spelling difference that reflects a common interchange of two similar Hebrew letters. The meaning of the name is uncertain. Strong's and Brown-Driver-Briggs connect it to the root hamam and suggest "raging." Because the Horites were not Israelites, the name may come from another language and only later have taken a Hebrew-sounding form.

Homam's importance comes from his people more than from anything he did. His aunt Timna, usually identified with the Timna who became the concubine of Eliphaz, Esau's son, bore Amalek, so Homam probably stands where Horite and Edomite families began to merge. The Horites were later driven out and destroyed, and Moses pointed to that history when he taught Israel that the LORD assigns lands to nations. By keeping Homam's name, Scripture shows that the God who gave Israel its inheritance also saw and recorded the families of a people whose land and independence were lost.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Homam is one individual, a son of Lotan the Horite and brother of Hori. Lotan appears first among the sons of Seir in Genesis 36:20. The NKJV of 1 Chronicles 1:39 reads, "And the sons of Lotan were Hori and Homam; Lotan's sister was Timna." Genesis 36:22 gives the same family with the spelling Hemam, which Strong's indexes separately as H1967. The two forms differ by one letter, a waw in Chronicles and a yod in Genesis. These letters were easily confused in copying, and the matching verses make it clear that Homam and Hemam are the same person.

The name's meaning is not settled. Strong's derives it from the Hebrew root hamam (H2000) and glosses it "raging," and Brown-Driver-Briggs places it under that root as well. The related root hum (H1949), "to murmur, roar," is similar in sound and sense, but it is not the derivation these sources give. Since the Horites were a non-Israelite people, often linked by scholars with the ancient Hurrians, the name may be foreign in origin and only resemble a Hebrew root. Scripture makes no wordplay on it, and nothing in the text ties the name's possible meaning to Homam's life.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Scripture tells no story about Homam himself. He belongs to the Horites, the earlier inhabitants of Mount Seir, the rugged country south and southeast of the Dead Sea that later became Edom. In Abraham's day, Chedorlaomer struck "the Horites in their mountain of Seir" (Genesis 14:6). Homam's aunt Timna "was the concubine of Eliphaz, Esau's son, and she bore Amalek to Eliphaz" (Genesis 36:12). If this is the same Timna named as Lotan's sister, as the placement in Genesis 36 suggests, then Amalek was Homam's first cousin. Amalek's descendants later attacked Israel in the wilderness (Exodus 17:8–16). By Moses' time the Horite families had lost their land: "The Horites formerly dwelt in Seir, but the descendants of Esau dispossessed them and destroyed them from before them, and dwelt in their place" (Deuteronomy 2:12).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Esau's descendants replaced the Horites. Deuteronomy 2:22 adds that the LORD Himself destroyed the Horites before them. Moses recorded this while leading Israel past Edom, and he compared it with the land "which the LORD gave" Israel (Deuteronomy 2:12). The point is that the LORD, not military chance, decides which nations hold which lands. The genealogy of Lotan, Hori, and Homam shows that these were real, established families, so Esau's possession of Seir was no small thing. Paul teaches the same truth in Acts 17:26: God "has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings."

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Scripture establishes no direct link between Homam and Christ. His name does serve the Chronicler's larger purpose. First Chronicles begins with Adam and traces the nations around Israel before narrowing to the line of David, from whom the promised King would come. Homam's line is one of the side branches that the account sets aside along the way. Those branches show that God's dealings with Israel took place on the stage of the whole world.

5. Spirit & Life

In the biblical account the Horites lost their land and their independence, and their territory became Edom's. Yet the Spirit-inspired record keeps the names of their families, including Homam twice, once in Genesis and once centuries later in Chronicles. God's record is not limited to the winners of history. A man whose people lost their homeland is still named in God's Word, and that should teach us that no family, however obscure, escapes God's notice. The same Lord who drew the boundaries of Seir draws the boundaries of every nation and every life.

6. Key Scriptures

  • 1 Chronicles 1:39 — The only occurrence of Homam, naming him as Lotan's son and Hori's brother.

  • Genesis 36:20–22 — The parallel record under the spelling Hemam, which places him among Seir's descendants.

  • Genesis 36:12 — Timna, probably Lotan's sister, bears Amalek to Eliphaz, linking Homam's family to Esau's line.

  • Deuteronomy 2:12, 22 — The Horites are dispossessed by Esau's descendants, and the LORD is named as the one who destroyed them.

  • Acts 17:26 — God sets the times and boundaries of nations.
