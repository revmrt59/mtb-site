SUBSTANCE, WEALTH, RICHES - (hôwn, Strong's H1952)

Summary

The Hebrew word hôwn means a person's substance: the wealth, goods and possessions that make up what someone owns. The Bible uses it most in the wisdom books, especially Proverbs, where wise teachers weigh what wealth can and cannot do. Wealth can honor God. "Honor the LORD with your possessions" (Proverbs 3:9). It can also become a person's false fortress. The rich man's wealth is "like a high wall in his own esteem" (Proverbs 18:11), yet Proverbs 11:4 warns that riches "do not profit in the day of wrath."

The word's most memorable uses mark the limits of wealth. All the wealth of a man's house cannot buy love (Song of Songs 8:7). The grave and the fire never say "Enough," and in Proverbs 30 that cry is this same word for sufficiency. God's testimonies bring the psalmist as much joy as all riches (Psalm 119:14). Tyre's glittering trade empire sinks into the sea (Ezekiel 27). Taken together, these passages teach that what people treat as solid substance is not what finally holds. Wisdom offers "enduring riches" (Proverbs 8:18), and the New Testament locates those treasures in Christ.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hôwn is an ordinary Hebrew word for what a person has: household goods, property and accumulated wealth. Its use is concentrated in a small group of books. The word occurs 26 times in 26 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and eighteen of them come from Proverbs. Three appear in the Psalms, one in the Song of Songs, and four in a single chapter of Ezekiel. The word does not appear in the Law or the historical books, which describe wealth with other Hebrew words.

That pattern helps explain the word's character. Hôwn is mostly the vocabulary of reflection. Wisdom teachers use it to ask what wealth is worth, and a prophet uses it to describe a commercial empire that God brings down.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Hôwn refers to material substance considered as something a person possesses and relies on. The NKJV translates it as "wealth," "riches," "possessions," "substance," "goods," and "luxury." Two senses are worth distinguishing:

1. Wealth or possessions: the goods and resources a person or a city has gathered, as in "Honor the LORD with your possessions" (Proverbs 3:9) or Tyre's trade goods (Ezekiel 27:12).

2. Sufficiency, enough: in Proverbs 30:15–16 the word appears as an exclamation, translated "Enough!" This is a poetic use. The idea seems to be that is plenty, a point at which what someone has finally counts as sufficient.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Proverbs, hôwn rarely sits neutrally on the page. It is usually being weighed against something else: righteousness (Proverbs 11:4), a prudent wife given by the LORD (Proverbs 19:14), or honest work (Proverbs 13:11). The moral question is usually how the wealth was gained and what the owner trusts. Proverbs never treats owning things as sinful in itself.

The setting also changes the force of the word. In the Psalms it measures delight (Psalm 119:14). In the Song of Songs it is a price that love refuses. In Ezekiel it becomes the cargo of a nation, and the word's repetition through the chapter piles up Tyre's goods until the ship goes down.

4. Theological Significance

These passages reveal a God who cannot be bought and who refuses to be replaced. People instinctively try to get three things from their substance: security against trouble, satisfaction, and even love. Scripture uses hôwn to show that wealth fails at all three.

God is also not a dealer who trades in people. In Psalm 44, a lament after national defeat, Israel complains that God has sold His people for next to nothing and has not grown rich from the sale (Psalm 44:12, paraphrased). The Hebrew phrase means roughly for no wealth. Conquering kings sold captives to fill their treasuries, but God gains nothing by His people's suffering. The psalm leaves the anguish unresolved. Even in that complaint, though, Israel's own words grant that God has not grown rich from His people. Whatever He is doing, it is not a trade for gain.

Scripture also redeems wealth. "Honor the LORD with your possessions, And with the firstfruits of all your increase" (Proverbs 3:9) turns hôwn into an act of worship. Psalm 112:3 promises that wealth and riches will be in the house of the man who fears the LORD, and that his righteousness endures forever. Possessions given back to God in this way are much harder to treat as a god.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 10:15; 18:11; 11:4 — the imaginary wall. "The rich man's wealth is his strong city" (Proverbs 10:15) is an honest observation: money really does protect people from many troubles. Proverbs 18:11 repeats the line and then adds a phrase that changes everything: "like a high wall in his own esteem." The wall exists in the rich man's mind. The previous verse supplies the contrast, since it calls the name of the LORD a strong tower where the righteous find safety (Proverbs 18:10, paraphrased). The two verses set two refuges side by side, one God provides and one a person builds for himself. Proverbs 11:4 shows what happens when trouble tests the wall: "Riches do not profit in the day of wrath, But righteousness delivers from death." Wealth can hold out many troubles, but it cannot keep out the last one.

Song of Songs 8:7 — love that cannot be bought. The Song reaches its climax by declaring that "love is as strong as death" (Song of Songs 8:6). "Many waters cannot quench love, Nor can the floods drown it. If a man would give for love All the wealth of his house, It would be utterly despised." The offer is not small. It is everything a man owns, and it is not simply refused. It is scorned. Trying to buy love insults love itself, because love can only be given. The passage describes human love between a man and a woman. Its principle, however, runs straight into the gospel. Peter says believers were redeemed not with perishable silver or gold but with the precious blood of Christ (1 Peter 1:18–19, paraphrased). When Simon offered the apostles money for the Spirit's power, Peter told him to keep his money (Acts 8:20, paraphrased). Isaiah invites the thirsty to come and buy without money and without price (Isaiah 55:1, paraphrased). The love of God is the one thing no amount of hôwn can touch, because it is the one thing God refuses to sell.

Proverbs 30:15–16 — the four that never say "Enough." "The leech has two daughters— Give and Give!" The sage then lists the grave, the barren womb, the earth that is not satisfied with water, and the fire, things that never say "Enough!" The cry they never utter is hôwn, that is sufficient. Each image is a hunger that feeds without filling. The grave keeps receiving the dead. Dry ground drinks up rain and stays thirsty. Fire consumes whatever is placed in it and reaches for more. Wealth is the very word for enough, yet these appetites never arrive there. Ecclesiastes makes the same point when it observes that the eye is never satisfied with seeing (Ecclesiastes 1:8, paraphrased).

Ezekiel 27 — the sinking ship. Ezekiel pictures Tyre as a magnificent merchant ship. Nations fill its hold: "Tarshish was your merchant because of your many luxury goods. They gave you silver, iron, tin, and lead for your goods" (Ezekiel 27:12). Its luxury goods enrich the kings of the earth (Ezekiel 27:33). Then the east wind breaks it in the midst of the seas, and its riches and wares fall into the seas with it on the day of its ruin (Ezekiel 27:26–27, paraphrased). Proverbs shows one man hiding behind his wealth, and Ezekiel shows a whole civilization doing the same thing.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

In Proverbs 8:18, Wisdom says, "Riches and honor are with me, Enduring riches and righteousness." Wisdom offers wealth that lasts, and it comes paired with righteousness. Paul calls Christ "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24) and says that in Him "are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3). The New Testament does not quote Proverbs 8 as a prophecy of Christ. Still, the connection fits the shape of Scripture: the enduring riches Wisdom offered have a face. Paul preached "the unsearchable riches of Christ" (Ephesians 3:8) and counted everything else loss "that I may gain Christ" (Philippians 3:8).

Jesus' parable of the rich fool echoes the warning of Proverbs 18:11. The rich man plans to build bigger barns and tell his soul, "you have many goods laid up for many years." God's answer is short: "Fool! This night your soul will be required of you" (Luke 12:19–20). In effect, the man built the wall Proverbs describes, and it was strong only in his own estimation. Jesus' conclusion names the alternative: "So is he who lays up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God" (Luke 12:21). The church at Laodicea said, "I am rich, have become wealthy, and have need of nothing," and did not know it was "poor, blind, and naked" (Revelation 3:17).

Revelation 18 echoes Tyre's shipwreck in its picture of Babylon's fall. The merchants who grew rich by her stand at a distance and cry, "For in one hour such great riches came to nothing" (Revelation 18:17), and shipmasters and sailors join the lament. Among the cargo listed are "bodies and souls of men" (Revelation 18:13). An economy that trades in people is the opposite of the God of Psalm 44, who is not enriched by His people. At the cross, the love the Song said could not be bought was given freely, at a price no human wealth could pay.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 119:14 shows where real substance lies: "I have rejoiced in the way of Your testimonies, As much as in all riches." The psalmist does not despise riches. He measures his delight by the joy that great riches bring, and he has found that same joy in God's ways. That comparison is a useful test for the heart. Could I honestly say that God's Word delights me the way an unexpected fortune would? If not, my substance may be doing a job God meant to do Himself.

The "Enough" of Proverbs 30 deserves special attention. Every believer knows the leech's daughters, "Give and Give!", in their own appetite for more. The answer is not greater self-control in getting things. It is a different source. When Paul begged for relief, Christ told him, "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Corinthians 12:9). The sufficiency that the grave and the fire never reach is a gift from Christ, and receiving it frees a person to hold possessions loosely and give them gladly.

The balance of Proverbs 3:9 matters here. The way out of trusting wealth is not to hate it but to honor the LORD with it, giving the firstfruits before anything else. That simple act takes bricks out of the imaginary wall and places them in God's hands.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Proverbs 1:13 — Sinners lure a young man with the promise that violence will fill their houses with precious possessions. Wealth is the bait of the first temptation in Proverbs.

Proverbs 13:7 — One man pretends to be rich yet has nothing, and another pretends to be poor yet has great riches. Appearances say little about a person's real substance.

Proverbs 13:11 — Wealth gained by dishonesty dwindles, while wealth gathered by honest labor grows. How wealth is gained affects whether it lasts.

Proverbs 19:14 — Houses and riches are an inheritance from fathers, but a prudent wife is from the LORD. Wealth can be passed down by parents, but some gifts come from God alone.

Proverbs 24:4 — By knowledge the rooms of a house are filled with precious and pleasant riches. In Proverbs, wisdom builds a household that wealth alone cannot.

Proverbs 28:8 — Wealth increased by exploiting the poor is eventually gathered for the one who pities them. God oversees where unjust gain finally ends up.
