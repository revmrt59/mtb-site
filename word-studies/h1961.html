BE, BECOME, COME TO PASS - (hâyâh, Strong's H1961)

Summary

The Hebrew verb hâyâh means "to be," "to become," or "to happen." English Bibles render it in many ways, including "was," "became," "it came to pass," and "it shall be." The common thread is reality taking hold. Something exists, something changes into a new condition, or something actually occurs in history. The word appears in God's first spoken command, "Let there be light" (Genesis 1:3). It carries the long chain of "and it came to pass" that moves Israel's story forward. It also stands behind the prophets' confident "it shall come to pass."

The verb reaches its greatest height when God names Himself at the burning bush: "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14). The God who simply is stands behind everything else that is. Because He is, His word brings things into being, His promises come to pass, and His pledge "I will be your God, and you shall be My people" holds firm from Sinai to the new creation. Jesus takes up that same divine "I AM" for Himself (John 8:58). The word also exposes the oldest lie, the serpent's promise that humanity "will be like God" apart from Him (Genesis 3:5).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hâyâh is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Old Testament. It appears across every kind of writing: narrative, law, poetry, wisdom, and prophecy. Its heaviest concentration is in the historical books, from Genesis through Kings, where it often opens a new scene.

English uses "is" as a quiet link between a subject and a description. Hebrew often leaves that link unstated. So when hâyâh does appear, it usually says something stronger. It marks something that exists, arrives, or comes about. That is why translators so often reach for "become," "happen," or "come to pass" instead of a plain "is."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, hâyâh describes existence that is real and present in time. Context determines which of three main senses is in view:

1. To be, to exist, to be present. This sense states that someone or something is there. "All those things exist," says the LORD of His creation (Isaiah 66:2).

2. To happen, to occur, to come to pass. This sense describes an event taking place, whether in the past ("it came to pass," Genesis 4:3) or in the future ("so it shall come to pass," Isaiah 14:24).

3. To become. This sense describes a move from one state into another, as when a man and his wife "shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The narrative hinge. The most frequent rendering in the NKJV is some form of "it came to pass" or "it happened." Hebrew storytellers used this form of the verb to open a new episode or to mark the next step in the story: "And in the process of time it came to pass that Cain brought an offering" (Genesis 4:3). Most of these occurrences carry no special theological weight on their own. Taken together, though, they present history as one connected sequence, each event linked to the next.

"Become." When the verb is joined to a word meaning "to" or "for," it often signals a change into a new condition. Two become one flesh (Genesis 2:24). Scattered nations become God's own people. The construction points to a real change of status, not simply a new description.

"It shall come to pass." In the prophets, the future form of the verb introduces what God has determined. Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Joel, and Zechariah use it again and again to open promises and warnings alike (Isaiah 2:2; Joel 2:28; Zechariah 14:7). The certainty in these passages does not come from the verb's grammar. It comes from the God who is speaking.

Command forms. God's "Let there be" (Genesis 1:3) is the same verb used as a command. In the mouth of God, a command to be brings about what it names.

4. Theological Significance

Ordinary uses of this verb describe things that depend on something else for their existence. Rain falls, a famine arises, a king dies. God alone uses the word of Himself without dependence on anything. His being has no cause and no beginning. Everything else exists because He speaks it into being.

This explains why the verb ties God's nature so closely to His word. The psalmist puts it plainly: "For He spoke, and it was done; He commanded, and it stood fast" (Psalm 33:9). Lamentations 3:37 asks who can speak and have it come to pass unless the Lord has commanded it. Human plans may or may not happen. When God speaks, the outcome is never in doubt.

The verb also defines what human beings are. We are creatures who become. We receive our existence and grow into what God intends. Isaiah 66:2 captures both sides: all things exist by His hand, yet He looks with favor on the one "who is poor and of a contrite spirit, and who trembles at My word." The God who needs nothing gives His attention to people who know they have nothing in themselves.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 3:14 — "I AM WHO I AM." Moses has just been sent to face Pharaoh, and he asks what name to give the enslaved Israelites. God answers with the first-person form of hâyâh three times: "I AM WHO I AM.... 'I AM has sent me to you.'" The Hebrew form can also be rendered "I will be who I will be." Both senses belong here. God is self-existent, dependent on nothing and no one. He is also the One who will be actively present, as He had just promised Moses He would be with him (Exodus 3:12). The name answers Moses' fear with God's own being, not with a description of His power. Many interpreters connect the covenant name, the LORD (YHWH), introduced in the very next verse, with this verb. If that connection holds, every time Israel spoke the name "LORD," they were confessing the God who is and who is present to save.

Genesis 1 — "Let there be... and there was." The creation account turns on this verb. "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light" (Genesis 1:3). The same pattern follows for the lights in the sky (1:14) and for the food God provides (1:29). No gap separates God's command from its fulfillment. A builder needs materials, time, and labor. God needs only His word. Hebrews 11:3 draws the lesson: "the worlds were framed by the word of God." The universe exists because God said "be."

Genesis 3:5 — "You will be like God." The serpent took the verb of God's own being and turned it into bait. He promised, "you will be like God, knowing good and evil." Adam and Eve had already been made in God's image. The lie offered them a way of being that no longer depended on Him, a self-made existence. They received exile and death instead. Grasping at independent being cut humanity off from the only Source of being.

Leviticus 26:12 and Ezekiel 37:27 — "I will be your God." At Sinai, God pledged, "I will walk among you and be your God, and you shall be My people" (Leviticus 26:12). Centuries later, after Israel had broken faith and gone into exile, Ezekiel heard the same promise renewed: "My tabernacle also shall be with them; indeed I will be their God, and they shall be My people" (Ezekiel 37:27). Israel's failure did not cancel the pledge. In both passages God's "I will be" is tied to His dwelling among His people. He binds Himself to them in relationship, and they become what they could never make themselves: His own people.

Isaiah 14:24 — "So it shall come to pass." "The LORD of hosts has sworn, saying, 'Surely, as I have thought, so it shall come to pass, and as I have purposed, so it shall stand.'" Here God confirms His word with an oath. His thought and the coming event are one and the same. Zechariah carries this certainty to the end of history, promising a day "known to the LORD" when "at evening time it shall happen that it will be light" (Zechariah 14:7). That light at evening recalls the first command, "Let there be light."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Greek translation of the Old Testament rendered God's name in Exodus 3:14 with the Greek verb "I am." Against that background, Jesus said, "Most assuredly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I AM" (John 8:58). He did not say "I was." Abraham came into existence; Jesus simply is. His hearers understood the claim, because they picked up stones (John 8:59). The God of the burning bush stood before them in the flesh.

The creation command also finds its fulfillment in the gospel. Paul writes that "it is the God who commanded light to shine out of darkness, who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). The same word that said "Let there be light" over the dark world now says it over dark hearts, and new creation begins.

The pledge "I will be your God" runs through the whole Bible to its final scene: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them, and they shall be His people. God Himself will be with them and be their God" (Revelation 21:3). Hosea had foreseen that those once called "not My people" would come to be called sons of the living God (Hosea 1:10), and Paul applies that promise to Gentiles brought in through Christ (Romans 9:25–26).

The "becoming" of marriage points to Christ as well. Paul quotes Genesis 2:24, "the two shall become one flesh," and adds, "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32). Ezekiel's valley of dry bones offers a related picture. There Ezekiel uses different Hebrew words, but the scene still shows the dead receiving breath and standing "an exceedingly great army" (Ezekiel 37:10). Through Christ and His Spirit, people who were spiritually dead come to be alive and belong to Him.

7. Spirit & Life

The name "I AM" tells believers what kind of God they are trusting. He is the same in every situation and dependent on nothing. When Moses felt inadequate, God did not answer with a list of Moses' strengths. He answered with Himself. Our confidence rests in the same place. The God who will be what He has promised to be is the One who goes with us.

Because God is, His promises will be. Isaiah 65:24 says, "It shall come to pass that before they call, I will answer." That word is as sure as "it came to pass" in Genesis or Kings. The narrative books record a long line of events that did happen. The prophets announce events that will happen. Both rest on the same God, so believers can lean their full weight on what He has said.

Genesis 3:5 also exposes a temptation that still works in the heart. It is the desire to define ourselves, to be our own source, to become someone without God. Spiritual life moves the other way. We do not produce our own holiness. We become what God makes us as we live in dependence on the One whose name is "I AM." The contrite person who trembles at His word (Isaiah 66:2) is not diminished by that dependence. That person is standing where a creature was always meant to stand, receiving life from the God who is.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ruth 1:1 — "Now it came to pass, in the days when the judges ruled, that there was a famine in the land." A familiar narrative opening introduces a story in which God quietly works through a famine to bring about the line of David.

  • Isaiah 12:2 — The worshiper confesses that the LORD has become his salvation. God does more than provide rescue. He Himself becomes the rescue.

  • Joel 2:28 — The promise that it shall come to pass afterward that God will pour out His Spirit on all flesh. Peter announces its fulfillment at Pentecost (Acts 2:16–17).

  • Ecclesiastes 1:9 — The Preacher observes that what has been is what will be. Life "under the sun" seems to go in circles, which makes the prophets' "it shall come to pass" stand out all the more.

  • Malachi 3:17 — The LORD of hosts declares of those who fear Him that they shall be His own on the day He makes up His treasured possession.
