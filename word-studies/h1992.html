THEY, THOSE, THESE - (hêm, Strong's H1992)

Summary

The Hebrew word hêm is the ordinary way of saying "they" when the group is masculine or mixed. It does more than stand in for a noun, though. Hebrew often builds a sentence with no verb for "is" or "are." In those sentences hêm names who or what is being described, and English translators supply the missing verb, as in "These are holy garments" (Leviticus 16:4). Hebrew verbs already show who is acting, so when a writer adds hêm to a verb, the pronoun draws attention to that group and often sets it apart from someone else. Placed after a noun, hêm points back to something already in view, as in "those days" or "those camps."

Noticing hêm helps readers see the reasoning inside a passage. It shows them who carries a responsibility, why one thing follows from another, and which group the writer has in mind.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

At heart, hêm is a personal pronoun meaning "they." It works in three settings. In a sentence that has no verb, hêm identifies the subject being described or classified. With a verb, it is usually extra, because a Hebrew verb's form already says "they did" or "they will do." That extra word therefore brings emphasis or contrast, much like English "they themselves." Joined to a noun with "the," hêm becomes a pointing word, "those," that reaches back to something already established. NKJV renderings vary widely, including "they," "these," "those," and "themselves," because English needs different words to carry these different jobs.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Identifying the subject in a sentence without a verb ("they," "these")

Genesis 3:7 reports that after eating the fruit, Adam and Eve "knew that they were naked." In Hebrew the clause is simply "naked [were] they," and hêm names the man and the woman as the ones exposed. Leviticus 16:4 lists the high priest's linen tunic, trousers, sash, and turban, then adds: "These are holy garments." Here hêm gathers the whole list and states what kind of clothing it is. The English "are" has been supplied by the translator.

Emphasizing or contrasting the subject ("they," "themselves")

Exodus 18:26 describes the judges Moses appointed: "the hard cases they brought to Moses, but they judged every small case themselves." The verb alone already means "they judged." Adding hêm sets the new judges' work against Moses' work. In Numbers 1:50, God appoints the Levites over the tabernacle and uses a longer form of the same pronoun: "they shall carry the tabernacle." Here the pronoun singles out the Levites from the other tribes being counted in that chapter.

Pointing back to something already named ("those")

Genesis 6:4 speaks of giants "in those days," using hêm to point back to the era the passage has been describing. Zechariah 14:15 does the same with a place, describing the plague on all the cattle "in those camps." In both verses hêm ties the new statement to a setting the reader already has in mind.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Moses' father-in-law warned him that judging every dispute alone would wear him out. Exodus 18:26 shows the solution actually working, and hêm makes the arrangement clear. Moses received the hard cases, while the appointed men handled ordinary matters "themselves." The pronoun marks a real handing over of responsibility. God's people were served better when the burden was shared, and the text takes care to show who carried which part.

Leviticus 16:4 shows a different kind of payoff. The sentence "These are holy garments" does more than describe the clothing. It supplies the reason for what comes next: "Therefore he shall wash his body in water, and put them on." Because the clothing belongs to God's holy service, the priest must be washed before wearing it. A short identifying sentence built around hêm leads straight into the instruction.

4. Reading Guidance

Do not assume every English "these" or "those" comes from the same Hebrew word. Hebrew has other words for "these," and hêm often does this pointing work as well. When you read "they are" in a Hebrew passage, remember that "are" is usually the translator's addition. In Isaiah 30:7 the NKJV keeps the Hebrew phrase as a name, "Rahab-Hem-Shebeth." Scholars debate whether hêm is part of a mocking title or a pronoun in a short sentence about Egypt sitting still, so it is wise not to build much on either reading.
