ROAR, BE DISQUIETED, YEARN - (hâmâh, Strong's H1993)

Summary

The Hebrew verb hâmâh describes something stirred up and making noise: the sea crashing against the shore, a city in uproar, a crowd of enemies, dogs growling at night. The Bible also turns the same word inward. A troubled soul is "disquieted," a grieving heart wails "like flutes," and a loving heart "yearns." The word covers both the outward roar of a restless world and the inward churning of a person who is deeply moved.

That range is what makes the word worth studying. Scripture uses it for the threatening noises of life, such as roaring waters, raging nations, and the loud call of folly, and it shows God as Lord over all of them. The psalmist hears the same unrest inside himself and answers it with "Hope in God." Most striking of all, God uses this word for His own heart. Over rebellious Ephraim He says, "My heart yearns for him; I will surely have mercy on him" (Jeremiah 31:20). The God who commands the roaring sea is Himself stirred with compassion for His wayward children.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hâmâh is a sound-and-motion word. It pictures something agitated, either heard or felt. It never appears in the five books of Moses. Its uses cluster in the Psalms, Proverbs, and the prophets, especially Isaiah and Jeremiah, which are the parts of Scripture where Israel gave voice to fear, grief, warning, and longing.

Hebrew often places strong emotion in the body's "inward parts," the gut and the depths of a person. Several uses of hâmâh work this way. Where the NKJV says "my heart yearns" or "my inner being," the Hebrew is describing something felt deep inside the body. That is why a single verb can describe both a stormy sea and a grieving heart. Both are churning.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To roar or make a loud noise, as with the sea, a crowd, an army, or a city. "Though its waters roar and be troubled" (Psalm 46:3).

2. To growl or moan, used of animals and of people who sound like them. Enemies "growl like a dog" (Psalm 59:6).

3. To be loud, restless, or turbulent in conduct, used of the foolish and the intoxicated. "A foolish woman is clamorous" (Proverbs 9:13).

4. To be inwardly stirred: disquieted, troubled, wailing, or yearning. "Why are you disquieted within me?" (Psalm 42:5); "My heart yearns for him" (Jeremiah 31:20).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning shifts with the subject. When waters or nations are the subject, the word describes threat. When fools or wine are the subject, it describes disorder: the adulteress is "loud and rebellious" (Proverbs 7:11), and strong drink is called a brawler (Proverbs 20:1). When a heart is the subject, the word describes feeling, and the feeling may be anguish, grief, or love.

Proverbs does not set noise against silence. Wisdom herself "cries out in the chief concourses" (Proverbs 1:21). "Concourses" renders this same word; the picture is the noisiest, busiest places in the city, which could be paraphrased at the head of the noisy streets. The difference between the two voices lies in their character. Folly is restless and empty, she "knows nothing" (Proverbs 9:13), and her feet "would not stay at home" (Proverbs 7:11). Wisdom goes into the noise with a message worth hearing.

Animal images carry spiritual meaning in some passages. David's enemies prowl the city at evening and growl like dogs (Psalm 59:6, 14). In Isaiah 59:11 the people of Israel say of themselves, "We all growl like bears, And moan sadly like doves; We look for justice, but there is none." Isaiah uses that frustrated growl and helpless moan to portray a people cut off from justice by their own sin. Where sin rules, human voices can sink into the sounds of predators and of despair.

4. Theological Significance

This word shows that God rules over every kind of roar. The sea, the nations, and great empires all make noise, but none of them threatens His throne. Jeremiah 31:35 describes the Lord as the One "Who disturbs the sea, And its waves roar," and Isaiah 51:15 names Him as the One "Who divided the sea whose waves roared." Many ancient peoples feared the sea as untamed chaos, and Scripture often uses it that way (Psalm 93:3–4). The prophets do not deny that the sea is dangerous. They name its Master. He stirs it up, and He sets its limits: though its waves roar, "yet they cannot pass over it" (Jeremiah 5:22).

The word also shows that God's mercy comes from His heart. Jeremiah 31:20 does not describe a judge coldly cancelling a debt. The Lord speaks as a Father whose inner being is stirred. The two uses meet in a single chapter. In Jeremiah 31 God's heart yearns for Ephraim (v. 20), and a few verses later the same verb describes the roaring sea that God commands (v. 35). That mastery over the sea is offered as His guarantee that He will not cast off Israel (vv. 36–37). The One whose power controls the sea is the same One whose heart is moved for His people.

Human love can stir a person in the same way. In Song of Solomon 5:4 the bride says, "My beloved put his hand By the latch of the door, And my heart yearned for him." Love that is real is felt deeply. Scripture uses the same word for the bride's longing and for God's longing, and the shallow noise of Proverbs' foolish woman stands in sharp contrast to that kind of desire.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 46: Roaring waters and raging nations. The psalm uses hâmâh twice in parallel. First the sea: "Though its waters roar and be troubled" (v. 3). Then humanity: "The nations raged, the kingdoms were moved" (v. 6). The disorder of the natural world and the hostility of nations are described with the same verb. Between these two verses the psalmist places a quiet river that makes "glad the city of God," and the promise "God is in the midst of her, she shall not be moved" (vv. 4–5). The nations were "moved," but the city where God dwells is not. One sentence then answers all the raging: "He uttered His voice, the earth melted" (v. 6). The roar of the world is real, and a single word from God is enough to end it.

Jeremiah carries the image forward into history and turns it against Babylon. A people from the north comes "against you, O daughter of Babylon," and "their voice shall roar like the sea" (Jeremiah 50:42). Then the roar is Babylon's own: the LORD is "plundering Babylon And silencing her loud voice, Though her waves roar like great waters" (Jeremiah 51:55). The attackers' roar serves "the counsel of the LORD that He has taken against Babylon" (Jeremiah 50:45), and Babylon's own roar is silenced by Him. Even the greatest empire of the day was only one more wave that God would quiet.

Psalms 42–43: The disquieted soul. Three times the psalmist questions himself: "Why are you cast down, O my soul? And why are you disquieted within me?" (Psalm 42:5, 11; 43:5). The roar is now inside him. He neither ignores the turmoil nor gives in to it. He speaks to his own soul and tells it where to look: "Hope in God, for I shall yet praise Him For the help of His countenance." The refrain comes back three times, which shows that this is a struggle repeated more than once, not a quick fix. Each time he brings his churning feelings to the face of God and to a future of praise that has not yet come.

Jeremiah 31:20 with Isaiah 16:11 and Jeremiah 48:36: The yearning heart of God. Ephraim had rebelled, and God had spoken against him. Even so, the Lord asks, "Is Ephraim My dear son?" and answers, "I earnestly remember him still; Therefore My heart yearns for him; I will surely have mercy on him." The mercy comes out of the yearning. The prophets use the same word even over judgment that falls on a foreign people. Isaiah says, "My heart shall resound like a harp for Moab" (Isaiah 16:11). In Jeremiah the Lord says, "My heart shall wail like flutes for Moab" (Jeremiah 48:36). The heart is pictured as an instrument that sounds out grief. God does not enjoy judging. His heart mourns even over Moab. Hosea expresses the same divine longing with different Hebrew words: "My heart churns within Me; My sympathy is stirred" (Hosea 11:8).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament teaches that the Lord commands the roaring sea. On the Sea of Galilee, Jesus rebuked the wind and told the sea to be still, and it became calm (Mark 4:39). His disciples asked who this could be, and the psalms and prophets had already given the answer. The One whose voice melts the earth in Psalm 46 was sitting in their boat.

Jesus also shows the other side of this word. When the prodigal's father "saw him and had compassion, and ran and fell on his neck" (Luke 15:20), Jesus was describing in story the same Father who said of Ephraim, "My heart yearns for him." The Greek verb for "had compassion" is built on the word for a person's inward parts, so it calls up the same picture of being moved deep within. No Greek verb translates hâmâh here, but the image carries over: Jesus reveals a Father who is deeply moved. Jesus Himself prayed "with vehement cries and tears" (Hebrews 5:7). He shared the anguish of Psalm 42 as well as the compassion of Jeremiah 31.

Zechariah 9:15 gives the story a surprising turn. When "The LORD of hosts will defend them," His people "shall drink and roar as if with wine." The image belongs to a scene of battle victory. It uses the noise of drunkenness, which Proverbs condemns, to describe the overflowing gladness of a people God has rescued. The link with Ephesians 5:18, "do not be drunk with wine... but be filled with the Spirit," is a resonance between passages rather than a stated fulfillment, but the direction is consistent. God does not only silence the roar of His enemies. He gives His people a new and joyful noise in its place.

7. Spirit & Life

Read Psalm 77:3 beside Jeremiah 31:20 and a striking pairing appears. Asaph says, "I remembered God, and was troubled." God says of Ephraim, "I earnestly remember him still; Therefore My heart yearns for him." The same verb describes both. When a believer remembers God in a dark season, the memory can stir up turmoil, because the believer cannot see what God is doing. Meanwhile, God's remembering of His child stirs His heart toward mercy. Our disquiet is real, and it is not the only heart that is moved. God's heart is moved too, and His is moved toward us.

This frees believers to pray honestly. David resolved to pray evening, morning, and noon, and to cry aloud (Psalm 55:17), using this word for his complaint. Hannah told Eli she had "poured out my soul before the LORD" (1 Samuel 1:15). Scripture does not require grief to be tidy before God will hear it. Spiritual maturity in Psalm 42 does not mean silencing the soul. It means turning the soul toward God and saying, "Hope in God."

The word also helps believers tell the different noises apart. Folly is loud because it is empty. A distracted city can be "full of noise" while judgment approaches (Isaiah 22:2). The roar of nations and empires is loud and passes away. Holy longing, honest grief, and the joy of the rescued are also loud, and God welcomes them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Kings 1:41 — As Adonijah's feast ends, Joab asks why the city is in "such a noisy uproar." The city is celebrating Solomon's anointing as king, and the noise announces that Adonijah's bid for the throne has already failed.

  • Psalm 39:6 — People "busy themselves in vain," heaping up wealth without knowing who will gather it. Restless activity is a kind of noise too.

  • Isaiah 17:12 — "Woe to the multitude of many people Who make a noise like the roar of the seas." The nations' roar is answered by God's rebuke in the verses that follow.

  • Isaiah 22:2 — Jerusalem is "a tumultuous city, a joyous city," celebrating at the very time God was calling for weeping (v. 12).

  • Jeremiah 4:19 — The prophet's own heart makes a noise within him as he hears the trumpet of coming war. His anguish echoes God's grief over His people.

  • Ezekiel 7:16 — Survivors of judgment mourn like doves of the valleys, each one over his own iniquity.
