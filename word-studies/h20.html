MELON - (ʼăbaṭṭîyach, Strong's H0020)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼăbaṭṭîyach means "melon," most likely the watermelon that grew well in Egypt's sandy soil along the Nile. It names an ordinary, refreshing fruit and carries no spiritual meaning of its own. It appears only once in the Bible, in Numbers 11:5. There the Israelites, tired of the manna, weep as they remember the foods of Egypt: "the fish which we ate freely in Egypt, the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlic."

Because the word appears only once, everything Scripture does with it happens in that one scene. The melons become a picture of how craving rewrites memory. Israel remembered Egypt's fruit as free and forgot the brick kilns and the cruelty of slavery. They called God's daily bread from heaven nothing at all. The story ends in a burial ground named for craving. Later Scripture holds this moment up as a warning, and Jesus answers it by offering Himself as the true bread from heaven.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼăbaṭṭîyach is a Hebrew noun found only in Numbers 11:5. It belongs to the produce of Egypt. In the verse it stands in a list with cucumbers, leeks, onions, and garlic, the garden crops of the Nile valley. That company helps identify it. Most interpreters understand it as the watermelon, a fruit valued in hot, dry lands for its sweetness and high water content. The exact variety cannot be settled beyond question, but nothing in the passage depends on botanical precision.

The setting is the wilderness journey. Israel had left Mount Sinai, and the people were living each day on manna that God sent from heaven.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means simply "melon," a cultivated fruit eaten for food and refreshment. It has one sense. Nothing in the word itself is sinful or symbolic. The melons in Numbers 11 are not wrong to eat. The weight they carry comes from the people who longed for them and from why they longed for them.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The single occurrence comes in a remembered menu spoken in complaint (Numbers 11:4–6). The list moves from fish to five garden crops, and then it turns sharply: "but now our whole being is dried up; there is nothing at all except this manna before our eyes!" (Numbers 11:6). The melons sit at the center of a contrast the people themselves draw. On one side is the moist, flavorful variety of Egypt. On the other is the manna, which they dismiss as "nothing at all." The word does its work inside that contrast.

4. Theological Significance

Numbers 11 shows that God does not treat a craving for the world's food as harmless nostalgia. The chapter opens with a complaint that "displeased the LORD," and fire burned at the edges of the camp (Numbers 11:1–3). The melon complaint follows immediately and draws a still more serious judgment. In God's answer the issue comes into the open. The people had "despised the LORD who is among you" and had wept before Him, asking why they had ever left Egypt (Numbers 11:20, paraphrased). Longing for the melons amounted to wishing undone the salvation God had worked for them.

Psalm 78 explains what lay underneath. Israel "tested God in their heart by asking for the food of their fancy" (Psalm 78:18). They doubted that God could "prepare a table in the wilderness" (78:19). This happened "because they did not believe in God, and did not trust in His salvation" (78:22). The psalm then sets God's generosity beside their unbelief: He "had rained down manna on them to eat, and given them of the bread of heaven. Men ate angels' food; He sent them food to the full" (78:24–25). The real conflict in Numbers 11 is between God's faithful provision and a heart that has stopped trusting it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 11:5 — The memory that forgot the price. Israel remembered the fish "which we ate freely in Egypt." By freely the people meant that the fish had cost them nothing to buy. Yet the life that went with that food had been anything but free. Exodus describes how the Egyptians "made their lives bitter with hard bondage—in mortar, in brick, and in all manner of service in the field" (Exodus 1:14). Pharaoh had ordered their baby boys killed (Exodus 1:16, 22), and Israel's groaning under slavery rose to God (Exodus 2:23). Yet now, freed from Egypt, the people recalled the melons and erased the whip. Craving edits memory this way. It keeps the sweetness of the old life and quietly removes the price tag. People rarely long to go back to bondage as bondage. They long for its melons and forget what the melons cost.

Numbers 11:4–9 — Melons against manna. The complaint began with "the mixed multitude who were among them," people who had come out of Egypt alongside Israel (Exodus 12:38). They "yielded to intense craving; so the children of Israel also wept again" (Numbers 11:4). The appetite spread from the edge of the camp to its heart. The people then set Egypt's water-rich fruit against the manna and claimed their whole being was "dried up" (11:6). Moses answers by describing the manna as it really was. It was like coriander seed, it could be ground, cooked, and made into cakes, its taste was "like the taste of pastry prepared with oil," and when the dew fell on the camp at night, the manna fell on it (11:7–9). The people said their whole being was dried up, while God's bread came to them with the dew. Craving had distorted their view of the present as surely as it had distorted their memory of the past.

Numbers 11:31–34 — The Graves of Craving. God gave them meat. Quail covered the ground, and while the meat was still between their teeth a plague struck. The place was named Kibroth Hattaavah, "graves of craving," because there they buried the people who had craved (11:34, paraphrased). Psalm 106:14–15 recalls the scene. God gave them what they asked for but sent leanness into their soul. A story that began with weeping for melons ended at a graveside.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The manna Israel despised points forward to Jesus. In John 6 a crowd reminded Him that their fathers ate manna in the wilderness (John 6:31). Jesus answered that the Father now gives the true bread from heaven, and He declared, "I am the bread of life" (John 6:32–35, paraphrased). The parallel goes further. The crowd grumbled at His words (6:41), just as Israel grumbled in Numbers 11. Jesus also drew the decisive difference. Their fathers ate manna and died, but whoever eats the bread He gives will live forever (6:49–51). The manna sustained Israel for a journey. Christ gives life that outlasts the grave.

Paul says the wilderness events happened as examples for the church, so that believers would not crave evil things as Israel did (1 Corinthians 10:6). Hebrews offers the opposite of the melon memory. Moses counted the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures of Egypt (Hebrews 11:26). Israel weighed Egypt's fruit against God's bread and chose the fruit. Moses weighed Egypt's treasures against Christ and chose Christ. Every believer makes that same weighing.

7. Spirit & Life

Temptation rarely presents the old life honestly. It shows the melons and hides the mortar and brick. When a believer starts to feel nostalgic for a former way of living, Numbers 11:5 gives a practical discipline: put the price back on the memory. Recall what that life actually did to you, and what it cost Christ to bring you out of it.

The passage also shows how dissatisfaction makes God's present faithfulness look small. The manna came every morning, and its very regularity became the complaint: "nothing at all except this manna." Daily fellowship with Christ can feel ordinary in the same way, and then the world's variety looks like refreshment. But the manna came down with the dew. The dryness Israel complained of lay in their hearts rather than in God's daily provision.

Craving also spreads. It started among the mixed multitude and soon the whole camp was weeping. What a believer listens to and whom he complains alongside will affect what he hungers for. And Psalm 106 gives a sober mercy to consider. God sometimes grants a stubborn request and lets the leanness teach what obedience would not. It is far better to ask Him for an appetite for the bread He has already given.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

ʼăbaṭṭîyach occurs only in Numbers 11:5, discussed above. Later retellings of the same episode do not repeat the word, but they interpret the event it belongs to. Psalm 78:17–31 recounts Israel's demand for "the food of their fancy" and God's anger. Psalm 106:14–15 recalls the craving and the leanness that followed. First Corinthians 10:6 applies the wilderness craving directly to the church.
