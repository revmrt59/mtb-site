BEHOLD - (hinnêh, Strong's H2009)

Summary

Hinnêh is the Hebrew Bible's pointing word. With it a speaker says, in effect, "Look at this," and directs the listener's attention to a person, an event, or a fact that has just come into view. The narrator uses it to put the reader inside a character's moment of discovery: Jacob waking beside Leah, or Elisha's servant seeing a mountain full of fiery chariots. God uses it when He announces what He is about to do, from the flood to the coming of His messenger. With a personal ending it becomes hinneni, "Here I am," the answer of Abraham, Samuel, and Isaiah when God called them. The NKJV often renders it "indeed," "surely," "suddenly," or "here," so the word is far more frequent and more vivid in Scripture than English readers usually notice. Learning to hear it teaches us to look where the speaker is pointing, and many times the one pointing is God.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Hinnêh does not name a feeling. It presents something to the listener and calls for attention to it now. Its force depends on who is pointing and what is being pointed at.

When a narrator uses it, the story shifts from a report into a character's line of sight. We stop being told about the scene and start seeing it as he saw it. When God uses it, He sets His coming action before His hearers as already in view and certain. When a person attaches it to himself, as in hinneni, he presents himself: I am here, present, and listening. The same small word can introduce a gift, a threat, a surprise, a vision, or a pledge of readiness. The surrounding passage decides which.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Answering a call: "Here I am." In Genesis 22:1, God calls Abraham by name, and he answers, "Here I am." The word recurs at important moments in the chapter. Abraham uses it to answer Isaac on the road (v. 7), and in that same verse Isaac uses it differently, pointing to the fire and the wood and asking where the lamb is. Abraham answers the Angel of the LORD with it at the moment the knife is raised (v. 11). The boy Samuel also answers "Here I am!" when the LORD calls him in the night (1 Samuel 3:4), but he thinks it is Eli calling and runs to the old priest: "Here I am, for you called me" (3:5). The narrator explains that Samuel "did not yet know the LORD" (3:7). Only after the third call, when Eli perceives what is happening and tells him how to reply, does Samuel answer the voice as the LORD's (3:8–10). Isaiah, newly cleansed, hears the Lord ask whom He should send and replies, "Here am I! Send me" (Isaiah 6:8).

God announcing His action. Before the flood, God says, "And behold, I Myself am bringing floodwaters on the earth" (Genesis 6:17). Through Jeremiah the same word introduces hope: "Behold, the days are coming, says the LORD, when I will make a new covenant" (Jeremiah 31:31). Malachi uses it twice in one verse: "Behold, I send My messenger... Behold, He is coming" (Malachi 3:1). God also uses the word when He presents the plants He has given for food (Genesis 1:29). It returns two verses later, when God surveys all He has made and finds it very good (1:31).

Sudden discovery in the story. After a night of deception, Jacob wakes up: "behold, it was Leah" (Genesis 29:25). The reader shares his shock. In Judges 14:5 the NKJV renders it with a phrase: "Now to his surprise, a young lion came roaring against him." Psalm 37:36 uses it for a quieter discovery about the wicked man: "behold, he was no more."

Visions opening. Prophets use the word to introduce what God showed them. Ezekiel writes, "Then I looked, and behold, a whirlwind was coming out of the north" (Ezekiel 1:4). Zechariah says, "I saw by night, and behold, a man riding on a red horse" (Zechariah 1:8). Daniel, seeking the meaning of his vision, finds that "suddenly there stood before me one having the appearance of a man" (Daniel 8:15).

3. Biblical Connections & Distinctions

The NKJV often pairs hinnêh with a verb of seeing, as in "I looked, and behold." A verb of seeing describes the act of looking. Hinnêh presents what was seen. Hebrew also has a shorter related particle, hēn, which is likewise rendered "behold" and overlaps in use. Neither word should be assumed behind every English "behold."

Several of hinnêh's great promises are quoted in the Gospels, where the Greek pointing word idou carries the same function: Isaiah 7:14 ("Behold, the virgin shall conceive") in Matthew 1:23, Zechariah 9:9 ("Behold, your King is coming to you") in Matthew 21:5, and Malachi 3:1 in Mark 1:2. Here the New Testament writers are deliberately reusing a pointer the prophets had already raised. When Jesus finally comes, the finger lands on Him.

Other links are thematic rather than lexical. Isaac's question in Genesis 22, spoken with hinnêh about the fire and the wood, asks where the lamb is. John the Baptist answers that question in another language and with a different Greek word: "Behold! The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Similarly, Hebrews 10:9 places on Christ's lips the words "Behold, I have come to do Your will, O God." That verse is Greek, quoting a psalm. Still, it gathers up what Abraham, Samuel, and Isaiah were reaching for when they said "Here I am": a servant fully present to do whatever the Father asks.

4. Spirit & Life

The "Here I am" of God's servants is spoken before the assignment is known. Abraham does not yet know about Moriah. Samuel, once Eli helps him recognize whose voice is calling, lies down and answers, "Speak, for Your servant hears" (1 Samuel 3:9–10), without knowing the hard message he will have to carry to Eli. Isaiah volunteers and only then hears how dull and resistant his audience will be. Their answer is shaped by the One who called them rather than by the task. That is what availability to God looks like in Scripture: answering the voice before reading the job description.

Isaiah turns the word around. To people who stop oppressing others and start feeding the hungry, God promises that when they cry out, He will answer, "Here I am" (Isaiah 58:9). The Lord who called Abraham by name binds Himself to answer His people the way His servants answered Him. Prayer is not shouting into an empty room.

The narrative uses of the word carry their own lesson. Elisha's servant saw only the Syrian army until the LORD opened his eyes, "And behold, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha" (2 Kings 6:17). Hinnêh marks the moment hidden reality became visible. Every divine "behold" of warning or promise is God doing the same thing ahead of time: showing His people what is coming so they can turn, trust, and be ready when it arrives.

5. Reading Guidance

Only a little over half of hinnêh's uses appear as "behold" in the NKJV. The rest are spread across "indeed," "surely," "look," "suddenly," "here," and others. When one of these words opens a sentence in the Old Testament, especially "Here I am," ask who is pointing and what they want you to see. Sometimes it is a character's shock. Sometimes it is a servant's readiness. Often it is God Himself, saying, "Look at what I am about to do."
