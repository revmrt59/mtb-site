MOUNTAIN, MOUNT, HILL - (har, Strong's H2022)

Summary

The Hebrew word har is the Old Testament's ordinary word for a mountain. It can name a single peak such as Carmel or Hermon, a whole range of high country such as "the mountains of Ephraim," or a modest rise that English translators call a "hill." Because the NKJV renders the same word as "mountain" in some verses and "hill" in others, readers can miss a connection. The trembling "mountain" of Sinai and "the hill of the LORD" in Psalm 24:3 are the same Hebrew word.

Scripture gives this common word unusual weight because God repeatedly chooses mountains as the places where He meets people. On Moriah, God provides a substitute for Isaac. At Sinai He speaks His law from fire, and the mountain is fenced off from the people. On Zion He chooses to dwell and sets His King. The prophets look toward a day when His mountain will be raised above every other and the nations will stream to it, and they promise that on that mountain death itself will be swallowed up. Mountains also show God's greatness: the oldest and most immovable things on earth melt and tremble before Him. Israel's high places, by contrast, show how people can turn a reminder of God into a site of idolatry. The story of God's mountains reaches its goal in Jesus Christ, through whom believers have come "to Mount Zion" (Hebrews 12:22) rather than to the mountain that burned with fire.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Har is a common Hebrew noun found throughout the Old Testament, from the flood narrative to Malachi. It is used most heavily in the Sinai chapters of Exodus and Deuteronomy, in the land descriptions of Joshua and Judges, in the Psalms, and in the prophets, especially Isaiah and Ezekiel.

In the ancient Near East, nations commonly regarded mountains as the homes of their gods, the places where heaven and earth met. Scripture does not borrow that belief. It overturns it. Mountains belong to the LORD because He made them: "He who forms mountains, and creates the wind... The LORD God of hosts is His name" (Amos 4:13). When God meets His people on a mountain, He has chosen it. The mountain does not hold Him there.

English readers should also know that the NKJV translates har as "mountain," "mount," "hill," "hill country," and even "mountaineers" (Judges 1:19). Hebrew does have a separate word for a smaller hill, gib'ah, but translators often use "hill" for har as well. So when the Psalms speak of God's "holy hill" (Psalm 2:6) or lifting one's eyes "to the hills" (Psalm 121:1), the Hebrew word is the same one used for Sinai.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A mountain or hill, meaning a single elevated landform, often named: Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18:19), Mount Hor (Numbers 20:22), Mount Sinai (Exodus 19:23), Mount Zion (Psalm 48:2).

2. A mountainous region or highlands, meaning a stretch of elevated country rather than one peak: "the mountains of Ephraim" (Judges 17:1) and the hill country Joshua conquered (Joshua 11:16).

3. A mountain used as a picture of a great power or obstacle. A massive difficulty is called "O great mountain" (Zechariah 4:7), and Babylon is "O destroying mountain" (Jeremiah 51:25).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In historical narrative, har mostly describes terrain. Israel's tribes receive mountain territory, armies camp on opposite ridges (1 Samuel 17:3), and boundaries run along named mountains.

Mountains also serve as places of refuge, though usually a hard and lonely refuge. Lot is told, "Escape to the mountains, lest you be destroyed" (Genesis 19:17), and he ends up living in a cave (Genesis 19:30). Israel hides from Midian in "the dens, the caves, and the strongholds which are in the mountains" (Judges 6:2). David hides from Saul "in the mountains in the Wilderness of Ziph" (1 Samuel 23:14). David, who knew that kind of refuge, answers the advice to "Flee as a bird to your mountain" with a deeper refuge: "In the LORD I put my trust" (Psalm 11:1).

One pattern of wording deserves notice. The word frequently appears with a name or owner attached: "the mountain of God" (Exodus 3:1), "the mountain of the LORD" (Isaiah 2:3), and especially God's own "My holy mountain" (Isaiah 11:9; Ezekiel 20:40). Phrases like these move the focus away from the height or size of the mountain and onto the One who claims it.

4. Theological Significance

God over the mountains. Mountains are Scripture's standard for what is ancient and immovable. Psalm 90:2 measures God's eternity against them: before the mountains were brought forth, He is God. Yet when the LORD appears, these immovable things give way. "The mountains gushed before the LORD, this Sinai" (Judges 5:5), and they melt like wax at His presence (Psalm 97:5). Because God is greater than the mountains, His people can say, "We will not fear... though the mountains be carried into the midst of the sea" (Psalm 46:2). In Micah 6:1–2, God calls the mountains to hear His case against Israel. Having stood through every generation of Israel's history, they serve as ancient witnesses as He contends with His people.

The first holy mountain. In his lament over the king of Tyre, Ezekiel joins two images: "You were in Eden, the garden of God" (Ezekiel 28:13) and "You were on the holy mountain of God" (Ezekiel 28:14). Genesis never uses har for Eden, but Ezekiel presents the first sanctuary as a mountain where God's presence dwelt. The one cast out of "the mountain of God" because of sin (Ezekiel 28:16) recalls Eden's own story, where sin's first result was exile from God's presence. Read this way, later mountains of meeting point back toward what was lost.

Mountains corrupted. Mountains were widely thought to be near the divine, and Israel took up the Canaanite practice of worship at hilltop shrines. Jeremiah reports that Israel "has gone up on every high mountain and under every green tree, and there played the harlot" (Jeremiah 3:6). Hosea describes people who "offer sacrifices on the mountaintops" (Hosea 4:13). God therefore orders Ezekiel to speak to the land itself: "O mountains of Israel, hear the word of the Lord GOD!... I will destroy your high places" (Ezekiel 6:3). The heights that should have lifted Israel's eyes to their Creator became places of betrayal. Yet in the same book God promises restoration beyond the judgment: "on My holy mountain... there all the house of Israel... shall serve Me" (Ezekiel 20:40).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Moriah: the mountain of provision (Genesis 22). God sends Abraham to offer Isaac "on one of the mountains of which I shall tell you" (Genesis 22:2). There God stops Abraham's hand and provides a ram in Isaac's place. Abraham names the place "The-LORD-Will-Provide," and a saying grows from that day: on the mount of the LORD it shall be provided (Genesis 22:14). Second Chronicles 3:1 then identifies Mount Moriah as the place where Solomon built the temple. The mountain where a substitute died so that a son could live became the mountain of Israel's sacrifices for generations.

Sinai: the mountain of holiness and the pattern (Exodus 19–25). At Sinai the mountain becomes a boundary line of holiness. Moses tells God, "The people cannot come up to Mount Sinai; for You warned us, saying, 'Set bounds around the mountain and consecrate it'" (Exodus 19:23). Anyone who touched it would die (Exodus 19:12–13). Moses later reminds Israel that "the mountain burned with fire to the midst of heaven, with darkness, cloud, and thick darkness" (Deuteronomy 4:11). Others went up only as far as God permitted: Aaron, Nadab, Abihu and seventy elders "saw the God of Israel" (Exodus 24:9–10), and Joshua went with Moses (Exodus 24:13). Yet only Moses was told to "come near the LORD" (Exodus 24:2) and went into the cloud to receive God's instruction (Exodus 24:12, 18). The fearful scene, however, had a gracious purpose. On the mountain, God showed Moses the pattern for the tabernacle (Exodus 25:40). The tabernacle was a tent that would carry God's presence into the middle of Israel's camp. In this way the God who could not be approached on Sinai gave the plan for living among His people.

Zion: the chosen mountain (Psalms 68, 2, 24). Zion is not an impressive mountain. Psalm 68 pictures the high ranges of Bashan glaring at it: "Why do you fume with envy, you mountains of many peaks? This is the mountain which God desires to dwell in; yes, the LORD will dwell in it forever" (Psalm 68:16). Zion's greatness comes from God's choice, not its height. On this mountain God declares, "I have set My King on My holy hill of Zion" (Psalm 2:6). Because har lies behind "hill" here, Psalm 24:3 brings Sinai's question to Zion: "Who may ascend into the hill of the LORD? Or who may stand in His holy place?" The answer is "clean hands and a pure heart" (Psalm 24:4). God's holiness still separates. Now, however, the mountain is where God lives among His worshipers.

The latter-day mountain (Isaiah 2; 25; Zechariah 14). Isaiah and Micah announce the same vision: "the mountain of the LORD's house shall be established on the top of the mountains... and all nations shall flow to it" (Isaiah 2:2; Micah 4:1). Sinai fenced the people out. This mountain draws the nations in: "Come, and let us go up to the mountain of the LORD... He will teach us His ways" (Isaiah 2:3). Weapons turn into farm tools (Isaiah 2:4). Isaiah 25 goes further. On this mountain the LORD spreads a feast of rich food for all peoples, removes the covering cast over all nations, and swallows up death forever (Isaiah 25:6–8). Zechariah sees the LORD's feet standing on the Mount of Olives, which splits to open a way of escape (Zechariah 14:4), and hears God promise, "I will return to Zion... the Mountain of the LORD of hosts, the Holy Mountain" (Zechariah 8:3).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The mountains of Scripture trace the shape of redemption. Eden, the holy mountain, was lost through sin. Moriah promised a substitute. Sinai revealed God's holiness and the barrier that sin creates. Zion became the place where God dwelt and set His King. The prophets' exalted mountain promised a feast where death is swallowed up.

The Gospels continue the pattern, using the Greek equivalent oros. Jesus teaches from a mountain (Matthew 5:1) in a way that recalls Moses at Sinai, yet He speaks on His own authority: "But I say to you" (Matthew 5:22). He is transfigured on a high mountain, where the glory once seen on Sinai shines from His own person. He speaks of a mountain being cast into the sea by faith in God (Mark 11:23), a saying that recalls Zechariah's "great mountain" becoming a plain "with shouts of 'Grace, grace to it!'" (Zechariah 4:7). He ascends from the Mount of Olives (Acts 1:12), the mountain where Zechariah saw the LORD's feet standing.

Moriah offers a fruitful connection, though the New Testament does not spell it out. Abraham's son was spared on the mountain of Jerusalem's later temple, and in that same city the Father did not spare His own Son.

Hebrews 12 states the climax directly. Believers "have not come to the mountain that may be touched and that burned with fire, and to blackness and darkness and tempest" (Hebrews 12:18). Instead they have come to Mount Zion, the city of the living God, and "to Jesus the Mediator of the new covenant" (Hebrews 12:22–24). The mountain once fenced off now welcomes people in, because the Mediator has gone up for us and opened the way.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 121 begins, "I will lift up my eyes to the hills—from whence comes my help?" The psalm does not say what the hills meant to the one looking at them. Elsewhere in Scripture, hills held shrines that promised help (Jeremiah 3:6) and offered places where desperate people fled (Psalm 11:1), and either may lie in the background. Whatever the hills suggest, the psalmist looks beyond them: "My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth" (Psalm 121:2). Believers today have their own high places that seem to offer help: savings, reputation, a place to hide. Psalm 121 directs our trust past the created height to the One who formed it.

Psalm 24:3 asks a question that no honest person can answer with confidence: who may ascend? Sinai shows that holiness kills the unprepared. The gospel does not lower God's mountain. It provides a Mediator with clean hands who goes up in our place and brings us with Him. That is why a Christian can approach God with reverence and without terror.

Zechariah 4:7 speaks to every person facing an impossible task. The obstacles to rebuilding the temple are pictured as a great mountain, yet God declared it would become a plain. The word to Zerubbabel just before promised success by God's Spirit, not by might or power (Zechariah 4:6). The capstone is brought out amid shouts of "Grace, grace." The mountain that seems to block the work of God can become the setting for His grace.

Jonah went "down to the moorings of the mountains," shut in by the earth's bars, "yet You have brought up my life from the pit, O LORD" (Jonah 2:6). No one sinks so low that God cannot reach him. The God of the summit is also the God of the mountains' roots, and He brings His people up.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 8:4 — The ark rests on the mountains of Ararat. The first mountain Genesis names is a place of rescue after judgment.

  • 1 Kings 18:19–20 — Elijah gathers Israel to Mount Carmel, where the LORD answers by fire and shows that Baal is no god.

  • 1 Kings 19:8, 11 — The discouraged Elijah journeys to Horeb, "the mountain of God," and meets the LORD in a still small voice instead of in the mountain-splitting wind.

  • Deuteronomy 34:1 — Moses climbs Mount Nebo to see the land he will not enter. The mountaintop gives him vision even as it marks the limit of his journey.

  • Isaiah 40:9 — Zion's messenger is told to climb a high mountain and announce good news to Judah's cities: "Behold your God!"

  • Psalm 114:4 — At the Exodus the mountains skip like rams. Creation responds with joy as well as fear when God redeems His people.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1389 (gib'ah, hill) — A distinct Hebrew word for a smaller hill, often paired with har in poetry. Isaiah 2:2 sets them side by side: the LORD's mountain is "established on the top of the mountains, and shall be exalted above the hills."
