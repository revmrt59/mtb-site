CONCEIVE, CONCEIVED - (hârâh, Strong's H2029)

Summary

The Hebrew verb hârâh means "to conceive" or "to become pregnant." It names the hidden beginning of a life, the moment a child starts to exist before anyone can see it. Most of its uses appear in the family stories of Genesis and the historical books. There, the Bible records the conceptions of Isaac, Jacob and Esau, the sons of Leah and Rachel, Moses, Samuel, and others. Again and again the text places God at the center: He opens a closed womb, remembers a grieving woman, answers a husband's prayer, and keeps His promise moving forward through children no one expected.

The prophets and wisdom writers use the same word for darker things. The wicked "conceive trouble" and bring forth falsehood, so sin, like a child, is conceived in secret before it shows itself. Hosea uses the word for children born to an unfaithful wife as a living picture of Israel's betrayal. Taken together, these uses show God bringing life where people could produce none, preserving His promised line through human failure, and exposing the hidden places where evil begins.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hârâh is the ordinary Hebrew verb for becoming pregnant. It belongs to the same family as the adjective hārê (H2030), which means "pregnant" or "with child." Nearly half of its uses fall in Genesis. They gather around Sarah, Rebekah, Leah, Rachel, Hagar, and Tamar, the women through whom Abraham's family grew. The word then marks the births of deliverers and leaders in Exodus, Judges, Samuel, and Kings. It also appears in Job, Psalms, and Isaiah as an image for planning evil, and in Hosea as part of a prophetic sign.

In Israel, children carried a family's name, its inheritance, and God's promise into the future. Barrenness was a source of deep grief and often of public shame (Genesis 30:23). That is why a single sentence like "she conceived" can carry so much weight in a biblical story.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one core meaning: to conceive and become pregnant. Context extends that meaning in two clear directions:

1. Physical conception. A woman becomes pregnant with a child, as when Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son (Genesis 21:2). A parent can also be described simply as the one who conceived someone. Jacob's blessing on Joseph uses the word for his "ancestors" (Genesis 49:26), and the Song of Solomon speaks of a mother as the one who conceived the speaker (Song of Solomon 3:4).

2. Figurative conception. A person forms a plan or purpose inwardly, almost always an evil one, before carrying it out: "They conceive trouble and bring forth futility" (Job 15:35).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The verb usually travels with a second verb meaning "bear" or "bring forth." Many verses follow the pattern "she conceived and bore a son," followed by the naming of the child. That pairing gives the word its natural movement from a hidden beginning to a visible result. The figurative uses depend on the same sequence. Psalm 7:14 says the wicked man "conceives trouble and brings forth falsehood," and Isaiah 59:13 speaks of "conceiving and uttering from the heart words of falsehood."

The surrounding words change the tone. In Genesis 29–30 the conceptions are wrapped in prayer, rivalry, and praise, and Leah names her sons for what the Lord has done. In Genesis 16:4–5, Hagar's pregnancy turns into contempt and family conflict. In Isaiah 26:18 the nation laments that it has been "with child" and brought forth only wind, which describes effort that produced nothing. Isaiah 33:11 tells God's enemies that they will conceive chaff.

4. Theological Significance

The most consistent truth these passages reveal is that God is the giver of life. Genesis states that the Lord saw Leah was unloved and opened her womb (Genesis 29:31). Later, God remembered Rachel and opened hers (Genesis 30:22). Rebekah conceived after Isaac pleaded with the Lord for his barren wife (Genesis 25:21). Hannah conceived Samuel after the Lord remembered her (1 Samuel 1:19–20). The Angel of the Lord announced Samson's conception to a woman who had no children (Judges 13:3). In each case Scripture describes an ordinary biological event as an act of God. He keeps His promise to Abraham's family one child at a time, and He often begins with a woman who cannot bear.

Hebrews draws this out when it says Sarah "received strength to conceive seed... because she judged Him faithful who had promised," so that from a man "as good as dead" came descendants "as many as the stars of the sky" (Hebrews 11:11–12). The life in these stories comes from God's faithfulness. Human strength could not produce it.

The figurative uses show the opposite side. People can conceive too, and what they conceive left to themselves is trouble, deceit, and futility (Job 15:35; Isaiah 59:4). God brings forth life from barrenness, while the sinful heart brings forth emptiness.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The promised offspring and the line God would not let die (Genesis 4:1; 38; 2 Samuel 11). Some of the most important conceptions in Scripture took place in shameful circumstances. Judah's sons by his Canaanite wife (Genesis 38:3–4) brought the family to the edge of extinction. Er was wicked, Onan refused his duty to his brother's widow, and Judah withheld his last son from Tamar. Tamar disguised herself and conceived by Judah himself (Genesis 38:18). From that union came Perez, the ancestor of David. Centuries later Bathsheba sent David a message that she had conceived (2 Samuel 11:5). David's adultery led to the murder of Uriah, and that child died. Yet God later gave the same couple Solomon, the heir to the throne. When Matthew traces the line of Jesus, he names Tamar and "her who had been the wife of Uriah" (Matthew 1:3, 6). God did not hide these chapters, and the promise did not depend on human goodness. Even the very first use of the word carries hope. Eve conceived Cain and spoke of gaining a man with the Lord's help (Genesis 4:1). Many readers hear in her words an expectation of the deliverer promised in Genesis 3:15. If so, her hope was badly misplaced in a son who became a murderer.

Moses and the burden of a people (Numbers 11:12). Worn out by Israel's complaining, Moses asks God whether he conceived all these people or gave birth to them. If he had not, why was he told to carry them like a nurse carrying an infant to the land God promised? It is a raw prayer, but it rests on a true point. Moses did not conceive Israel; God did. God then answered by sharing the Spirit with seventy elders so that Moses would not carry the people alone (Numbers 11:16–17).

Hosea's children (Hosea 1–2). God told Hosea to marry Gomer, a woman of harlotry, and her three conceptions became sermons (Hosea 1:3, 6, 8). The children received the names Jezreel, Lo-Ruhamah ("no mercy"), and Lo-Ammi ("not My people"). Hosea 2:5 explains the picture. The mother who conceived them acted shamefully and credited her lovers with giving her bread and water. Israel did the same when it thanked Baal for harvests that God had given (Hosea 2:8). The blessing of fruitfulness became evidence of betrayal.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The conceptions recorded with this word move toward one Child. Isaiah announced, "the virgin shall conceive and bear a Son, and shall call His name Immanuel" (Isaiah 7:14). That verse uses the related adjective hārê rather than this verb, but it belongs to the same story. The Old Testament shows God opening barren wombs, and it shows Him carrying His promise through people stained by sin. In Mary, God did something greater than both. The angel told her that the Holy Spirit would come upon her, so the Child born would be called the Son of God (Luke 1:35). This Son came through the line of Judah, Tamar, and David, and He came without inheriting their guilt.

Hosea's sad names are reversed in the gospel. God promised to show mercy to the one who had "no mercy" and to call "not My people" His people (Hosea 2:23). Paul and Peter apply that promise to Jews and Gentiles gathered in Christ (Romans 9:25–26; 1 Peter 2:10). The figurative uses also find their answer in Christ. Isaiah's people lamented that they had labored and brought forth only wind (Isaiah 26:18). The very next verse promises that the dead will live, and Jesus' resurrection opens the way to that life.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 7:14 and James 1:15 describe the same pattern across the two Testaments: "when desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death." Sin rarely appears all at once. It is first welcomed, then quietly nourished, and only later born into words and actions. The best time to deal with temptation is at conception, before the thought has been given room to grow. Isaiah locates the problem "from the heart" (Isaiah 59:13), so the battle is won or lost where no one else can see.

The barren women offer a different kind of hope. Hannah, Rachel, and Rebekah could not make life happen, and God did not shame them for their tears. He heard them. Believers who face closed doors, whether in a family, a ministry, or their own spiritual life, can bring the same honest grief to the same faithful God.

Moses' cry belongs in every pastor's prayers. Leaders do carry people, and Paul could describe himself as being "gentle among you, just as a nursing mother cherishes her own children" (1 Thessalonians 2:7) and even as laboring in birth until Christ was formed in the Galatians (Galatians 4:19). Yet the people belong to the God who conceived them. Bringing that burden back to Him is faithfulness, not failure.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 16:4–5 — Hagar conceived and then despised Sarai. The scheme that was supposed to solve Sarai's barrenness instead brought contempt into the home.

  • Genesis 19:36 — Lot's daughters conceived by their father. Their sons became Moab and Ammon, and yet Ruth the Moabitess later appears in Christ's genealogy (Matthew 1:5).

  • Exodus 2:2 — Moses' mother conceived and hid her son, quietly defying Pharaoh's decree.

  • 2 Kings 4:17 — The Shunammite woman conceived at the time Elisha had promised.

  • Job 3:3 — Job curses the night on which a male child was announced as conceived, an honest cry of suffering that the book does not silence.

  • Isaiah 8:3 — The prophetess conceived a son whose name announced coming judgment on Damascus and Samaria.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H2030 (hārê)** — A lexical relative, the adjective "pregnant" or "with child." It appears in Isaiah 7:14 for the virgin who would conceive and bear Immanuel.
