ONAM - (ʼÔwnâm, Strong's H0208)

Summary

Onam is the name of two different men in the Old Testament, and they should not be confused with each other or with Onan (H0209), the son of Judah whom the LORD put to death (Genesis 38). The first Onam was a Horite, a son of Shobal, from the people who lived in Mount Seir before Esau's descendants took the land (Genesis 36:23; 1 Chronicles 1:40). The second Onam belonged to the tribe of Judah. He was a son of Jerahmeel by a wife named Atarah, and he was the father of Shammai and Jada (1 Chronicles 2:26, 28). The name most likely means "strong" or "vigorous." It appears to come from a Hebrew word for strength or vigor, though the derivation is probable rather than certain.

Neither man has a recorded story. Each appears only in a family list. The Judahite Onam still matters more than his two brief mentions suggest (1 Chronicles 2:26, 28), because most of the Jerahmeelite genealogy in 1 Chronicles 2 flows through his sons. When the Chronicler wrote for Israelites returning from exile, he kept the record of this southern clan beside the royal line of David. That choice shows a God who keeps track of the whole family He has called, including branches far from the throne.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Two separate individuals bear this name. Onam the Horite was one of the five sons of Shobal, a son of Seir the Horite (Genesis 36:20, 23). His name is repeated in the parallel list in 1 Chronicles 1:40, where some of his brothers' names are spelled slightly differently ("Alian" for "Alvan," "Shephi" for "Shepho"). Onam the Judahite lived in a different people and probably a different period. He descended from Judah through Perez, Hezron, and Jerahmeel, Hezron's firstborn (1 Chronicles 2:5, 9, 25–26). Nothing in Scripture connects the two men beyond the shared name.

The name ʼÔwnâm is generally linked to the Hebrew noun 'ôn (H0202), which carries the sense of "strength," "vigor," or "wealth." On that basis "strong" or "vigorous" is the probable meaning. Some suggest "their strength," but that reading is less secure. Scripture makes no wordplay on the name. Nothing in the text suggests that either man's life was marked by special strength, and the name should not be read as a summary of his character.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Onam the Horite. The Horites lived in Mount Seir, the rugged country south and southeast of the Dead Sea. Genesis 36 lists their clans alongside the descendants of Esau. Moses later explained what became of them: "The Horites formerly dwelt in Seir, but the descendants of Esau dispossessed them and destroyed them from before them, and dwelt in their place" (Deuteronomy 2:12). Onam's name survives as a record of the people who lived in that land before Edom took it.

Onam the Judahite. Chronicles records that "Jerahmeel had another wife, whose name was Atarah; she was the mother of Onam" (1 Chronicles 2:26). By naming her, the Chronicler sets Onam's branch apart from the sons of Jerahmeel's first wife (2:25). Onam's sons were Shammai and Jada (2:28). The record then follows his descendants for several generations. Through Shammai came Nadab and Abishur and, eventually, a man named Sheshan. Through Jada came Jether and Jonathan (2:28–33). Sheshan had daughters but no sons, so he gave a daughter in marriage to Jarha, his Egyptian servant, and that line continued for many more generations (2:34–41). The Jerahmeelites are later known as a clan of Judah's southern region. David sent them a share of the spoil from his victory over the Amalekites (1 Samuel 30:29; see also 27:10).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

What Scripture states is simple. One Onam belonged to a people displaced from Seir, and the other belonged to Judah's family record. The larger meaning comes from where Chronicles places the Judahite Onam. Jerahmeel was Hezron's firstborn, yet the royal line passed through his younger brother Ram to David (1 Chronicles 2:9–15). Even so, the Chronicler gives Jerahmeel's family, and especially Onam's branch, a detailed record. A fair inference is that Chronicles was written to show returning exiles that the whole of Judah belonged in God's story, not only the house of David. The Horite Onam serves a different purpose. His name helps document the peoples around Israel and the shifts of territory that, in Moses' telling, happened under God's rule (Deuteronomy 2:12).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Onam is not in the ancestry of Jesus. Matthew and Luke trace Christ's line through Ram, not Jerahmeel. The connection is broader. Scripture affirms that "our Lord arose from Judah" (Hebrews 7:14), and the Chronicler's careful record of Judah's many clans is part of the history that God preserved around that promised line. No direct typology attaches to Onam himself.

5. Spirit & Life

The Bible tells us nothing about Onam's faith, but his place in the record still says something about God. A returning Jerahmeelite could open the history of his people and find his own family named, generation by generation, from a branch with no claim to the throne. God does not keep records only of kings. The same list also shows God carrying a family forward when it seemed about to end. Sheshan, a descendant of Onam, had no son, yet his line continued through a daughter and a foreign servant (1 Chronicles 2:34–35). The Chronicler did not hide that unusual turn. He wrote it down as part of Judah's story.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:20, 23 — Names Onam as a son of Shobal among the Horite clans of Seir.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:40 — Repeats the Horite genealogy, with variant spellings.

  • Deuteronomy 2:12 — Explains that Esau's descendants dispossessed the Horites.

  • 1 Chronicles 2:9, 25–26 — Places the Judahite Onam as Jerahmeel's son through Atarah.

  • 1 Chronicles 2:28–41 — Traces Onam's descendants, including Sheshan's line through Jarha.

  • 1 Samuel 30:29 — Shows the Jerahmeelites as a recognized clan in David's time.

  • Hebrews 7:14 — Affirms that the Lord arose from Judah.
