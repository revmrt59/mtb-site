OPHIR - (ʼÔwphîyr, Strong's H0211)

Summary

Ophir names both a man and a land in the Old Testament. The first is a man, a son of Joktan in the line of Shem, listed in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:29; 1 Chronicles 1:23). The second, and by far the more prominent, is a distant land so famous for its gold that "gold of Ophir" became Israel's way of naming the finest gold there was. Solomon's ships, built at Ezion Geber on the Red Sea and crewed alongside Hiram's experienced seamen, sailed there and returned with hundreds of talents of gold, along with almug wood and precious stones. The meaning of the name Ophir is uncertain. No derivation has been established, and the exact location of the land remains a matter of debate, with southern Arabia, India, and East Africa all proposed.

What gives Ophir its weight in Scripture is how the Bible uses its gold. David gave Ophir gold from his personal treasure for the house of God before Solomon's fleet ever sailed (1 Chronicles 29:3–4). When Jehoshaphat later tried to revive the Ophir trade in partnership with Ahab's son Ahaziah, his ships were wrecked before they left port. The poets and prophets then take this standard of earthly wealth and place it beneath something greater. Wisdom cannot be bought with it (Job 28:16), God Himself is the better treasure (Job 22:24–25), and in the day of the LORD's judgment a human being will be harder to find than "the golden wedge of Ophir" (Isaiah 13:12). In Psalm 45, a psalm Hebrews applies to the Son of God, the queen stands at the King's right hand "in gold from Ophir." The best gold on earth turns out to be a gift to offer God, an ornament fit for the King's bride, and a lesser thing than God Himself.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name אוֹפִיר (ʼÔwphîyr) names both a man and a land, whose histories should be distinguished.

Ophir the person. Ophir is the eleventh of the thirteen sons of Joktan, son of Eber, in the line of Shem (Genesis 10:26–29; 1 Chronicles 1:20–23). Genesis 10:29 reads, "Ophir, Havilah, and Jobab. All these were the sons of Joktan." Scripture records nothing about his life. Like many names in the Table of Nations, his name seems to stand for a people and the territory they settled as much as for an individual. His brothers Sheba and Havilah carry names that elsewhere identify regions, which points to the Arabian peninsula and its surroundings as the Joktanite homeland (Genesis 10:30).

Ophir the land. Ophir is also a far-off source of gold reached by sea from Ezion Geber, "near Elath on the shore of the Red Sea, in the land of Edom" (1 Kings 9:26). Many scholars think the land took its name from the Joktanite people descended from Ophir. That connection is likely, but the text does not state it. The location of Ophir is unknown. Southern Arabia fits the Joktanite setting and was known for gold. India has been suggested because of the almug wood. East Africa, including the region some identify with the Egyptians' Punt, is a third proposal. Scripture tells us only that Ophir was reached by sea from the Red Sea port, far enough away that a royal fleet and skilled sailors were needed. A destination to the south or east is an inference from that departure point and from these proposals.

The meaning of the name. The meaning of Ophir is uncertain. Proposals connect it to ideas such as "ashes" or "rich," but none has solid support, and the Bible makes no wordplay on the name. What carries meaning in Scripture is the reputation of the place, not the etymology of the word. In poetry "Ophir" can stand almost alone for its gold; Job 22:24 places "the gold of Ophir" in parallel with plain gold.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Ophir among the Nations

Ophir first appears as a name in Shem's family tree, one of many peoples spreading across the earth after the flood (Genesis 10:29–32). The chronicler repeats the line at the start of his history of Israel (1 Chronicles 1:23). Here Ophir is only a branch of the human family, without wealth or reputation. His place in the record still matters, because Genesis 10 presents every nation as part of one family under God's hand. That family is the world into which God would call Abram from the line of Shem through Eber's other son, Peleg (Genesis 11:16–26).

David's Gift for God's House

Before Ophir becomes a symbol of Solomon's trade empire, it appears in an act of worship. Near the end of his life, David tells the assembly that beyond everything he had gathered for the temple, he is giving his own personal treasure "because I have set my affection on the house of my God." That gift included three thousand talents of gold of Ophir, intended for overlaying the walls of the temple buildings (1 Chronicles 29:3–4). The leaders followed his example and gave willingly, and David blessed God by confessing that all things come from Him and that Israel was only giving back what He had first given (1 Chronicles 29:6–14). The finest gold the nation knew was given gladly to the place where God would put His name.

Solomon's Fleet and the Golden Age

Solomon then turned Ophir from a source known by reputation into a route his own ships traveled. "King Solomon also built a fleet of ships at Ezion Geber," and "Hiram sent his servants with the fleet, seamen who knew the sea, to work with the servants of Solomon" (1 Kings 9:26–27). "And they went to Ophir, and acquired four hundred and twenty talents of gold from there, and brought it to King Solomon" (1 Kings 9:28). The parallel account in 2 Chronicles 8:18 records the amount as four hundred and fifty talents.

The voyages brought more than gold. "The ships of Hiram, which brought gold from Ophir, brought great quantities of almug wood and precious stones from Ophir" (1 Kings 10:11; compare 2 Chronicles 9:10, where the wood is called algum). Solomon used that wood for supports in the house of the LORD and the royal palace, and for harps and stringed instruments for the singers (1 Kings 10:12; 2 Chronicles 9:11). So Ophir supplied the temple's music as well as its gold.

The same chapters show how much wealth poured into Jerusalem (1 Kings 10:14–27). Deuteronomy 17:17 had told Israel's king not to multiply silver and gold for himself, and 1 Kings 11 records that Solomon's heart was later turned aside by his many foreign wives. The text does not blame Ophir gold for Solomon's decline. The narrative does, however, set his dazzling prosperity directly beside his spiritual drift, and after his death the people complained of the heavy burden he had placed on them (1 Kings 12:4). The glory and the danger arrived together.

Jehoshaphat's Wrecked Ships

Generations later, Jehoshaphat of Judah tried to reopen the route. "Jehoshaphat made merchant ships to go to Ophir for gold; but they never sailed, for the ships were wrecked at Ezion Geber" (1 Kings 22:48). Chronicles, which describes the ships as built to go to Tarshish rather than naming Ophir, explains why. Jehoshaphat had joined with Ahaziah king of Israel, "who acted very wickedly," to build the fleet. The prophet Eliezer son of Dodavah told him that because he had allied himself with Ahaziah, the LORD had destroyed his works (2 Chronicles 20:35–37). Kings adds that afterward Ahaziah proposed sending his own servants on the ships, and Jehoshaphat refused (1 Kings 22:49), which suggests he took the lesson to heart. The ships never left the harbor Solomon's fleet had sailed from.

Jehoshaphat was a godly king in many respects, but this was not the first time he had joined himself to Ahab's house (2 Chronicles 18:1; 19:2). The route to Ophir stayed closed because God closed it.

Ophir in Wisdom and Prophecy

After the history, Ophir's gold becomes a fixed measure of value in Israel's poetry. In Job, Eliphaz urges Job to return to the Almighty: "Then you will lay your gold in the dust, And the gold of Ophir among the stones of the brooks. Yes, the Almighty will be your gold And your precious silver" (Job 22:24–25). In Job 28, a long poem about the search for wisdom, the speaker describes men tunneling into the earth for precious metals, yet wisdom cannot be found there and cannot be valued in the gold of Ophir or bought with any jewel (Job 28:1–19). Its source is God, and "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom" (Job 28:23–28).

Psalm 45, a royal wedding song, pictures the king in majesty and his bride beside him: "Kings' daughters are among Your honorable women; At Your right hand stands the queen in gold from Ophir" (Psalm 45:9). Isaiah uses the same gold in a much darker setting. In his oracle against Babylon, the day of the LORD comes with such devastation that God declares, "I will make a mortal more rare than fine gold, A man more than the golden wedge of Ophir" (Isaiah 13:12).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

What Scripture states plainly is that Ophir was a real place, that its gold was the finest Israel knew, that David gave it for God's house, that Solomon obtained it by sea, and that God wrecked Jehoshaphat's fleet because of his alliance with a wicked king. Wisdom literature and prophecy then use Ophir's gold as the highest earthly standard of worth.

Several implications follow when these passages are read together. Ophir marks the outer edge of Israel's visible prosperity under Solomon, the point at which God's promise of riches to Solomon (1 Kings 3:13) was most fully seen. The same wealth also brought the testing Deuteronomy 17 anticipated. Gold is a gift from God, but it does not keep a king faithful.

The difference between David and Jehoshaphat is also instructive. David gave Ophir gold away for the house of God, and God received it. Jehoshaphat reached for Ophir gold through partnership with a king who did evil, and God stopped him in the harbor. The gold was the same in both cases. What differed was the heart and the alliance behind it.

Finally, the poets and prophets repeatedly place Ophir beneath something greater. It ranks below the Almighty Himself (Job 22:25), below wisdom and the fear of the LORD (Job 28:16, 28), and in Isaiah's vision of judgment it becomes easier to find than surviving people (Isaiah 13:12). Isaiah's point is the terrible extent of Babylon's destruction. Still, the image carries an irony that is hard to miss. People themselves will become rarer than the gold they spend their lives pursuing. Scripture uses the world's best gold to teach that it is never the best thing.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Ophir is not named in the New Testament. One connection, however, rests on explicit New Testament teaching. Hebrews 1:8–9 quotes Psalm 45:6–7 and applies the psalm's royal words to God's Son: His throne is forever, and He has loved righteousness. That means the King beside whom the queen stands "in gold from Ophir" is ultimately Christ. Reading the queen as a picture of the church is a canonical development rather than an explicit New Testament citation of verse 9. It is well supported, though, by the New Testament's description of Christ's people as His bride, presented to Him glorious and without blemish (Ephesians 5:25–27; Revelation 19:7–8). Revelation clothes that bride in fine linen, the righteous acts of the saints, rather than in gold. Even so, the psalm's picture holds: the King's bride is adorned with the best there is, and it is His gift that makes her beautiful.

Other links are thematic and do not involve the name directly. Job 28 asks where wisdom can be found when no gold can buy it, and the New Testament answers that Christ Jesus "became for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30) and that in Him all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden (Colossians 2:3). Jesus' parables of the hidden treasure and the pearl of great price (Matthew 13:44–46), along with His call to lay up treasure in heaven (Matthew 6:19–21), carry forward the logic of Job 22:25. Peter goes further, saying we "were not redeemed with corruptible things, like silver or gold," but with the precious blood of Christ (1 Peter 1:18–19). The gold of Ophir could adorn a temple, but it could not ransom a single soul.

The Bible's final picture of gold also deserves mention. In the New Jerusalem, "the city was pure gold, like clear glass," and "the street of the city was pure gold, like transparent glass" (Revelation 21:18, 21). Ophir is not mentioned there, and the city is not said to fulfill Ophir. The contrast is still striking. Kings once sent ships across the sea for gold; in God's city, gold is the pavement under the feet of His people, because the treasure of that city is God's presence with them (Revelation 21:3, 22–23).

5. Spirit & Life

David's gift is the first place Ophir gold appears in Israel's story, and it tells us something about God. He does not need gold, yet He received the best gold of the age as an offering of love. David's prayer explains why giving is possible at all: "all things come from You, and of Your own we have given You" (1 Chronicles 29:14). God invites us to give Him our best, not because He lacks anything, but because a heart that loves Him wants to give it.

Jehoshaphat's broken ships carry a sober lesson. A good man pursued a good-looking goal through a partnership God opposed, and God loved him enough to stop it at the dock. Sometimes the failure of a venture is God's mercy. Kings adds that Jehoshaphat refused Ahaziah's later proposal to send his servants on the ships (1 Kings 22:49). Scripture does not state his reason, but it is a fair inference that he had learned not to try again.

Eliphaz was wrong about Job, and God later said the friends had not spoken rightly of Him (Job 42:7). Yet the words "the Almighty will be your gold" express what the psalmists say elsewhere: "Whom have I in heaven but You?" (Psalm 73:25–26; compare Psalm 16:5). The image is pointed. Picture a man laying the finest gold in the world among the stones of a creek bed, as if it were gravel, because he has found something better. That is the freedom God offers to people who are anxious about money, defined by what they own, or envious of those who have more.

Isaiah 13:12 asks us to take God's judgment seriously. Babylon gathered the world's treasure, and God declared that in the day of His wrath people would be scarcer than its gold. No amount of wealth shields anyone from that day. The only safety is in the One whose blood is more precious than gold.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:29; 1 Chronicles 1:23 — Ophir the son of Joktan in the line of Shem, and the likely source of the land's name.

  • 1 Chronicles 29:3–4, 14 — David gives three thousand talents of Ophir gold from his own treasure for God's house and confesses that all things come from God.

  • 1 Kings 9:26–28; 2 Chronicles 8:17–18 — Solomon's fleet from Ezion Geber, crewed with Hiram's seamen, brings gold from Ophir.

  • 1 Kings 10:11–12; 2 Chronicles 9:10–11 — Almug wood and precious stones from Ophir, used in the temple and for the singers' instruments.

  • 1 Kings 22:48–49; 2 Chronicles 20:35–37 — Jehoshaphat's ships are wrecked because of his alliance with Ahaziah.

  • Job 22:24–25 — Ophir's gold laid among the stones of the brooks; the Almighty as true treasure.

  • Job 28:16, 28 — Wisdom cannot be valued in the gold of Ophir; the fear of the Lord is wisdom.

  • Psalm 45:9; Hebrews 1:8–9 — The queen in gold from Ophir beside the King whom Hebrews identifies as the Son.

  • Isaiah 13:12 — In the judgment on Babylon, a mortal becomes rarer than the golden wedge of Ophir.

  • Deuteronomy 17:17; 1 Peter 1:18–19; Revelation 21:18, 21 — Wider canonical context: the warning against royal hoarding, redemption by a price greater than gold, and the golden city of God's presence.
