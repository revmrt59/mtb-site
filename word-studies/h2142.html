REMEMBER, MENTION - (zâkar, Strong's H2142)

Summary

The Hebrew verb zâkar means "to remember." In Scripture, remembering is much more than recalling a fact. It means giving focused attention to a person, a promise or a past event in a way that leads to action. When God remembers Noah, the waters go down. When God remembers His covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, the Exodus begins. When Israel is told to remember the years in Egypt and the wilderness, the purpose is humility, obedience and mercy toward the poor. The same verb can also mean to "mention," that is, to speak a name aloud in worship or testimony.

The word reaches its greatest depth where God's memory meets human sin. Scripture warns that God will "remember their iniquity" and act in judgment. It also records His astonishing promise: "their sin I will remember no more" (Jeremiah 31:34). God's people forget Him and drift into idolatry, yet He remembers His promise and restores them. Because His memory is never idle, believers can pray "Remember me," fight despair by recalling His works, and rest in the knowledge that in Christ their sins will never again be brought against them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Zâkar is a common Hebrew verb found from Genesis to Malachi. Its heaviest use is in the Psalms, which account for about a quarter of its occurrences. It also appears often in Isaiah, Ezekiel, Jeremiah and Deuteronomy. Those books show the range of settings where the word appears: Israel's prayers and praise, Moses' preaching to a people about to enter the land, and the prophets' charges against a nation that had forgotten its God.

The word appears in ordinary human situations as well. Joseph asks Pharaoh's cupbearer to remember him, and the cupbearer forgets (Genesis 40:14, 23). In Israel's culture, memory was kept alive through feasts, the Sabbath, the tassels on garments (Numbers 15:39), and the repeated telling of God's deeds. Remembering was something a person and a community did, not only something that happened inside the mind.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Zâkar has two main senses.

1. To remember, keep in mind, be mindful of. This means bringing someone or something into focused attention, usually with a response that follows. God "remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob" (Exodus 2:24). Israel is told to "remember that the LORD your God led you all the way these forty years in the wilderness" (Deuteronomy 8:2).

2. To mention, call to remembrance aloud, invoke. In this sense, remembering is spoken. Israel must "make no mention of the name of other gods" (Exodus 23:13). The psalmist promises, "I will make mention of Your righteousness, of Yours only" (Psalm 71:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word in a given passage depends largely on who is remembering. When God is the subject, His remembering is followed at once by action. He remembered Rachel and opened her womb (Genesis 30:22), and He remembered Hannah, who then conceived Samuel (1 Samuel 1:19). When God remembers iniquity, the action that follows is judgment (Hosea 8:13).

When people are the subject, the word usually appears as a call to faithfulness. "Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy" (Exodus 20:8) is a command to keep a day as well as to recall it. The prophets condemn Israel's failure to remember as a moral failure, the forgetfulness that leads to idolatry.

Two grammatical patterns change how the word reads. The first is the command addressed to God: "Remember, O LORD." Psalms, Nehemiah, Jeremiah and Lamentations all use it. It is the language of bold prayer, in which a worshiper appeals to God to act on what He has promised. The second is the negative request, "do not remember," where a worshiper asks God not to act on his sins (Psalm 25:7; Isaiah 64:9).

4. Theological Significance

When Scripture says that God remembers, it never suggests that He had forgotten. Exodus 2:24 says, "So God heard their groaning, and God remembered His covenant." The next verse says that God looked on the children of Israel and acknowledged them. His remembering marks the moment His long-standing promise moves into action. The same pattern appears from the beginning. God remembered Noah and sent a wind over the earth (Genesis 8:1). He remembered Abraham and brought Lot out of Sodom (Genesis 19:29). Psalm 105:8 sums up the pattern: "He remembers His covenant forever, The word which He commanded, for a thousand generations." God's memory is His faithfulness at work.

Human memory is weak by comparison and needs training. This is why Deuteronomy uses the word so often. Moses tells Israel to remember that they were slaves in Egypt and that the LORD redeemed them, and he ties that memory to Sabbath rest, generosity to freed servants and justice for the stranger and widow (Deuteronomy 5:15; 15:15; 24:18). He tells them to remember the wilderness, where God humbled them and tested "what was in your heart" (8:2). Later in the same chapter he warns them that when they become wealthy they must remember that the LORD gives them power to get wealth (8:18). Forgetting God's past grace leads to pride. A people who remember where they came from stay humble and become merciful.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Remembered iniquity and forgotten sin (Hosea 8:13; Isaiah 43:25; Jeremiah 31:34). God's memory can be a terrifying thing. Hosea announces, "Now He will remember their iniquity and punish their sins." Psalm 109:14 even prays, "Let the iniquity of his fathers be remembered before the LORD." In these passages, for God to remember sin is for Him to act against it. That background gives the promises of grace their full weight. "I, even I, am He who blots out your transgressions for My own sake; And I will not remember your sins" (Isaiah 43:25). In Jeremiah's promise of a new covenant, God says, "For I will forgive their iniquity, and their sin I will remember no more" (Jeremiah 31:34). The all-knowing God does not lose information. He makes a decision as Judge that He will never again act against His people on the basis of their sins. Isaiah adds that He does this "for My own sake," which means the promise rests on God's own character and not on Israel's improvement.

Ezekiel 16: forgetting, remembering and the return of memory. Ezekiel pictures Jerusalem as a foundling whom God rescued and married, who then gave herself to other lovers. God names the root of her unfaithfulness. In all her abominations she did not remember the days of her youth, when she lay naked and helpless (16:22, 43). After the long indictment comes the turn in verse 60. God says that He will remember His covenant with her "in the days of your youth" and establish an everlasting covenant. The same days she forgot, God remembered. His remembering then restores hers. She will remember her ways and be ashamed (16:61), and when He provides atonement for all she has done, she will remember and never again open her mouth in pride (16:63). Human forgetfulness opened the door to idolatry. God's memory opens the door to grace, and grace gives His people back their memory in the form of humble, grateful repentance.

Psalm 25 and Nehemiah: asking God to direct His memory. David asks God to remember two things and forget one. "Remember, O LORD, Your tender mercies and Your lovingkindnesses, For they are from of old" (Psalm 25:6). Then he asks God not to remember the sins of his youth, and instead to remember him according to His mercy and goodness (25:7). David appeals to God's own character against his own record. Nehemiah prays differently. Throughout his memoir he asks God to remember him for good (Nehemiah 5:19; 13:14, 22, 31) and to remember his opponents according to their works (6:14). When human recognition failed, Nehemiah placed his labor and his reputation in God's keeping.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of zâkar runs toward Christ along both of its main lines. God's remembering of His covenant, which began the Exodus, reappears at the coming of Jesus. In the Gospel of Luke, Zechariah praises God for raising up salvation in the house of David to show mercy and remember His holy covenant (Luke 1:72). The promise God never forgot finds its "Yes" in His Son.

The New Testament also states plainly that Jeremiah's promise is fulfilled in Christ. Hebrews quotes it twice: "their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more" (Hebrews 8:12; 10:17). The second quotation sits within an argument that Christ's one offering has perfected those who are sanctified. God can justly decline to act on sin because Christ has borne it. Paul's words carry the same comfort: "Blessed is the man to whom the LORD shall not impute sin" (Romans 4:8). Ezekiel's promise of atonement and an everlasting covenant is fulfilled at the cross.

Remembering also moves from Israel's Passover to the church's table. Israel ate unleavened bread so that they would remember the day they came out of Egypt (Deuteronomy 16:3). Jesus took bread at Passover and told His disciples to do this in remembrance of Him. The New Testament does not tie these together with this Hebrew word, but the pattern carries forward: God's people keep their identity by remembering a redemption God accomplished for them.

7. Spirit & Life

Remembering is one of the ways a believer fights through darkness. Psalm 77 is honest that memory can hurt at first: "I remembered God, and was troubled" (77:3). The psalmist does not stop there. He resolves to remember the works of the LORD and His wonders of old (77:11). Psalm 42 makes the same choice in the middle of grief: "O my God, my soul is cast down within me; Therefore I will remember You" (42:6). Jonah, sinking in the sea, says, "When my soul fainted within me, I remembered the LORD; And my prayer went up to You" (Jonah 2:7). In Lamentations the prophet admits, "My soul still remembers And sinks within me" (3:20). Then he deliberately turns his mind to something else: "This I recall to my mind, Therefore I have hope" (3:21). What he recalls is the LORD's mercies, which are new every morning. Hope is not a mood that happens to arrive. It comes when we take hold of what God has already done and set it against what we feel.

Remembering also becomes speech. Worship that only thinks about God falls short of what this word means. Isaiah tells God's people to "Declare His deeds among the peoples, Make mention that His name is exalted" (Isaiah 12:4). The names we speak with honor reveal whom we trust. Israel was forbidden even to let the names of other gods be "heard from your mouth" (Exodus 23:13). Psalm 71:16 is a good resolve for any believer: "I will make mention of Your righteousness, of Yours only."

Finally, God's memory gives a believer freedom. We may pray "Remember me" with confidence, because God's remembering always leads to action on behalf of His people. And we need not live under the record of forgiven sin. The God who keeps every promise has chosen not to keep that record against us.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 40:23 – The cupbearer did not remember Joseph but forgot him. Human forgetting delayed Joseph's rescue, yet God was not delayed in fulfilling His purpose.

  • Numbers 15:39–40 – The tassels on Israel's garments were meant to help them remember and do all God's commandments, a visible aid for a forgetful heart.

  • Psalm 8:4 – David marvels that God is mindful of mere man. God's attention to frail humanity is itself a wonder.

  • Psalm 103:14 – God remembers that we are dust. Here His memory leads Him to pity and gentleness toward our weakness.

  • Ecclesiastes 12:1 – "Remember now your Creator in the days of your youth." This is a call to give one's strongest years to God before age and weakness come.

  • Malachi 4:4 – The Old Testament closes with a call to remember the Law of Moses, so that a waiting people would hold fast to God's word until He acts again.
