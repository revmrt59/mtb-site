SONG, PSALM, SINGING - (zâmîyr, Strong's H2158)

Summary

The Hebrew word zâmîyr means a song, especially a song of praise lifted to God. The NKJV renders it "songs," "psalms," "singing," and once "psalmist." It is a rare word, used only six times in the Old Testament. Those six uses cover nearly the whole range of life before God: a dying king, a sufferer in the dark, a traveler far from home, a congregation entering God's presence, a land waking up in spring, and a world under judgment.

The best-known use comes from Elihu, who speaks of "God my Maker, Who gives songs in the night" (Job 35:10). God does not wait for morning to give His people something to sing. Elsewhere David, Israel's anointed king, is called "the sweet psalmist of Israel" (2 Samuel 23:1). A psalmist declares that God's statutes have been his songs while he lives as a stranger on the earth (Psalm 119:54). Isaiah hears songs of "Glory to the righteous!" rising from the ends of the earth while the world is shaken (Isaiah 24:16). Together these verses show that true song comes from God. It belongs to His King, it is fed by His Word, and it will finally fill the whole earth.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Zâmîyr comes from the Hebrew verb zâmar, "to sing praise" or "make music," often with instruments. The far more common Hebrew word for song is shîyr, so zâmîyr stands out wherever it appears. It occurs in a historical book (2 Samuel), in the poetry and wisdom books (Job, Psalms, Song of Solomon), and in the prophets (Isaiah). Every use is poetic or elevated speech, and nearly every one connects song with God or His Word.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

A zâmîyr is a composed song, a piece of music meant to be sung. Two closely related uses appear:

1. A song or psalm of praise, the actual piece that is sung to God or about Him, as in "Let us shout joyfully to Him with psalms" (Psalm 95:2).

2. Singing as an activity or occasion, as in "The time of singing has come" (Song of Solomon 2:12).

In 2 Samuel 23:1 the word helps form a title for David. The NKJV translates the phrase "the sweet psalmist of Israel," describing David as the one whose songs were pleasant to Israel.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines much of what the song means. In Psalm 95:2 it is the corporate praise of worshipers gathering before the Lord. In Job 35:10 and Psalm 119:54 it is the private song of someone in hardship: a sufferer at night and a sojourner away from home. In Song of Solomon 2:12 it marks the joy of a renewed landscape. In Isaiah 24:16 it becomes a worldwide sound of praise heard over the ruins of judgment. In 2 Samuel 23:1 it describes a person, the king remembered for his songs.

The word for pruning in Song of Solomon 2:12 deserves a note. Hebrew has a similar-sounding word meaning "pruning," and some translations render that verse "the time of pruning has come." The NKJV's "singing" fits the turtledove's voice in the next line, though a poet may have enjoyed the double sound.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these passages teach that God is the source of His people's song. Job 35:10 says it plainly: He is the God "Who gives songs." Worship is first His gift and only then our offering. The God who made us also gives us the music with which we answer Him.

The verses also show that song belongs to every condition of life. Israel sang in the courts of worship, but it also sang in the night, on the road, and amid the collapse of nations. Because God is "the great King above all gods" (Psalm 95:3), no circumstance places Him beyond praise. Song also stays tied to truth. The psalmist sings God's statutes, and the remnant sings God's righteousness. Biblical song is not mood music. It is truth about God set to melody.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Job 35:10 — "Songs in the night." Elihu observes that people cry out under oppression (Job 35:9), "But no one says, 'Where is God my Maker, Who gives songs in the night.'" Sufferers groan about their pain without seeking the One who could meet them in it. The detail that matters is the timing. God gives songs in the night, before dawn and before the situation changes. Elihu is a human speaker, and parts of his argument with Job fall short, but this line agrees with the rest of Scripture. Psalm 42:8 voices the same confidence from inside grief: "in the night His song shall be with me." Acts 16:25 shows it in action. Beaten and chained, "at midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them." Their song did not come from their circumstances, and the other prisoners heard the difference.

2 Samuel 23:1 — The sweet psalmist. David's last words open with his titles: "the man raised up on high, The anointed of the God of Jacob, And the sweet psalmist of Israel." The anointed king and the singer are the same man. Israel remembered David as a warrior, and it also remembered him as the one who gave the nation its songs. The next verse explains where those songs came from: the Spirit of the Lord spoke through him (2 Samuel 23:2). God's chosen ruler led His people in praise as well as in battle, and his songs became Israel's prayer book.

Psalm 119:54 — Statutes as songs. "Your statutes have been my songs In the house of my pilgrimage." A "house of pilgrimage" is a temporary lodging, the place of someone who does not belong where he lives. Strangers in a foreign land often feel insecure and homesick. The psalmist found that God's commands, which many would treat as a burden, had become what he sang. The following verse continues the thought: he remembers the Lord's name in the night (Psalm 119:55). God's Word turned an outsider's rented room into a place of worship.

Psalm 95:2 — Entering His presence. "Let us come before His presence with thanksgiving; Let us shout joyfully to Him with psalms." The psalm calls God's people to approach Him out loud and together, with joy and thanksgiving. Reverence follows soon after, as worshipers bow and kneel before their Maker (95:6). The psalm then warns them not to harden their hearts as Israel did in the wilderness (95:7–11). Joyful song and a soft heart belong together. Singing to the great King prepares a people to hear His voice.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

David, the anointed singer, points ahead to his greater Son. Hebrews 2:12 places Psalm 22 in the mouth of Jesus: "In the midst of the assembly I will sing praise to You." The Messiah is the King who leads His brothers and sisters in praise to the Father, the sweet psalmist fully realized.

Jesus also sang a song in the night. On the night He was betrayed, the Gospels record that He and His disciples sang a hymn before going out to the Mount of Olives (Mark 14:26). Facing the darkest night anyone has known, the Son praised the Father. Because He went through that night and rose again, the "songs in the night" of Job 35:10 can belong to everyone who is in Him.

The story ends where Isaiah 24 points. That chapter describes the earth emptied and shaken, with "the mirth of the land" gone (24:11). Yet from distant shores voices rise to glorify the Lord (24:14–15): "From the ends of the earth we have heard songs: 'Glory to the righteous!'" The prophet cannot join in yet. He cries, "I am ruined, ruined! Woe to me!" because treachery still fills the earth (24:16). His honesty matters. The final song is sure, but it has not yet arrived everywhere, and believers live between hearing it from afar and singing it in full. Zephaniah 3:17 adds the deepest note of all: the Lord Himself "will rejoice over you with singing." The God who gives songs also sings over His redeemed people.

7. Spirit & Life

Elihu's complaint still describes people today. Many cry out about their pain and never ask, "Where is God my Maker?" Job 35:10 invites the sufferer to look to the Giver instead of the gift. You do not have to manufacture a song in the dark. Ask God for one. Psalm 42:8 and the jail in Philippi show that He gives it.

Psalm 119:54 explains how such songs are sustained. A believer who fills his heart with God's Word will find that Scripture rises up as song when life feels unsettled. Hebrews 11:13 and 1 Peter 2:11 describe Christians as strangers and pilgrims, so every believer lives in the "house of my pilgrimage." The statutes that seem like rules from a distance become melodies in a lonely room. The hymns and psalms that carry Scripture are among the best gifts a church can give its members for the nights ahead.

Song of Solomon 2:12 adds a gentler note. After winter come flowers, the turtledove, and "the time of singing." The verse is love poetry, and it should not be forced into an allegory. Still, it reflects a pattern found throughout Scripture: when God brings life back, joy finds its voice. The pruning wordplay, if the poet intended it, offers a fitting picture. Jesus said the Father prunes every fruitful branch "that it may bear more fruit" (John 15:2). The cutting back is not the end. Believers in a cold or painful stretch of life can expect a time of singing to follow.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because zâmîyr occurs only six times, every occurrence has been discussed above: 2 Samuel 23:1, Job 35:10, Psalm 95:2, and Psalm 119:54 in Section 5; Isaiah 24:16 in Section 6; and Song of Solomon 2:12 in Sections 3 and 7.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H2167 (zâmar), "to sing praise, make music":** the verb from which zâmîyr comes, used often in the Psalms for singing praise to God.

  • **H7892 (shîyr), "song":** a distinct and far more common Hebrew word for song. It is a related word in meaning, not the same word.
