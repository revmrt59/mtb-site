ZIMRAN - (Zimrân, Strong's H2175)

Summary

Zimran is a son of Abraham by Keturah. He is named first among her six sons in Genesis 25:2 and again in the genealogy of 1 Chronicles 1:32. Those two verses are the only places Scripture mentions him, and neither records anything he said or did. The meaning of his name is uncertain. It most likely comes from the Hebrew root zamar, which can mean "to sing" or "make music," suggesting something like "musician" or "celebrated." The same letters can also mean "to prune," which would point to "vine-dresser." No one can say with confidence which sense lies behind the name, and Scripture makes nothing of it.

Zimran's importance comes from where Genesis places him. Abraham "gave all that he had to Isaac," but to the sons of the concubines he gave gifts and sent them "eastward, away from Isaac his son, to the country of the east" (Genesis 25:5–6). Zimran therefore stands among Abraham's real children who were blessed but did not carry the covenant line and inheritance given to Isaac. Paul later draws out the principle behind that separation: "In Isaac your seed shall be called" (Romans 9:7). Zimran's quiet place in the record shows both how widely God's blessing on Abraham spread and how carefully God kept the line of promise that led to Christ.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Zimran (Hebrew זִמְרָן) is an individual person, a son of Abraham and Keturah. His brothers are Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak, and Shuah (Genesis 25:2; 1 Chronicles 1:32), and Isaac and Ishmael are his half-brothers. He is listed first among Keturah's sons. That order may reflect birth order, but the text does not call him the firstborn. No other person in Scripture bears this name. He should not be confused with Zimri (H2174), a separately indexed name borne by several people in the Old Testament.

The meaning of the name is probable at best. Most explanations trace it to the Hebrew root zamar. One branch of that root means "to sing, make music, play an instrument," and would give a sense like "musician" or "celebrated." A second root with the same letters means "to prune or trim," which would yield "vine-dresser." The exact derivation remains debated. Genesis offers no wordplay on the name and attaches no meaning to it, so it is wise not to read Zimran's life through either possibility.

Genesis 25:1 says that Abraham "again took a wife, and her name was Keturah," and 1 Chronicles 1:32 calls her "Abraham's concubine." The account comes after Sarah's death in the order of Genesis, but the text does not say exactly when Abraham's relationship with Keturah began.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Scripture records Zimran's birth and his sending away, and nothing more. After Genesis lists Keturah's sons and grandsons (Genesis 25:1–4), it reports what Abraham did before his death: "And Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac. But Abraham gave gifts to the sons of the concubines which Abraham had; and while he was still living he sent them eastward, away from Isaac his son, to the country of the east" (Genesis 25:5–6). Zimran was one of those sons. He received a share of his father's goods and settled somewhere in the lands east and southeast of Canaan, generally understood as Arabia.

Centuries later, the Chronicler repeated Zimran's name (1 Chronicles 1:32) as part of tracing the nations descended from Abraham before narrowing the account to Israel. Older commentators tried to link Zimran's descendants to particular Arabian places or peoples, including a town called Zabram on the Red Sea coast. These remain proposals without firm evidence. Some have also suggested that "all the kings of Zimri" in Jeremiah 25:25 were his descendants. That name is indexed separately, however, and the connection is unproven.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Abraham gave everything he had to Isaac and sent his other sons away "from Isaac his son." The reason is not spelled out in Genesis 25, but the wider story supplies it. God had already said that His covenant would be established with Isaac (Genesis 17:19, 21), and Abraham's actions while "still living" may well have been meant to settle the matter before it could be disputed, though Genesis does not state his motive. The same pattern had appeared earlier with Ishmael (Genesis 21:10–12).

Zimran also shows that God kept His word to make Abraham "a father of many nations" (Genesis 17:4). Abraham's blessing reached beyond Israel and Ishmael to the peoples of Keturah's sons. Zimran was not forgotten or cut off from all good. He received real gifts from his father. The line of promise, though, went through Isaac alone. This contrast between gifts and the full inheritance is an observation drawn from the passage, not a doctrine Genesis itself develops. It is still worth noticing.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never names Zimran, but Paul states the principle that governs his place in Genesis: "nor are they all children because they are the seed of Abraham; but, 'In Isaac your seed shall be called.' That is, those who are the children of the flesh, these are not the children of God; but the children of the promise are counted as the seed" (Romans 9:7–8). Paul illustrates the point with Ishmael and Isaac. Keturah's sons stand in the same position with respect to Isaac's line. Paul does not name them or pass judgment on any of them personally, so this is an analogy about natural descent being insufficient, not a verdict on Zimran's own standing before God. Paul goes on to identify the promised Seed as Christ, and those who belong to Christ are Abraham's seed and heirs (Galatians 3:16, 29). The narrowing of the line in Genesis 25 serves that later opening, because God's promise that all the families of the earth would be blessed in Abraham (Genesis 12:3) reaches the nations through Isaac's line and finally through Christ.

The direction of Zimran's departure has a thematic echo in Genesis. Adam and Eve were driven from Eden, and the cherubim were placed at its east side (Genesis 3:24). Cain went to dwell east of Eden (Genesis 4:16), and Lot chose the plain and journeyed east (Genesis 13:11). Zimran was likewise sent eastward, away from the son of promise. This is a resonance rather than a stated teaching, and the east is not a cursed direction in Scripture. Job was "the greatest of all the people of the East" (Job 1:3), and wise men from the East came to worship Jesus (Matthew 2:1).

5. Spirit & Life

Zimran's brief record reveals something of God's ways. God was generous to Abraham's household well beyond the line of promise, yet He did not blur that line. Salvation does not come by natural descent, even from Abraham. It has always come through trusting the God who makes and keeps His promises, and that promise is fulfilled in Christ. Genesis does not tell us whether Zimran shared Abraham's faith. A person can live near the blessings of God's people, and even be family to them, without receiving the inheritance itself. Zimran did not receive Isaac's earthly inheritance or the covenant line, but the inheritance that matters most, being Abraham's heir in Christ, is now offered freely to anyone, from east or west, who belongs to Christ by faith (Galatians 3:29).

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 25:1–4 — Names Zimran among Keturah's sons and places him in Abraham's later family.

  • Genesis 25:5–6 — Records the gifts given to the concubines' sons and their sending to the east, away from Isaac.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:32 — Preserves Zimran in the genealogy of Abraham's descendants.

  • Genesis 17:4, 19–21 — God's promise that Abraham would father many nations, and His covenant established with Isaac.

  • Romans 9:7–8 — Paul's teaching that the children of promise, not merely physical descendants, are counted as Abraham's seed.

  • Galatians 3:16, 29 — Christ as the promised Seed, and believers as Abraham's heirs in Him.
