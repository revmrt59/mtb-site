ARM, SHOULDER, STRENGTH - (zᵉrôwaʻ, Strong's H2220)

Summary

The Hebrew word zᵉrôwaʻ means "arm." It names the actual limb of a person, or the shoulder cut of a sacrificed animal. In the Old Testament, though, the arm most often stands for strength put into action, the power to fight, rescue, build, carry, or rule. Some English translations render it "strength," "power," "authority," or even military "forces." When Scripture speaks of the LORD's arm, it means God acting in history. The central picture is the "outstretched arm" that brought Israel out of Egypt.

The word draws a clear line between two kinds of confidence. One is the "arm of flesh," the human strength that God breaks. The other is the arm of the LORD, which saves. That same mighty arm gathers lambs and holds up God's people as "the everlasting arms." Isaiah then leads to a surprise. When the arm of the LORD is finally revealed, it appears as a despised and suffering Servant. John applies this passage to Jesus. In Him, God's greatest display of strength takes the form of self-giving love.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Zᵉrôwaʻ is an ordinary Hebrew word for the arm. In its plainest uses it describes Samson's arms bound with ropes (Judges 15:14; 16:12), the arrow that struck Jehoram "between his arms" (2 Kings 9:24), and the bracelet on Saul's arm (2 Samuel 1:10). In the laws for sacrifice, the same word names the foreleg or shoulder of an animal. The NKJV translates it "shoulder" in Numbers 6:19 and Deuteronomy 18:3, so English readers may not notice that this is the same Hebrew word as "arm."

In the ancient world, the arm held the sword, drew the bow, and carried burdens. People naturally used it to speak about strength and the ability to act. The word appears across the Old Testament, with its richest use in Deuteronomy, the Psalms, Isaiah, and Ezekiel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The physical arm or shoulder. This sense covers the limb of a person, or the shoulder portion of a sacrificial animal (Judges 15:14; Deuteronomy 18:3).

2. Strength or power in action. This sense describes the ability to accomplish, defend, or overpower. Jeremiah 17:5 speaks of the man who "makes flesh his strength." The word translated "strength" there is zᵉrôwaʻ, so the man relies on a human arm.

3. Military force or ruling authority. In Daniel 11 the word describes armies and political power: "the forces of the South shall not withstand him" (Daniel 11:15; also 11:6, 22, 31).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the arm depends on whose arm it is. A human arm may be honest strength, as with the capable woman who strengthens her arms for work (Proverbs 31:17). It may also be the arrogant reach of the wicked, as in Psalm 10:15: "Break the arm of the wicked and the evil man." God's arm is His personal, visible action, and it always has a purpose: to redeem, judge, create, or carry.

The word often appears beside "hand." The two together form a fixed pair: "With a strong hand, and with an outstretched arm, For His mercy endures forever" (Psalm 136:12). The verb attached to the arm also matters. An arm that is stretched out, made bare, broken, strengthened, or revealed tells the reader what kind of power is moving and toward whom.

4. Theological Significance

The God of Israel is not a distant force. His arm reaches into Egypt, into the courts of kings, and into the lives of the weak. Scripture describes His power with a body word because His power is personal and aimed at something.

That arm also belongs to the Creator. Out of the whirlwind, God answers Job: "Have you an arm like God? Or can you thunder with a voice like His?" (Job 40:9). Psalm 89 joins victory and ownership. The psalmist, speaking to God, says, "You have scattered Your enemies with Your mighty arm" (Psalm 89:10), and a few lines later, "You have a mighty arm" (Psalm 89:13). Between these two lines stand the heavens and the earth, which belong to Him (Psalm 89:11–12). Jeremiah likewise records that God made the earth by His great power and outstretched arm (Jeremiah 27:5; 32:17). The arm that saves Israel is the same arm that made the world, and so no power on earth can outmatch it.

Because God's arm holds all real strength, every human arm works only by His permission. "The upraised arm is broken" (Job 38:15). God warned the corrupt house of Eli, "I will cut off your arm and the arm of your father's house" (1 Samuel 2:31). In that warning, losing the arm meant losing the family's future strength. Ezekiel even acted this out. God told him to lay siege to Jerusalem drawn on a clay tablet (Ezekiel 4:1) and said, "your arm shall be uncovered" (Ezekiel 4:7). The prophet's bare arm pictured God's own power rolled up and ready, this time against His rebellious city.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The outstretched arm and its reversal (Exodus 6:6; Jeremiah 21:5). Before a single plague fell on Egypt, God promised, "I will redeem you with an outstretched arm and with great judgments" (Exodus 6:6). The rest of the Old Testament keeps returning to this phrase. Moses repeats it in Deuteronomy (Deuteronomy 4:34; 5:15; 26:8), and Israel sings it in Psalm 136:12. The outstretched arm became Israel's shorthand for the night God came down and set them free. That history makes Jeremiah 21:5 startling. To Jerusalem under siege by Babylon in Zedekiah's day, God says: "I Myself will fight against you with an outstretched hand and with a strong arm, even in anger and fury and great wrath." The familiar words are rearranged and turned around. The arm that once struck Pharaoh now fights for Babylon against God's own people. Being chosen never gives anyone a claim on God's power for their own sin.

An arm of flesh (2 Chronicles 32:8; Jeremiah 17:5; Psalm 44:3). When the Assyrian army surrounded Jerusalem, Hezekiah answered with a single comparison: "With him is an arm of flesh; but with us is the LORD our God, to help us and to fight our battles." The Assyrian army was real, but it was only flesh. Jeremiah 17:5 turns the same contrast into a warning: "Cursed is the man who trusts in man And makes flesh his strength, Whose heart departs from the LORD." The verse ties trust in a human arm to a heart that turns away from God. Psalm 44:3 remembers Israel's history in the same way: "Nor did their own arm save them; But it was Your right hand, Your arm, and the light of Your countenance." Trusting human strength in place of God is a form of idolatry, because it gives flesh the confidence that belongs to the LORD alone.

Broken arms and a transferred sword (Ezekiel 30:21–25). God says of Pharaoh, "I have broken the arm of Pharaoh king of Egypt; and see, it has not been bandaged for healing, nor a splint put on to bind it" (Ezekiel 30:21). Then God goes further: "I will strengthen the arms of the king of Babylon and put My sword in his hand" (Ezekiel 30:24). God weakens one empire and gives strength to another. The sword Babylon carries is called "My sword." No nation's strength is its own. God lends it, uses it, and can withdraw it.

The ruling arm that carries lambs (Isaiah 40:10–11). Isaiah announces the Lord's coming in two directions at once. First he says, "His arm shall rule for Him" (Isaiah 40:10). Then, without a break, "He will gather the lambs with His arm, And carry them in His bosom, And gently lead those who are with young" (Isaiah 40:11). The arm that rules nations is the same arm that lifts the smallest member of the flock. Isaiah presents God's strength and His tenderness as one action of one God.

The arm of the LORD revealed (Isaiah 51:9; 52:10; 53:1). Isaiah's later chapters build toward a climax. First comes a cry for God's arm to awake, as in the days when He cut Rahab apart (Isaiah 51:9). Next comes the promise that the LORD will make His holy arm bare in the sight of all the nations (Isaiah 52:10). Readers expect a warrior. Instead Isaiah asks, "To whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?" (Isaiah 53:1, as quoted in John 12:38). He then describes a Servant who grows up like a tender plant out of dry ground and who is despised, rejected, and wounded for others' sins (Isaiah 53:2–5). God's arm is revealed through His suffering Servant.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

John explains Israel's unbelief toward Jesus with Isaiah's question: "Lord, who has believed our report? And to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?" (John 12:38). This is a stated fulfillment. Jesus' signs and ministry are the arm of the LORD at work, yet many did not recognize Him (John 12:37). Many in Israel expected a political deliverance like the Exodus, and they overlooked a humble, rejected Man who was going to a cross.

Isaiah had already shown that God's arm acts when no one else can. When God saw no one to intervene, His own arm brought salvation (Isaiah 59:16; 63:5). In Christ, God Himself stepped in. The Exodus pattern then reaches its fullest meaning. God's outstretched arm brought Israel out of slavery in Egypt, and Christ's death brings His people out of slavery to sin and death. Many readers have also seen Jesus' arms stretched out on the cross as the final "outstretched arm." Scripture does not state this link directly, but it fits the shape of the story. The power that broke Pharaoh's arm conquered sin by accepting weakness.

7. Spirit & Life

Every believer leans on some arm. Hezekiah's words still apply to frightening situations: the threat is "an arm of flesh," and the LORD is with His people "to help us and to fight our battles." Money, skill, alliances, and reputation are not evil in themselves. They become a curse when the heart rests on them and turns away from the LORD (Jeremiah 17:5). Isaiah 9:20 shows where that misplaced trust ends. A people who left God turned on each other until "every man shall eat the flesh of his own arm." Isaiah pictures civil war, with Manasseh and Ephraim tearing at one another (Isaiah 9:21). Sin feeds on the very strength it relies on.

The other arm offers rest. Moses' final blessing gave Israel these words at the edge of the land: "The eternal God is your refuge, And underneath are the everlasting arms" (Deuteronomy 33:27). The arms are underneath, below the lowest place a person can fall. Hosea adds a father's memory: "I taught Ephraim to walk, Taking them by their arms; But they did not know that I healed them" (Hosea 11:3). God often holds us up long before we notice His help.

The arm also calls for surrender. Under the law, the shoulder, the "arm" of the sacrifice, went to the priest (Deuteronomy 18:3). At the end of a Nazirite vow, the boiled shoulder was placed in the Nazirite's hands, and the priest then waved it before the LORD (Numbers 6:19–20). The law does not explain the symbolism. Still, in a language where the arm means strength, the ritual of giving the arm to God fittingly pictures giving Him our strength.

Love asks to be held by that strength. "Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm; For love is as strong as death" (Song of Solomon 8:6). The bride wants to be bound to both the affection and the action of the one she loves. A similar picture appears when the high priest carried Israel's names on his shoulders before the LORD (Exodus 28:12; a different Hebrew word). Christians have long heard the Song's request as the believer's prayer to Christ. Read that way, the request is answered: those who belong to Him are carried by His strength and covered by His jealous love.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 49:24 — Jacob says Joseph's arms were made strong by the hands of the Mighty God of Jacob. Human strength is a gift God gives.

  • Exodus 15:16 — At the Red Sea, Israel sings that by the greatness of God's arm the nations would be as still as stone.

  • Psalm 77:15 — The psalmist remembers that God redeemed His people with His arm, and that memory gives him hope in a dark night.

  • Psalm 98:1 — God's right hand and holy arm have gained Him the victory, and that victory is the reason for a new song.

  • Isaiah 33:2 — The people pray that the LORD would be their arm every morning. They ask for strength one day at a time.

  • Psalm 71:18 — An old man asks not to be forsaken until he has declared God's strength to the next generation.
