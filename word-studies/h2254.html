PLEDGE, DESTROY, BRING FORTH - (châbal, Strong's H2254)

Summary

The Hebrew word châbal covers a surprising range: taking a pledge for a loan, acting corruptly or bringing ruin, and writhing in labor to bring forth a child. A closely related noun, which older numbering lists with this word, gives a shepherd's staff its name, "Bonds." Behind several of these uses lies the picture of something wound or drawn tight. A creditor binds a borrower with collateral. Ruin twists a thing out of shape. Labor grips a mother's body. A cord holds people together.

Scripture uses this word to show how human beings are bound and how God loosens what binds them. His law limits what a lender may take, so that no one's coat or millstone becomes the price of his survival. The prophets use the same word to expose the wealthy who exploited the poor through pledges and to describe sin as something the wicked labor to bring forth (Psalm 7:14). Against all of this, God promises that the oppressor's yoke "will be destroyed" (Isaiah 10:27). He also declares that even the destroyer is His creature and cannot overturn His promises (Isaiah 54:16–17). Zechariah's staff "Bonds," broken after the shepherd is rejected, points toward the true Shepherd who gathers God's scattered people into one.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châbal is a Hebrew verb found in the Law, the wisdom books, poetry, and the Prophets. Its settings shift sharply. In Exodus and Deuteronomy it belongs to the courtroom and the marketplace, in laws about loans and collateral. In Job and Amos it appears in accusations against people who used those same practices to crush the poor. In Nehemiah, Isaiah, and Micah it describes corruption and destruction. In the Psalms and the Song of Solomon it can describe the labor of giving birth.

Many Hebrew dictionaries treat these meanings as separate words that happen to share the same spelling. Strong's groups them under one entry. For that reason, the study below lets each context define its own sense rather than forcing one root idea into every verse.

In Israel, a lender often held something belonging to the borrower until the debt was repaid. Poor people had little to offer beyond a cloak, a work animal, or household tools. That background explains why the pledge laws matter so much.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To take a pledge: to hold something belonging to a borrower as security for a debt, as when a neighbor's garment is taken (Exodus 22:26).

2. To act corruptly, spoil, or destroy: to ruin something, whether a relationship with God (Nehemiah 1:7, "very corruptly"), a vineyard (Song of Solomon 2:15), or a yoke (Isaiah 10:27).

3. To writhe in labor or bring forth: to give birth, whether literally (Song of Solomon 8:5) or figuratively, of the wicked producing evil (Psalm 7:14).

Related noun, Bonds: in Zechariah 11:7 and 14 a staff is named "Bonds," representing the tie that binds a people together. Older numbering lists this under châbal, but current STEPBible data indexes it as a related noun from the same family (H2256C), so it is a family connection rather than a fourth sense of the verb.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Taking a pledge is one major use of the word. In the Law it appears inside commands that restrain the lender. Elsewhere it becomes evidence in an indictment, as when Eliphaz says to Job, "you have taken pledges from your brother for no reason" (Job 22:6). The same practice could be lawful in one setting and wicked in another. The difference lay in what was taken, from whom, and for what reason.

When the word means "destroy," its subject decides whether the ruin is evil or good. People act corruptly against God (Nehemiah 1:7). Foxes spoil vines (Song of Solomon 2:15). Defiled land will "destroy, Yes, with utter destruction" (Micah 2:10). God, however, destroys the oppressor's yoke (Isaiah 10:27). The birth sense can be tender, as with the mother who "brought you forth" under the apple tree (Song of Solomon 8:5). It can also be dark, as with the wicked who bring forth iniquity.

4. Theological Significance

The passages that use châbal show a God who pays attention to what binds people. He notices the poor man's cloak at sundown. He knows which tool keeps a family fed. He sees the wicked plotting harm long before the plan is carried out.

Taken together, these uses also show that ruin does not have the final word. Corruption, oppression, and destruction are real, and the word names them honestly. Yet God's word to Zion in Isaiah 54 places the destroyer under His authority. Even where this word describes real ruin, that chapter assures the people of God that they are not left at the mercy of the destroyer.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 22:26; Deuteronomy 24:6, 17; Amos 2:8: the pledge and the covering. The Law says, "If you ever take your neighbor's garment as a pledge, you shall return it to him before the sun goes down" (Exodus 22:26). The next verse explains why. The garment is the man's only covering, and God says He will hear the man's cry, because He is gracious. God did not abolish lending. He limited it, so that a creditor's rights stopped where a debtor's survival began. Deuteronomy draws the same line: "No man shall take the lower or the upper millstone in pledge, for he takes one's living in pledge" (Deuteronomy 24:6). A family ground its grain every day, so taking the millstone meant taking the family's daily bread. The widow's garment could not be taken at all (Deuteronomy 24:17).

Amos shows what happened when Israel ignored these laws: "They lie down by every altar on clothes taken in pledge" (Amos 2:8). The cloaks that should have gone home at sunset became bedding for worshipers at pagan altars. The poor were stripped of their covering, and the powerful turned it into a cushion for their idolatry.

Psalm 7:14: the birth of iniquity. "Behold, the wicked brings forth iniquity; Yes, he conceives trouble and brings forth falsehood." David describes evil as a pregnancy: it is conceived, carried, and finally delivered. Sin, in this picture, is not a sudden slip. It grows inside a person who keeps feeding it until it comes out as lies and harm. The psalm then shows the outcome. The wicked man digs a pit and falls into it himself, and his trouble returns on his own head (Psalm 7:15–16). What he labored to bring forth turns against him. James later uses the same picture when he describes desire conceiving and giving birth to sin, and sin bringing forth death (James 1:15).

Song of Solomon 2:15: the little foxes. "Catch us the foxes, The little foxes that spoil the vines, For our vines have tender grapes." The danger in this verse is small. Little foxes slip through gaps in a wall and gnaw at young fruit before it can ripen. In the Song, the vineyard belongs to the love between bride and groom. Elsewhere Scripture often pictures God's people as His vineyard. Love that is new and still growing is most easily ruined by small things left unchecked. The same word appears on a national scale in Nehemiah's confession that Israel had acted "very corruptly" (Nehemiah 1:7).

Isaiah 10:27 and 54:16–17: the destroyer under God's hand. Isaiah promises that Assyria's yoke "will be destroyed because of the anointing oil." The vocabulary of ruin is turned against the instrument of slavery, so that the yoke itself is broken. The phrase translated "anointing oil" can also be read as "fatness," but either reading credits the deliverance to God rather than to Israel's strength. In Isaiah 54:16, God says that He created the smith who forges weapons and that He created the spoiler to destroy. The next verse promises that no weapon formed against His people will prosper. These two verses belong together. The promise of verse 17 stands because of the claim in verse 16. The one who made the destroyer sets its limits, and those limits protect His heritage.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Zechariah 11 provides the clearest movement toward Christ. The prophet shepherds "the flock for slaughter" with two staffs, "Beauty" and "Bonds" (Zechariah 11:7). He breaks Beauty first. He is then paid off with thirty pieces of silver. After that he cuts Bonds in two to break the brotherhood between Judah and Israel (Zechariah 11:14). Matthew connects this thirty-piece payment to the price paid for Jesus (Matthew 27:9–10). The order of events in Zechariah carries the message: after the shepherd is valued at thirty pieces of silver, the sum the Law set for a slave gored by an ox (Exodus 21:32), and dismissed, the tie that holds the people together snaps.

The rest of Scripture moves toward the repair of that break. Ezekiel saw two sticks, Judah and Joseph, joined into one in God's hand (Ezekiel 37:15–22). Jesus speaks of one flock and one shepherd (John 10:16). John explains that Jesus would die to gather together in one the scattered children of God (John 11:52). The rejected Shepherd becomes the One who binds His people together again.

Isaiah's two promises also find their fullest expression in Jesus. The Anointed One announced liberty to captives (Luke 4:18), which connects with Isaiah 10:27 as a strong echo rather than a direct quotation. At the cross, the rulers did what God's hand had determined beforehand (Acts 4:27–28). The cross shows the truth of Isaiah 54:16 at its deepest level: the forces that destroyed Jesus served God's purpose, and their weapon failed to prevail.

7. Spirit & Life

The pledge laws reveal God's heart in very practical terms. He cared whether a poor man slept warm. He told lenders to return a pledged cloak before the sun went down. A God who legislates that kind of mercy cares about His people's daily bread and stands against demands that strip vulnerable people of their livelihood. People who follow Him are to treat the vulnerable the same way, whether that means a debt, a wage, or an obligation that could quietly crush another person.

Psalm 7:14 calls for honesty about where sin starts. What a person keeps conceiving in private tends to come to birth in public. The time to deal with sin is when it is still a thought being nursed. The little foxes teach the same lesson about relationships. A marriage, a friendship, or a walk with God is rarely ruined by one great blow. More often it is worn down by small bitterness, neglected prayer, and excuses nobody bothered to catch. Tender grapes need guarding most.

Isaiah 54 gives settled ground under all of this. Believers face real spoilers, but none of them operates outside God's authority. That truth frees God's people to stand firm without denying the danger.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Job 17:1 — "My spirit is broken, My days are extinguished." Job applies the language of ruin to his own inner life, showing that a person, and not only property, can be undone.

Ezekiel 18:16 — The righteous man is described as one who has not oppressed anyone nor "withheld a pledge." In this way Ezekiel makes obedience to the pledge laws a mark of true righteousness.

Proverbs 20:16; 27:13 — These proverbs advise holding the garment of someone who guarantees a stranger's debt as security. They show the ordinary, lawful use of a pledge in prudent dealings.

Ecclesiastes 5:6 — The Preacher warns that rash words before God, excused afterward as an error, can provoke God to "destroy the work of your hands." A careless vow is not a small matter.

Micah 2:10 — "Because it is defiled, it shall destroy, Yes, with utter destruction." A land defiled by its people's sin becomes the source of their own ruin.
