ROSE, CROCUS - (chăbatstseleth, Strong's H2261)

Summary

The Hebrew word chăbatstseleth names a wildflower that the NKJV, following long English tradition, translates "rose." Scripture uses it only twice. Most students of the land of Israel think it was a spring meadow flower that grows from a bulb, such as a crocus, narcissus, or asphodel. It was not the cultivated garden rose of later centuries. It was the kind of flower that covers an open field after the rains, lovely and common at the same time.

The two uses sit at opposite ends of the landscape. In Song of Solomon 2:1 the bride calls herself "the rose of Sharon," a wildflower of a fertile plain. Her beloved answers that she stands out "like a lily among thorns." In Isaiah 35:1 the same flower blooms in the desert, where it has no natural right to grow, because the people "shall see the glory of the LORD." One verse shows love giving honor to what seems ordinary. The other shows God's presence bringing sudden life to land that looked dead. Christians have long applied "Rose of Sharon" to Jesus, and both pictures lead to Him: the Servant who grew up "as a root out of dry ground" is the One who makes the wilderness bloom.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chăbatstseleth is a Hebrew noun, and it appears only in poetry. Its exact botanical identity is uncertain. Ancient Greek translators rendered it "flower" in the Song and "lily" in Isaiah, which suggests that even they were working with a general picture of a spring bloom. The word may be related to a Hebrew term for "bulb," and that would fit a crocus-type plant. This connection is plausible but not certain, and it helps mainly by reminding us what kind of flower is in view.

Readers should set aside modern associations, such as long-stemmed red roses, romantic bouquets, and the thorns of a rosebush. Neither verse depends on those images.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means a meadow flower, most likely a bulb-grown spring bloom. It has one sense. The NKJV's "rose" is a traditional rendering of that sense and does not point to a different flower or a second meaning.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The two occurrences differ mainly in setting. In Song of Solomon 2:1 the flower grows where it naturally belongs, on the plain of Sharon. That coastal region was known for its fertile pasture and abundant wildflowers, so a flower of Sharon was lovely but one among thousands. In Isaiah 35:1 the flower appears in "the desert," where nothing should bloom, and the word becomes part of a simile: the desert will "blossom as the rose." The flower is the same in both places, but its meaning changes with the ground it grows in. In fertile ground it pictures humble beauty, and in barren ground it pictures a miracle.

4. Theological Significance

Both passages show God acting toward what has nothing to recommend it. In the Song, love sees one ordinary flower and treasures it. In Isaiah, God's glory comes to a wasteland and makes it rejoice. In both cases the value and the life come from outside the flower. They are given to it.

Isaiah's flower also stands apart from his usual flower imagery. Elsewhere in his book flowers are pictures of things that do not last. Ephraim's pride is "a fading flower" (Isaiah 28:1), and "all flesh is grass," whose loveliness "is like the flower of the field," fading "because the breath of the LORD blows upon it" (Isaiah 40:6–7). Human glory blooms and then withers under God's breath. The desert flower of Isaiah 35 does the opposite. It blooms because God comes near. That contrast matches Isaiah 40:8: "the word of our God stands forever." What God Himself plants does not fade the way human pride does.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:1–2. The bride says, "I am the rose of Sharon, And the lily of the valleys." Many readers have heard this as a claim to great beauty. In the setting, though, she is comparing herself to one wildflower in a field full of them, and the line reads best as modest self-description. Her beloved's reply changes the picture: "Like a lily among thorns, So is my love among the daughters" (2:2). He takes her own image of a field flower and sets it among thorns. In his eyes, every other woman is a bramble beside her. She describes herself as common, and he answers that she is incomparable.

This exchange shows how love works. The bride does not earn his admiration by being rare. His love is what sets her apart. Paul describes the same pattern when he writes that "God has chosen the weak things of the world" and "the things which are despised" (1 Corinthians 1:27–28).

Isaiah 35:1–2 against Isaiah 34. Isaiah 35 has to be read right after chapter 34. There the prophet describes "the day of the LORD's vengeance" on Edom. Its streams turn to pitch, its smoke rises forever, and "thorns shall come up in its palaces, Nettles and brambles in its fortresses" (34:8–13). Jackals and owls take over the land. Then, with no break in the poetry, the scene reverses: "The wilderness and the wasteland shall be glad for them, And the desert shall rejoice and blossom as the rose" (35:1).

Verse 2 gives the reason for the change: "They shall see the glory of the LORD, The excellency of our God." The flowering is not a lucky change in the weather. It happens because God shows Himself. The desert even receives "the excellence of Carmel and Sharon," so the land that had nothing is given the beauty of Israel's most fertile regions, including the very plain where the bride's flower grew. Thorns marked judgment in chapter 34, and the blossoming flower marks salvation in chapter 35. The same chapter goes on to promise opened eyes, leaping lame men, and "waters" that "burst forth in the wilderness" (35:5–6).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Thorns first appear in Scripture as part of the curse: "Both thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you" (Genesis 3:18). Isaiah 34 brings the thorns back as a sign of judgment. The prophets look ahead to the reversal, when God "will make her wilderness like Eden, And her desert like the garden of the LORD" (Isaiah 51:3) and will "plant in the wilderness the cedar and the acacia tree" (Isaiah 41:19). The blossoming desert flower belongs to this story of a curse being undone.

Jesus stands at the center of that story. When John the Baptist asked whether He was the Coming One, Jesus answered by describing His own works: the blind see, the lame walk, the deaf hear (Matthew 11:4–5). Those are the signs Isaiah 35 attached to the blooming desert. The kingdom Isaiah saw was arriving in Jesus' ministry.

The title "Rose of Sharon" for Christ comes from Christian devotion and hymns, not from the Song itself, where the bride is the speaker. The application still fits Scripture's picture of Him. Isaiah describes the Servant growing up "as a tender plant, And as a root out of dry ground," with "no form or comeliness" (Isaiah 53:2). Like a field flower, Jesus came without outward majesty, and He came up out of the dry ground of a fallen world. The One who took a lowly form is the One whose glory makes deserts bloom. His people share His lowliness and receive their beauty from His love, much as the bride's ordinary flower became "a lily among thorns" in her beloved's eyes.

7. Spirit & Life

A bulb flower spends the dry season hidden underground. To anyone walking across the desert, nothing appears to be alive. But the life has been kept safe below the surface, waiting for rain, and when the rain comes the bloom is sudden. This is a natural picture and not something the text explicitly teaches. Still, it fits how God works with His people. Paul tells believers, "your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:3). Hosea says the LORD "will come to us like the rain" (Hosea 6:3), and Isaiah links the pouring of water on thirsty ground with the pouring out of God's Spirit (Isaiah 44:3). A dry season does not prove that life is gone. What God has planted can wait under hard ground until He comes.

Isaiah 35 does not leave this as a private comfort. Right after the flowering desert, the prophet gives a command: "Strengthen the weak hands, And make firm the feeble knees. Say to those who are fearful-hearted, 'Be strong, do not fear!'" (35:3–4). The hope of the blooming desert is meant to be spoken to people who are fainting. Its message is "He will come and save you." Hope like this is something believers carry to one another.

The Song adds something more personal. Many believers see themselves the way the bride did, as one ordinary flower in a crowded field. The Beloved does not argue with her. He simply tells her what she is to him. Believers find their worth in the same place she did, in being chosen and loved, and not in standing out by their own effort.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Chăbatstseleth appears only in Song of Solomon 2:1 and Isaiah 35:1, and both verses are developed in Section 5. The related desert-renewal passages, Isaiah 41:18–19 and 51:3, use different Hebrew words. They support the same theme but are not further occurrences of this word.
