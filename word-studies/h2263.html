EMBRACE, FOLD - (châbaq, Strong's H2263)

Summary

The Hebrew verb châbaq means "to embrace": to wrap one's arms around someone or something and hold on. Most often it describes a real embrace. Laban greets his nephew Jacob, Esau receives the brother who cheated him, and an old, nearly blind Jacob holds the grandsons he never expected to see. Elisha uses the word for a promise to a childless woman: "you shall embrace a son." In the Song of Solomon, the bride rests in her beloved's arms.

The same picture also describes what a person chooses to hold onto. Proverbs urges the young to embrace Wisdom and warns against the arms of a seductress. Ecclesiastes shows a fool who "folds his hands," hugging only himself. Job and Lamentations use the word for people so stripped of shelter and comfort that they cling to rocks and ash heaps. Together these passages teach that a life can be read by what it embraces. They also show a God who restores broken relationships, places His promises into empty arms, and gives His people a place of secure rest.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châbaq is a Hebrew verb found only in the Old Testament. Its uses gather in two settings. The first is family stories in Genesis and 2 Kings, where the word describes greetings, reunions, and blessings. The second is the poetry of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, the Song of Solomon, Job, and Lamentations, where the physical gesture becomes a picture of loyalty, laziness, love, or desperation. The New Testament carries the image forward with different Greek words, most memorably when the father in Luke 15 "fell on his neck" to welcome his son home.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, châbaq means to clasp or hold something in one's arms. Context determines how the word applies:

1. To embrace another person in greeting, reunion, or affection, as Esau embraced Jacob (Genesis 33:4).

2. To hold something close by choice and loyalty, as when a son is told to embrace Wisdom (Proverbs 4:8).

3. To cling to something for lack of anything better, as the homeless "huddle around the rock for want of shelter" (Job 24:8).

4. To fold the arms or hands against oneself, as the idle fool does (Ecclesiastes 4:5).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What changes most across these uses is the object of the embrace. When the arms close around another person, the word carries warmth, welcome, or blessing. When the object is Wisdom or a seductress, the embrace becomes a matter of loyalty that has consequences.

Ecclesiastes 4:5 turns the word inward: "The fool folds his hands And consumes his own flesh." Here the same arms that could greet a brother or take hold of Wisdom close around the fool himself. His self-embrace produces nothing, and he ends up eating away at his own life. The irony is intentional. Arms that could reach out to another person or to useful work become a picture of a person wasting himself.

Ecclesiastes 3:5 sets embracing among life's appointed seasons: "A time to embrace, And a time to refrain from embracing."

4. Theological Significance

The passages that use châbaq show several truths about God. He restores relationships that sin has broken. In Genesis, the brother Jacob wronged becomes the brother who holds him. God also keeps His word in ways people can physically hold. A promised son and a pair of grandsons end up literally in someone's arms. Those who once had good things and then turned away from God can lose every comfort, until they are left clinging to the dust. Proverbs adds that what people hold close is a moral choice, and that choice decides where their lives end up. The God who embraces His people also calls them to embrace what is good.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 33:4 — the embrace Jacob did not deserve. Twenty years earlier Jacob had stolen his brother's blessing. Now Esau was coming with four hundred men. Jacob split his household into two camps so that one might escape an attack (Genesis 32:7–8), and he prayed, "Deliver me, I pray, from the hand of my brother... for I fear him" (32:11). In the same prayer he confessed, "I am not worthy of the least of all the mercies" (32:10). Then the meeting came: "But Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept." Jacob expected to face judgment. The man he had wronged ran toward him, and the reunion ended in tears.

The details matter. Esau runs, so the offended brother closes the distance himself. To embrace someone, a person has to open his arms and put aside anything he would use to fight. Esau's embrace showed that the hostility was over, and in that moment the debt was not raised. Genesis keeps returning to this image. Joseph falls on Benjamin's neck (45:14) and later on his father's (46:29). The Bible's great reconciliation scenes are told with arms around necks.

Genesis 48:10–11 and 2 Kings 4:16 — arms filled by God's faithfulness. Old Jacob, "dim with age, so that he could not see," held Joseph's sons and said, "I had not thought to see your face; but in fact, God has also shown me your offspring!" For years he had believed Joseph was dead. God gave him back his son and added two grandsons, and Jacob held them before blessing them. Elisha's promise to the Shunammite woman uses the same verb: "About this time next year you shall embrace a son." Her reply, "do not lie to your maidservant," shows how much it hurt to hope again. God did not leave the promise as words. He made it a child she could hold (4:17).

Proverbs 4:8 and 5:20 — two embraces, two outcomes. The father tells his son to treat Wisdom like a cherished bride: "She will bring you honor, when you embrace her." A chapter later he asks, "For why should you, my son, be enraptured by an immoral woman, And be embraced in the arms of a seductress?" Proverbs uses the same image for both, because each one asks for the heart's loyalty. One embrace leads to honor. The other draws a young man toward death (5:5). A person cannot give himself to both.

Lamentations 4:5 and Job 24:8 — clinging to nothing. After Jerusalem fell, the poet writes: "Those who were brought up in scarlet Embrace ash heaps." Children who were raised in royal comfort now hold refuse. The verb is striking. They do not simply lie on the ash heaps. They embrace them, as though rubbish were all that remained to love. Job describes the oppressed poor in the same way: they "huddle around the rock for want of shelter." People are made to hold onto something. When judgment or cruelty takes away every comfort, that need remains, and the arms close around cold stone and ashes.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Genesis reunions point ahead to Jesus' parable of the prodigal son. The returning son "was still a great way off" when "his father saw him and had compassion, and ran and fell on his neck and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). The father runs and falls on his son's neck, just as Esau did. Jesus told the parable to show how the Father receives sinners. The welcome Jacob never earned becomes the welcome God offers every repentant sinner through His Son.

Proverbs' call to embrace Wisdom finds its fullest answer in Christ, "the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24). Taking hold of Wisdom finally means taking hold of Him.

The Song of Solomon twice gives the bride's words, "His left hand is under my head, And his right hand embraces me" (2:6; compare 8:3). His hand supports her and holds her at rest. Paul says that marriage points to "Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). On that basis, many have read the Song's embrace as a picture of the secure, cherished place Christ gives His people. That reading is a fruitful connection rather than an explicit statement of the Song, and it agrees with what the rest of Scripture says about Christ's love for His church.

7. Spirit & Life

Every use of this word asks what our arms are holding. Esau shows that grace moves first: it runs, puts away its weapons, and holds the person who did wrong. Those who have been received that way by the Father are called to welcome others the same way.

The Shunammite woman shows how hard it can be to believe that God will place something real in empty arms. Old Jacob shows that God can give back more than was lost. The fool of Ecclesiastes is a sober warning that a life folded in on itself slowly consumes itself.

The exiles hugging ash heaps show where life ends when people let go of God. Even then, the need to hold onto something remains. That need is a reason to turn back to the One who runs to meet us.

Ecclesiastes adds that there is "a time to embrace, And a time to refrain from embracing." Love includes wisdom about timing. There are seasons for closeness and seasons for restraint, and both are under God's hand.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 29:13. Laban "ran to meet him, and embraced him and kissed him." This warm welcome came from the uncle who later deceived Jacob, which shows that an embrace is only as true as the heart behind it.

Song of Solomon 8:3. Near the end of the Song, the bride repeats her words about her beloved's supporting embrace. The repetition shows that the security she found earlier has lasted.

The remaining occurrences are discussed above.
