COMPANION, COMPANIONS - (châbêr, Strong's H2270)

Summary

The Hebrew word châbêr means a companion, associate, or fellow: someone joined to others by a shared cause, a shared life, or a shared loyalty. It can describe tribes standing together, a friend who lifts you when you fall, or the friends of a bridegroom. The word itself carries no moral value. Scripture speaks of the companions of thieves as readily as the companions of those who fear God. Across its uses, however, one pattern stands out: the people you are joined to reveal what you love and where you are headed.

The word reaches its height in Psalm 45:7, where God anoints the righteous King "with the oil of gladness more than Your companions." Hebrews 1:9 applies that verse to Jesus Christ. The Son of God truly has companions. He shares our humanity and calls us brethren, and yet He stands above all of them as the uniquely anointed King. Ezekiel 37 adds a promise that God Himself will rejoin divided companions into one people under one king. Read together, these passages show that God made people for companionship, that sin corrupts and breaks it, and that God restores it through Christ.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châbêr is a Hebrew noun. It belongs to a family of words connected with joining or associating together, but its meaning is best learned from how Scripture actually uses it. The word occurs 12 times in 11 verses of the Hebrew Bible. It occurs in a historical narrative (Judges), in poetry and wisdom (Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Solomon), and in the prophets (Isaiah, Ezekiel). That spread is wide for so few occurrences, and in each setting the word names people who belong together for some reason. The reason may be war, friendship, love, crime, or God's promise.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Châbêr refers to a person joined with others as a companion or associate. Context supplies the nature of the bond. Two senses can be distinguished:

1. A close companion who shares life and help with another person, such as the friend who lifts up his fallen companion (Ecclesiastes 4:10) or the bridegroom's friends who keep their flocks near his (Song of Solomon 1:7).

2. An associate or fellow member of a group bound by a common cause or allegiance, such as the tribes "united together as one man" (Judges 20:11), the companions of thieves (Isaiah 1:23), or the tribes grouped with Judah and Joseph (Ezekiel 37:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most often the word appears in the plural, "companions," and the people it describes are defined by whoever stands at the center of the group. In Psalm 45 they are the King's companions. In Ezekiel 37 they are Judah's companions and Joseph's companions. In Isaiah 1:23 they are companions "of thieves." To know what a châbêr is in a given verse, ask who or what he is joined to.

Judges 20:11 shows this clearly. There the word stands in the description of how Israel assembled, and the NKJV renders the idea "united together." The verse describes a unity of purpose. It also carries a sobering irony, because the rest of the tribes joined together to make war on one of their own, the tribe of Benjamin. Being joined together is good or bad depending on what people join together to do.

4. Theological Significance

God made people for companionship. Ecclesiastes 4 surveys the emptiness of a man who toils alone, with "neither son nor brother" (Ecclesiastes 4:8). Then it sets a plain fact beside that emptiness: "For if they fall, one will lift up his companion. But woe to him who is alone when he falls, For he has no one to help him up" (Ecclesiastes 4:10). The verse plainly reckons with falling ("if they fall") and treats it as a real possibility for anyone, so the value of a companion is the hand that lifts him up. Isolation is dangerous because human beings are frail. Needing others is part of how God made us, so no one should treat that need as a weakness to be ashamed of.

Because people are made to join themselves to others, their companions reveal their allegiance. The psalmist chooses his fellowship by its devotion to God: "I am a companion of all who fear You, And of those who keep Your precepts" (Psalm 119:63). Family, rank, or convenience do not define his circle. Shared reverence for God does. Isaiah 1:23 describes the opposite. Jerusalem's princes are "rebellious, And companions of thieves," and their alliance shows itself in what they neglect: "They do not defend the fatherless, Nor does the cause of the widow come before them." Their companionship with thieves is more than a social detail. It exposes the theft in their own hearts. Proverbs 28:24 and Isaiah 44:11 carry the pattern further. Shared sin creates a shared identity, and in Isaiah's words a shared shame.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 45:7 — The King anointed above His companions. Psalm 45 celebrates a royal wedding. The king is praised for his justice: "You love righteousness and hate wickedness; Therefore God, Your God, has anointed You With the oil of gladness more than Your companions." His companions are presumably those who share his royal world: fellow kings, nobles, and the attendants of his celebration. The psalm sets him among them and also above them, and it gives the reason. His love of righteousness is the ground of his greater anointing. Hebrews 1:8–9 quotes and applies these verses as words spoken "to the Son," addressing Him as "O God" whose throne is "forever and ever." The NKJV wording differs slightly ("You have loved righteousness and hated lawlessness" in Hebrews 1:9), but the application to Christ is explicit. The psalm therefore holds two truths together. The King truly has companions, and He is far greater than they are.

Ezekiel 37:16–22 — Two sticks made one. Ezekiel's vision of the dry bones shows God raising a dead nation. God then gives the prophet a second sign. Ezekiel writes on one stick, "For Judah and for the children of Israel, his companions," and on another, "For Joseph, the stick of Ephraim, and for all the house of Israel, his companions" (Ezekiel 37:16). Each stick names a leading tribe and the tribes joined to it. These stood for the two kingdoms that had split apart after Solomon and at times fought each other. God declares that He will make them one stick "in My hand" (Ezekiel 37:19) and promises, "one king shall be king over them all; they shall no longer be two nations" (Ezekiel 37:22). The word companions reminds the reader that these tribes once belonged together. Ezekiel shows that their reunion will come from God's hand; the passage credits it to God, not to any treaty or human effort. In this chapter God's restoration includes raising His people from death and also healing the brotherhood that sin had broken.

Song of Solomon 1:7; 8:13 — The beloved, not his companions. The bride asks her beloved where he feeds his flock at noon, "For why should I be as one who veils herself By the flocks of your companions?" (Song of Solomon 1:7). His companions are near, and they shepherd flocks close to his own, but she will not settle for their company. She wants him. At the close of the Song the beloved speaks to the bride, the one who dwells in the gardens: "The companions listen for your voice—Let me hear it!" (Song of Solomon 8:13). Now the companions listen for her voice, and he asks to hear it for himself. The longing runs both ways: she would not settle for his companions instead of him, and he is not content merely that others hear her. The Song is love poetry, and its first meaning is human love. Many readers have also heard in it the soul's longing for the Lord. Read that way, these verses carry a clear lesson: good companions, even godly ones, cannot stand in for the Bridegroom Himself, and the Lord in turn desires to hear the voice of His people personally.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hebrews develops Psalm 45:7 carefully. The Greek word Hebrews uses for the King's "companions" in Hebrews 1:9 appears again two chapters later, where believers are called partakers of a heavenly calling (Hebrews 3:1) and those who "have become partakers of Christ" if they hold their confidence firm to the end (Hebrews 3:14). By using the same word, Hebrews invites the reader to see the King's companions as including His people. Hebrews 2:11 explains how this can be: "both He who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all of one, for which reason He is not ashamed to call them brethren." The eternal Son took on flesh, suffered, and became "the captain of their salvation" (Hebrews 2:10), and so He has companions in the fullest sense. He remains above them, the One whose throne is forever. Yet He has truly joined Himself to them.

Ezekiel's promise moves in the same direction. The divided tribes become one people under "one king," and Ezekiel later names that king as God's servant David (Ezekiel 37:24). The New Testament presents Jesus as the Son of David who gathers a scattered and divided people into one flock under one Shepherd (John 10:16). The broken companionship of Isaiah's princes and Israel's warring kingdoms finds its answer in a King who joins Himself to His people and joins them to one another.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 119:63 asks every believer a searching question: Whose companion am I? The psalmist does not wait for good company to find him. He declares himself joined to "all who fear You," whatever their background. Isaiah's princes show the warning side of the same truth. Isaiah does not describe how they reached that point, but people seldom become companions of thieves in a single step. Wrong friendships can quietly reorder a conscience until it no longer hears the widow's cry. The friendships we choose reveal what we love, and over time they also strengthen it.

Ecclesiastes 4:10 removes any pride in self-sufficiency. Every believer will fall at some point, and God's provision for that moment is often a companion's hand. Being that companion is also a calling. The person who lifts a fallen brother is doing exactly what the word describes.

The Song of Solomon guards against a quieter danger. A believer can be surrounded by Christ's companions, active in church and close to godly people, and still be content to stay near "the flocks of your companions" instead of seeking the Lord Himself. The bride's question still asks every reader whether they are content with nearness to Christ's people or are seeking Christ Himself.

Psalm 45 gives the deepest encouragement of all. The King anointed "with the oil of gladness" is not ashamed of His companions. Scripture connects anointing with God's Spirit: Jesus was anointed "with the Holy Spirit and with power" (Acts 10:38), and believers too are anointed by God (2 Corinthians 1:21; 1 John 2:20). This connection is a canonical resonance rather than a direct statement in Psalm 45, but it is a rich one. The joy that rests on Christ above all others is the joy He shares with those He calls brethren.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Judges 20:11 — "All the men of Israel were gathered against the city, united together as one man." Israel's unity here served a grim civil war, which shows that being joined together is only as good as its purpose.

Proverbs 28:24 — A man who robs his parents and says "It is no transgression" is "companion to a destroyer." His excuse places him in the company of those who ruin homes.

Isaiah 44:11 — The idol-maker's companions "would be ashamed… They shall be ashamed together." People bound together in idolatry share its disgrace.
