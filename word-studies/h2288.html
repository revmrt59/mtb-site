CLEFTS - (chăgâv, Strong's H2288)

Summary

The Hebrew word chăgâv means a cleft or crevice in a rock: a narrow, hidden opening high in a cliff where a bird can nest or a people can shelter out of reach. Scripture uses it only three times, and each time the NKJV renders it "the clefts of the rock." Because the word is so rare, each use carries weight, and the three uses form a striking contrast.

Twice, in Jeremiah 49:16 and Obadiah 3, the clefts are the mountain strongholds of Edom. The prophets show how Edom's natural security grew into the "pride of your heart" that deceived the nation into thinking no one could bring it down, and the LORD answers, "I will bring you down from there." In Song of Solomon 2:14, the same clefts shelter a timid dove, and the beloved calls gently, "Let me see your face, let me hear your voice." The rocky hiding place is the same in all three verses. What differs is the heart of the one hiding and the response that heart receives. Pride that hides to shut God out meets His judgment. Timidity that hides out of fear meets a voice of love calling it out into the open.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chăgâv is a Hebrew noun for a fissure or crevice in rock. It appears only in poetry, in the love poetry of Song of Solomon and in prophetic oracles against Edom, and always in the plural. The landscape behind the word is the rugged terrain of the Near East, where cliffs and gorges are cut with cracks and ledges. Rock doves nest in places like these, and people could take refuge in them.

Edom lived in exactly this kind of country. Its territory around Mount Seir, south and east of the Dead Sea, was a land of high red sandstone cliffs and narrow canyons. The region later known for the rock city of Petra is often associated with it. For an ancient nation, such ground was a natural fortress that was hard to reach and harder to conquer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chăgâv means a cleft, crevice, or crack in a rock face, a recessed place that offers concealment and protection. The word describes geography. It carries no moral meaning of its own; context determines whether the hiding place is good or bad.

1. A natural rock crevice that serves as a dwelling or stronghold, as with Edom, "you who dwell in the clefts of the rock" (Obadiah 3).

2. A hidden refuge in the poetic image of a bird sheltering in the cliffs, as with the dove "in the clefts of the rock" (Song of Solomon 2:14).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The difference between the two uses lies in who is hiding and why. In the prophets, the clefts belong to a proud nation that "hold[s] the height of the hill" (Jeremiah 49:16). There the word sits beside images of height and inaccessibility: a nest "as high as the eagle," a habitation that "is high." The cleft is part of a boast.

In the Song, the cleft belongs to a dove, one of the gentlest creatures in Scripture. The parallel phrase "the secret places of the cliff" stresses concealment, not dominance. The dove is hidden, and the speaker longs for her to come out where she can be seen and heard. The prophetic uses stress being unreachable. The poetic use stresses being hidden but sought.

4. Theological Significance

All three passages ask where a person finds security, and they show that God responds to the heart behind the hiding place, not to the rock itself.

Edom's clefts reveal how easily a natural advantage becomes a spiritual danger. Nothing was wrong with the mountains, which were God's own handiwork. The trouble was what Edom concluded from them: that height and stone placed the nation beyond the reach of anyone, including God. Both prophets name this as deception. Pride lies to the heart that holds it and makes security feel like sovereignty. Against that lie stands God's plain claim to rule: "I will bring you down from there."

The Song shows the other side of God's ways. Hiddenness born of weakness or shyness does not provoke His anger. The voice that speaks to the hidden dove commands no assault and makes no rebuke. It speaks with desire and delight: "your voice is sweet, and your face is lovely." Scripture as a whole shows the same pattern. God resists the proud and draws near to the lowly, and the same God who brings down one hidden people calls tenderly to another.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Jeremiah 49:16 and Obadiah 3: the stronghold that deceived. These two prophets use nearly identical wording, which shows that this word against Edom was well known and weighty. Obadiah opens the charge with the heart: "The pride of your heart has deceived you, you who dwell in the clefts of the rock, whose habitation is high." He then lets Edom speak: "You who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?'" Edom's boast is a question it assumes no one can answer, because the cliffs seem to answer it already.

Jeremiah adds "your fierceness," Edom's reputation for terrifying others, to the list of things that deceived it. He also extends the image upward: "Though you make your nest as high as the eagle, I will bring you down from there." The eagle's nest is the highest refuge a person can imagine, and God says it still lies within His reach. Obadiah carries the same thought forward in the next verse, saying that even if Edom set its nest among the stars, the LORD would bring it down. The clefts did not cause Edom's pride, but they fed it. A people who could not be easily reached began to believe they could not be reached at all.

Song of Solomon 2:14: the dove called out of hiding. This verse belongs to a passage of invitation. The beloved has just announced that winter is past and the rain is gone. Flowers have appeared, "the voice of the turtledove is heard in our land," and the fig tree is ripening, and he calls, "Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away!" (2:10–13). Then he addresses her as a dove still tucked into the clefts and "the secret places of the cliff."

The details matter. He knows exactly where she is, but he does not force his way into her hiding place. He asks for two things: to see her face and to hear her voice. Then he gives his reasons, which are not demands but delight: her voice is sweet and her face is lovely. Fear and shyness are met with welcome. The season outside has changed, and he wants her to come out into it with him.

The same image with opposite meanings. Placed side by side, these passages show the same picture of someone hidden high in the rock carrying opposite spiritual realities. Edom hid in order to be untouchable, and its stronghold became the place of its humbling. The dove hid because she was vulnerable, and her hiding place became the place where love found her and called her by name. God's answer to Edom is "I will bring you down." His answer to the dove is "Let me hear your voice."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Scripture repeatedly shows people trying to hide from God, from Adam and Eve among the trees of Eden to the final day in Revelation 6:15–16. On that day "the kings of the earth, the great men, the rich men," and all the rest "hid themselves in the caves and in the rocks of the mountains" and cried out, "Fall on us and hide us from the face of Him who sits on the throne and from the wrath of the Lamb!" Edom's clefts preview that end. Rock cannot shelter anyone from the face of God.

Scripture also offers a true Rock. David sings, "The LORD is my rock and my fortress and my deliverer" (Psalm 18:2). Paul says of Israel in the wilderness that "they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them, and that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4). Exodus 33:22 uses a different Hebrew word, but it gives the clearest picture of God Himself hiding a man: "I will put you in the cleft of the rock, and will cover you with My hand while I pass by." There Moses is sheltered in order to receive God's revealed goodness.

Christian readers have long heard in the Song the voice of Christ calling His people, and the Song invites that reading even though it is first a poem of human love. Read this way, the contrast in chăgâv points to the gospel. Every self-made stronghold will be brought down. The safe place is the Rock that God provides, and from that refuge Christ calls His hidden, timid people to show their faces and lift their voices to Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Everyone has some kind of cleft. For some it is money, competence, reputation, or a sharp mind. These are the high places from which a heart quietly asks, "Who will bring me down?" Edom's story warns that the most dangerous lies are the ones our strengths tell us. The heights we are most proud of can become the places where we feel least need of God, and those are the places He promises to humble.

Other believers hide for very different reasons: shame, fear, past wounds, or the sense that their prayers are not worth hearing. To them the Song speaks a gentle word. The Lord is not offended that you have hidden. He knows where you are. He does not break down the rock; He calls. He wants to see your face and hear your voice, and He calls that voice sweet. Prayer begins here, with a hidden soul coming out of the shadows because it has been invited and wanted.

Both lessons send us to the same place. Leave the clefts of self-sufficiency, and do not stay hidden in fear. Hide instead in the Rock who is Christ, where being sheltered and being seen are no longer opposites.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Chăgâv occurs only three times: Song of Solomon 2:14, Jeremiah 49:16, and Obadiah 3. All three are developed above. The related "rock" passages cited in Sections 6 and 9 use different Hebrew words and serve as canonical background, not as further occurrences of this word.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H5366 (neqarah)**: a distinct Hebrew word, also translated "cleft," used in Exodus 33:22 where God puts Moses "in the cleft of the rock." It is not a lexical relative of chăgâv. It is a thematic parallel that pictures God Himself providing the hiding place.

  • Thematic connection, "rock" as refuge: Psalm 18:2 ("The LORD is my rock") and 1 Corinthians 10:4 ("that Rock was Christ") supply the image of the true Rock that stands against Edom's false one. Revelation 6:15–16 shows the final failure of hiding in rocks from God.
