HADAD - (Chădad, Strong's H2301)

Summary

Hadad was the eighth listed of the twelve sons of Ishmael, which made him a grandson of Abraham through Hagar. Under this Strong's entry his name appears only once, in the Chronicler's list of Ishmael's sons (1 Chronicles 1:30). The parallel list in Genesis 25:15 names the same son "Hadar," a difference usually explained by the close resemblance of two Hebrew letters. His name comes from a Hebrew root meaning "to be sharp" or "keen," and lexicons give the sense "fierce." Scripture gives no reason to read that meaning into his character. This Hadad is not Hadad the Edomite, the adversary God raised up against Solomon (1 Kings 11:14), and he is not one of the Edomite kings who share that name.

Scripture records nothing Hadad said or did. His place in the Bible still matters, because he is part of the answer to a father's prayer. When Abraham pleaded, "Oh, that Ishmael might live before You!" God answered that He would make Ishmael fruitful and that "He shall beget twelve princes" (Genesis 17:18, 20). Hadad is one of those twelve, counted in Genesis and counted again centuries later in Chronicles. His name stands as quiet evidence that God keeps His word to the letter. He kept it even concerning a line through which the promised Redeemer would not come.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Hadad (H2301) is the eighth listed son of Ishmael, listed between Massa and Tema in 1 Chronicles 1:30. He belongs to the patriarchal period, the second generation after Abraham. His father Ishmael was Abraham's son by Sarah's maid Hagar, and Isaac was his uncle.

The name is linked to the Hebrew root chadad (H2300), "to be sharp, keen," and lexicons gloss the personal name as "fierce." That derivation comes from the lexicon. The biblical text offers no wordplay on the name, and nothing in Scripture describes Hadad himself as fierce.

Genesis 25:15 lists the same son as "Hadar" (H2316). The two spellings differ by a single letter, and in Hebrew those two letters (dalet and resh) look very much alike. The difference is commonly explained as a copying variation. Both lists name the same twelve sons in the same order, so they plainly refer to one person.

Three other uses of the name should be kept separate from this man:

  • Hadad (H1908), the Edomite. This is a separately indexed name. It belongs to Edomite kings (Genesis 36:35; 1 Chronicles 1:46, 50) and to "Hadad the Edomite," Solomon's adversary (1 Kings 11:14).

  • Ben-Hadad (H1130). This compound name was borne by Aramean kings.

  • The storm god Hadad. Ben-Hadad and related Aramean names are generally connected with this deity, but no connection between that deity and Ishmael's son is established.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Hadad's story begins before his birth. In Genesis 17, God told Abraham that the long-awaited son would come through Sarah. Abraham's thoughts went to the son he already had, and he prayed, "Oh, that Ishmael might live before You!" (Genesis 17:18). God gave a double answer. He would establish His covenant with Isaac (17:19). He also promised, "And as for Ishmael, I have heard you. Behold, I have blessed him, and will make him fruitful, and will multiply him exceedingly. He shall beget twelve princes, and I will make him a great nation" (17:20).

Genesis 25 records that the promise came true. Ishmael's sons are named, Hadar/Hadad eighth in the list. The text then adds, "These were the sons of Ishmael and these were their names, by their towns and their settlements, twelve princes according to their nations" (25:16). The phrase "twelve princes" deliberately echoes God's word in chapter 17. The reference to towns, settlements, and nations suggests that these names came to identify tribal groups as well as individuals. Their territory stretched "from Havilah as far as Shur, which is east of Egypt as you go toward Assyria" (25:18). That is the desert region of northern Arabia and the lands bordering it. The exact territory of Hadad's own clan is not recorded.

Centuries later, after the exile, the Chronicler opened his history with genealogies running from Adam onward. He recorded Ishmael's twelve sons again, Hadad among them (1 Chronicles 1:28–31). Only after that did he trace Isaac, Israel, and finally the house of David.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Several things are stated in Scripture. God promised twelve princes from Ishmael, and Genesis says twelve princes came. Chronicles lists the same twelve again. God also stated that His covenant would be established with Isaac rather than Ishmael (Genesis 17:19–21).

Other points are drawn by reasonable inference. The Chronicler had a purpose in recording Ishmael's line before narrowing his focus. He showed returning Israelites that their God governs the history of the surrounding peoples too, and that He had kept His ancient word to Abraham about them. Hadad's single line in Chronicles therefore does real work. It places Israel's story inside God's larger dealings with the nations.

Hadad also illustrates a distinction Paul later draws out. Physical descent from Abraham brought real blessing: fruitfulness, princes, and a nation. It did not make a person an heir of the promise. "Nor are they all children because they are the seed of Abraham; but, 'In Isaac your seed shall be called'" (Romans 9:7).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No passage of Scripture connects Hadad himself to Christ, and he should not be treated as a type. His connection to the larger story is indirect. He stands among the branching lines of Abraham's family that Genesis honors and then sets aside, as it later does with Esau, while the promise moves through Isaac toward Christ. Paul uses Hagar and Ishmael to contrast bondage with the freedom of promise (Galatians 4:21–31). He concludes that "the children of the promise are counted as the seed" (Romans 9:8). The same God who kept His word to Ishmael's sons kept His far greater word to Isaac's line, and that word reached its goal in Jesus.

5. Spirit & Life

A name like Hadad is easy to skim past, yet it shows something specific about God. He heard Abraham's prayer for a son who was not the child of promise, and He answered it in detail. He promised twelve princes, and twelve are named in Genesis and named again in Chronicles. God does not speak loosely, even about the peripheral branches of the story. If He keeps His word that precisely to Ishmael, His promises in Christ are certainly not empty talk.

Hadad's quiet place in the record also brings a sober clarity. Blessing from God's hand is not the same as belonging to His family by promise. Ishmael's sons became princes, yet the promise of salvation was still received by faith in what God had said.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:18–20. Abraham's prayer for Ishmael and God's promise of twelve princes.

  • Genesis 25:13–18. Fulfillment of that promise. The same son is listed as "Hadar," and the passage notes the twelve princes and their territory.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:28–31. The only occurrence of H2301, where Hadad appears in the Chronicler's record of Ishmael's sons.

  • 1 Kings 11:14. Hadad the Edomite (H1908), a different man who must not be confused with this one.

  • Romans 9:7–8. Physical descent from Abraham is distinguished from being counted among the children of the promise.
