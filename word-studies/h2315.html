CHAMBER, BEDROOM, INNER ROOM - (cheder, Strong's H2315)

Summary

Cheder is the Hebrew word for the inner room of a house: the bedroom, the private chamber, the room farthest from the street. Joseph went there to weep when he could no longer hold back his tears. A defeated king ran there to hide, and a bride rejoiced that "the king has brought me into his chambers." Because it was the most private place a person had, the word carries ideas of intimacy, secrecy, refuge, and danger. Proverbs goes further and speaks of the inner rooms of a person's own being, the hidden places of motive and desire.

Scripture uses this ordinary room to show something searching about God. No chamber is closed to Him. Frogs reached Pharaoh's bed. A prophet reported what a Syrian king said in his bedroom. The Lord showed Ezekiel what Israel's elders were doing in their rooms of idols. Yet the same God calls His people into their chambers to hide until His wrath has passed. He also used a bedroom to save the infant Joash and preserve David's royal line. Jesus' words take up these threads when He tells His disciples to go into their room, shut the door, and pray to the Father who sees in secret. The inner room people once used to hide from God becomes the place where they meet Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Cheder is a common Hebrew noun for an interior room. In an ordinary home it was the inner or back room, set apart from the space where guests were received. In a palace it was the king's private quarters. The NKJV translates it "chamber," "bedroom," "room," "inner," "inmost," and "depths." The word appears in the narrative books, in Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Solomon, and in three prophets.

The actions attached to the word tell its story. People go in, shut the door, lie on the bed, weep, whisper, and hide. The uses of cheder build on that simple picture of a room you enter to be away from other eyes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. An inner or private room in a house or palace, often a bedroom. This is the word's ordinary meaning, as when Ishbosheth "was lying on his bed in his bedroom" (2 Samuel 4:7).

2. A hidden inner space or storeroom in a larger structure. The word can name the inner rooms of the temple plan (1 Chronicles 28:11) or rooms filled with riches (Proverbs 24:4). Poetry extends it to the hidden "chambers of the south" from which God sends the storm (Job 9:9; 37:9).

3. The innermost parts of a person. In Proverbs the word describes the hidden depths of a human being, where words settle and motives live (Proverbs 18:8; 20:27).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most narrative uses are literal, but the setting determines what the room means. For Joseph it is a place to grieve privately (Genesis 43:30). For Amnon it is a trap (2 Samuel 13:10). For the bride in the Song it is a place of welcome. The room stays the same while its meaning moves from tender to terrible depending on who is inside and why.

One Hebrew expression is worth noticing. In several verses the word is repeated, giving roughly a room within a room. The NKJV renders this as "an inner chamber" or "inner room" (1 Kings 20:30; 22:25; 2 Chronicles 18:24; 2 Kings 9:2). The doubling points to the most hidden room a building had. Ben-Hadad fled there when his army collapsed. Micaiah told the false prophet Zedekiah that he would one day go "into an inner chamber to hide" (1 Kings 22:25), on the day his false assurance was exposed and Micaiah's warning proved true. Elisha told a young prophet to take Jehu into such a room and anoint him king (2 Kings 9:2).

4. Theological Significance

The main truth carried by this word is that walls do not hide anything from God. Ezekiel was shown "what the elders of the house of Israel do in the dark, every man in the room of his idols," and he heard their reasoning: "The LORD does not see us, the LORD has forsaken the land" (Ezekiel 8:12). These were the nation's leaders, and in the dark each one kept his own shrine. They believed a closed door shut God out. The vision proves the opposite, because God took His prophet straight into the room.

The same truth runs through other passages. During the plagues God promised that frogs would come "into your bedroom, on your bed" (Exodus 8:3). The psalmist remembered that the land swarmed with frogs "even in the chambers of their kings" (Psalm 105:30). The king's private quarters were the most guarded rooms in Egypt, and God's judgment walked past every guard. Later, Syria's king suspected a traitor among his officers and was told that "Elisha, the prophet who is in Israel, tells the king of Israel the words that you speak in your bedroom" (2 Kings 6:12). Ecclesiastes turns this into plain counsel: "Do not curse the rich, even in your bedroom; For a bird of the air may carry your voice" (Ecclesiastes 10:20). In Scripture the bedroom is where people think their most private thoughts and make their plans, and none of it stays sealed.

God also has chambers of His own. Job praises the One who "made the Bear, Orion, and the Pleiades, And the chambers of the south" (Job 9:9), and later in the book Elihu says that "from the chamber of the south comes the whirlwind" (Job 37:9). The contrast is striking. God enters every human room, but no one enters His. His hidden rooms in creation belong to Him alone, and the storm comes out at His command.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 20:27 and the inner rooms of a person. "The spirit of a man is the lamp of the LORD, Searching all the inner depths of his heart." The word behind "inner depths" is cheder. Proverbs pictures a person as a house with inner rooms, and God has placed a lamp inside that house. The human spirit, with its conscience and self-awareness, is the lamp God uses to search rooms that no one else can see.

Proverbs uses the same word for what enters those rooms. "The words of a talebearer are like tasty trifles, And they go down into the inmost body" (Proverbs 18:8; repeated in 26:22). Gossip tastes good, so it is swallowed and goes all the way down. It does not stay on the surface of the mind. It settles in the same inner rooms the Lord's lamp is searching. Proverbs 20:30 then says that painful blows and stripes reach those inner depths and cleanse away evil. Sometimes only discipline goes deep enough to clean what has settled there.

Two other verses show where these rooms lead. "By knowledge the rooms are filled With all precious and pleasant riches" (Proverbs 24:4). The house of the seductive woman, by contrast, leads down to "the chambers of death" (Proverbs 7:27). Every life is being furnished. The question is whether wisdom is filling the rooms or whether they are becoming a tomb.

Bedrooms of betrayal and the bedroom that saved a king. The narrative books show how little a bedroom can protect anyone. Ishbosheth was murdered and beheaded while "lying on his bed in his bedroom" (2 Samuel 4:7). Amnon told Tamar, "Bring the food into the bedroom," and there he violated his sister (2 Samuel 13:10). Samson's enemies were "lying in wait, staying with her in the room" (Judges 16:9). The room meant for rest became a place of murder, abuse, and betrayal.

Against that background stands 2 Kings 11:2. When Athaliah set out to kill every royal heir, Jehosheba "took Joash the son of Ahaziah, and stole him away from among the king's sons who were being murdered; and they hid him and his nurse in the bedroom." Then "he was hidden with her in the house of the LORD for six years" (11:3). A bedroom in itself was no safer than Ishbosheth's had been. Joash's survival kept alive the lasting house God had promised David, and God used a courageous woman and a hidden room to keep that promise. Safety in these stories does not come from walls. It comes from God's care over His purposes.

Isaiah 26:20 and the shut door of refuge. "Come, my people, enter your chambers, And shut your doors behind you; Hide yourself, as it were, for a little moment, Until the indignation is past." Isaiah's words call to mind the Passover night. Israel struck the doorposts with blood, and "none of you shall go out of the door of his house until morning" (Exodus 12:22) while judgment passed through Egypt. In Isaiah the inner room again becomes shelter. God's people are not told to fight the storm or outrun it. They are told to go in, close the door, and wait on Him. The phrase "a little moment" carries mercy, because the hiding has an end, and God sets it.

The Song of Solomon and Joel 2:16. The bride sings, "The king has brought me into his chambers. We will be glad and rejoice in you" (Song of Solomon 1:4). Later she brings her beloved into "the chamber of her who conceived me" (3:4). In the Song the inner room is where married love is welcomed and celebrated, and Scripture honors it. Joel shows what happens when the day of the Lord draws near: "Let the bridegroom go out from his chamber, And the bride from her dressing room" (Joel 2:16). The Law excused a newly married man from war for a year (Deuteronomy 24:5), yet Joel calls even newlyweds out to the assembly of repentance. The call does not despise marriage. It shows how urgent the hour is, when even the most rightful private joy gives way so that the whole people can seek God's face together.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The chamber that saved Joash kept David's line alive, and that line leads to Jesus, the Son of David. A hidden child in a bedroom stands in the long story of God protecting the promised King.

Jesus also takes up the language of the inner room directly. The Greek word tameion, meaning inner room or storeroom, is the New Testament counterpart to cheder. Jesus says, "When you pray, go into your room, and when you have shut your door, pray to your Father who is in the secret place" (Matthew 6:6). The ancient Greek translation of Isaiah 26:20 used this same word for "chambers," so Jesus' instruction can be heard as an echo of the prophet's call to enter and shut the door. In His teaching the inner room is no longer only a shelter from wrath. It is where a child speaks with the Father "who sees in secret."

Jesus also confirms the warning that runs from Ezekiel to Ecclesiastes: "What you have spoken in the ear in inner rooms will be proclaimed on the housetops" (Luke 12:3). The same God who sees the secret idol sees the secret prayer. One will be exposed, and the other "will reward you openly."

The temple plan points in the same direction. David gave Solomon plans for "its inner chambers, and the place of the mercy seat" (1 Chronicles 28:11). The temple's inner rooms led toward the place of God's presence. The New Testament calls believers God's temple, indwelt by His Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16). Comparing the inner rooms of the heart to the temple's inner chambers is a theological connection rather than a stated fulfillment, but it fits Scripture's movement. The inner room of a person was made for God's presence, not for hidden idols.

7. Spirit & Life

Proverbs asks every believer to consider what is furnishing their inner rooms. Gossip goes down "into the inmost body," and knowledge fills the rooms "with all precious and pleasant riches." What we listen to, laugh at, and repeat does not pass through harmlessly. It settles where we live. Guarding the ears is part of guarding the heart.

The lamp of the Lord is already searching. A believer can resist that search or welcome it, as David did when he prayed, "Search me, O God" (Psalm 139:23). Ezekiel's elders thought their dark rooms were unseen. The believer who opens those same rooms to God finds that the light that exposes is also the light that heals.

The shut door also means something new for those in Christ. In seasons of trouble, Isaiah's call and Jesus' command meet in the same simple act: go in, close the door, and pray. That small private room, with an unremarkable door, is where the Father meets His children, and He is not limited by any wall.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 43:30 — "He went into his chamber and wept there." Joseph's private room held the grief he could not yet show his brothers, and the scene prepares for his tearful reconciliation with them.

  • Deuteronomy 32:25 — Moses' song warns that the sword will destroy outside while terror reigns within the house. When Israel turns from God, even the inner rooms offer no peace.

  • Judges 3:24 — Eglon's servants waited outside his locked chamber, assuming he wanted privacy, while Ehud had already killed him. The closed room that seemed to protect the king actually hid his death.

  • 1 Kings 1:15 — Bathsheba went to the aged David in his chamber and pleaded for Solomon's succession. The question of the throne was settled in the king's private room.

  • Job 37:9 — Elihu says, "From the chamber of the south comes the whirlwind." Creation has hidden storerooms that only its Maker commands.
