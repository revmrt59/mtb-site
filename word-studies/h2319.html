NEW, FRESH - (châdâsh, Strong's H2319)

Summary

The Hebrew word châdâsh means "new" or "fresh." It can describe something just made or never used, such as a new cart, new ropes, or a new house. It can also describe someone newly arrived, or something God does that has never been done before. In ordinary life it marks the difference between the untried and the worn. In the hands of the psalmists and prophets it becomes one of Scripture's great words of hope: a new song, a new heart and spirit, a new name, a new covenant, and finally new heavens and a new earth.

The word also exposes a contrast that runs through the Old Testament. Left to itself, human life repeats itself, and Ecclesiastes says that nothing is new under the sun. When Israel went looking for novelty on its own, it chose "new gods" and found ruin. Real newness comes from the LORD. His compassions are "new every morning" (Lamentations 3:23). Through the prophets He promised a new covenant and a new heart, and Jesus fulfilled that promise when He took the cup and called it "the new covenant in My blood" (Luke 22:20).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châdâsh is a Hebrew adjective used 53 times in the Old Testament. The NKJV almost always translates it "new." It renders it "fresh" once, in Job 29:20, and in Isaiah and Jeremiah supplies "thing" or "things" to complete the phrase "new thing(s)." The word belongs to the same family as the Hebrew verb for "renew" and the noun for the new moon, or month, which marked the fresh start of each lunar cycle.

The word appears across a wide range of settings. In the Law and the historical books it usually describes ordinary objects such as houses, carts, ropes, a garment, a bowl, a sword, and even a Jerusalem gate named the "New Gate" (Jeremiah 26:10; 36:10). In the Psalms it describes songs. In Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel it gathers around God's promises of rescue and restoration.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, châdâsh describes what is new in contrast to what is old, used, or already known. Context decides which kind of newness is meant:

1. Recently made or never used. The Philistines build "a new cart" for the ark and hitch it to cows "which have never been yoked" (1 Samuel 6:7). Samson's enemies bind him with "two new ropes" (Judges 15:13). A man may build "a new house" that he has not yet dedicated (Deuteronomy 20:5).

2. Newly arrived or previously unknown. A new king arose over Egypt who did not know Joseph (Exodus 1:8). Israel turned to "new gods," recent arrivals their fathers had never feared (Deuteronomy 32:17).

3. Fresh or freshly supplied. Job remembers when "my glory is fresh within me" (Job 29:20), and God's compassions are "new every morning" (Lamentations 3:23).

4. Never done before. God announces "new things... hidden things" that "are created now and not from the beginning" (Isaiah 48:6–7).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the word describes objects, it often marks something untouched by ordinary use. The new cart, the unyoked cows, and the grain offering at the Feast of Weeks (Leviticus 23:16) all fit this pattern. Each is set apart and offered fresh.

That pattern needs one caution. Philistines returned the ark on a new cart without harm (1 Samuel 6). When David later set "the ark of God on a new cart" (2 Samuel 6:3), Uzzah died after he reached out to steady the ark (2 Samuel 6:6–7). The newness of the cart was not the problem. The Law required Levites to carry the ark (Numbers 4:15), and David later admitted that Israel had failed to seek God "about the proper order" (1 Chronicles 15:13–15). A fresh, well-made cart could not replace God's revealed way.

In the Psalms and Isaiah, châdâsh repeatedly describes a "song" (Psalms 33:3; 40:3; 96:1; 98:1; 144:9; 149:1; Isaiah 42:10). Most of these verses are commands to sing, and they call for praise that answers a fresh act of God.

Ezekiel uses the word in two forms that belong together. In one place God commands, "get yourselves a new heart and a new spirit" (Ezekiel 18:31). In another He promises, "I will give you a new heart" (36:26). Section 5 explains why both matter.

4. Theological Significance

Ecclesiastes uses this word to describe a world that turns in circles. Nothing is new under the sun, and when someone says, "See, this is new," it turns out to have existed long ago (Ecclesiastes 1:9–10). That is honest about life in a fallen world: people cannot produce real newness by their own effort.

Against that background, Isaiah presents the power to bring about new things as a mark of the true God. In Isaiah 41–48 the LORD challenges the idols to explain the past or announce the future, and they cannot (41:21–24). He alone declares "new things" before they happen (42:9). He even announces them in advance so that Israel cannot say, "Of course I knew them" (48:7).

This gives the "new gods" of Deuteronomy 32:17 and Judges 5:8 a dark irony. Israel's new gods were merely unfamiliar, and choosing them led to war in the gates. The God who actually does new things is the oldest One, the LORD their fathers knew. Human novelty in religion leads back into bondage, while God's newness sets people free.

His newness also comes in different sizes. It arrives daily in mercy (Lamentations 3:23), in history through acts of rescue (Isaiah 43:19), and finally across the whole creation (Isaiah 65:17).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Jeremiah 31:31 — the new covenant. "Behold, the days are coming, says the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah." Hebrews quotes the whole promise and supplies its details. This covenant would be "not according to the covenant that I made with their fathers" when God led them out of Egypt, "because they did not continue in My covenant" (Hebrews 8:9). Hebrews says God was "finding fault with them" (8:8). The failure lay with the people, not with God's law. The new covenant therefore changes the people themselves: "I will put My laws in their mind and write them on their hearts" (8:10). It also deals with their guilt: "their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more" (8:12). This single use of châdâsh gives Christians the name "New Testament."

Ezekiel 11:19; 18:31; 36:26 — the new heart and spirit. God does not patch up the old heart. He promises to "take the heart of stone out of your flesh and give you a heart of flesh" (36:26). The surprising detail is that Ezekiel 18:31 has already commanded Israel to "get yourselves a new heart and a new spirit. For why should you die, O house of Israel?" The command shows what God rightly requires and how urgent it is. The promise shows that only God can supply it. Ezekiel 36:27 goes on to say that God will put His Spirit within them, which is how He gives what He commands.

Isaiah 43:18–19 and 48:6–7 — the new thing. "Do not remember the former things, Nor consider the things of old," God says (43:18). Just before this, He described Himself as the One who made a way through the sea and overthrew chariot and horse (43:16–17), which is the Exodus. Israel's greatest memory is set aside, and yet the new thing looks like that memory enlarged: "I will even make a road in the wilderness And rivers in the desert" (43:19). God's new acts echo His old ones while surpassing them. He does not have to repeat Himself, and He never contradicts Himself.

Lamentations 3:23 — new every morning. Lamentations mourns the burning of Jerusalem. In the middle of that grief the poet remembers that the LORD's mercies keep His people from being consumed and that His compassions do not fail (3:22). Then he adds: "They are new every morning; Great is Your faithfulness." God's mercy is not a store laid up long ago and slowly running out. Like the manna, it is given fresh each day, even among the ruins.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jeremiah's promise reaches its explicit fulfillment at the Last Supper: "This cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is shed for you" (Luke 22:20). The forgiveness Jeremiah foresaw, "I will remember no more," is purchased by Christ's death. The new heart Ezekiel promised is given by the Spirit whom Christ sends. Paul gathers both into one statement: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; old things have passed away; behold, all things have become new" (2 Corinthians 5:17).

The Torah's "new grain offering" points in the same direction. This offering was presented fifty days after the first sheaf, on "the day of the firstfruits" at the Feast of Weeks (Leviticus 23:16; Numbers 28:26). The Greek name for that feast is Pentecost, and on that day the Spirit was poured out (Acts 2:1–4). Acts does not call the event a fulfillment of the grain offering. Still, the timing fits: the feast of the new firstfruits became the day God began gathering the first harvest of the new covenant.

Identity is renewed as well. Zion, once called "Forsaken," is promised: "You shall be called by a new name, Which the mouth of the LORD will name" (Isaiah 62:2). The following verses give those names: "Hephzibah," meaning "My delight is in her," and "Beulah," meaning "married" (62:4). The risen Christ makes the same kind of promise to the one who overcomes, who will receive "a white stone, and on the stone a new name written" (Revelation 2:17).

The word's path ends at the largest scale possible. God says He creates new heavens and a new earth, where the former things will not be remembered (Isaiah 65:17), and He promises that they will remain before Him, just as His people's descendants and name will remain (66:22). John sees this future: "Now I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away" (Revelation 21:1). The newness that began in one person's heart finally fills the whole creation.

7. Spirit & Life

The psalmist's new song came from somewhere. God drew him up from the pit and the miry clay, and then "He has put a new song in my mouth" (Psalm 40:3). Fresh praise grows out of fresh deliverance. That is why the command "Sing to Him a new song" (Psalm 33:3) is more than a call for new music. It calls believers to notice what God is doing now. Worship goes stale when we live only on the memory of past mercies and stop expecting the mercies God gives each morning. The psalm also adds that "many will see it and fear, And will trust in the LORD." When one person is rescued, others come to faith.

Isaiah 41:15 shows what God's renewal does to weak people. He addresses "you worm Jacob" (41:14) and then says, "I will make you into a new threshing sledge with sharp teeth; You shall thresh the mountains." This is a picture, and its point is clear. God does not simply restore His people to their former strength. He makes them a new and sharp instrument for a task far beyond them.

David's new cart warns believers in a different way. Fresh methods, sincere zeal, and well-built plans cannot replace obedience to what God has said. The new things that matter come from God's hand on God's terms. They are a new heart that loves His law and a new name that He alone gives.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joshua 9:13 — The Gibeonites claimed their cracked wineskins were "new" when filled. They used the contrast between new and worn to deceive Israel.

  • 1 Kings 11:29–30 — The prophet Ahijah tore his new garment into twelve pieces to show how the kingdom would be divided. Tearing something new made the loss vivid.

  • 2 Kings 2:20 — Elisha asked for a new bowl with salt to heal Jericho's water. God's healing came through a fresh, unused vessel.

  • Deuteronomy 22:8 — A new house required a parapet around its roof so that the owner would not bring the guilt of bloodshed on his household. Care for others' lives was built in from the start.

  • Leviticus 26:10 — In the promised blessing, God's provision would be so abundant that Israel would clear out the old harvest to make room for the new.

  • Jeremiah 31:22 — Shortly before the new-covenant promise, the LORD announces that He has created a new thing in the earth, showing that the coming restoration is His creative work.
