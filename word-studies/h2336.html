THISTLE, THORN, HOOK - (chôwach, Strong's H2336)

Summary

The Hebrew word chôwach names a thistle, thorn, or bramble, the prickly wild growth that takes over where nothing good is planted. In two passages the same word names a hook, the sharp tool used to seize a powerful creature by the jaw or to lead away a captive.

Scripture uses both pictures to teach about God and human pride. When King Amaziah of Judah grew arrogant, a rival king compared him to a thistle demanding a marriage alliance with a cedar. In Isaiah and Hosea, thorns spread through ruined palaces and abandoned tents, showing what becomes of human achievement when God withdraws His blessing. The hook teaches a related lesson. God asks Job whether he can put a hook in Leviathan's jaw, and no human can. Yet the same word describes how the LORD had Manasseh, Judah's most wicked king, taken "with hooks" to Babylon. There Manasseh humbled himself and was restored.

The word also appears in two images about the inner life. A lily among thorns pictures a beloved treasured in a hostile world. A thorn in a drunkard's hand warns about handling wisdom without feeling its sharp point. Across the Bible, thorns mark the curse that followed Adam's sin. In the Gospels, thorns are finally twisted into a crown and pressed onto the head of Jesus.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chôwach is a Hebrew noun. It appears in historical narrative, wisdom literature, love poetry, and the prophets. The word does not identify one exact plant. It is a general term for the spiny weeds and brambles that overrun neglected fields and ruins. For farmers who fought such weeds every year, these plants meant wasted ground and painful work.

The NKJV translates chôwach as "thistle," "thorn," "brambles," and twice as "hook." The shared idea is a sharp, piercing point. Some suggest that hardened thorns once served as hooks, but the evidence does not settle exactly how the meaning "hook" developed. What the passages make clear is the hook's purpose: to grip and control something that would otherwise resist.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A thistle, thorn, or bramble. This is a prickly wild plant, useless for food or timber, that often marks neglected or ruined land. Job speaks of "thistles" growing "instead of wheat" (Job 31:40).

2. A hook. This is a sharp tool for seizing and leading a powerful creature or a captive. God asks Job whether he can "pierce his jaw with a hook" (Job 41:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses chôwach in three main ways.

  • As a comparison. In a fable (2 Kings 14:9) and in two similes (Proverbs 26:9; Song of Solomon 2:2), the thorn stands for something worthless, dangerous, or harsh. It is set beside something of real value, such as a cedar or a lily.

  • As a sign of desolation. In prophecy, thorns grow where people once lived. Hosea pictures them settling into homes: "Thorns shall be in their tents" (Hosea 9:6).

  • As a hook of capture. The hook appears only where a mighty creature or a defiant king is brought under control (Job 41:2; 2 Chronicles 33:11).

4. Theological Significance

Thorns appear early in the Bible's story. After Adam's sin, God said of the ground, "Both thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you" (Genesis 3:18). Genesis uses different Hebrew words, but it is the same picture. From that point on, prickly weeds stand for a world that resists human effort because of sin.

The prophets use chôwach to show that this curse can reclaim even the greatest human achievements. Isaiah says of Edom, "thorns shall come up in its palaces, nettles and brambles in its fortresses" (Isaiah 34:13). Hosea sees Israel's treasures overrun: "Nettles shall possess their valuables of silver; Thorns shall be in their tents" (Hosea 9:6). Palaces, fortresses, silver, and homes are exactly what people build to feel secure. When God removes His blessing, brambles take over, and the land returns to the condition that followed Eden.

The word also shows God's rule over pride. The proud thistle is trampled. The hook that no person can set in Leviathan is in God's hand. Whatever resists Him, whether a weed in the field or a king on a throne, remains under His authority.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The thistle and the cedar (2 Kings 14:9; 2 Chronicles 25:18). Amaziah of Judah had just defeated Edom. Then, in a strange reversal, he "brought the gods of the people of Seir, set them up to be his gods, and bowed down before them" (2 Chronicles 25:14). When God sent a prophet to rebuke him, Amaziah silenced him: "Cease! Why should you be killed?" (25:16). Full of confidence, he then challenged Jehoash, king of Israel, to battle.

Jehoash answered with a fable. "The thistle that was in Lebanon sent to the cedar that was in Lebanon, saying, 'Give your daughter to my son as wife'; and a wild beast that was in Lebanon passed by and trampled the thistle" (2 Kings 14:9). The thistle saw itself as the cedar's equal. In reality, a thistle has no strength and gives no shade. The cedar does not even have to act, because an animal passing by is enough to crush the thistle. Jehoash explained the fable directly: "your heart has lifted you up" (14:10). He meant it as an insult, but it was an accurate description of Amaziah.

Events proved the fable true. Judah was routed, four hundred cubits of Jerusalem's wall were broken down, and the gold and silver of the house of the LORD were carried off (14:12–14). The Chronicler explains what lay beneath the defeat: "it came from God, that He might give them into the hand of their enemies, because they sought the gods of Edom" (2 Chronicles 25:20). Amaziah's pride was more than a sin. It was a false picture of reality. He had abandoned the only source of his strength and still believed he was a cedar.

The hook in Leviathan's jaw and in Manasseh (Job 41:2; 2 Chronicles 33:11). In His answer to Job, God describes Leviathan, a creature beyond human control, and asks, "Can you put a reed through his nose, Or pierce his jaw with a hook?" (Job 41:2). The expected answer is no.

The same word appears in the account of Manasseh. Manasseh filled Jerusalem with idolatry and innocent blood (2 Kings 21:16) and refused to listen when the LORD spoke to him (2 Chronicles 33:10). Then "the LORD brought upon them the captains of the army of the king of Assyria, who took Manasseh with hooks, bound him with bronze fetters, and carried him off to Babylon" (33:11). Whether these were literal hooks or a figure of speech, the picture is of a dangerous animal subdued and led away. Assyria held the hook, but the Chronicler says the LORD brought the army.

The story does not end in Babylon. In his affliction Manasseh pleaded with the LORD and humbled himself greatly. God heard him and brought him back to Jerusalem, and "Manasseh knew that the LORD was God" (paraphrasing 33:12–13). The hook was both a humiliation and a mercy. God used it to drag a king who would not listen to the place where he finally prayed.

The thorn in a drunkard's hand (Proverbs 26:9). Proverbs 26 describes the fool in a series of word pictures. Verse 9 says, "Like a thorn that goes into the hand of a drunkard Is a proverb in the mouth of fools." The image can be read two ways. It may describe a thorn piercing a hand too numb to feel it, or a thorny branch waved carelessly by a man who cannot control it. Either way, the point is the same. A fool can repeat true words without feeling their point in his own conscience, and in his hands those words cause harm. Wisdom that has not first corrected the speaker becomes dangerous when he uses it on others.

The lily among thorns (Song of Solomon 2:2). The bride speaks modestly of herself as a common wildflower: "I am the rose of Sharon, And the lily of the valleys" (2:1). Her beloved answers, "Like a lily among thorns, So is my love among the daughters." To him, the other women are like brambles in comparison, and she stands out among them. Christian readers have long heard in these words the love of Christ for His people. Read that way, the verse shows the Lord treasuring His people as a lily growing in the middle of a thorny world.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The thorns that entered the world through Adam's sin reappear at the cross. "When they had twisted a crown of thorns, they put it on His head" (Matthew 27:29; see John 19:2). The Gospels use a Greek word, so this is a connection of imagery rather than an occurrence of chôwach. Still, the picture fits the larger story. The soldiers meant to mock Jesus as a false king, but they crowned the true King with the symbol of the curse. Paul states the meaning plainly: Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us (Galatians 3:13). Isaiah and Hosea pictured thorns spreading over the homes of people under judgment. At the cross, Jesus wore those thorns Himself.

The hook also points forward. God promised to put hooks in Pharaoh's jaws, "I will put hooks in your jaws" (Ezekiel 29:4), and to do the same to Gog (Ezekiel 38:4). These passages use a related image, and they show that the God who alone can master Leviathan also masters proud nations. That authority comes to full expression in Christ, who disarmed the powers arrayed against God (Colossians 2:15). Manasseh's story also anticipates the gospel. If the worst king in Judah's history could be humbled and restored, then no rebel is beyond the reach of God's grace.

7. Spirit & Life

Amaziah's ruin began on the day of his victory. Success over Edom made him careless enough to worship Edom's gods and proud enough to silence God's prophet. The thistle-and-cedar fable warns that a season of blessing is often when we are most likely to forget how small we are and how much we depend on God. A heart that is "lifted up" does not grow taller. It only loses sight of its own frailty.

Manasseh's hook shows the purpose behind God's hardest discipline. The bronze chains and the march to Babylon were not God's final word. His final word was "Manasseh knew that the LORD was God." When God brings us low, He is aiming at that kind of knowing. The pain is real, but it leads toward repentance and restoration rather than destruction.

Proverbs 26:9 asks whether God's Word still pricks us. It is possible to quote Scripture fluently while feeling nothing, like a drunkard who does not notice the thorn in his hand. The remedy is to let the Word correct us before we use it on anyone else.

The lily among thorns offers comfort. Believers live among brambles: a world under the curse, sharp people, and hostile places. The Beloved does not see His people as part of the thorns. He sees a lily, and He delights in her.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Job 31:40. "Then let thistles grow instead of wheat, And weeds instead of barley." Job closes his defense by staking his own fields on his integrity. If he has lived falsely, he asks that his harvest turn to thistles, the curse of Genesis 3 falling on one man's land.

The remaining occurrences of chôwach are discussed above.
