THREAD, CORD, LINE, STRAND - (chûwṭ, Strong's H2339)

Summary

The Hebrew word chûwṭ means a thread or cord. It is a thin, spun fiber, and the NKJV translates it "thread," "cord," "line," or "strand." The word appears only seven times in the Old Testament, yet those few uses carry real weight. A single thread can stand for the smallest possible amount of anything, as when Abram refuses even "a thread" from the king of Sodom. A thread can picture weakness, as when Samson snaps new ropes "like a thread." Twisted together, threads become a "threefold cord" that is hard to break. Stretched out, a cord becomes the measuring line used to measure the bronze pillars of Solomon's temple.

The best-known use is the "scarlet cord" Rahab tied in her window at Jericho. Something as slight as a piece of colored thread marked her household for rescue when the city fell. That cord echoes the Passover blood on Israel's doorposts, and it led a Canaanite woman into Israel and into the family line of Jesus Christ. Across its uses, chûwṭ shows a pattern in God's ways. He attaches great purposes to small things. He exposes the weakness of what people use to bind, and He gives lasting strength to what He holds together.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chûwṭ is a common Hebrew noun for a thread or cord. Weaving and sewing were part of daily household life in Israel, so everyone knew how a single strand could snap and how twisted strands could hold. The word is usually lighter than a heavy rope. That is why it works well as a picture of something small or fragile.

The seven uses come from many kinds of writing: patriarchal history (Genesis 14), conquest and judges narratives (Joshua 2; Judges 16), temple construction and destruction (1 Kings 7; Jeremiah 52), wisdom literature (Ecclesiastes 4), and love poetry (Song of Solomon 4).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chûwṭ names a thread, string, or cord. Context determines how thin or strong it is and what it is used for.

1. A single thread or strand, the thinnest spun fiber. It can mark the smallest possible amount ("from a thread to a sandal strap," Genesis 14:23), something easily broken (Judges 16:12), or a fine line of color (Song of Solomon 4:3).

2. A cord used to tie, hang, or bind, sometimes made from several strands twisted together (Joshua 2:18; Ecclesiastes 4:12).

3. A measuring line, a cord of known length used by builders (1 Kings 7:15; Jeremiah 52:21).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses compare something to a thread or measure something by one. In Genesis 14:23 the thread stands at one end of a phrase covering everything from the least item to the next least, so the whole phrase means "nothing at all." Judges 16:12 uses the word in a comparison: ropes break "like a thread." Ecclesiastes 4:12 turns the image around. A single strand is weak, but three strands twisted together are strong.

Twice the thread is scarlet. Rahab's cord is a "scarlet cord" (Joshua 2:18), and the bride's lips are "like a strand of scarlet" (Song of Solomon 4:3). The Hebrew wording in these two verses is closely parallel, which makes the link between them more than a matter of English translation.

In the temple accounts the word has a purely practical meaning. It is a builder's line twelve cubits long, recorded once at the building of the pillars and again at their removal by Babylon.

4. Theological Significance

The uses of chûwṭ show that God measures things differently than people do. To the king of Sodom, a thread was worthless. To Abram, accepting even a thread would have given someone other than "the LORD, God Most High, the Possessor of heaven and earth" (Genesis 14:22) a share of the credit for his blessing. To Jericho, a cord in a window meant nothing. To God, it marked a household under His promise of mercy. Small things become significant because of what they are tied to.

The word also shows where real strength comes from. Philistine ropes could not hold a man whom God had empowered, while the cord that God's people honored kept Rahab's family safe. God's dwelling, too, was built by exact measurement. When judgment came, the same measurements were recorded again. Nothing about His house, whether its building or its loss, happened carelessly or outside His notice.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Joshua 2:18 — The scarlet cord. Rahab hid Israel's spies and then asked them to swear by the LORD and "give me a true token" (Joshua 2:12) that her family would be spared. The token was a "scarlet cord," which the spies told her to "bind… in the window through which you let us down." The cord came with conditions. Her whole family had to be gathered inside her house, and anyone who went out into the street would be responsible for his own death (Joshua 2:18–19).

The parallel with Passover is clear. In Egypt, God said, "the blood shall be a sign for you on the houses where you are. And when I see the blood, I will pass over you" (Exodus 12:13). In both cases a visible scarlet mark sets apart a house, the family must stay inside it, and judgment passes over those under the sign. When Jericho burned, the spies brought out Rahab and "all that she had" (Joshua 6:23), and Joshua 6:25 adds, "So she dwells in Israel to this day." Hebrews 11:31 explains what the cord meant: "By faith the harlot Rahab did not perish with those who did not believe." The cord had no power in itself. It was faith made visible, Rahab's open trust in Israel's God, hung where judgment would arrive.

Genesis 14:23 — Not even a thread. After Abram rescued Lot, the king of Sodom offered him the goods. Abram had already sworn to God that he would "take nothing, from a thread to a sandal strap… lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich.'" He did not refuse because the goods were evil. He refused because Sodom's king must have no claim on his story. He let his allies take their fair share (Genesis 14:24), so this was not a rule for everyone. It was a personal decision that God alone would be his source.

Judges 16:12 and Ecclesiastes 4:12 — Strength broken and strength woven. Delilah bound Samson with new ropes, but "he broke them off his arms like a thread." His strength was God's gift, and later in the same chapter it left him only when "the LORD had departed from him" (Judges 16:20, paraphrased). Ecclesiastes uses the same word to make the opposite point. "Two are better than one" (4:9), and "a threefold cord is not quickly broken" (4:12). A lone thread fails. Strands bound together hold.

1 Kings 7:15 and Jeremiah 52:21 — The measured pillars. Solomon cast two bronze pillars, and "a line of twelve cubits measured the circumference of each." Later they were named Jachin and Boaz, names usually understood as "He shall establish" and "In it is strength" (1 Kings 7:21). Jeremiah 52:21 records the same twelve-cubit measurement in the account of Babylon breaking up the temple's bronze. The repetition has a sobering force. The pillars that stood for God's establishing strength were measured at their building and measured again at their fall, because the people had abandoned the God those pillars proclaimed.

Song of Solomon 4:3 — A strand of scarlet. The bridegroom tells his bride, "Your lips are like a strand of scarlet, And your mouth is lovely." In this verse the image describes beauty in love poetry and nothing more. Still, because the Hebrew phrase so closely matches Rahab's scarlet cord, a reader of the whole Bible may notice the scarlet thread reappearing. In Joshua it marked a woman for rescue. In the Song it describes a bride delighted in.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Rahab's cord does not end at Jericho. Matthew names her in the opening lines of Jesus' family record: "Salmon begot Boaz by Rahab" (Matthew 1:5). The woman saved under a scarlet sign became an ancestor of the Messiah. Her rescue also anticipates the gospel. The New Testament calls Christ "our Passover" (1 Corinthians 5:7), and judgment passes over those who shelter under His blood. Like Rahab's family, believers are kept safe by gathering under the sign God appointed, and they have no safety outside it.

The scarlet strand of the Song resonates with the larger biblical story. The Scriptures do not state this as a fulfillment, but it fits the way the Bible develops. Christ cleanses His church "that she should be holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27, paraphrased). The same grace that rescues those under judgment also makes them beloved.

The measured pillars point forward as well. Solomon's bronze pillars were broken up and carried to Babylon. Jesus spoke of His own body as the temple that would be destroyed and raised (John 2:19–21). He promises that whoever overcomes will be made "a pillar in the temple of My God" that will never be removed (Revelation 3:12, paraphrased). What fell under the measuring line of judgment will be established again in Christ.

7. Spirit & Life

Rahab teaches what faith looks like in an ordinary place. She did not wait for Jericho's walls to fall before she acted. She tied the cord while the city still stood and gathered her family under it. Faith takes God at His word before the outcome is visible, and it is willing to be seen.

Abram's "not even a thread" calls for honest self-examination. The danger he guarded against was not a large bribe but a small claim, a debt that would let someone else say, "I made him." Believers can ask whom they want to be able to take credit for their lives. Abram had just sworn by the "Possessor of heaven and earth," and a man who belongs to that God can afford to leave Sodom's threads behind.

Samson's broken ropes and the threefold cord hold two truths together. Whatever God strengthens, human restraints cannot finally hold. Yet no believer was meant to stand alone. Ecclesiastes speaks of human companionship, and many Christians have also seen the Lord Himself in the third strand. That reading is an application rather than the verse's direct statement, but it is well grounded: we are strongest when bound together with one another and held by Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This word occurs only seven times, and every occurrence has been discussed above: Genesis 14:23; Joshua 2:18; Judges 16:12; 1 Kings 7:15; Ecclesiastes 4:12; Song of Solomon 4:3; and Jeremiah 52:21. Jeremiah 52:21 deserves a second look. It repeats the twelve-cubit measuring line of 1 Kings 7:15 in the account of the temple's destruction, linking the building of God's house to its loss.
