HIVITE, HIVITES - (Chivvîy, Strong's H2340)

Summary

"Hivite" is the name of one of the peoples living in Canaan before and during Israel's entry into the land. The word means nothing beyond the name of this people. Its importance comes from the role the Hivites play in God's dealings with Israel. Usually they appear in the familiar lists of nations God promised to drive out, and those lists repeatedly remind Israel that the land would be received by the Lord's power, since these peoples were "greater and mightier" than Israel (Deuteronomy 7:1).

Several Hivite stories stand out. Shechem the Hivite violated Jacob's daughter Dinah, and his father then offered intermarriage, which later became Israel's downfall in Judges. The Hivites of Gibeon tricked Joshua into a treaty, yet the oath held, and these people ended up serving at the altar of the Lord. Under Solomon the Hivites who were left in the land, descendants Israel had not destroyed, were subordinated to his labor levy. Taken together, the Hivites show God's faithfulness to His promise, the danger of easy compromise, and the reach of His mercy toward an outsider who seeks peace.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chivvîy is a Hebrew ethnic name. The Table of Nations traces the Hivites to Canaan, son of Ham, and lists them among Canaan's descendants as "the Hivite, the Arkite, and the Sinite" (Genesis 10:17; compare 1 Chronicles 1:15). Scripture places them in more than one region. Some lived in central Canaan, at Shechem (Genesis 34) and Gibeon (Joshua 9). Others lived in the far north, "below Hermon in the land of Mizpah" (Joshua 11:3) and "in Mount Lebanon, from Mount Baal Hermon to the entrance of Hamath" (Judges 3:3).

One textual puzzle is worth knowing. Genesis 36:2 calls Zibeon "the Hivite," while Genesis 36:20 lists Zibeon among "the sons of Seir the Horite." Many scholars think an early copying slip lies behind the difference, because two Hebrew letters that distinguish the names look alike. Nothing of theological weight depends on it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has a single sense. It names a member of the Hivite people, or the people as a group. NKJV renders it "Hivite" or "Hivites." The word carries no hidden meaning, and readers should not try to derive one from its letters. What Scripture teaches through the Hivites comes from the passages where they appear.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most occurrences come in stock lists of the peoples of Canaan, usually six or seven names in varying order. These lists appear at major moments: the burning bush (Exodus 3:8, 17), the giving of the law (Exodus 23:23; 34:11), Moses' final sermons (Deuteronomy 7:1; 20:17), the crossing of the Jordan (Joshua 3:10), and Joshua's farewell (Joshua 24:11).

The rest of the uses appear in narratives where Hivites act as real people with names, cities, and choices. Shechem, Hamor, the men of Gibeon, and the northern Hivites of Lebanon all step out of the lists into the story. That movement from a name on a list to a neighbor at the door is where most of the word's teaching lies.

4. Theological Significance

The lists of nations are the backbone of God's land promise. When God appeared to Moses, He said, "I have come down to deliver them," and He named the land He would give as "the place of the Canaanites and the Hittites and the Amorites and the Perizzites and the Hivites and the Jebusites" (Exodus 3:8). The promise was concrete. God named the nations who held the land.

Deuteronomy 7:1 adds a frank assessment: "seven nations greater and mightier than you." Israel was never told the conquest would be easy or that they were strong enough. At the Jordan, Joshua attached the same list to a sign. As the ark crossed into the river ahead of the people, Israel would know "that the living God is among you, and that He will without fail drive out from before you" the Hivites and the others (Joshua 3:10–17). The crossing confirmed God's presence, and the named nations expressed the promise that presence guaranteed. Each time the Hivites are named in these lists, the passage looks back to the promise to Abram (Genesis 15:18–21) and forward to its keeping. The repetition testifies that God remembers what He has sworn and is able to carry it out against odds His people cannot match.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 34 and Judges 3: the offer that became a snare. The first Hivite in the biblical story enters with violence: "Shechem the son of Hamor the Hivite, prince of the country, saw her, he took her and lay with her, and violated her" (Genesis 34:2). His father's response was a business proposal: "make marriages with us; give your daughters to us, and take our daughters to yourselves" (Genesis 34:9). Hamor offered to merge the two families into one people sharing the land, and he never mentioned the wrong done to Dinah. Jacob's sons answered deceitfully, pretending to consent that the two families would "become one people" (Genesis 34:13–16), and then carried out a slaughter (34:25–31). Their revenge is not presented as a model of covenant loyalty, and neither side comes out clean. Still, the shape of Hamor's offer matters. Through Moses God later warned Israel to make no covenant with these peoples, "lest it be a snare in your midst" (Exodus 34:12), and then spelled out the danger of intermarriage leading to worship of their gods (Exodus 34:15–16).

Judges 3 shows the warning coming true. "The children of Israel dwelt among the Canaanites, the Hittites, the Amorites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites" (Judges 3:5). The next verse describes the same exchange of daughters that Hamor proposed, and its result: they served the gods of these nations (Judges 3:6). Generations later and by gradual drift, Israel lived out the kind of merging Hamor had proposed at Shechem. Living side by side without any distinct loyalty to God ended in idolatry.

Joshua 9 and 11: the Hivites who sought peace. The Gibeonites are the only Canaanite city that made peace with Israel. Joshua 11:19 states that no city did so except the Hivites who lived in Gibeon. They knew what had happened to Jericho and Ai, so they disguised themselves as travelers from a far country. The men of Israel were suspicious: "Perhaps you dwell among us; so how can we make a covenant with you?" (Joshua 9:7). The decisive failure came in Joshua 9:14. Israel's leaders examined the moldy bread but did not ask counsel of the Lord. Their own judgment failed them, though the evidence was in their hands.

Three days later the truth came out, yet the leaders would not break their oath, because they had sworn by the Lord (Joshua 9:18–20). Generations later, God showed that He took that oath seriously. When famine struck in David's day, the Lord said it was because Saul had killed the Gibeonites (2 Samuel 21:1–2). A promise made in God's name, even one obtained by trickery, could not be set aside without guilt.

Joshua then assigned the Gibeonites to be woodcutters and water carriers for the congregation and for the altar of the Lord (Joshua 9:27). People under sentence of destruction were given a permanent place beside the worship of Israel's God. Later tradition often connects them with the temple servants listed in Ezra 2:43, though Scripture does not state that link directly. Their motives were fear and self-preservation, and their method was a lie. Even so, they ran toward Israel's God instead of fighting Him, and they lived.

1 Kings 9 and 2 Chronicles 8: the survivors under Solomon. Under Solomon, "all the people who were left of the Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites, who were not of the children of Israel" were drafted as forced labor (1 Kings 9:20; 2 Chronicles 8:7). These were descendants "whom the children of Israel had not been able to destroy completely" (1 Kings 9:21). The peoples who had resisted Joshua and tempted Israel through the judges were not removed; their survivors were subordinated to the labor levy of David's son. This also recalls Noah's words that Canaan would serve his brothers (Genesis 9:25).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Hivite story moves from a promise, through compromise and unexpected mercy, to the subordination of the survivors under David's heir. That last step points ahead. Psalm 110:1 speaks of David's Lord seated until His enemies are made His footstool, and the New Testament applies this to Jesus, who must reign until God has put all enemies under His feet (1 Corinthians 15:25). Solomon's partial, earthly rule over the remnant of the Hivites is a small picture of that greater reign, in which Christ's conquest of every enemy will be complete as Solomon's never was. Scripture does not call it a type, but the pattern fits.

Gibeon looks forward in another way. Outsiders under judgment came to the people of God, were bound to them by an oath sworn in the Lord's name, and were given a place serving at His altar. That anticipates the gospel, in which Gentiles "who once were far off" are brought near (Ephesians 2:13). In Christ the oath is God's own and involves no deception, and those who come are received as sons as well as servants.

7. Spirit & Life

Joshua 9:14 identifies a failure that is easy to repeat. Israel's leaders had real evidence in front of them, and they trusted it instead of asking God. Most damaging compromises in a believer's life do not come from open enemies. They come from situations that look reasonable when we decide them on our own.

Genesis 34 and Judges 3 show that compromise usually arrives as an offer of peace, partnership, and shared prosperity: "the land shall be before you." What Hamor proposed openly, Israel later drifted into gradually. Faithfulness requires a settled loyalty to the Lord that shapes whom we bind ourselves to.

Gibeon shows the other side of God's character. The God who judges the nations of Canaan also gave lasting shelter to Hivites who came to Him in fear and with mixed motives. He then held His own people to their promise for centuries. Anyone who comes to Him seeking peace will not be turned away, and He expects His people to keep their word as He keeps His.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 23:28 — God promises to send the hornet ahead of Israel to drive out the Hivite, the Canaanite, and the Hittite. The land would be cleared by means Israel could not supply.

  • Exodus 33:2 — Even after the golden calf, God promises to send His Angel ahead of Israel and drive out the Hivite and the others. Israel's sin did not cancel His word.

  • Deuteronomy 20:17 — The Hivites are named among the peoples Israel must utterly destroy, so that they would not teach Israel their abominations. The concern is spiritual corruption, not ethnic hatred.

  • Judges 3:3 — The northern Hivites "who dwelt in Mount Lebanon" were left to test Israel. Nations that remained in the land became a test of Israel's obedience.

  • 2 Samuel 24:7 — David's census takers pass through "the cities of the Hivites," a sign that Hivite towns were still a recognized part of the land late in David's reign.
