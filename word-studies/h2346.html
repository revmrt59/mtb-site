WALL, WALLED - (chôwmâh, Strong's H2346)

Summary

The Hebrew word chôwmâh means "wall," most often the massive stone wall that surrounded an ancient city. A wall separated a secure city from an open village, kept enemies out, gave defenders a place to stand and watch, and gave the people inside a sense of identity and permanence. For that reason the Old Testament's story of walls is also a story about where people place their trust. Jericho's wall fell flat at a shout. Jerusalem's walls were broken through when the nation abandoned the Lord. Nehemiah's rebuilt wall became the setting for purified worship and joy.

The word also reaches beyond stone. At the Red Sea the waters stood as "a wall" on Israel's right and left. God promised to make Jeremiah "bronze walls" against his opponents. Proverbs pictures a man without self-control as a city without walls. Most importantly, the prophets say God Himself will be His people's wall. Isaiah foresees a city whose walls are called "Salvation." Zechariah hears the Lord say, "I... will be a wall of fire all around her, and I will be the glory in her midst" (Zechariah 2:5). Scripture moves from the walls people build to the protection only God can give, and that movement reaches its goal in Christ.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chôwmâh is a Hebrew noun for a protective wall, especially the fortification around a city. Hebrew has other words for walls. Qiyr, for example, usually describes the wall of a house or building. Chôwmâh is the wall of defense, the structure that made a town a fortified city.

That distinction carried legal weight in Israel. Under the Jubilee law, "a house in a walled city" could be redeemed for only one year. After that it "shall belong permanently to him who bought it" (Leviticus 25:29–30). By contrast, "the houses of villages which have no wall around them shall be counted as the fields of the country" and return to the family at the Jubilee (25:31). A wall marked settled, permanent city life. An unwalled village belonged with the open land, which God returned to its families in the fiftieth year.

The word appears most heavily in Nehemiah, in the historical books' accounts of kings who built and lost walls, and in the prophets' oracles of siege, judgment and restoration. It also appears in a handful of vivid poetic uses in Proverbs and the Song of Solomon.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chôwmâh means a wall that encloses and protects. Context shows three main ways it is used:

1. A city wall or fortification, the stone defenses around a town such as Jericho, Jerusalem, Tyre or Babylon (Joshua 6:5; 2 Kings 25:10; Jeremiah 51:58).

2. A wall-like barrier that is not made of stone, as when the parted waters "were a wall to them on their right hand and on their left" (Exodus 14:22).

3. A figurative wall, meaning a person or power that stands firm and protects. Examples include Jeremiah made into "bronze walls" (Jeremiah 1:18), the young woman who says "I am a wall" (Song of Songs 8:10), and the Lord as "a wall of fire" (Zechariah 2:5).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Kings build and strengthen walls as a sign of a secure reign. Solomon built "the wall of Jerusalem" (1 Kings 9:15), and Hezekiah repaired the broken wall and built another outside it as Assyria approached (2 Chronicles 32:5). Walls were also places of public life and danger. Watchmen stood on them (2 Samuel 18:24), defenders shot from them (2 Samuel 11:20), and citizens listened from them during negotiations. The Assyrian envoys deliberately shouted in Hebrew so that "the people who are on the wall" would hear their threats (2 Kings 18:26–27).

Prophetic and poetic writers turn the wall into an image. In those passages the wall's strength or failure represents a person's character or a nation's standing before God. When God is the subject, the wall becomes His own protecting presence.

4. Theological Significance

Throughout Scripture, a wall is only as strong as the people's relationship with God. Isaiah tells Judah that its sin is "like a breach ready to fall, a bulge in a high wall, whose breaking comes suddenly, in an instant" (Isaiah 30:13). The danger lay inside the nation. Long before any army arrived, its sin had already weakened the wall.

Amos records, "the Lord stood on a wall made with a plumb line, with a plumb line in His hand" (Amos 7:7). A plumb line is a weighted cord that builders used to make sure a wall stood straight. The wall had been built true. Now God holds that same line against His people and finds them crooked (7:8). Lamentations shows the result: "The LORD has purposed to destroy the wall of the daughter of Zion. He has stretched out a line" (Lamentations 2:8). The measuring line that once laid out the wall now marks it for demolition. The poet then pictures the stones themselves grieving, saying God "caused the rampart and wall to lament; they languished together." Later in the chapter the wall is even called on to weep like a river day and night (2:18). The structure built to guard Zion becomes the city's chief mourner.

God also uses walls to mark holiness. In Ezekiel's vision of the temple, the surrounding wall exists to separate what is holy from what is common (Ezekiel 42:20). A wall can keep enemies out, and it can also mark off what belongs to the Lord.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 14: the sea as a wall. The first time Scripture uses the word, the wall is made of water: "the waters were a wall to them on their right hand and on their left" (Exodus 14:22). The sea was the very thing that trapped Israel. At God's command it stood up and guarded them as they crossed. Verse 29 repeats the phrase after the waters have returned over Pharaoh's army. The same water was a wall for the redeemed and a grave for their oppressors. Before Israel ever built a city wall, they learned that their first wall was something God made.

Joshua 2 and 6: Rahab on the doomed wall. Jericho's wall was the Canaanites' confidence. Yet Rahab "let them down by a rope through the window, for her house was on the city wall; she dwelt on the wall" (Joshua 2:15). Then "the wall fell down flat" at the trumpet and shout (6:20). The irony is striking. The one household that sheltered God's spies was built into the very structure God brought down, and Rahab was still brought out alive (6:22–23). God's judgment of a false refuge and His rescue of someone who trusted Him took place at the same wall.

Nehemiah: rebuilding a wall to restore worship. Nehemiah's grief began with the report that the survivors were "in great distress and reproach. The wall of Jerusalem is also broken down" (Nehemiah 1:3). A broken wall meant shame for God's people. Nehemiah inspected the ruins by night (2:13) and called the people to build so that they would no longer be a reproach (2:17). Under mockery and threat, the builders kept a weapon ready while they worked (4:17), and they finished the wall in fifty-two days (6:15). The book's real climax comes at the dedication. The priests and Levites purified themselves, the people, the gates and the wall (12:30). Two great thanksgiving choirs then marched along the top of it (12:31, 38), and the joy of Jerusalem was heard far away (12:43). The wall had been rebuilt to make room for worship.

Zechariah 2:5 with Isaiah 26 and 60: God as the wall. In Zechariah 2, a young man sets out to measure Jerusalem, and the angel stops him. The restored city will spread out like towns without walls because of its many people (2:4). The Lord gives the reason: "For I,' says the LORD, 'will be a wall of fire all around her, and I will be the glory in her midst'" (2:5). A stone wall limits a city's size, while a wall of fire made by God's own presence sets no such limit. The fire recalls the pillar that guarded Israel in the wilderness, and the glory in the city's midst is the Lord living among His people. Isaiah describes the same reality in song: "We have a strong city; God will appoint salvation for walls and bulwarks" (Isaiah 26:1). He adds, "You shall call your walls Salvation, and your gates Praise" (60:18). In each of these passages, God's saving presence takes the place of the stone.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Read across the whole Bible, the story of walls moves from human self-protection toward God's own presence. People build walls to feel permanent and safe. God brings down the ones raised against Him, lets His own city's walls fall when the people trust the stones instead of Him, and finally promises to be the wall Himself.

That promise points to Jesus Christ. Zechariah joins protection to presence: the wall of fire around the city and the glory in her midst. In Christ the glory of God came to live among His people (John 1:14), and those who belong to Him are kept by Him. Isaiah had already pictured outsiders, eunuchs and foreigners who held fast to the Lord, receiving a place and a name within God's walls (Isaiah 56:5). Paul announces what Christ has done at the deepest level of separation between peoples: "For He Himself is our peace, who has made both one, and has broken down the middle wall of separation" (Ephesians 2:14). Paul writes in Greek and uses a different word, but the connection is real. The same Lord who guards His people tears down the hostility that kept Jew and Gentile apart. The Bible closes with a holy city whose wall and gates are named for God's people, with the Lamb as its light (Revelation 21:12–23). There the promise "Salvation" for walls is fully realized.

7. Spirit & Life

Proverbs sets two walls side by side. The rich man imagines his wealth as a strong city and a high wall (Proverbs 18:11), and Proverbs says this exists only in his own estimation. The opposite danger is real: "Whoever has no rule over his own spirit is like a city broken down, without walls" (25:28). The protection people tend to trust, such as money, status and careful self-protection, cannot keep the soul safe. The protection they tend to neglect, a spirit governed by God, decides whether temptation and anger simply walk in.

The Song of Solomon gives that inner wall a quiet dignity. Her brothers weigh their young sister: "If she is a wall, we will build upon her a battlement of silver; and if she is a door, we will enclose her with boards of cedar" (Song of Songs 8:9). A door opens to anyone, while a wall holds firm. She answers, "I am a wall... Then I became in his eyes as one who found peace" (8:10). Her faithfulness did not make love cold. It gave love a secure place to rest.

God also builds walls out of people. He told a young and fearful Jeremiah, "I have made you this day a fortified city and an iron pillar, and bronze walls against the whole land" (Jeremiah 1:18). Jeremiah's strength was given to him, and God renewed the promise when the prophet was near despair (15:20). A believer standing for the truth today does not need to manufacture toughness. The Lord who made the sea stand up can make a trembling servant stand firm. When our own defenses lie in rubble, He tells His people that their walls are continually before Him (Isaiah 49:16).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Samuel 11:20–24 — Joab knew the rule of siege warfare: "Did you not know that they would shoot from the wall?" He used that known danger to arrange Uriah's death. The wall's lethal reach became a cover for David's sin.

  • 1 Samuel 31:10–12 — The Philistines fastened Saul's body to the wall of Beth Shan as a public display of Israel's defeat. The men of Jabesh Gilead risked a night journey to take it down and give him an honorable burial.

  • Psalm 51:18 — After confessing his own sin, David prays that God would build the walls of Jerusalem. He knew the city's security rested on God's mercy.

  • Isaiah 62:6 — The Lord sets watchmen on Jerusalem's walls who will never be silent, calling on Him day and night until He establishes the city.

  • Ezekiel 38:11 — In the last days Israel dwells safely, without walls, bars or gates. Gog sees this as weakness, but God Himself defends the people.
