OUTSIDE, STREET, OUTER - (chûwts, Strong's H2351)

Summary

The Hebrew word chûwts means outside. It names whatever lies beyond a boundary: outside a tent, a house, a veil, a city wall, or the camp of Israel. Because the outside of a town is its open public space, the word is often translated "street" or "streets." Occasionally it means "fields," the open country beyond settled land. The word itself is plain and physical. Its weight comes from what stands on the inside and why God drew the line where He did.

In the Law of Moses, the LORD dwelt at the center of Israel's camp, so the outside became the place of the unclean, the excluded, and the condemned. It was also where the body of the sin offering was carried and burned. Hebrews builds on that detail to explain the cross: Jesus "suffered outside the gate," and Hebrews calls believers to "go forth to Him, outside the camp, bearing His reproach" (Hebrews 13:12–13). In the Prophets and Proverbs, the streets are where Wisdom calls aloud, where prophets preach, and where judgment falls in full public view. Following this ordinary word through Scripture shows how seriously God guards His holiness, and how far Christ went to bring outsiders in.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chûwts belongs to the everyday world of tents, houses, walled towns, and a nation camped around the tabernacle. It answers a simple question: is this inside or outside? It runs from Genesis to Zechariah and gathers most heavily in three places. In Leviticus and Numbers it marks the edge of the camp. In Jeremiah and Lamentations it names the streets of Jerusalem. In Ezekiel 40–47 it measures the outer walls and courts of a visionary temple. The word carries no moral meaning in itself. The boundary gives it significance: the sanctuary, the camp, the house, or the city.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Outside, exterior. This is the area beyond a defined space, whether a tent, house, veil, sanctuary, camp, or city. For example, the table was set "outside the veil" (Exodus 26:35).

2. Street, public square. This is the open public area of a town, where people buy and sell, talk, and gather. For example, Wisdom "raises her voice in the open squares" after calling "outside" (Proverbs 1:20).

3. Open country, fields. This means land beyond settled areas. For example, God "sends waters on the fields" (Job 5:10).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In narrative, chûwts often marks the moment someone crosses a threshold. When Joseph fled from Potiphar's wife, he left his garment and ran outside (Genesis 39:12). Crossing a threshold often brings exposure. Ham "saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brothers outside" (Genesis 9:22). Ham carried a private shame out into the open, while his brothers went in backward and covered it (9:23). In Gibeah, the Levite brought his concubine out to the men of the city (Judges 19:25); the house that should have sheltered her did not.

The streets are also where messages are announced to everyone. David's grief over Saul and Jonathan begs, "Proclaim it not in the streets of Ashkelon" (2 Samuel 1:20), because the streets are where enemies would celebrate. God uses the same public space for His own word. "Wisdom calls aloud outside" (Proverbs 1:20). He tells Jeremiah to proclaim "in the streets of Jerusalem, saying: 'Hear the words of this covenant and do them'" (Jeremiah 11:6). Because the message is spoken in public, no one can plead that it was kept from them.

The streets also become the place where judgment can be seen. Jeremiah describes death cutting off children from outside (Jeremiah 9:21). In Lamentations, children faint at the head of every street (2:19), and "those who ate delicacies are desolate in the streets" (4:5). The poets push the image further and speak of the defeated as street mud. David says of his enemies, "I cast them out like dirt in the streets" (Psalm 18:42). Micah says the enemy who taunted, "Where is the LORD your God?" will "be trampled down like mud in the streets" (Micah 7:10). Zechariah says God's restored people will "tread down their enemies in the mire of the streets" (Zechariah 10:5).

4. Theological Significance

The central truth this word carries is that a holy God chose to live among His people, and His presence required boundaries. A man with leprosy lived alone outside the camp (Leviticus 13:46). Moses was told to put every defiled person outside so that they would not defile the camps where God dwelt (Numbers 5:3–4). The blasphemer and the Sabbath-breaker were taken outside the camp to be stoned (Leviticus 24:14; Numbers 15:35). Being outside meant being cut off from the place where God lived. These boundaries existed because God was really present. An empty tent would need no such lines.

The same word draws lines inside the tabernacle itself. The table and lampstand stood "outside the veil," and Aaron tended the lamp "outside the veil which is before the Testimony" (Exodus 26:35; 27:21). Even the priests, who served daily, worked one step removed from the Most Holy Place. Inside God's house, nearness to Him came in stages.

Ezekiel's temple vision returns to this concern after the disaster of the exile. Ezekiel had seen the sword outside the city and famine within it (Ezekiel 7:15). Then the vision measures outer walls, outer courts, and outer gates with great care. Even the sin offering is placed carefully: "burn it in the appointed place of the temple, outside the sanctuary" (Ezekiel 43:21). God meant to dwell among His people again, and the defilement that had ruined Jerusalem would not be allowed to cross into His house.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The sin offering carried outside (Exodus 29:14; Leviticus 4:12, 21; 16:27). When a sin offering was made for the priest or for the whole congregation, the blood went inward toward the sanctuary. The body of the bull went the opposite way. It was carried outside the camp to a clean place where the ashes were poured out, and there it was burned. The same pattern appears at the ordination of the priests and again on the Day of Atonement. On that day the blood of the bull and the goat was brought into the Holy Place, and their bodies were taken outside and burned (Leviticus 16:27). The detail is easy to miss. The fat of the sin offering was burned on the altar (Leviticus 4:8–10; Exodus 29:13), but the rest of the animal, its hide, flesh, head, legs, entrails, and offal, was not burned there or eaten by the priests (Leviticus 4:11–12; Exodus 29:14). That body was removed from the place where God dwelt. In this way the sacrifice acted out the removal of sin from His presence.

Inside the house marked by blood (Exodus 12:46; Joshua 2:19). On Passover night the lamb was eaten in one house, and none of its flesh was to be carried outside. Israel was told that no one should go out of the door until morning, because the destroyer would pass through the land (Exodus 12:22–23). On that night, staying inside meant life. Jericho followed the same pattern. The spies told Rahab that anyone who went outside the doors of her house into the street would have his blood on his own head (Joshua 2:19). The scarlet cord marked her house much as the Passover blood had marked Israel's doors. In both stories, the threshold separated those under God's provision from those exposed to His judgment.

Brought outside by God (Genesis 15:5; 19:16–17). In two stories the movement runs the other way, and God leads someone out. Abram was childless and troubled about it (15:2–3), and the LORD brought him outside and told him to count the stars. Abram believed the LORD there under the open sky (Genesis 15:6). Lot, by contrast, lingered in a doomed city, so the angels took him by the hand and "set him outside the city" (19:16). One man was led out to see a promise, and the other was pulled out to escape fire. In both cases God would not leave His servant in a space that was too small or too compromised.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hebrews connects the cross to the sin offering in plain terms: "For the bodies of those animals, whose blood is brought into the sanctuary by the high priest for sin, are burned outside the camp. Therefore Jesus also, that He might sanctify the people with His own blood, suffered outside the gate" (Hebrews 13:11–12). Hebrews was written in Greek, but it deliberately draws on the Levitical "outside." Jesus did not die in the temple courts. He died beyond the city wall, in the place reserved for what had to be removed. Jesus was condemned on a charge of blasphemy and taken outside the city, much as the blasphemer of Leviticus 24 was taken outside the camp. Paul's words explain what happened there: "He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Jesus took the place of the leper, the condemned man, and the burned offering, so that those who had been outsiders could be made holy.

An earlier scene points in the same direction. After the golden calf, Moses pitched the tabernacle of meeting outside the camp, and everyone who sought the LORD went out to it (Exodus 33:7). Hebrews issues a similar call: "Therefore let us go forth to Him, outside the camp, bearing His reproach" (Hebrews 13:13). The story ends with a final boundary. Of the New Jerusalem, Revelation says, "But outside are dogs and sorcerers and sexually immoral and murderers and idolaters, and whoever loves and practices a lie" (Revelation 22:15). Christ went outside so that no one who trusts Him would have to stay there.

7. Spirit & Life

Hebrews 13 makes discipleship a matter of location. Believers are not told to secure a respected place inside the world's religious camp. They are called to go where Jesus is, and He is outside, bearing reproach. The cross itself took place in the open, in the most public kind of shame, and following Christ means accepting some share of that disgrace. Yet the Passover house remains true as well. The person who goes out to Christ is sheltered under His blood. Such a believer has nothing to fear from the judgment that falls in the streets.

The word also shows that God speaks in the open. Wisdom does not whisper only to insiders; she calls in the squares where the careless pass by. God still sets His truth before ordinary people in ordinary places. Like Abram, a believer may need to be led out of a cramped tent of disappointment to look again at what God has promised.

The Song of Songs adds a longing worth noticing: "If I should find you outside, I would kiss you; I would not be despised" (Song of Songs 8:1). The bride wants her love to be open, without scorn. Applying her words to Christ is an analogy rather than the passage's direct teaching. Even so, her words express something every believer knows: the desire to love the Lord openly, in the street as well as in private, and one day to do so without reproach.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 14:40–41 – Stones infected with disease were pulled from a house and thrown outside the city into an unclean place. The law applied the inside-and-outside line to ordinary homes.

  • Deuteronomy 23:12–14 – Israel was to keep a place outside the camp for relieving themselves, because the LORD walked in the midst of the camp. God's presence even governed basic matters of daily life.

  • Job 31:32 – Job says no sojourner had to lodge in the street, because he opened his doors to travelers. Godly hospitality brings the outsider inside.

  • Isaiah 42:2 – The LORD's Servant would not cry out or make His voice heard in the street. Matthew 12:19 applies this description to Jesus, whose authority came without noisy self-promotion.

  • Nehemiah 13:8 – Nehemiah threw Tobiah's household goods out of the temple room, cleansing God's house of a compromise that should never have been allowed inside.

  • 2 Kings 4:3 – Elisha told the widow to borrow empty vessels from everywhere, from all her neighbors. The Hebrew expression means from outside her household. The supply of oil then lasted until the last borrowed vessel was full.
