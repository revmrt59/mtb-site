HOLE, CAVE, SOCKET - (chôwr, Strong's H2356)

Summary

The Hebrew word chôwr means a hole: a hollow or opening in something solid. The word occurs 7 times in 7 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and in each place the setting tells you what kind of hole it is. It can be a cave in the earth, a hole bored in a wooden lid, a breach in a wall, the opening in a door, or the hollow where an eye sits in the skull. The word itself is simple. What makes it worth studying is what happens at these openings.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

At a hole in the temple wall, God shows Ezekiel the idolatry Israel's elders thought no one could see. At the opening of a door in the Song of Solomon, the beloved reaches toward the woman he loves and wakes her heart. Frightened Hebrews hide in holes in the ground, and the lion of Assyria fills its caves with prey; God sees both the fearful and the violent. Across these passages a pattern appears. Walls, doors, and caves stand between people and the Lord, yet He is not kept out. He enters through the opening to expose sin, to judge violence, and to draw His people into fellowship with Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chôwr is a concrete Hebrew noun for a cavity or opening. It belongs to physical description: a hollow in rock, a hole cut into wood, a gap in masonry, a space in the body. It appears in historical narrative (1 Samuel, 2 Kings), in poetry (Job, Song of Solomon), and in prophecy (Ezekiel, Nahum, Zechariah). Because the word is so ordinary, English translations render it by whatever fits the scene. The NKJV has "holes," "hole," "caves," "latch," and "sockets."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chôwr means a hole or hollow space. Three uses can be distinguished:

1. A natural hollow in the ground or rock, a cave or den, as when the Philistines mock the Hebrews for "coming out of the holes where they have hidden" (1 Samuel 14:11).

2. An opening made in a manufactured object, such as the hole Jehoiada bored in a chest lid (2 Kings 12:9) or the opening in a door (Song of Solomon 5:4).

3. A hollow in the body, the eye sockets of Zechariah 14:12.

The word does not carry ideas of secrecy, intimacy, or judgment in itself. Those ideas come from the passages where it appears.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the hole is in the earth, it is usually a refuge. Frightened soldiers hide in such holes, and outcasts are forced to live in them (Job 30:6). A predator stores its kill in one (Nahum 2:12).

When the hole is in something people built, it becomes a point of passage. Money passes through the chest lid in 2 Kings 12. A hand reaches toward the door's opening in the Song of Solomon. In Ezekiel's vision the prophet sees a hole in the wall, digs, finds a door, and goes in (Ezekiel 8:7–10).

In Zechariah the hollow belongs to the human body. The context there is judgment, and the empty socket marks what remains after the eye has dissolved.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show the same truth in different settings: the barriers people rely on do not stand between them and the Lord. A wall does not hide the elders from Him. Caves do not shelter Assyria from Him. A closed door does not keep the beloved from making his presence known. Hebrews 4:13 states the principle plainly: "there is no creature hidden from His sight, but all things are naked and open to the eyes of Him to whom we must give account."

The holes of the earth gather very different people. Some hide in them out of fear (1 Samuel 14:11; compare the scene in 13:6). Others are driven into them as outcasts (Job 30:5–6), and Hebrews honors faithful sufferers who wandered "in dens and caves of the earth" (Hebrews 11:38). Living in a cave is not itself a sign of unbelief. The violent, however, use their dens to store plunder, and their confidence rests in what they have taken (Nahum 2:12). Against every refuge of that kind the Psalms set a different one before God's people: "The LORD is my rock and my fortress and my deliverer" (Psalm 18:2). A hole in the rock offers concealment. God offers deliverance. At the end of the age, Revelation shows the proud and powerful hiding in the caves and begging the mountains and rocks to "hide us from the face of Him who sits on the throne and from the wrath of the Lamb!" (Revelation 6:15–16). On that day the caves of the earth will not protect anyone.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Ezekiel 8:7–12: The hole that exposed the dark room. Ezekiel is carried in a vision to the temple. "When I looked, there was a hole in the wall" (Ezekiel 8:7). God tells him, "Son of man, dig into the wall," and the digging uncovers a door (v. 8). Inside, Ezekiel sees creeping things, abominable beasts, and "all the idols of the house of Israel, portrayed all around on the walls" (v. 10). Seventy elders stand there offering incense, and among them is Jaazaniah the son of Shaphan, possibly from a family otherwise known for faithful service. The elders' excuse explains why they felt hidden there: "The LORD does not see us, the LORD has forsaken the land" (v. 12). They worshiped behind a wall because they believed God was absent. A single hole was enough to prove them wrong. The worship they hid in God's own house was fully visible to Him all along.

Song of Solomon 5:2–6: The hand at the opening of the door. The beloved knocks at night: "Open for me, my sister, my love" (v. 2). She hesitates. She has already undressed and washed her feet (v. 3). Then "my beloved put his hand by the latch of the door, and my heart yearned for him" (v. 4). The NKJV's "latch" translates chôwr, the opening in the door through which a hand could reach toward the bolt. He does not force the door. His hand at that small opening is enough to move her. When she rises, her fingers drip with myrrh on the handles of the lock (v. 5). But "my beloved had turned away and was gone. … I sought him, but I could not find him" (v. 6). Delayed love becomes searching love. Christians have long read the Song as a picture of Christ and His people, and that reading is an analogy rather than the poem's stated meaning. The scene is nonetheless a true portrait of how love approaches: gently, at the threshold, waiting to be welcomed.

Nahum 2:12–13: The lion's caves. Nahum describes Assyria as a lion that "filled his caves with prey, and his dens with flesh" (Nahum 2:12). For generations Nineveh had grown rich on plundered nations, and its dens looked like proof of security. In the next verse the Lord declares that He is against this lion and will cut off its prey from the earth (v. 13, paraphrased). The caves that held the empire's spoils would not protect it from the God who saw how the spoils had been taken.

Zechariah 14:12: The empty sockets. Zechariah foresees a day when the Lord Himself fights for Jerusalem and His feet stand on the Mount of Olives (Zechariah 14:3–4, paraphrased). Against those who attacked the city, He sends a plague: "Their eyes shall dissolve in their sockets." These are the nations gathered to battle against Jerusalem (14:2). Now their own bodies give way, and the eyes that looked on God's city with hostility are gone. The picture is horrifying, and it is meant to be. Jesus called the eye "the lamp of the body" (Matthew 6:22). Those who set themselves against the Lord's King are left with nothing but empty hollows.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pattern of these openings points toward Christ. In Ezekiel, a hole reveals sin hidden behind a temple wall. In Christ, God's searching sight comes in person. Jesus "knew what was in man" (John 2:25), and He exposed hidden hypocrisy in the temple itself. In the Song, the beloved stands at the door and puts his hand by the latch. Revelation 3:20 reads much like that scene in its own words: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me." John does not quote the Song, but the resemblance is striking. The Lord who could break down any door waits to be welcomed.

Zechariah's day of the Lord standing on the Mount of Olives looks ahead to the final victory of God's King over every nation that opposes Him. The larger story also explains why no one must flee to the caves when that day comes. Hebrews speaks of a "new and living way" opened "through the veil, that is, His flesh" (Hebrews 10:20). The great barrier between God and humanity was not breached by anyone's digging. Christ opened it through His own body, wounded for us. Those who hide in Him do not need to beg the rocks to cover them from the Lamb.

7. Spirit & Life

Ezekiel's vision asks every believer an honest question: what room of idols have I walled off, assuming the Lord does not see? The elders' belief that God had forsaken the land led them into secret worship. Hidden sin often begins with the quiet conclusion that God is distant. For a believer, God's exposure of such a hidden room is a mercy, because it brings the matter into the light while repentance is still possible.

The Song of Solomon brings a gentler word. The Lord often approaches His people quietly, through a knock, a stirring of the heart, a hand at the door. The bride's mistake was not that she lacked love. Her own words suggest that she delayed because she was comfortable (Song of Solomon 5:3). Her yearning heart and her lost moment teach believers to answer His nearness when it comes, without waiting until their circumstances are convenient.

Even the plainest use carries wisdom. Jehoiada's bored lid turned years of neglected repairs into a reliable way to pay for the work on God's house (2 Kings 12:6–11). Devotion to the Lord is well served by faithful, open handling of what His people give. Jesus Himself sat opposite the temple treasury and watched how people gave (Mark 12:41, paraphrased).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

1 Samuel 14:11: The Philistines mock, "Look, the Hebrews are coming out of the holes where they have hidden." Their sneer is turned upside down when the Lord gives Israel victory that day through Jonathan's daring faith.

Job 30:6: Job describes outcasts who "had to live in the clefts of the valleys, in caves of the earth and the rocks." Job is now mocked by the sons of such despised men, which shows how far he has fallen.

2 Kings 12:9: Jehoiada "bored a hole in its lid, and set it beside the altar." After years in which the priests had not repaired the temple (2 Kings 12:6, paraphrased), this simple chest made steady giving and faithful repair possible.
