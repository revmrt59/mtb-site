SIGNET, SEAL - (chôwthâm, Strong's H2368)

Summary

The Hebrew word chôwthâm means a signet or seal. A signet was a small engraved stone or metal object, worn as a ring or on a cord around the neck. Its owner pressed it into soft clay or wax to leave his own distinctive mark. Because the impression was a distinctive, recognized mark of that one carving, the signet stood for the person himself. It proved who he was, guaranteed his word, and carried his authority. When Judah handed his signet to Tamar, he handed over his own identity. When Jezebel sealed letters with Ahab's signet, Naboth died as surely as if the king had spoken the order himself.

Scripture builds on this ordinary object to say remarkable things about God and His people. The names of Israel's tribes were cut into the high priest's stones "like the engravings of a signet," so that he carried the people on his shoulders and over his heart before the LORD. God warned King Coniah that even if he were the signet on God's right hand, God would pull him off. Later He told Zerubbabel, of the same royal line, that He would make him like a signet ring, "for I have chosen you" (Haggai 2:23). The bride in the Song of Solomon pleads, "Set me as a seal upon your heart." Read together, these pictures point toward Jesus Christ, who carries His people permanently before the Father and holds the authority God's signet represents. The pictures also prepare us to understand what it means that believers are sealed with the Holy Spirit.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, a seal served as a person's signature. Some seals were rings. Others were cylinders worn on a cord around the neck and rolled across clay to leave a continuous design. Genesis 38:18 mentions Judah's "signet and cord," which fits the cord-worn kind. A craftsman carved the design into hard stone or metal with great care. Clay jars, letters, and legal documents were then stamped with it to show who owned them or who authorized them.

Two features of the object matter most for reading Scripture. The carving was permanent, so "like the engravings of a signet" became a natural way to describe skilled, lasting engraving. The impression was also distinctive and recognized as his, so the seal stood for its owner's identity and authority. The word is related to the Hebrew verb meaning to seal or to shut up.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chôwthâm names the engraved object itself, the signet or seal. Context draws out three closely related uses:

1. A personal seal that identifies its owner and acts in his name. Judah's signet works as a pledge in Genesis 38:18. Ahab's seal gives Jezebel's letters royal force in 1 Kings 21:8.

2. A standard for permanent engraving. The phrase "like the engravings of a signet" in Exodus 28:11 describes how the names of Israel's sons were cut into stone.

3. A figure of speech for something stamped, claimed, or closed tight. Clay takes form "under a seal" (Job 38:14). Leviathan's scales are shut "as with a seal" (Job 41:15). The bride asks to be a seal on her beloved (Song 8:6). God's chosen servant is His signet (Haggai 2:23).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The narrative uses establish what a signet could do. In Genesis 38:18, Tamar asks Judah for "your signet and cord, and your staff that is in your hand" as a pledge. Later, facing execution, she produces these items and asks him to identify the man they belong to (38:25). Judah acknowledges them and admits, "She has been more righteous than I" (38:26). The seal spoke for him even when it exposed him.

First Kings 21:8 shows the same power used for evil: Jezebel "wrote letters in Ahab's name, sealed them with his seal." The elders and nobles of Naboth's city did as the letters said, and an innocent man was stoned (21:11–13). A signet does not check the character of the hand that presses it. In the wrong hand, delegated authority becomes a weapon.

The poetic and prophetic uses turn the object into a metaphor. In the Song of Solomon, the image is applied to a person. In Jeremiah and Haggai, God speaks of a person as His signet, and the image carries His own authority.

4. Theological Significance

The signet passages teach that God marks and claims what belongs to Him, and that His mark rests on His own faithfulness. Israel's names were not painted on the priest's garments. They were engraved, as a signet is engraved. God's choice of Zerubbabel is described as making him like a signet ring. God's purposes for His people are not left loose or provisional; He sets His mark on them.

Job 38:14 adds a striking picture of God's power over creation. Speaking from the whirlwind, God describes the dawn spreading over the earth: "It takes on form like clay under a seal, And stands out like a garment." In darkness the landscape is shapeless, like a blank lump of clay. When light comes at God's command, hills and valleys stand out sharply, as an impression appears when the seal is pressed. The earth does not shape itself. It receives the form God gives it. This is the God Job must reckon with: He defines reality the way a seal defines clay.

The signet also teaches something sobering about authority. Ahab's seal in Jezebel's hand brought murder. God's signet is different because the hand that bears it is holy. The question is always whose authority is being pressed into the world, and to what end.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The high priest's engraved names (Exodus 28 and 39). Six occurrences, in six verses of Exodus (28:11, 21, 36; 39:6, 14, 30), describe the high priest's garments. God commanded, "With the work of an engraver in stone, like the engravings of a signet, you shall engrave the two stones with the names of the sons of Israel" (Exodus 28:11). These two onyx stones sat on the shoulders of the ephod. Twelve more stones on the breastplate each bore one tribe's name, engraved the same way (28:21). Aaron carried the names on his shoulders as a memorial before the LORD (28:12), and on the breastplate over his heart whenever he entered the holy place (28:29).

The details fit together. The shoulders are where a burden is carried. The heart is the seat of affection. Every tribe was carried by his strength and held close to his heart, and no tribe was left off or merely written in ink. The same signet-style engraving appeared on the gold plate on his turban, which declared holiness to the LORD (28:36). That plate was there so the people's offerings would be accepted before God (28:38). The people's names and God's holiness were engraved in the same lasting way on the same man. Israel came near to God through a mediator who carried both.

The signet removed and restored (Jeremiah 22:24; Haggai 2:23). Jeremiah delivers a crushing word to David's line: "'As I live,' says the LORD, 'though Coniah the son of Jehoiakim, king of Judah, were the signet on My right hand, yet I would pluck you off.'" A signet on the right hand was among the most personal and authoritative things a king wore. God says that even if Coniah were that close to Him, He would tear him away. Coniah (also called Jehoiachin) was carried captive to Babylon (2 Kings 24:15). His uncle Zedekiah still reigned eleven years in Jerusalem (2 Kings 24:17–18), but the exile that followed ended the reigning line of David's kings.

Decades later, after the return from exile, Haggai speaks to Zerubbabel, Coniah's grandson, who led the returned exiles: "I will take you, Zerubbabel My servant, the son of Shealtiel... and will make you like a signet ring; for I have chosen you." This is the same word, now spoken in grace. Zerubbabel never sat on a throne; he served as "governor of Judah" (Haggai 1:1). Yet God publicly marked David's line as His chosen instrument again. Matthew's genealogy names both Jeconiah (Coniah) and Zerubbabel in the line that leads to Jesus (Matthew 1:12). The signet God pulled off in judgment, He placed again in mercy, and His promise to David was not lost in exile.

A seal upon the heart (Song of Solomon 8:6). The bride says, "Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm; For love is as strong as death." A seal was worn close, on a cord over the chest or on the hand. It also declared ownership. The bride asks to be that near and that fully claimed. She wants a place in his affections and in the strength of his arm, and she wants it to last. Her reason is the strength of love itself, which holds on like death and burns like "a most vehement flame." She is not asking for a passing feeling but for a belonging that will not let go.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

These lines of Scripture meet in Jesus. The writer of Hebrews presents Him as the High Priest who continues forever and always lives to intercede for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:24–25). Read alongside Hebrews, Aaron's engraved stones picture that ministry. Aaron eventually died and handed on the garments. Christ carries His people before the Father without interruption, with their names held in His strength and His heart, and with His own holiness covering them.

The Coniah–Zerubbabel story moves the same way. God's signet was removed in judgment and restored by His choice. That signet represented God's authority entrusted to a son of David, and it finds its full holder in the Son of David who said all authority in heaven and on earth had been given to Him (Matthew 28:18). Haggai does not name Jesus. Still, the promise to Zerubbabel keeps alive the hope that Christ fulfills, and in Christ the signet finally rests on a hand that will never misuse it.

The New Testament also speaks of believers as sealed. It uses a Greek word, so these verses are not uses of chôwthâm, but they draw on the same picture: "having believed, you were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise" (Ephesians 1:13). God "has sealed us and given us the Spirit in our hearts as a guarantee" (2 Corinthians 1:22). Much of what the signet meant in the Old Testament carries over here. The seal identifies the owner, the pledge guarantees what was promised, and the mark rests on the faithfulness of the God who set it.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride's prayer and the priest's breastplate belong together. In Exodus, God commanded that His people be engraved over the priest's heart before they ever asked for it. In the Song, the bride longs to be set as a seal upon her beloved's heart. Grace works in that order. Christ carries us first, and then a heart touched by His love wants nothing less than to be fully His. When believers pray to be held closer, they are asking for what God already set out to give.

Job's two pictures offer a quiet contrast. The dawn presses form into the earth "like clay under a seal," while Leviathan's scales are his pride, "shut up tightly as with a seal." The first is open, receiving the shape God gives. The second is sealed against everything outside itself. Paul says the God "who commanded light to shine out of darkness" has shone in our hearts "to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). The comparison is an illustration rather than the direct teaching of Job, but it is a searching one. A heart can lie open like clay to receive Christ's likeness, or it can harden itself the way Leviathan's scales are closed by pride.

Jezebel's misuse of Ahab's seal is a warning to anyone who acts in Jesus' name. Carrying a name is a trust. The seal of the Spirit marks believers as belonging to a holy King, and what we do under that seal should look like Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 28:21 — Each of the twelve breastplate stones received one tribe's name "like the engravings of a signet," so every tribe was represented individually.

  • Exodus 39:6, 14 — The craftsmen carried out the command exactly, engraving the onyx and breastplate stones as signets are engraved. God's pattern was followed, not adjusted.

  • Exodus 39:30 — The holy gold plate for the turban was engraved like a signet, completing the priest's garments as the LORD commanded Moses.

  • Job 41:15 — Leviathan's scales are "shut up tightly as with a seal," a picture of a defense closed so tightly that nothing can get through.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H2856 (chatham), the Hebrew verb to seal or to shut up, which belongs to the same word family as chôwthâm.

  • Thematic connection: The Greek sealing language of Ephesians 1:13 and 2 Corinthians 1:22, where believers are sealed with the Holy Spirit. This is a different word in a different language, but it uses the same image of an identifying, guaranteeing mark.
