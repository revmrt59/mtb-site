SEE, BEHOLD, ENVISION - (châzâh, Strong's H2372)

Summary

The Hebrew verb châzâh means to see, look at, or behold. Most often it describes careful, attentive seeing: watching something closely enough to understand it. Scripture uses it for ordinary observation, such as noticing a skilled worker or looking over a neglected field. It is also the word for what the prophets "saw" when the Lord gave them His message, and for the false visions of prophets who spoke from their own hearts.

The word's richest use is for seeing God Himself. David's one desire was "to behold the beauty of the LORD" (Psalm 27:4). Job, in the middle of his suffering, insisted that in his own flesh he would see God. The same verb also turns the other way: the Lord's eyes behold and test every person, and His face looks with favor on the upright. Châzâh gathers up the hope of Israel's worship and the hope of the believer, that the God who sees us will one day be seen by us, and we will be satisfied.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châzâh is a Hebrew verb for seeing. It is less common than the everyday Hebrew verb for seeing (ra'ah). Most of its uses come in poetry and prophecy: Job, Psalms, Isaiah, Ezekiel, and the opening lines of several prophetic books. Two well-known nouns belong to the same word family: the "seer" (chozeh) and the "vision" (chazon). Because the word so often appears where people are contemplating God, weighing what they see, or receiving revelation, it tends to describe deliberate, focused seeing rather than a passing glance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To see or look at with attention: to observe, consider, or look for. Proverbs 22:29 asks, "Do you see a man who excels in his work?" In Exodus 18:21 the NKJV translates the word as "select," because Moses must look for and choose men "such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness."

2. To see something revealed by God. This is the prophet's seeing of the word or message of the Lord (Amos 1:1; Micah 1:1). The same sense describes false prophets who "envisioned" what God never showed them (Ezekiel 13:6).

3. To behold God, His face, His beauty, or His works, as an act of worship and hope (Psalm 27:4; Job 19:26).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb makes a large difference. When people see a worker or a field, the word is plain observation. When a prophet is the subject, it becomes the language of revelation. Isaiah 1:1, 2:1 and 13:1, Amos 1:1, Micah 1:1 and Habakkuk 1:1 all introduce a message the prophet "saw." Amos 1:1 speaks of "the words of Amos... which he saw." This joining of hearing and seeing is striking. God's word did not reach the prophets as bare information. They perceived it as a reality from God, weighty and near, before they ever spoke it. Balaam describes himself in the same way, as one who hears God's words and "sees the vision of the Almighty" (Numbers 24:4, 16).

When God is the subject, the word describes His searching, evaluating gaze (Psalm 11:4). When the people of God are the subject and God is the object, it becomes longing, worship, and hope.

4. Theological Significance

God means to be seen, and He means His works to be seen. In Exodus 24:11 the elders of Israel went up the mountain at Sinai, saw God, and ate and drank in His presence without being struck down. That meal shows that the holy God was willing to receive His people into His presence and share fellowship with them.

God also calls His people to watch His actions in history: "Come, behold the works of the LORD, Who has made desolations in the earth" (Psalm 46:8). Human events are not a random sequence. They are the setting where God acts, and faith opens its eyes to see His hand. That seeing brings comfort to the righteous, who "shall rejoice when he sees the vengeance" (Psalm 58:10), and shame to the stubborn. Isaiah 26:11 says of the wicked, "LORD, when Your hand is lifted up, they will not see. But they will see and be ashamed." Refusing to see God at work does not keep anyone from eventually facing what He has done.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalms 27:4; 63:2; 17:15: Beholding now and seeing at last. David names "one thing" he wants above everything else: to dwell in the Lord's house "To behold the beauty of the LORD, And to inquire in His temple." What David wants most is God Himself, not something God might give him. Psalm 63:2 shows that same longing in practice: "So I have looked for You in the sanctuary, To see Your power and Your glory." In worship, David has already caught sight of God's glory.

Psalm 17:15 carries that longing forward to its end: "As for me, I will see Your face in righteousness; I shall be satisfied when I awake in Your likeness." What David glimpses in the sanctuary becomes, in this verse, a face-to-face seeing. The verse joins seeing God with being satisfied and being made like Him. Isaiah 33:17 gives the same hope to the righteous: their eyes will see the King in His beauty.

Job 19:26–27: "Whom I shall see for myself." Job has lost his children, wealth, health, and the sympathy of his friends. Out of that loss he says: "That in my flesh I shall see God, Whom I shall see for myself, And my eyes shall behold, and not another. How my heart yearns within me!" Châzâh appears twice. Job's hope is bodily ("in my flesh"), personal ("for myself"), and direct ("not another"). He does not expect to have his flocks restored before he can rest. What he holds onto is the sight of God, his Redeemer (Job 19:25). That is the final answer to suffering Job could not explain, and it is why his heart yearns.

Psalm 11:4, 7: The gaze that tests and the face that welcomes. God is also the one who sees. "His eyes behold, His eyelids test the sons of men." The image is of narrowed eyes examining closely, and nothing escapes that look. Yet the psalm ends: "For the LORD is righteous, He loves righteousness; His countenance beholds the upright." The same seeing that exposes the wicked rests with favor on the upright. Being seen by God is fearful for those who are hiding, and it is a delight for those who walk with Him.

Ezekiel 13:6–8, 23; Lamentations 2:14; Isaiah 30:10: Seeing that God never gave. Ezekiel condemns prophets who "envisioned futility" and spoke what they had never received from the Lord. Lamentations 2:14 states the cost: "Your prophets have seen for you False and deceptive visions; They have not uncovered your iniquity." Their visions were comfortable, and so they left sin hidden and captivity unbroken. Isaiah 30:10 shows that the people wanted it that way: "Do not prophesy to us right things; Speak to us smooth things, prophesy deceits." Both verbs translated "prophesy" there are châzâh. People hunger for spiritual sight, but a heart unwilling to be corrected will pay for invented visions. Zechariah 10:2 likewise speaks of diviners who "envision" lies. True sight from God always exposes sin and calls people to return to Him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament longing to see God reaches its answer in Jesus Christ. The elders who ate in God's presence at Sinai, David seeking God's beauty in the temple, and Job's insistence that he would see his Redeemer all wait for the One who said, "He who has seen Me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). Jesus is the face of God turned toward us.

Isaiah's promise that "your eyes will see the King in His beauty" (Isaiah 33:17) fits the King Jesus is. The hope of Psalm 17:15, awaking in God's likeness, is echoed in the New Testament: "we know that when He is revealed, we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is" (1 John 3:2). Psalm 17:15 does not name Christ, but its hope of seeing God's face and being made like Him finds its fullest answer in the return of Christ.

Job's hope of seeing God "in my flesh" anticipates the resurrection of the body that Christ's own resurrection guarantees. The prophets "saw" God's word in part, but in Christ the Word Himself became flesh and was seen (John 1:14).

7. Spirit & Life

What we look at with longing shows what we love. David's "one thing" exposes how scattered most of our desires are. He sought God in the sanctuary because he expected to see something of God's power and glory there. Worship, Scripture, and prayer are the places where believers still look for Him. Psalm 17:15 tells us how that looking ends: in satisfaction and likeness. Paul describes believers beholding the Lord's glory and being transformed into the same image (2 Corinthians 3:18). Even now, the people who behold God most attentively are the ones being most changed.

Job teaches suffering believers where to set their hope. Comfort may or may not return in this life. The promise that does not fail is that we will see God for ourselves.

Psalm 11 frees us to live honestly before God's gaze. His eyes test us, and we cannot manage what He sees. For the person who walks uprightly through Christ, though, His look is welcome. That frees us from craving the "smooth things" of Isaiah 30:10. A heart secure in God's favor can bear the truth that uncovers sin, and it will not want visions that flatter.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 22:29 — "Do you see a man who excels in his work? He will stand before kings." Wisdom invites careful observation of how diligence is rewarded.

  • Proverbs 24:32 — The wise man looks at the overgrown field of the lazy man, considers it, and learns from what he saw.

  • Job 23:9 — Job cannot find God to behold Him, whether he turns left or right. This is the honest darkness behind his hope in chapter 19.

  • Ezekiel 12:27 — Israel dismisses Ezekiel's vision as being for distant times. God's word does not wait for people to find it convenient.

  • Micah 4:11 — The nations want to "look" upon Zion's ruin, but they do not know the Lord's plans.

  • Song of Solomon 6:13 — The call to "look upon" the Shulamite uses the same verb for an admiring, attentive gaze.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2377 (chazon), "vision" — A noun from the same word family. It names the revelation the prophet "saw" (Isaiah 1:1).

  • H2374 (chozeh), "seer" — A noun from the same word family. It names the person who receives God's visions (Isaiah 30:10).

  • H7200 (ra'ah), "to see" — A distinct and more common Hebrew verb for seeing. It is not a lexical relative, but it is often used alongside châzâh (Job 19:27; Isaiah 30:10).
