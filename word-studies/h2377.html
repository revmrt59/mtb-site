Summary

Chāzôn is a Hebrew word for a vision or revelation given by God. It can describe a prophetic encounter, the message communicated through it, or an authoritative prophetic word preserved for God's people. In 1 Samuel 3:1 revelation was rare; in Proverbs 29:18 its absence is connected with people casting off restraint. Biblical vision is therefore more than a striking image or an inspiring personal plan: it concerns God's own disclosure of His truth.

Habakkuk is told to write the vision and wait for its appointed fulfillment, while Jeremiah condemns prophets who offer visions drawn from their own hearts. Daniel's visions show that terrifying empires remain subject to God's purposes. The essential question is not how impressive a vision sounds, but whether it truly comes from God and calls His people to faithful trust and obedience.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chāzôn is a Hebrew noun related to the verb chāzâ, "to see" or "to perceive." The same family of words gives us an older title for a prophet, "seer." The NKJV usually translates chāzôn "vision." In two places, 1 Samuel 3:1 and Proverbs 29:18, it uses "revelation."

The word belongs mostly to the prophets. Daniel and Ezekiel use it most often, and it opens several prophetic books. It also appears in a few important places outside the prophetic books: the dark days before Samuel, a proverb about the life of God's people, and the accounts of God's promise to David. Readers should know that Daniel also uses other Hebrew words translated "vision." For that reason, not every English "vision" in Daniel 8–10 is chāzôn.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its heart, chāzôn means something revealed by God to a prophet. Two senses stand out.

1. A revelatory experience. This is a vision received, often with images and scenes the prophet sees and struggles to understand. "A vision appeared to me—to me, Daniel" (Daniel 8:1).

2. A revealed message or prophetic word. This is the content God makes known, whether it came by sight or by word, including a whole collection of prophecies. "The vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz, which he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem" (Isaiah 1:1) introduces sixty-six chapters, most of them spoken oracles.

The second sense explains why Nathan's message to David can be called "all this vision" (1 Chronicles 17:15). Nothing in that chapter suggests Nathan saw pictures. What made it a chāzôn was where it came from.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

As a book title. Isaiah, Obadiah, and Nahum all begin with this word. Nahum calls his work "the book of the vision" (Nahum 1:1). What God showed the prophet became a written scroll for later generations.

In apocalyptic visions. In Daniel 8–11, chāzôn describes the sweeping revelations about empires and the end of their power. These visions leave Daniel bewildered and needing an angel to explain them (Daniel 8:15–17).

As something God's people depend on. In 1 Samuel 3:1, Proverbs 29:18, and Lamentations 2:9, the word refers to God's active guidance for His people. Its absence is a crisis.

As something people counterfeit. Jeremiah and Ezekiel apply the word to prophets who claimed visions God never gave. The word itself stays the same. Scripture judges such a "vision" by its source.

As something people doubt. In Ezekiel 12:22–27, the people mock God's warnings with a saying that the days drag on and every vision comes to nothing. God answers that His word will no longer be delayed.

4. Theological Implications

The God of Israel speaks. Chāzôn exists because He chooses to show His servants what they could never discover on their own. Hosea 12:10 records the Lord saying that He spoke by the prophets and multiplied visions. He did not leave His people to guess at His will. He kept speaking, even to a people who refused to listen.

That speech holds a community together. The passages examined below in Section 5 show that when God's vision is absent, the people lose their footing. When His revealed word is honored, there is blessing. God's word gives His people life, and their spiritual health depends on hearing it.

Because the vision is His, God guards it jealously. Prophets who spoke their own dreams in His name drew some of the sharpest rebukes in the Old Testament. Behind those rebukes is a simple conviction: God's name must not be attached to human wishes, however comforting those wishes sound.

A true chāzôn also carries God's own reliability. The vision Habakkuk received "will not lie," and the visions given to Daniel reveal that the timeline of history is set in heaven, not by the empires that seem to control it. People mock the delay, as in Ezekiel 12, but God's word cannot be hurried and cannot fail.

The thread runs straight to Christ. Psalm 89:19 recalls the vision through Nathan: "Then You spoke in a vision to Your holy one, And said: 'I have given help to one who is mighty; I have exalted one chosen from the people.'" God's promise of an everlasting throne for David's line was given by revelation long before it was fulfilled. The angel's announcement that Jesus would receive "the throne of His father David" (Luke 1:32) is that same vision coming to pass. Habakkuk's vision likewise opens onto the gospel. The words that follow it, "the just shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4), become a foundation of Paul's preaching of righteousness through faith (Romans 1:17).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

When the vision is missing (1 Samuel 3:1; Proverbs 29:18; Lamentations 2:9). In Eli's day "the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation." The setting explains why. Eli's sons were corrupting the priesthood, and the nation had little word from God. Proverbs 29:18 states the principle: "Where there is no revelation, the people cast off restraint; But happy is he who keeps the law." The second line pairs revelation with God's law, which tells us what kind of vision is meant. Lamentations 2:9 shows the same loss as part of Jerusalem's ruin. Gates have sunk, "The Law is no more, And her prophets find no vision from the LORD." Among all the losses of the city's fall, the poet counts this silence from God as one of the most bitter.

The vision that waits (Habakkuk 2:2–3). Habakkuk had complained that God seemed to tolerate injustice. God answered: "Write the vision And make it plain on tablets, That he may run who reads it." Then He described the vision: it "is yet for an appointed time... it will not lie. Though it tarries, wait for it; Because it will surely come." From where Habakkuk stood, the promise looked slow. From God's side, it was already scheduled. The fact that it was written down meant it could be checked against what happened later, and it meant waiting people had something solid to hold.

Visions of the heart (Jeremiah 14:14; Ezekiel 13:16). The Lord tells Jeremiah, "I have not sent them, commanded them, nor spoken to them; they prophesy to you a false vision, divination, a worthless thing, and the deceit of their heart." Jeremiah 23:16 adds that such prophets speak a vision from their own heart rather than from the Lord's mouth. Ezekiel shows what those false visions sounded like: prophets "who see visions of peace for her when there is no peace." Counterfeit vision tends to tell people what they want to hear, and it asks nothing of them. The true word of God in those same years called for repentance.

Visions of empires (Daniel 8–9). Daniel's chāzôn in chapter 8 shows a ram and a goat, kingdoms rising and breaking, and a proud ruler who sets himself against God. The vision frightened Daniel, but its purpose was to steady him. The angel explains that the vision concerns the time of the end (Daniel 8:17), which means God already knows the end of every empire. In chapter 9 the seventy weeks are determined, among other purposes, to bring in everlasting righteousness and to seal up vision and prophecy (Daniel 9:24, paraphrased). God's exiled people could look at Babylon and Persia and know their days were numbered.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Obadiah 1:1 — "The vision of Obadiah. Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom." The title and the messenger formula stand side by side, so the "vision" is plainly God's spoken message.

Nahum 1:1 — "The book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite." The revelation is identified as a book.

Ezekiel 7:26 — In coming disaster, people will seek a vision from a prophet and not find one, a picture of guidance sought too late.

Daniel 1:17 — God gave Daniel understanding in all visions and dreams, so the ability to understand revelation is itself His gift.

Isaiah 29:7 — The nations that fight against Jerusalem will vanish like a dream, a vision of the night. Here the word describes how quickly they disappear.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Proverbs 29:18 is often quoted in the older wording, "where there is no vision, the people perish," to promote goal-setting or leadership plans. The verse is not about human foresight. Its chāzôn is God's revealed word, paired with "the law" in the next line. The NKJV's "revelation" helps here. The proverb warns a people who stop listening to God, and it promises happiness to the one who keeps what God has said. For believers today, that word comes to us in the Scriptures, which grew out of the very visions these prophets wrote down.
