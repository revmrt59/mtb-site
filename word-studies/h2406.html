WHEAT - (chiṭṭâh, Strong's H2406)

Summary

The Hebrew word chiṭṭâh means wheat, the most valued grain of ancient Israel. Scripture uses it for the growing crop, the threshed grain, and the flour baked into bread. Because wheat was the best of Israel's daily food, it became a natural measure of God's care. He promised to feed a listening people with "the finest of wheat" (Psalm 81:16). He required wheat flour for the bread that consecrated Israel's priests, and the Feast of Weeks celebrated "the firstfruits of wheat harvest" (Exodus 34:22). When His people turned from Him, Jeremiah described wheat sown and thorns reaped.

The "wheat harvest" also gives several important stories their setting. During the wheat harvest the ark returned to Israel, Ruth gleaned in Bethlehem, and Ornan kept threshing wheat on the ground that would become the site of the temple. The New Testament takes up the same image with its own Greek word for wheat. Jesus calls Himself the grain of wheat that must die to produce much grain, and He pictures His people as the wheat gathered into God's barn. The everyday grain of Israel's fields becomes a picture of the gospel.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun chiṭṭâh occurs 30 times in 30 verses of the Hebrew Bible, from Genesis to Joel. The NKJV renders it "wheat" every time. In Israel, wheat ripened in late spring and early summer, after barley, and the two grains are often named together (Ruth 2:23; Joel 1:11). Wheat made finer bread than barley and was worth more, so it served as both everyday food and a form of wealth.

The word appears on farms and threshing floors, at the altar, in royal trade agreements, and in the poetry of blessing and judgment. Threshing loosened the grain from the stalk, usually on an open, hard-packed floor, and winnowing then let the wind carry the chaff away. That ordinary process gives the setting for several key scenes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chiṭṭâh names a particular grain. The context shows whether the writer has the plant or its product in view.

1. Wheat as a growing crop, sown, struck by weather, or harvested. When hail struck Egypt, the barley was already in the head and was struck, but "the wheat and the spelt were not struck, for they are late crops" (Exodus 9:31–32). In Jeremiah 12:13 the people "have sown wheat but reaped thorns."

2. Wheat as harvested grain or flour, used for food, offerings, and trade. Examples include "wheat flour" for the priests' bread (Exodus 29:2) and the wheat Solomon paid to Hiram (1 Kings 5:11).

The word itself means wheat. Its associations with blessing, worship, or judgment come from the passages that use it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The phrase "wheat harvest" often dates an event. Reuben found mandrakes in that season (Genesis 30:14), and Samson visited his wife then (Judges 15:1). When Samuel called down thunder and rain during the wheat harvest, the unseasonable storm was a sign that Israel had done wrong in asking for a king (1 Samuel 12:17).

In worship, wheat supplied the bread of consecration and the firstfruits of the Feast of Weeks. In national life, it measured prosperity. Solomon paid Hiram with wheat for temple timber (1 Kings 5:11; 2 Chronicles 2:10, 15), and Jotham received wheat as tribute from the Ammonites (2 Chronicles 27:5). In poetry and prophecy, an abundance of wheat shows God's favor, and its failure shows His judgment. Job pictures that reversal when he says that if he has sinned, "let thistles grow instead of wheat" (Job 31:40).

4. Theological Significance

Wheat teaches that God gives His people bread, and He wants them to receive it as His gift. Moses describes the land as a land of wheat and barley (Deuteronomy 8:8). Deuteronomy 32:14 then lists God's gifts to the people, ending with "the choicest wheat." Psalm 147:14 joins two gifts: "He makes peace in your borders, And fills you with the finest wheat." The people's security and their full tables come from the same Lord.

Psalm 81:16 shows God's heart most clearly. God grieves that Israel would not listen, and He says what He had wanted to give them: "He would have fed them also with the finest of wheat; And with honey from the rock I would have satisfied you." This psalm shows that God was not looking for an excuse to give His people the bare minimum. He wanted to give them His best, and their refusal to listen was what stood in the way (Psalm 81:11–12).

Firstfruits taught Israel where that bread came from. At the Feast of Weeks, the first of the wheat harvest went to the Lord (Exodus 34:22), and the priests were consecrated with bread made of wheat flour (Exodus 29:2). Ezekiel's vision of the restored temple still requires a set portion of wheat (Ezekiel 45:13). In effect, bringing the first portion to God was a regular confession that He, and not the fertility gods of Canaan, made the fields grow.

The same God could withhold the wheat. Jeremiah 12:13 describes hard work that produces nothing: "They have put themselves to pain but do not profit... Because of the fierce anger of the LORD." In Joel 1:11 the farmers are told to be ashamed and the vinedressers to wail over the wheat and the barley, "because the harvest of the field has perished." Joel's chapter also says that grain offerings were cut off from the house of the LORD (Joel 1:9, 13). When the wheat failed, the people lost both their food and the offerings they brought to God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Judges 6:11 and 1 Chronicles 21:20–23: God meets people at the threshing floor. Gideon "threshed wheat in the winepress, in order to hide it from the Midianites." Threshing normally happened in the open wind, so the detail shows how far Midian's raids had driven Israel into hiding (6:2–6). Yet the Angel of the LORD came to Gideon at that hidden work and greeted him as a man of valor (6:12). God called a deliverer while Gideon was still hiding.

Ornan's story goes further. A plague was falling on Israel. Ornan turned and saw the angel, and his four sons who were with him hid themselves, "but Ornan continued threshing wheat" (1 Chronicles 21:20). David came to buy the floor for an altar. Ornan offered the oxen, the threshing implements for wood, and the wheat itself for the grain offering (21:23). David insisted on paying, built the altar, and the plague stopped. That threshing floor became the site of Solomon's temple (2 Chronicles 3:1). God showed mercy and chose His dwelling place in a working farmer's field, with wheat still on the floor.

1 Samuel 6:13 and Ruth 2:23: The wheat harvest as a time of restoration. "The people of Beth Shemesh were reaping their wheat harvest in the valley; and they lifted their eyes and saw the ark, and rejoiced to see it." God's presence came home from Philistine captivity while the people were bent over the harvest God had given. In Ruth, a widow from Moab gleaned "until the end of barley harvest and wheat harvest" in the fields of Bethlehem. Those weeks of gathering leftover grain led to Boaz's redemption of Ruth and Naomi, and through them to the line of David. In both stories, God carried out His purposes during the ordinary seasons He Himself provided.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament uses a different Greek word for wheat. Even so, it continues the same picture, and that picture reaches its fullest meaning in Christ.

Jesus describes His death with this grain: "unless a grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it produces much grain" (John 12:24). He spoke these words just after announcing, "The hour has come that the Son of Man should be glorified" (John 12:23). In this picture, the burial of seed in the earth explains why He had to die, and the harvest that follows explains what His death would produce. Paul uses the same picture for the resurrection of believers. We sow "mere grain—perhaps wheat," and "God gives it a body as He pleases" (1 Corinthians 15:37–38).

The Gospels also picture God's people as the wheat of the harvest. John the Baptist says the coming One "will thoroughly clean out His threshing floor, and gather His wheat into the barn; but He will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire" (Matthew 3:12). The threshing floor, which in Ornan's day became the place of an altar, becomes in John's preaching a picture of the final judgment. In the parable of the wheat and the tares, the owner says, "gather the wheat into my barn" (Matthew 13:30). That barn can be set beside the barns of the rich fool of Luke 12:18–21, who built bigger barns for himself and lost his soul that same night. Read together, the two passages show that the only safe storehouse belongs to God.

The Feast of Weeks, which celebrated the firstfruits of the wheat harvest, is the feast the New Testament calls Pentecost. On that day the Spirit was poured out and the first great harvest of believers was gathered (Acts 2:1, 41). Luke does not call this a fulfillment of the wheat offering, but the timing fits the pattern of the feast. Bethlehem, where Ruth gleaned wheat and where Christ was born, means house of bread in Hebrew. The One born there called Himself the bread of life (John 6:35).

7. Spirit & Life

Gideon and Ornan were both at work, one afraid and one steady, when God came to them. God is not waiting for His people to step out of ordinary life before He speaks to them. The floor where Ornan threshed became an altar, and the place where Gideon hid became the place he was called.

Psalm 81:16 also exposes a common fear, that God gives grudgingly. The God who said "I would have satisfied you" grieved over what Israel refused to receive. A believer can bring the firstfruits to God freely, because the Giver is generous and the offering confesses where everything came from.

When Jesus told Peter that Satan had asked "that he may sift you as wheat" (Luke 22:31), He also said that He had prayed for Peter's faith not to fail (Luke 22:32). Satan's sifting would shake Peter violently, but Jesus' prayer surrounded him, and Jesus was already speaking of the day Peter would return and strengthen his brothers. Wheat also has to be ground into flour before it becomes bread for the altar (Exodus 29:2). That detail can serve as an analogy, though the text does not teach it as a lesson. It fits Christ's own pattern of falling into the ground and dying before bearing fruit. Believers who follow Him should expect that being broken can lead to fruitfulness.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Samuel 17:28 — Friends brought wheat and other provisions to David as he fled from Absalom. God cared for His king when he was at his lowest.

  • Isaiah 28:25 — The farmer plants wheat in rows, and the next verse says his God teaches him to do it. Even ordinary skill in farming comes from the Lord.

  • Ezekiel 4:9 — Ezekiel bakes bread from wheat mixed with lesser grains as a sign of the coming siege, when the people would eat scarce, mixed food.

  • Jeremiah 41:8 — Ten men save their lives by telling Ishmael that they have "treasures of wheat, barley, oil, and honey in the field." The scene shows how desperate life had become after Jerusalem fell.

  • Song of Solomon 7:2 — "Your waist is a heap of wheat Set about with lilies." The poem uses a full heap of grain to praise the bride's beauty and life.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G4621 (sitos) — the Greek word for wheat or grain. It is not a lexical relative of chiṭṭâh, but it is the New Testament word in John 12:24, Matthew 3:12, Matthew 13:25–30, and Luke 22:31, where the wheat imagery is applied to Christ and His people.

  • Barley (canonical pairing) — Barley regularly appears alongside wheat as the other main grain harvest (Ruth 2:23; Joel 1:11; Job 31:40; Ezekiel 45:13).
