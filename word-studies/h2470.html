SICK, WEAK, ENTREAT - (châlâh, Strong's H2470)

Summary

The Hebrew verb châlâh most often means to be sick, weak, wounded, or worn down. It describes kings on their deathbeds, a soldier struck by an arrow, Samson drained of strength, a heart sick with grief, and a bride sick with love. The prophets use it for the "severe" wound of a nation that has turned from God, a wound no human remedy can treat. The same verb also has a second use: to entreat someone's favor, to plead for mercy. Moses uses it when he pleads for Israel. Manasseh uses it when he cries out in bronze fetters in Babylon. The nations use it in Zechariah's vision as they stream to Jerusalem to pray before the LORD.

Taken together, these two uses tell a story. Human beings are sick in ways we cannot heal, and God responds when the afflicted plead for His favor. Through Ezekiel, God promises to come Himself and strengthen His sick sheep. Malachi exposes priests who seek God's favor while handing Him sick animals. Most strikingly, Isaiah says of the Servant, "He has put Him to grief," using this same verb: the LORD made His Servant sick with the affliction our sin deserved. In the light of the gospel, because the Servant bore that sickness, the weak can now come boldly before God's face.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châlâh is a Hebrew verb used throughout the Old Testament, from Genesis 48:1 to Malachi 1:13. Strong's describes its basic idea as being rubbed or worn, and from that idea come being weak, sick, or afflicted. When the verb is used in a causative sense, it means to make someone sick or to grieve them.

The verb also appears in a distinctive phrase for prayer. A person entreats the face of the LORD or of a ruler. The NKJV usually renders this as "entreat the favor," "plead," "implore," or "pray." The traditional explanation pictures someone softening or stroking another's face to win kindness. Some lexicons treat this entreating use as a separate Hebrew root. Either way, the phrase plainly means seeking someone's favor, and Scripture sometimes sets the two uses side by side to great effect.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To be sick or ill in body, often gravely. Hezekiah "was sick and near death" (2 Kings 20:1).

2. To be weak, injured, or severely wounded, including wounds used as a picture of national disaster. Samson says he will become "weak" (Judges 16:7), and Jeremiah cries, "My wound is severe" (Jeremiah 10:19).

3. To be grieved, heartsick, or overcome with longing. The king asks Nehemiah why his face is sad when he is not sick (Nehemiah 2:2), and the bride says she is "lovesick" (Song of Solomon 2:5).

4. To entreat favor, to plead or implore. "Moses pleaded with the LORD his God" (Exodus 32:11).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the historical books, the word often marks a crisis in a royal house. Jeroboam's son Abijah "became sick" (1 Kings 14:1), and the prophet Ahijah announced the boy's death together with the end of Jeroboam's dynasty (1 Kings 14:10–13). Ahab and Josiah are "wounded" in battle (1 Kings 22:34; 2 Chronicles 35:23). Hezekiah's illness is the clearest case. His life and Jerusalem's safety are bound together: the same promise that adds fifteen years to his life also delivers the city from Assyria (2 Kings 20:6).

The prophets carry the word from bodies to nations. Jeremiah mourns, "Woe is me for my hurt! My wound is severe" (Jeremiah 10:19). Later God tells Zion that her affliction is incurable and her wound severe (Jeremiah 30:12). Nahum says the same of Nineveh: "Your injury has no healing, Your wound is severe" (Nahum 3:19). In these passages the wound is sin and its judgment, and no human remedy can reach it.

One grammatical pattern matters. Hebrew can turn a verb into a making happen form, so that be sick becomes make sick. God speaks this way in Micah 6:13, where He says He will make Israel sick by striking them. The most important example is Isaiah 53:10, where the LORD Himself makes His Servant sick.

The entreating sense covers human relationships as well. Proverbs 19:6 notes that many entreat the favor of a generous noble, and Psalm 45:12 pictures the rich among the people seeking the favor of the king's royal bride. These everyday examples help explain the prayer passages: the one who entreats comes as a petitioner before someone who has the power to show kindness.

4. Theological Significance

The word shows a God who answers those who plead with Him. Moses, the faithful mediator, pleaded at the height of God's anger over the golden calf (Exodus 32:11), and Israel was spared. Manasseh stands at the opposite end. He was one of Judah's worst kings, and he ended up bound in bronze fetters in Babylon. "When he was in affliction, he implored the LORD his God, and humbled himself greatly" (2 Chronicles 33:12), and God heard him and brought him home. Jehoahaz "pleaded with the LORD, and the LORD listened to him" (2 Kings 13:4). Even Jeroboam, with his hand withered for opposing God's prophet, begged the man of God, "Please entreat the favor of the LORD your God," and the hand was restored (1 Kings 13:6). God's responsiveness reaches the righteous intercessor and the broken rebel alike.

Second Chronicles also places the word's two uses in close proximity. Hezekiah was "sick" and prayed (2 Chronicles 32:24). One chapter later, his son Manasseh is crushed and "implored" the LORD. The word itself does not teach a doctrine, but the pattern is plain: being brought low is often where people finally seek God's face. Daniel's confession shows the other side. Israel suffered disaster, he says, "yet we have not made our prayer before the LORD our God" (Daniel 9:13). The tragedy was not only the wound. It was failing to plead for mercy while wounded.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Ezekiel 34: the Shepherd who strengthens the sick. God's charge against Israel's leaders uses this word twice in one verse: "The weak you have not strengthened, nor have you healed those who were sick… but with force and cruelty you have ruled them" (Ezekiel 34:4). These shepherds ate the fat and wore the wool (Ezekiel 34:3). Later in the chapter God turns to the flock itself and judges between sheep and sheep: the fat and strong sheep have pushed with side and shoulder and butted all the weak ones with their horns (Ezekiel 34:17–21). God's answer is personal: "Indeed I Myself will search for My sheep and seek them out" (Ezekiel 34:11). Then He promises to do exactly what the shepherds refused to do: bind up the broken and strengthen what was sick (Ezekiel 34:16). The shepherds, and the strong sheep after them, used their power against the weak. God uses His power for them.

Malachi 1: sick offerings and smooth words. The priests of Malachi's day brought blind, lame, and sick animals to the altar (Malachi 1:8, 13). Malachi asks whether their governor would accept such a gift. Then he tells them, with heavy irony, to entreat God's favor so that He may be gracious, and asks whether He will accept them while they act this way (Malachi 1:9). The same verb appears in both places. Malachi's irony exposes the attempt to seek God's favor while handing Him something sick. Seeking God's face cannot be separated from what we actually bring Him.

Judges 16: weak like any other man. Three times Samson tells Delilah what would make him "weak" and like any other man (Judges 16:7, 11, 17). Judges ties Samson's strength to his consecration as a Nazirite, not to muscle (Judges 16:17). When his consecration was broken, the decisive loss is stated in Judges 16:20: the LORD had departed from him. His weakness was the absence of God's empowering presence.

Isaiah 53:10: the Servant put to grief. "Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise Him; He has put Him to grief." Here the verb is in its make sick form, and the LORD is the subject. The prophets had described Israel's sin as a severe wound that no one could heal. In Isaiah 53, "the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6). The surrounding verses (Isaiah 53:3–5) call Him a Man of sorrows who bore our griefs and was wounded for our transgressions. Biblical sickness language can embrace body, mind, and soul together, and the surrounding verses describe the Servant's suffering in all those ways. Read in the light of the cross, Jesus' death was no accident of history. The verse ties the bruising to "an offering for sin" and to the Servant's future: "He shall see His seed, He shall prolong His days."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word follows the Bible's storyline from diagnosis to cure. The prophets name the wound and say it is incurable. Even good kings like Hezekiah fall sick, and their recoveries are temporary. Isaiah then shows the remedy coming from God: the LORD Himself afflicts the Servant with the sickness belonging to others. Matthew later sees Isaiah 53 fulfilled in Jesus' healing ministry as He takes up our infirmities (Matthew 8:17).

Ezekiel's promise that God Himself would shepherd and strengthen the sick comes to life in Jesus, who calls Himself the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11). Ezekiel 34 places that divine shepherding alongside a coming shepherd from David's line (Ezekiel 34:23). Jesus fulfills both, and He does so by going to the cross for His flock.

Isaiah also gives a glimpse of the result. In the restored Zion, no inhabitant will say, "I am sick," and the people who live there will be forgiven their iniquity (Isaiah 33:24). Sickness and sin are healed together. Zechariah adds what happens to prayer in that day. People from one city will urge those of another to go and pray before the LORD, and many peoples and strong nations will come to Jerusalem to seek Him and pray before Him (Zechariah 8:21–22). Pleading once came from isolated people in desperate trouble, such as Moses at the golden calf or Manasseh in chains. In Zechariah's vision it becomes the eager, shared pursuit of the nations.

7. Spirit & Life

Weakness is not a barrier to God. Manasseh had no record to stand on, only affliction and humility, and God heard him. Paul learned the same lesson in the language of the New Testament: "My strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). The person who feels worn down is in the right place to seek God's face. Daniel's warning applies as well: being worn down helps only when we actually pray.

Samson's story warns us that real spiritual strength has never been our own. We can drift from God's presence without noticing. Samson "did not know that the LORD had departed from him" (Judges 16:20) until he tried to rise and found nothing there. Our vitality depends on God's presence with us.

Malachi asks what we are bringing when we seek God's favor. Leftover devotion offered with polished words insults the God we are asking to be gracious.

David models a heart that reflects God's own. "When they were sick, My clothing was sackcloth; I humbled myself with fasting" (Psalm 35:13). These were men who repaid him with evil, yet he mourned for them in their sickness. What David did in sackcloth, Christ did completely when He bore our grief.

Finally, Scripture leaves room for a holy kind of sickness. The bride in the Song of Solomon is so overcome with love that she asks to be sustained with raisin cakes (Song of Solomon 2:5), and she charges the daughters of Jerusalem to tell her beloved that she is lovesick (5:8). The psalmists speak the same way about God, thirsting for Him and fainting for His courts (Psalm 42:1–2; 84:2). That is the right kind of heartsickness for those whose deeper sickness has been healed: longing for Christ Himself. "I entreated Your favor with my whole heart; Be merciful to me according to Your word" (Psalm 119:58).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Nehemiah 2:2. The king sees that Nehemiah is not sick, yet his face is sad. The word recognizes that sorrow of heart can look like illness.

Proverbs 13:12. Hope that keeps being delayed makes the heart sick. The proverb gives an honest name to the weariness of waiting.

Psalm 77:10. The psalmist calls his troubled questioning about God "my anguish," then turns to remember the works of the Most High.

Amos 6:6. The comfortable leaders of Israel drink wine from bowls but are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph. Their failure to feel sick over the nation's condition is part of their guilt.

1 Samuel 22:8. Saul complains that none of his men is sorry for him. It is a self-pitying use of the word that exposes his isolation.

Daniel 8:27. After his vision, Daniel was sick for days before returning to the king's business. God's revelation could leave even a faithful servant physically spent.
