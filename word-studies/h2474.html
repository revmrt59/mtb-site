WINDOW - (challôwn, Strong's H2474)

Summary

The Hebrew word challôwn means a window: an opening in the wall of a house, palace, city wall, ark, or temple. Ancient windows had no glass. They were open gaps, often covered with wooden lattice, that let in light and air and allowed people inside to watch the world outside. Because a window breaks through a protective wall, Scripture repeatedly places important moments at this boundary between inside and outside, safety and danger, seeing and being seen.

Through windows, God's people escape when the doors are guarded. Rahab lowers the spies from her house on Jericho's wall and later ties the scarlet cord there, and Michal lets David down to flee from Saul. People who look out from windows often reveal what is in their hearts, as Michal does when she despises David's worship and Jezebel does when she paints her eyes before her fall. The prophets warn that "death has come through our windows" when God's judgment arrives, while Elisha has King Joash open the east window to shoot "the arrow of the LORD's deliverance." The window is a simple architectural word, yet the passages that use it show how God saves people where human defenses fail and how He sees into the heart.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Challôwn is an ordinary Hebrew building term. In the ancient world, a window was usually small and high. It was left open or fitted with a wooden grating, and shutters might close it. A latticed window let the person inside see out while staying mostly hidden. Several passages pair the window with a lattice, so the word carries that sense of watching from concealment (Judges 5:28; Proverbs 7:6; Song of Solomon 2:9).

The word appears in narrative, wisdom, poetry, and prophecy. Its largest group of uses comes in Ezekiel's vision of the temple (Ezekiel 40–41), where it occurs twelve times.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Challôwn means a window: an opening in a wall or structure for light, air, and looking out. It can describe a window in a house (Joshua 2:15), a palace (2 Kings 9:30), the ark (Genesis 8:6), or the temple (1 Kings 6:4). The word has one plain meaning. Its spiritual weight comes from the stories and prophecies in which windows appear.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses windows in four main ways.

1. A window serves as an escape route when the doors are blocked (Joshua 2:15; 1 Samuel 19:12).

2. A window is a place for watching. Someone looks out and discovers something, waits for something, or passes judgment on what is happening below (Genesis 26:8; Judges 5:28; 2 Samuel 6:16; Proverbs 7:6).

3. In prophetic warnings, a window is a weak point in a wall, where invaders or death get in (Jeremiah 9:21; Joel 2:9).

4. Windows are part of the sacred design of God's house (1 Kings 6:4; Ezekiel 40:16).

What the window means in each case depends on who is at it and which way they face. When a person looks outward, the passage usually exposes that person's heart. When something comes inward, it usually brings judgment. When someone goes out through the window, the story is usually about rescue.

4. Theological Significance

Windows show that human walls cannot set the terms of God's work. A walled city or a royal palace looks secure, but its windows remain open. Joel pictures God's invading army running on the wall until "They enter at the windows like a thief" (Joel 2:9). Jeremiah gives the image its most chilling form: "For death has come through our windows, Has entered our palaces, To kill off the children" (Jeremiah 9:21). The opening that was built to let in light and breath becomes the way death enters. Zephaniah completes the picture with Nineveh in ruins, where birds' "voice shall sing in the windows" (Zephaniah 2:14). Wealth and fortifications cannot keep out the God whom Judah and Nineveh had defied.

The same truth works in the other direction for those who trust Him. Where human authorities have closed every door, God opens a way through a window. He is not boxed in by walls, whether the walls belong to His enemies or to the people He is rescuing.

Windows also belong to God's dwelling. Solomon made "windows with beveled frames" for the temple (1 Kings 6:4). The exact construction of these frames is uncertain. In Ezekiel's vision, windows are noted again and again in the gate chambers, vestibules, and sanctuary, alongside palm-tree carvings (Ezekiel 40:16; 41:16, 26). God's house was built with deliberate care. Light entered it by design, as everything about approaching Him was ordered by His instructions.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Rahab's window (Joshua 2:15, 18, 21). Jericho's king had men searching for the spies, and the city gate was shut. Rahab "let them down by a rope through the window, for her house was on the city wall" (Joshua 2:15). The spies then told her to bind a line of scarlet cord in that same window as the sign for sparing her household, and she tied it there (Joshua 2:18, 21). The window through which she saved them became the mark of her own salvation, set in the very wall that was about to fall. The parallel with the first Passover is striking. In Egypt, Israel struck the doorposts with blood, and "none of you shall go out of the door of his house until morning" (Exodus 12:22). In Jericho, the sign hangs from a window, and everyone inside the marked house is spared. Joshua does not state this parallel outright, but the pattern is clear: God marks a threshold, and the household that stays behind it lives.

Michal at two windows (1 Samuel 19:12; 2 Samuel 6:16). Michal's story uses windows twice. First, with Saul's men watching the house, "Michal let David down through a window. And he went and fled and escaped" (1 Samuel 19:12). Years later, when the ark entered Jerusalem, "Michal, Saul's daughter, looked through a window and saw King David leaping and whirling before the LORD; and she despised him in her heart" (2 Samuel 6:16; compare 1 Chronicles 15:29). The woman who once opened a window to save David now stands at one and looks down on him. The narrator calls her "Saul's daughter" at this moment. She watches God's joy from above and from a distance, and the window frames the spiritual distance in her heart.

Michal belongs to a wider pattern of royal women at windows. Sisera's mother "looked through the window, And cried out through the lattice, 'Why is his chariot so long in coming?'" (Judges 5:28). She was waiting for plunder from a battle her son had already lost. Jezebel "put paint on her eyes and adorned her head, and looked through a window" (2 Kings 9:30) as Jehu arrived. Jehu then looked up to that window and called for someone on his side, and she was thrown down from it (2 Kings 9:32–33). In each scene, a woman of a doomed royal house looks out with confidence, unaware that God has already turned events against her.

Elisha's east window (2 Kings 13:17). As Elisha lay dying, he told King Joash, "Open the east window," and then, "Shoot." He declared it "The arrow of the LORD's deliverance and the arrow of deliverance from Syria." Opening the window was an act of obedience that faced the enemy and expected God to act. In the next verses, Joash strikes the ground only three times, and Elisha is angry that he stopped so soon (2 Kings 13:18–19). The window had been opened, but the king's expectation was small. God's promise was real, and Joash's half-hearted response limited how much of it he saw.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Rahab's scarlet cord points forward along the same line as the Passover blood. In both, God provides a marked place of refuge when judgment falls on a whole city or nation. The story continues into the New Testament. Rahab is named in Matthew's genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5) and honored for her faith in Hebrews 11:31. The woman whose window was marked for rescue became part of the family line of the Savior, whose blood marks the true refuge for everyone who trusts Him.

The Song of Solomon offers a tender image of love that comes looking: "He is looking through the windows, Gazing through the lattice" (Song of Solomon 2:9). In the following verse, he calls his beloved to rise and come away. The Song describes human love. Many believers have also heard in it the voice of the Lord who comes to His people, and Scripture elsewhere calls Christ the Bridegroom (Matthew 9:15; Ephesians 5:25–27). The lattice hides the beloved, but his gaze still finds her.

7. Spirit & Life

Michal's two windows confront every believer. A person can share in God's work at one stage of life and later stand back from it, critical and cold, while others rejoice before the Lord. A window is a comfortable place to judge from, because the person watching never has to come down into the street. David's answer was that he was celebrating "before the LORD" (2 Samuel 6:21). A heart that keeps its distance from God's joy needs humility more than better arguments.

Jehoiakim's windows offer a related warning. He said, "I will build myself a wide house with spacious chambers, And cut out windows for it" (Jeremiah 22:14), while he made his neighbors work without wages. God contrasted him with his father Josiah, who defended the poor, and asked whether this was not what it means to know Him (Jeremiah 22:15–16). Large windows did not give Jehoiakim the sight that mattered.

Faith opens windows of a different kind. Daniel, in his upper room "with his windows open toward Jerusalem," prayed three times a day after the king's decree was signed (Daniel 6:10). That passage uses a different word, but the picture fits. Like Joash at the east window, Daniel turned toward the place of God's promise rather than toward the threat. The lesson of Joash is that a believer should not stop striking too soon. When God says "Open" and "Shoot," He invites us to expect great things from Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 8:6 — "Noah opened the window of the ark which he had made." After forty days, Noah opened the window to look for signs that the judgment of the flood had ended.

  • Genesis 26:8 — Abimelech "looked through a window, and saw" Isaac with Rebekah. The window revealed the truth that Isaac's deception had tried to hide.

  • Proverbs 7:6 — "For at the window of my house I looked through my lattice." From behind the lattice, the teacher watches a naive young man walk toward destruction and writes so that others will not follow him.

  • Ezekiel 41:16 — Windows appear throughout the inner rooms of the visionary temple, part of a house planned in every detail for God's presence.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H699 (ʾărubbâh) — A distinct Hebrew word used for the "windows of heaven," which open for the flood (Genesis 7:11) and for God's blessing (Malachi 3:10). It is a related image, not a form of challôwn.

  • H3551 — Daniel 6:10 — The Aramaic word for Daniel's open windows is a different term. It is a thematic parallel to Elisha's east window, not a lexical relative of challôwn.
