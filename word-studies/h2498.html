CHANGE, PASS ON, RENEW - (châlaph, Strong's H2498)

Summary

The Hebrew verb châlaph describes movement from one state to another. Something passes by, sweeps through, pierces through, is exchanged for something else, or springs up fresh. English versions translate it with words as varied as "changed," "substitute," "pass," "sprout," "renew," and "abolish." The word itself is neutral. It can describe Jacob's household putting on clean garments before worship, Laban cheating Jacob by changing his wages, grass springing up in the morning, or an arrow driving through an enemy.

What gives châlaph its weight in Scripture is what is being changed and who is changing it. People change God's ordinance and defile the earth (Isaiah 24:5). God changes the heavens and the earth like an old cloak and remains Himself unchanged (Psalm 102:26), and the letter to the Hebrews applies that truth to the Son. In the best-known use of all, those who wait on the LORD "shall renew their strength" (Isaiah 40:31). Worn-out human strength gives way to the strength God supplies. The God who changes everything else does not change, and that is why weary people can rely on Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châlaph appears 28 times in 27 verses, and more than half of those uses come in Job, Psalms, and Isaiah. The earlier narratives use the word for everyday exchanges: clothing, wages, and animals vowed to God. The poets and prophets carry it into larger questions. They ask how fleeting human life is, what kind of hope the dead have, how God's judgment sweeps through a land, and how God renews His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic idea is passing from one condition or place to another. In use, the word carries three main senses:

1. To pass by or through. Something moves past a point or penetrates a barrier, as when a spirit "passed before my face" (Job 4:15) or a bronze bow "will pierce him through" (Job 20:24).

2. To change or substitute. One thing is exchanged for another, as with garments (Genesis 41:14), wages (Genesis 31:7), or a vowed animal (Leviticus 27:10).

3. To spring up anew or be renewed. Fresh life or vigor replaces what was spent, as with a stump that will "sprout again" (Job 14:7) or strength that is renewed (Isaiah 40:31).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes its tone a great deal. When Laban changes Jacob's wages "ten times" (Genesis 31:7, 41), the change is fraud. When the law forbids anyone to substitute a different animal for one vowed to the LORD (Leviticus 27:10), the point is that what has been given to God cannot be traded back. In Isaiah 9:10, Israel boasts that it will replace its fallen sycamores with cedars. That is human renewal driven by pride after God has already struck the nation.

The "passing" sense often expresses how fleeting life is or how hidden God can be. Job laments that his days pass by like swift ships (Job 9:26). He also says that God passes by him and he does not perceive Him (Job 9:11). Even Zophar uses the word for God passing by in judgment (Job 11:10). Elsewhere the same word carries the force of violence: Jael "struck through his temple" (Judges 5:26), and Assyria "will pass through Judah" like a flood (Isaiah 8:8). The Song of Solomon gives the word a gentle setting. There the rain is over and gone because winter has passed and spring has come (Song of Solomon 2:11).

4. Theological Significance

Across these uses one truth stands out: everything in creation passes and changes, but God does not. Grass "grows up" in the morning and "in the evening it is cut down and withers" (Psalm 90:5–6). Human days pass like ships. Even the heavens wear out like clothing. Against this background of constant change, Scripture presents God as the One who brings change rather than the one who undergoes it.

Because God is fixed, His order is fixed as well. Isaiah 24:5 says the earth "is also defiled under its inhabitants, because they have transgressed the laws, changed the ordinance, broken the everlasting covenant." Humanity's attempt to substitute its own rules for God's does not stay private. The whole earth bears the defilement, and the chapter goes on to describe the land mourning and languishing. Changing what God has established brings ruin.

God's judgment, when it comes, is a change no one can resist. Assyria floods through the land like a river (Isaiah 8:8), and Babylon sweeps on in arrogance, "ascribing this power to his god" (Habakkuk 1:11). In the Day of the LORD, "the idols He shall utterly abolish" (Isaiah 2:18). The idols do not slowly fade. They pass away completely and leave the LORD alone exalted.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 40:31 and 41:1: two attempts at renewal. Isaiah 40 ends with a picture of exhausted people: "even the youths shall faint and be weary" (40:30). Then comes the promise: "But those who wait on the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings like eagles, they shall run and not be weary, they shall walk and not faint" (40:31). Because châlaph so often means exchanging one thing for another, the renewal Isaiah describes fits naturally with that idea of exchange. Failing strength gives way to strength drawn from "the everlasting God" who "neither faints nor is weary" (40:28). One verse later the same verb returns with sharp irony. God summons the nations to court: "Keep silence before Me, O coastlands, and let the people renew their strength! Let them come near, then let them speak" (41:1). The coastlands are told to gather their own strength and argue their case against God, and the rest of the chapter shows that their idols cannot answer. Two ways of renewal stand side by side. The nations try to stir up strength of their own and come up empty. Those who wait on the LORD receive His strength.

Psalm 102:26: the changing garment. The afflicted psalmist, whose "days are like a shadow," turns to God's permanence. He says the heavens and earth "will perish, but You will endure; yes, they will all grow old like a garment; like a cloak You will change them, and they will be changed." The verb appears twice. God is the active One who changes creation, and creation is the thing that is changed. The whole universe is pictured as a coat God will take off and replace. This gives the frail psalmist hope. If God outlasts the heavens, He can be trusted with "the children of Your servants" (102:28).

Job 14:7: hope for a tree. Job observes, "For there is hope for a tree, if it is cut down, that it will sprout again, and that its tender shoots will not cease." A stump pushes up new growth, but a man lies down and does not rise (14:10–12). The very contrast drives Job to his great question of whether a man who dies will live again (14:14). Nature's ability to sprout again sharpens Job's longing for a renewal that only God can give a human being.

Changing garments: Genesis 35:2, 41:14; 2 Samuel 12:20. Three times a change of clothes marks a turning point. Jacob tells his household, "Put away the foreign gods that are among you, purify yourselves, and change your garments," before they go up to Bethel to build an altar. Joseph is brought "quickly out of the dungeon; and he shaved, changed his clothing, and came to Pharaoh." After his child dies, David "arose from the ground, washed and anointed himself, and changed his clothes; and he went into the house of the LORD and worshiped." Idolatry, imprisonment, and mourning are each left behind. The outward act shows that a person is ready to stand in a new place, and in two of the three cases that place is before God Himself.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hebrews 1:10–12 takes Psalm 102 and addresses it directly to the Son: "Like a cloak You will fold them up, and they will be changed. But You are the same, and Your years will not fail." Jesus Christ is the One who laid the foundations of the earth and who will one day change the whole creation like worn clothing. Everything that passes, whether grass, human days, empires, or idols, passes before Him. The writer uses this unchanging nature to show that the Son is far above the angels and is the One in whom God's final word has been spoken.

The other themes of châlaph find their answer in Him as well. Job asked whether a man cut down could sprout again like a tree. In Christ, God answers with resurrection life, and the Gospel accounts show Him alive again from the dead. Isaiah's promise of renewed strength takes concrete form in Paul's experience. The Lord told him, "My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness," and Paul learned to boast in weakness "that the power of Christ may rest upon me" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Isaiah does not name Christ, but the pattern is the same: human strength runs out, and God's strength takes its place. The changed garments of Jacob's household also resonate with the New Testament call to put off the old and put on the new. That is a thematic echo rather than a stated fulfillment, but it is a fitting one.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 41:1 is worth sitting with. God is not opposed to strength. He even invites the coastlands to "renew their strength" for their day in court. But strength gathered in order to argue with God collapses, while strength received by waiting on God carries a person through. Many believers live like the coastlands. They summon one more burst of effort, their own renewal, to face what only God can carry. Waiting on the LORD is the place where tired strength is exchanged, not a passive delay. The order in Isaiah 40:31 matters: first mounting up, then running, then walking without fainting. God's renewal reaches the long ordinary walk as well as the moments that feel like flight.

Psalm 102 speaks to anyone whose life feels like a fading shadow. The psalmist does not find comfort by pretending he is strong. He looks past his own frailty, and past the heavens themselves, to the One whose "years will not fail." The most stable thing a believer can hold is the unchanging Christ, because everything else, however solid it looks, is a garment.

Finally, Jacob's household and David both show that coming to God involves a real change. Foreign gods are put away and grief is set down before the house of the LORD. The new garments meant something because the old allegiances were truly left behind.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 31:7, 41 — Jacob protests that Laban changed his wages ten times. It is a portrait of human unreliability, set against God, who kept Jacob from harm.

  • Leviticus 27:10 — An animal vowed to the LORD may not be substituted, good for bad or bad for good. What is given to God stays His.

  • Job 9:11 — Job says God passes by him unseen. His words express the anguish of feeling that God is hidden.

  • Job 29:20 — Job recalls a time when his glory was fresh and his bow was renewed in his hand. Even that remembered vigor proved temporary.

  • Isaiah 9:10 — Israel boasts that it will replace its fallen sycamores with cedars. This is self-made renewal in defiance of God's discipline.

  • Song of Solomon 2:11 — Winter is past, and the rain is over and gone. Here the word gives a gentle picture of one season giving way to the next.
