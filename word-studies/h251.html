BROTHER, BRETHREN, ANOTHER - (ʼâch, Strong's H0251)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼâch means "brother." Its starting point is a male sibling, such as Cain and Abel or Joseph and his brothers. Scripture widens it to cover a nephew, a clansman, a fellow Israelite, or a fellow Levite serving at the house of God. In a common idiom, "a man to his brother," it simply means "one another." The NKJV renders it most often as "brother," "brothers," and "brethren."

The word carries the weight of obligation. Genesis shows brotherhood broken almost from the start: Cain kills Abel, Esau plans to kill Jacob, and Joseph's brothers sell him. Yet God keeps working through those broken families until Joseph weeps over the brothers who betrayed him. The Law then calls every Israelite "your brother" and makes that bond the basis for generosity, fair lending, and release from debt. The prophets condemn those who betray a brother, and Proverbs speaks of "a brother … born for adversity" and of "a friend who sticks closer than a brother." The word points forward to the One who "is not ashamed to call them brethren" (Hebrews 2:11).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼâch is one of the basic family words of biblical Hebrew. It appears throughout the Old Testament, and the settings change from book to book. Genesis uses it heavily because Genesis is a book of families. The Law uses it to describe how Israelites must treat one another. Chronicles, Ezra, and Nehemiah use it for priests, Levites, singers, and gatekeepers who served together. The prophets use it when they accuse people of betraying those closest to them.

In Israel's world, a brother was responsible for his kin. He was expected to protect the family's land, avenge wrongs, and redeem a relative who had fallen into debt (Leviticus 25:25, 48). That background explains why the word so often appears alongside commands.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A male sibling, a son of the same father or mother. Cain "rose up against Abel his brother" (Genesis 4:8).

2. A near relative or clansman. Abram tells his nephew Lot that they are brethren (Genesis 13:8), and Laban calls Jacob his relative (Genesis 29:15).

3. A fellow member of the same people or the same group, especially a fellow Israelite (Deuteronomy 15:7), a fellow Levite or temple servant (1 Chronicles 15:5–18), or an associate (2 Kings 9:2).

4. In reciprocal phrases, "one another" or "the other." The cherubim face "one another" (Exodus 25:20).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines how wide the circle is. In the Joseph story, "brothers" means Jacob's sons. In Deuteronomy, "your brother" includes any poor Israelite at your gate. In 1 Chronicles 25, "their brethren" are the musicians who were trained with Heman, Asaph, and Jeduthun.

One construction deserves attention. Hebrew often says "a man … his brother" where English would say "one another." Sometimes this is plain description, as when the cherubim face each other. Elsewhere the family word adds moral weight. When Malachi asks why Israel deals treacherously "one against another" (Malachi 2:10), the underlying phrase reminds the hearer that the person being wronged is a brother.

4. Theological Significance

God defines brotherhood by His own redeeming work. In Leviticus 25 and Deuteronomy 15, Israelites are brothers because the Lord brought them all out of Egypt, and that shared rescue governs how they handle money. When a brother becomes poor, Israel must support him and must not profit from his need with interest (Leviticus 25:35–36; Deuteronomy 23:19). A brother sold into servitude must not be treated as a slave (Leviticus 25:39), and he goes free in the seventh year (Deuteronomy 15:12). A brother's stray ox or lost garment must be returned to him (Deuteronomy 22:1–4). In this law, a person's dealings with money and property show whether he believes that God redeemed his neighbor too.

God also treats betrayal of a brother as an offense against Himself. Malachi asks whether Israel does not have one Father and one Creator, and then asks why they betray one another (Malachi 2:10). Jeremiah describes a society so corrupt that no one could trust a brother, because every brother would "supplant" (Jeremiah 9:4). That is the word Esau used of Jacob (Genesis 27:36), and Hosea recalls that Jacob took his brother by the heel in the womb (Hosea 12:3). Jeremiah's Israel had become as untrustworthy as its ancestor at his worst.

The same standard applies to other nations. Edom is judged "because he pursued his brother with the sword, and cast off all pity" (Amos 1:11), and Obadiah condemns Edom's violence against "your brother Jacob" (Obadiah 10). Tyre is judged because it "did not remember the covenant of brotherhood" (Amos 1:9). God remembers family ties that people would rather forget.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis: brotherhood broken and restored. The first time Scripture develops this word, it describes a murder. After Cain kills Abel, God asks him where his brother is. Cain asks whether he is his brother's keeper, and God answers that his brother's blood cries out from the ground (Genesis 4:9–10). Family ties offer no protection from sin. Later Esau plans to kill Jacob (27:41), and Joseph's brothers hate him so much that they cannot speak peaceably to him (37:4).

The Joseph story shows how God reverses this. Judah's argument for selling Joseph was that he was their brother and their own flesh (37:26–27), as though selling him were kinder than killing him. Years later the same Judah offers himself as a slave in Benjamin's place (44:33). Then Joseph tells them, "I am Joseph your brother, whom you sold into Egypt" (45:4), and he kisses them and weeps. Afterward, the text says, his brothers talked with him (45:15). Brothers who once could not speak peaceably to Joseph now talk with him. After Jacob dies, they fear revenge (50:15), and Joseph tells them that they meant evil but God meant it for good, to save many people alive (50:20). God brought salvation through the betrayal itself.

Joshua: no rest until all have rest. Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh received their land east of the Jordan first. Joshua told them to cross over armed ahead of their brethren and help them until the Lord gave their brethren rest as He had given them (Joshua 1:14–15). Years later he commended them for not leaving their brethren during all those days (22:3). Their land was already secure, but they could not settle into it while their brothers were still fighting.

Proverbs: the brother in the hard day. "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity" (Proverbs 17:17). Family is meant to stand with a person when trouble comes. Proverbs 18:24 admits that this does not always happen: "there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother." Taken together, the two proverbs describe what a brother should be and also acknowledge that brothers often fail.

Psalm 133 and Nehemiah: unity worth defending. "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!" (Psalm 133:1). David compares that unity to the anointing oil running down Aaron's beard and to the dew of Hermon, and he says that there the Lord commanded the blessing, life forevermore. Aaron himself is often called Moses' "brother" in Exodus (4:14; 28:1). Nehemiah fought for this kind of unity. He urged the builders to fight for their brethren (Nehemiah 4:14). Then he confronted nobles who were lending at interest to their own brethren. He said the returned exiles had bought back their Jewish brethren from foreign nations, and these men were now selling their brethren again (5:7–8). Exploitation inside the community threatened the restoration as much as the enemies outside the walls did.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of brotherhood in the Old Testament keeps pointing beyond itself. Moses promised that God would raise up a Prophet "from your midst, from your brethren" (Deuteronomy 18:15, 18), and Peter applies this promise to Jesus (Acts 3:22). The Law provided that a poor Israelite could be redeemed by one of his brothers (Leviticus 25:48), yet the psalmist admits that none can redeem his brother from death or pay God a ransom for him (Psalm 49:7). The redeemer Israel needed had to be a true brother who could also do what no human brother could do.

Hebrews answers that need. "Both He who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all of one, for which reason He is not ashamed to call them brethren" (Hebrews 2:11). Hebrews then quotes Psalm 22:22, in which the suffering psalmist declares God's name to his "brethren," and puts those words in Christ's mouth (Hebrews 2:12). Christ "had to be made like His brethren, that He might be a merciful and faithful High Priest … to make propitiation for the sins of the people" (Hebrews 2:17).

Proverbs never names Christ, but its brother "born for adversity" fits Him well. He entered human flesh to share our hardest day, the judgment of sin and the fear of death. Like Joseph, He was rejected and handed over by His own people, and He turned that rejection into salvation for those who had rejected Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Cain's question, whether he is his brother's keeper, was meant to dismiss responsibility. All of Scripture answers it with yes. The Law answers it in practical terms: return the lost ox, lend without interest, release the debtor, open your hand to the poor brother (Deuteronomy 15:7–11). A believer who calls other Christians "brothers" accepts that same kind of responsibility, and it will show up in how he spends his money and his time.

Joshua's charge to the eastern tribes speaks to anyone who has found rest in Christ while others are still struggling. Those tribes had their land, their cities, and their families settled, and they still crossed the Jordan to fight for their brothers. Spiritual maturity includes staying with other believers in their struggle and not leaving them on their own.

Chronicles adds a quieter lesson. Long lists of Levites, singers, and gatekeepers are each recorded "with their brethren" (1 Chronicles 15:16–18; 25:9–31; 26:8–12). The house of God was served by companies of brothers and never by a solitary star. Heman's sons were trained in the songs of the Lord together with their brethren (25:7). Ministry is family work, and the honor of it is shared.

Underneath all of this stands the Brother who did not fail. When friends and family let us down, as Proverbs 18:24 admits they will, Christ is not ashamed of us. Joseph forgave his brothers, and Christ restores His people in the same way. That is what makes brothers out of former rivals and keeps them together in unity.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 13:8 — Abram ends the quarrel between his herdsmen and Lot's by appealing to kinship: they are brethren. He gives Lot first choice of the land to keep peace in the family.

  • Leviticus 19:17 — Israel must not hate a brother in the heart and must rebuke a neighbor honestly. Brotherhood requires truthfulness as well as kindness.

  • Micah 7:2 — In a corrupt land, people hunt one another, each his brother, with a net. The prophet describes the complete collapse of trust.

  • Jeremiah 31:34 — Under the new covenant, no one will need to tell his brother to know the Lord, because all will know Him. In that promise, knowing God joins brothers together.

  • Hosea 2:1 — After judgment, God tells Israel to say to their brethren, "My people." Restoration with God brings restoration among His people.
