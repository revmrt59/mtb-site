DESIRE, COVET, DELIGHT IN - (châmad, Strong's H2530)

Summary

The Hebrew verb châmad describes a strong inward attraction, the pull of the heart toward something it finds beautiful, valuable, or pleasing. English translations render it "covet," "desire," "delight," "pleasant," or "desirable," and each choice depends on what the heart is reaching for. In Eden God Himself made trees "pleasant to the sight" (Genesis 2:9). David says God's judgments are "more to be desired… than gold" (Psalm 19:10). God Himself desires Mount Zion as His dwelling (Psalm 68:16). The same word also names the craving behind the first sin, Achan's theft, the oppression of the poor, and the final commandment: "You shall not covet."

The word reveals what a person treasures. Scripture uses it to trace a recurring pattern. The eyes see, the heart desires, and the hands take. Isaiah adds a sobering line about the coming Servant of the Lord. When people looked at Him, they saw nothing that made them desire Him (Isaiah 53:2). Human desire is broken enough to crave what destroys and to overlook the Savior. Scripture's answer is to redirect desire, so that God's word becomes sweeter than honey and the believer finds rest under the shade of the Beloved.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châmad is a Hebrew verb that appears in every major part of the Old Testament: the Law, the historical books, the poetic and wisdom books, and the prophets. Its settings are vivid. They include Eden's trees, the Ten Commandments, the spoil from a conquered city, a young man's temptation, a love song, pagan groves, and land seized from the poor. A closely related noun, chemdah (H2532), names a desirable or precious thing. This study concerns the verb and its descriptive forms ("pleasant," "desirable," "precious").

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its heart châmad means to be strongly drawn to something because it appears good or beautiful. The word itself does not carry a moral judgment. Its rightness or wrongness depends on what it reaches for and whether that object belongs to the one desiring it.

1. To desire with a longing that moves toward possession, often rendered "covet." This is desire that already leans toward getting the thing, as when people "covet fields and take them by violence" (Micah 2:2).

2. To delight in or take pleasure in something, as when the bride says, "I sat down in his shade with great delight" (Song of Songs 2:3), or when scorners delight in their scorning (Proverbs 1:22).

3. In descriptive forms, to be desirable, pleasant, or precious. The word describes the trees God made "pleasant to the sight" (Genesis 2:9) and the "desirable treasure" stored in the house of the wise (Proverbs 21:20).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The object determines the moral weight of the word. When châmad is directed toward God's provision, His word, or a rightful beloved, it is healthy. When it is aimed at a neighbor's wife, a pagan idol, or forbidden fruit, it becomes "covet" or "lust."

Scripture repeatedly links this desire to the eyes and the heart. Proverbs warns, "Do not lust after her beauty in your heart, Nor let her allure you with her eyelids" (Proverbs 6:25). Beauty enters through the eyes, but the danger lies where the desire settles, which is "in your heart."

The word also tends to reach beyond feeling toward taking. Before Israel's three yearly pilgrimages, God promised that no one would covet their land while the men were away worshiping (Exodus 34:24). Coveting land in that setting means having designs on it.

One grammatical feature matters for understanding. Some forms describe a thing as desirable instead of describing a person as desiring. In Psalm 19:10 God's judgments are "more to be desired," which presents them as objectively worthy of longing. The same kind of form describes the forbidden tree as "desirable to make one wise" (Genesis 3:6). Eve's judgment of its worth was the crucial mistake.

4. Theological Significance

God is not against desire. He planted Eden with trees that were "pleasant to the sight and good for food" (Genesis 2:9) before any command was given. He made human beings to enjoy beauty, and the word's first appearance describes His generosity.

God Himself also desires. In Psalm 68:16 the high mountains of Bashan are pictured as fuming with envy because "This is the mountain which God desires to dwell in." God's desire is holy and generous. He wants to live among His people, and He does not want to take what belongs to someone else. Every grasping human craving in Scripture stands in contrast with this desire.

Because desire is so powerful, Scripture treats misplaced desire as close to idolatry. Moses told Israel not to covet the silver and gold on pagan images, because that desire would become a trap (Deuteronomy 7:25). Isaiah warned that Israel would be ashamed "of the terebinth trees Which you have desired" and embarrassed by "the gardens Which you have chosen" (Isaiah 1:29). These sacred groves were places of pagan worship. The garden imagery recalls Eden's God-planted trees and exposes idolatry as a counterfeit paradise. Isaiah 44:9 says the idol-makers' precious things "shall not profit." Paul later names covetousness as idolatry (Colossians 3:5), and these passages show why. Whatever the heart desires most functions as its god.

God also rules over desire. In Exodus 34:24 He promised to restrain the appetites of hostile nations, so that no one would covet Israel's land while Israel worshiped. His protection extended to the hidden longings of other nations, and obeying Him did not leave His people exposed.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Eden and Achan: seeing, desiring, taking (Genesis 3:6; Joshua 7:21). Eve "saw that the tree was good for food, that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree desirable to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate." God had already filled the garden with desirable trees. Eve judged that the one forbidden tree offered something God was withholding, and her desire overruled her trust in Him. Centuries later Achan confessed the same pattern after Jericho: "When I saw… a beautiful Babylonian garment, two hundred shekels of silver, and a wedge of gold… I coveted them and took them. And there they are, hidden in the earth in the midst of my tent." He saw the spoil, he coveted it, and he took it. Then he hid it. Sin rarely begins with the hand. It begins with a desire that has decided God's boundary is not good.

The Tenth Commandment and its fruit (Exodus 20:17; Deuteronomy 5:21; Micah 2:2). Most of the Ten Commandments forbid deeds. The last forbids a desire: "You shall not covet" a neighbor's house, wife, servants, ox, or donkey. The commandment reaches to the place where murder, adultery, and theft begin. It also calls for contentment, because coveting a neighbor's goods implies that God has not given enough. Micah shows how quickly the inward desire turns outward: "They covet fields and take them by violence, Also houses, and seize them. So they oppress a man and his house, A man and his inheritance." In Israel a family's land was its God-given inheritance, so coveting a field meant robbing a family of its place among God's people. Greed does not stay private.

Two kinds of gold (Psalm 19:10). Achan desired gold and buried it under his tent. David says God's judgments are "More to be desired are they than gold, Yea, than much fine gold; Sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb." Both passages use the same verb and measure desire against the same metal. The difference lies in the object. One desire brought death to Achan's household in the Valley of Achor. The other gives life to the soul, as the surrounding verses of Psalm 19 say: God's law converts the soul, makes wise the simple, and rejoices the heart. The cure for coveting is a stronger and truer desire.

The Servant no one desired (Isaiah 53:2). Isaiah describes the Servant of the Lord growing up like a tender plant from dry ground, with no stately form or beauty that would make people desire Him. Eve desired a tree that promised wisdom and delivered death. Here the One who brings life stands before human eyes, and they find nothing desirable in Him. The eyes that were drawn to the forbidden fruit pass over the Servant. Isaiah does not blame the Servant's appearance. He exposes the people who looked at Him and preferred what impresses.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah 53 finds its fulfillment in Jesus, and the Gospels record the rejection Isaiah foresaw: "He came to His own, and His own did not receive Him" (John 1:11). Paul describes the same blindness of desire. Jews wanted a sign and Greeks wanted wisdom, but "we preach Christ crucified," who is foolishness to the world yet "the power of God and the wisdom of God" to those who are called (1 Corinthians 1:22–24). Eve reached for a tree that was desirable "to make one wise." True wisdom came to the world in a crucified Messiah whom people did not desire. God hid His salvation in a form that the eye's craving for glory would overlook.

Jesus also carried châmad's warning about the eyes and heart to its depth. Proverbs 6:25 placed lust "in your heart," and Jesus taught that looking at a woman with lust is already adultery in the heart (Matthew 5:28). Paul found that the commandment "You shall not covet" exposed his own sin (Romans 7:7). The final commandment shows that every heart needs more than rules. It needs a Redeemer.

The Song of Songs offers a striking canonical contrast with Eden. The bride says of her beloved, "Like an apple tree among the trees of the woods… I sat down in his shade with great delight, And his fruit was sweet to my taste" (Song of Songs 2:3). The poem first celebrates human love between bride and groom. Scripture also describes God's relationship with His people as a marriage, which makes the contrast fitting. In Eden desire grasped forbidden fruit. Under the beloved's tree, desire sits down, rests, and receives fruit that is freely given. This is a resonance and not a stated fulfillment, but it fits the larger biblical story. God's own desire for Zion (Psalm 68:16) points toward the day when He dwells with His people forever, and their desire finally matches His.

7. Spirit & Life

James traces the same sequence that châmad reveals in Eden and in Achan's tent: "each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed. Then, when desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death" (James 1:14–15). The battle for holiness is fought first in what we desire. By the time a hand reaches for a neighbor's field or a forbidden pleasure, the heart has usually already said yes.

That is why suppressing desire is not enough. God made us to desire, and empty hearts do not stay empty. Job 20:20 describes the greedy man who "knows no quietness in his heart." The covetous heart never rests. Psalm 19 shows a better way. Desire is redirected toward something worthier than gold, until Scripture tastes sweeter than honey. The psalmist prays, "Incline my heart to Your testimonies, And not to covetousness" (Psalm 119:36). That prayer admits that we cannot redirect our own hearts and asks God to do it.

Isaiah 53:2 also gives believers an honest self-examination. Left to ourselves, we are drawn to what looks impressive and unmoved by a humble, suffering Christ. When a believer finds Jesus beautiful, that is evidence of grace, because the heart has been taught to see what it once passed over. The God who desires to dwell among His people is reshaping their desire until they want Him too.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 21:20 — The house of the wise holds desirable treasure, while the foolish squander what they have. Desirable things are not evil in themselves. Wisdom stores and stewards them.

  • Proverbs 12:12 — The wicked covet the catch of evil men, while the root of the righteous yields fruit. Coveting feeds on others, but righteousness grows its own fruit.

  • Proverbs 1:22 — The scorners delight in their scorning. Desire can become attached even to mockery and folly.

  • Psalm 39:11 — God's rebuke makes a person's beauty melt away like a moth. What people find desirable in themselves is fragile before Him.

  • Deuteronomy 7:25 — Israel must burn carved images and not covet the silver or gold on them, because that desire would become a snare.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H2532 (chemdah)** — A lexical relative of châmad. This noun means a desirable or precious thing, the object that draws desire.

  • Canonical pairing with covetousness as idolatry — The final commandment's "You shall not covet" connects in the New Testament with Paul's statement that covetousness is idolatry (Colossians 3:5) and with his account of the law exposing desire (Romans 7:7). These are connections of theme and teaching. They do not show that the same Hebrew word appears in those verses.
