SUN, HEAT - (chammâh, Strong's H2535)

Summary

The Hebrew word chammâh means "heat," and in the Old Testament's poetry it becomes a name for the sun, the sun as people feel it. It does not simply point to a light in the sky. It describes the sun's burning warmth, its dazzling brightness and its reach into every corner of the earth. The word appears only six times, all in poetry and prophecy. The NKJV renders it "heat" once and "sun" five times.

The few uses that remain are memorable. David says that "there is nothing hidden from its heat" (Psalm 19:6), and the sun's unavoidable warmth becomes a picture of how God's glory confronts every person. Job, burning with fever and grief, says he goes about blackened "but not in the sun" (Job 30:28). The bride in the Song is "clear as the sun" (Song of Songs 6:10). Isaiah then uses the word for the end of history. In one passage the sun is "ashamed" when the LORD reigns in glory (Isaiah 24:23). In another its light grows "sevenfold" on the day God heals His wounded people (Isaiah 30:26). The brightest thing in creation helps us see something brighter still: the glory of God, which Revelation finally locates in the Lamb.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hebrew normally calls the sun shemesh, a common word that names the sun as a heavenly body, the marker of days and seasons. Chammâh is a rarer, poetic word that comes from the idea of being hot. Using it is a little like calling the sun "the blaze" in English, because the name itself draws attention to what the sun does to you. Every occurrence falls in Job, Psalms, the Song of Solomon or Isaiah. People in the ancient Near East lived under a fierce sun, and they knew it as the source of growing crops and also as a danger that could scorch and kill.

Israel's neighbors also worshiped the sun, and some honored it as the god of justice who saw everything. Moses warned Israel not to look up at "the sun, the moon, and the stars" and "feel driven to worship them" (Deuteronomy 4:19). Against that background, the biblical poets describe the sun's heat as something God made and set on its course, and nothing more.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one sense with two closely related uses:

1. Heat, especially the warmth that comes from the sun. This is the literal meaning, as in Psalm 19:6: "there is nothing hidden from its heat."

2. The sun itself, named by its heat and brightness. This is how the NKJV translates most occurrences, as in Isaiah 30:26: "the light of the sun will be sevenfold."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines which side of the sun's power is in view. In Psalm 19 it is the sun's reach, because its heat touches everything. In Job 30 it is the sun's power to darken and burn. In the Song it is brightness that makes people stare in wonder. In Isaiah the sun stands for the greatest glory in the natural world. That glory is either outshone by the LORD (Isaiah 24:23) or magnified by Him (Isaiah 30:26). In three of the six uses the word appears beside the moon, which is a common pairing in Hebrew poetry for the two great lights of creation.

4. Theological Significance

These passages teach that the most powerful thing human eyes can see belongs to God's creation and does not rival Him. Genesis calls the sun "the greater light to rule the day" (Genesis 1:16). Its rule is real, but God assigned it, and it always answers to Him. Psalm 19 has the sun run a circuit God set for it. Isaiah has it blush with shame before Him.

The passages also show that God's presence cannot be avoided. The same nearness that searches people can heal them. Job feels heat as torment. Isaiah promises light and warmth that come with healing. Scripture holds both experiences together without contradiction: no one can hide from God, and God does not search people in order to abandon them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 19:6: nothing hidden. David begins with "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1). This speech has no words, yet "their voice" goes "through all the earth" (19:3–4). Then he describes the sun, running "like a bridegroom" and "like a strong man" (19:5). He ends that description with the heat: "there is nothing hidden from its heat." A person can avoid looking at the sun, but no one can avoid feeling its warmth. In the same way, creation's witness to God reaches every person, so that people are "without excuse" (Romans 1:20). The psalm then moves straight to "The law of the LORD is perfect" (19:7). The sun, which other nations honored as the all-seeing judge, simply runs its course, and God's word is what actually searches and restores the soul. Hebrews 4:13 states the truth behind the image: "there is no creature hidden from His sight."

Job 30:28: burning without the sun. Job says, "I go about mourning, but not in the sun." A little later he describes skin turned black and bones burning with fever (Job 30:30). On the traditional reading, which is also the NKJV's, Job looks like someone scorched by long days in the open, yet the sun did not do this. The burning comes from inside, from disease and from the sense that God has turned against him. Some interpreters read the line differently, as "without comfort." Either way, the verse names suffering that no shade can relieve, and Job cries for help in the assembly while that suffering goes on.

Isaiah 30:26: sevenfold light on the day of healing. This verse uses the word twice: "the light of the moon will be as the light of the sun, And the light of the sun will be sevenfold, As the light of seven days." The verse ties this light to "the day that the LORD binds up the bruise of His people And heals the stroke of their wound." The surrounding verses show what that day involves. The LORD waits "that He may be gracious" (30:18). Weeping ends (30:19). The people hear "This is the way, walk in it" (30:21). They throw away their idols (30:22), and rain, bread and rivers follow (30:23–25). The sun's heat could have been a threat in that land. Here it becomes overflowing life. "The light of seven days" can be read as a whole week of light concentrated into one day, and the number seven also recalls the completed week of creation (Genesis 2:1–3). That connection is suggestive rather than stated, but the main point is clear: when God heals, He does more than restore the old normal. He brings His people into more light than they have ever known.

Isaiah 24:23: the sun ashamed. Isaiah 24 describes God punishing "the host of exalted ones" on high and "the kings of the earth" below (24:21). Then "the moon will be disgraced And the sun ashamed; For the LORD of hosts will reign On Mount Zion... gloriously." Even the ruler of the day hides its face when the true King appears. Isaiah 30 brightens the sun, and Isaiah 24 shames it. Both passages point in the same direction: whether God magnifies creation's light or eclipses it, all glory gives way to His.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah's picture continues to develop through the rest of Scripture. Isaiah 60:19 says the sun "shall no longer be your light by day... But the LORD will be to you an everlasting light." Revelation 21:23 completes the thought: the city "had no need of the sun or of the moon to shine in it, for the glory of God illuminated it. The Lamb is its light." The glory that shames the sun in Isaiah 24 has a face, and it is the crucified and risen Christ.

Malachi uses the more common Hebrew word for sun, but his image brings together the two sides of the sun's power that this word displays. To those who fear God, "The Sun of Righteousness shall arise With healing in His wings" (Malachi 4:2). The same chapter speaks of a burning day for the proud. Like Isaiah 30, Malachi promises that God's heat will bring healing to His people. Christ is the One in whom the searching light of Psalm 19 and the healing light of Isaiah 30 meet.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 19 offers no hiding place, and that is good news before it is frightening news. The God whose gaze reaches everything is also the God who in Isaiah 30 waits "that He may be gracious." Being fully seen by Him is the first step toward being healed by Him.

Job's words give believers language for pain that has no visible cause. A person can be blackened and burning while everyone around them sees only cloudy skies. Scripture does not shame that grief. It records it, and it places alongside it the promise of a day when the LORD "binds up the bruise of His people."

The Song's bride, "Clear as the sun, Awesome as an army with banners" (Song of Songs 6:10), adds another note. Her radiance is beautiful, and it also inspires awe. Many Christians have read this as a picture of what Christ is making of His church: holy and without blemish, as Paul describes it in Ephesians 5. Her brightness is borrowed, just as the sun's is. The light that will one day shame the sun is the same light that now makes His people shine. Whatever dazzles us today, whether power, beauty or success, will look dim on the day the Lamb is the light.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This word occurs in only five verses, and all of them are discussed above. Song of Songs 6:10, which is treated under Spirit & Life, is the only use in which the sun's brightness describes a person, and its comparison joins brightness with awe.
