CURVES - (chammûwq, Strong's H2542)

Summary

The Hebrew word chammûwq means a curve or rounded contour of the body. The NKJV translates it "curves," and older translations such as the KJV render it "joints." It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 7:1, where the bridegroom praises his bride: "The curves of your thighs are like jewels, The work of the hands of a skillful workman." Because the word is used only once, everything we know about its use comes from this single line of love poetry.

Within the Song, the word carries real spiritual weight. In the same breath that he describes her body, the bridegroom calls her "O prince's daughter," and he compares the shape of her body to jewelry made by a master artisan. Scripture does not treat the body as an embarrassment to be ignored or as an object to be used. In the setting of married love, a husband looks at his wife's body with wonder and honors her as a person of dignity. The comparison also points beyond the craftsman to the Creator who made her.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chammûwq is a Hebrew noun related to the verb chamaq (H2559), which means "to turn" or "to turn away." That verb appears earlier in the Song, when the bride opens the door at night and finds that her beloved has already turned away and gone (Song 5:6). The family connection helps explain why a noun for a body's rounded shape could come from a verb of turning: the thigh curves where the body bends and turns. The root explains how the word was formed, but the verse itself shows what the word means.

The word appears in a particular kind of ancient love poem in which the speaker praises a beloved's appearance one feature at a time. Earlier in the Song, the bridegroom describes his bride from head downward (Song 4:1–7). In chapter 7 the description starts at her feet and moves upward. This is the language of joyful admiration between a husband and wife, and it was never meant as clinical anatomy or crude description.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chammûwq refers to the rounded contour of the body. In Song 7:1 it describes the curve of the thighs or hips, the place where the body's shape turns. "Curves" (NKJV) emphasizes the outline of the body. "Joints" (KJV) emphasizes the place where the body bends and moves. The word is purely physical and descriptive. The honor, the artistry, and the delight in the verse come from the comparisons and the address that surround the word.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The single use of the word belongs to a poetic description of the bride, and three details in the verse determine how the word should be heard.

First, the bridegroom addresses her as a "prince's daughter," a title of nobility and honor. Second, he compares her curves to "jewels," that is, to precious ornaments. Third, he credits those jewels to "the work of the hands of a skillful workman," so that the beauty he sees comes from skilled design and did not happen by accident.

Together, these details turn a simple anatomical noun into language of reverent praise. The word itself says only "curve." The verse makes that curve an occasion for honor.

4. Theological Significance

The Song's praise of the body rests on what Genesis teaches. "God created man in His own image... male and female He created them" (Genesis 1:27), and when God finished His work He saw everything He had made and called it very good (Genesis 1:31). The human body is part of that good creation. When the bridegroom compares his bride's form to the craftsmanship of a master workman, he is echoing the Creator's own verdict.

David reaches the same conclusion from the inside: "I will praise You, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made; Marvelous are Your works" (Psalm 139:14). David marvels at how God formed him. The bridegroom marvels at how his bride has been formed. Both responses end in praise.

Some people have treated the body as less spiritual than the soul, or as something shameful in itself. The Song of Solomon stands in Scripture as a correction to that view. God included in His Word a poem that delights in a wife's body with words such as "jewels." That inclusion tells us that married love and physical beauty belong within His good design and need not be hidden from His sight.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 7:1 — beauty joined to dignity. The verse opens with "How beautiful are your feet in sandals, O prince's daughter!" Only after giving her this title does the bridegroom mention the curves of her thighs. The order matters. He does not reduce her to her body. Instead, he praises her body while affirming her nobility. The jewel comparison carries the same thought further, because jewels are kept with care and worn with honor, and no one uses them carelessly. In this verse, desire and respect grow together. A love that admires a person's body while forgetting that person's dignity is a lesser love than the one the Song describes.

Song of Solomon 6:13 — a living, moving presence. The verse just before 7:1 calls out, "Return, return, O Shulamite; Return, return, that we may look upon you!" and then asks, "What would you see in the Shulamite— As it were, the dance of the two camps?" The word "return" here translates a different Hebrew verb from the one related to chammûwq, so the connection between the verses comes from the scene they share rather than from shared vocabulary. The scene is one of motion: she turns, she dances, she is watched with delight. The praise that follows begins with her feet in sandals and moves to the curves of her thighs, which are the very parts of the body a dance sets in motion. Read in this setting, the bridegroom is admiring a living woman in graceful movement, and his praise is aimed at her person and not at a still portrait. Love delights in the presence of the beloved, in the way she walks and turns and fills the moment.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song is first of all about real human love between a husband and wife, and it should be read that way. Yet Scripture also uses marriage to describe the relationship between Christ and His people. Paul writes, "Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ also loved the church and gave Himself for her" (Ephesians 5:25). Christ's purpose is "that He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing" (Ephesians 5:27).

Paul does not cite Song 7:1, so the connection is a resonance between the two passages rather than a stated fulfillment. Still, the resonance is real. The bridegroom in the Song calls his bride noble and compares her to finished jewelry. Christ makes His bride glorious by giving Himself for her and cleansing her "with the washing of water by the word" (Ephesians 5:26). In the Song, the bride's beauty draws her husband's praise. In the gospel, Christ's love produces the beauty He then delights in. He does not merely put up with His people. He intends to present them to Himself radiant and honored.

7. Spirit & Life

The Song teaches believers how to see the body. A Christian does not need to look at the human form, either their own or a spouse's, with suspicion or contempt. Your body is part of what God called very good, and Psalm 139 invites you to praise Him for how He made you. For a married couple, Song 7:1 gives words for delight that honors the other person. That delight is full of admiration, and it is offered to a wife or husband as someone with royal dignity, never as an object.

The verse also gives a test that runs against much of our culture. Wherever attraction cuts the body off from the person, it has left the pattern of Scripture behind. The bridegroom never praises her curves without also honoring her as a "prince's daughter." Husbands and wives can practice the same habit: noticing, naming, and delighting in each other, and treating each other with honor.

Finally, the passage says something about how God regards His people. The One who formed us with the skill of a master workman, and who in Christ gave Himself to make His church glorious, does not look at those He loves with reluctance. His love sees, honors, and rejoices.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Chammûwq occurs only in Song of Solomon 7:1, which is discussed above. The supporting passages, Song 6:13, Genesis 1:27, Psalm 139:14, and Ephesians 5:25–27, are treated in Sections 4–6.
