TURNED AWAY, GAD ABOUT - (châmaq, Strong's H2559)

Summary

The Hebrew verb châmaq means to turn away or slip off, and in one of its uses it means turning this way and that. It appears only twice in the Old Testament, and both times it describes what goes wrong between two people who love each other. In the Song of Solomon the bride finally opens the door, only to find that "my beloved had turned away and was gone" (Song 5:6). In Jeremiah the Lord asks His unfaithful people, "How long will you gad about, O you backsliding daughter?" (Jer 31:22). The first use pictures a lover who slips away after being kept waiting. The second pictures a wife who keeps drifting and dodging instead of coming home.

These two verses show how love is wounded by hesitation and evasion. The bride lost a moment of welcome because she preferred her comfort to her beloved's knock. Israel wore herself out wandering when God had marked a clear road back. Jeremiah does not leave the problem there. In the same breath God announces that He "has created a new thing in the earth," a promise that soon opens into the new covenant, where God writes His law on the heart. Human restlessness is finally cured by God's own work of grace.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châmaq is a rare Hebrew verb with only two uses, one in poetry and one in prophecy. Both settings use the language of marriage. The Song of Solomon is a love poem between a bride and her beloved. Jeremiah 30–31, often called the "Book of Consolation," is a set of promises of restoration addressed to Israel and Judah after judgment. Throughout those chapters God speaks as a father to Ephraim and as a husband to "the virgin of Israel." Because the word appears so seldom, its meaning has to be learned from these two contexts.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic idea is turning away or slipping off. The two uses show two related senses:

1. To turn away or withdraw. One person leaves the place where another expected to find him. "My beloved had turned away and was gone" (Song 5:6).

2. To turn this way and that, or to wander aimlessly. Jeremiah uses a form of the verb that describes repeated, back-and-forth turning. The NKJV renders it "gad about" (Jer 31:22).

The word does not by itself mean final abandonment. It describes slipping away or drifting, and the surrounding passages show the damage that drifting causes.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The two uses differ mainly in who is turning. In the Song the beloved turns away from the bride. In Jeremiah the bride, Israel, is the one turning. One passage shows the pain of an absent lover. The other shows the restlessness of an unfaithful partner.

Jeremiah's form of the verb matters for understanding the verse. It describes a person who keeps turning around and changing direction, unable to commit to one path. That fits the setting well. In the previous verse God has told Israel to "set your heart toward the highway" (Jer 31:21). "Gad about" is the opposite of walking a straight road home.

4. Theological Significance

Both passages show that love calls for a response. The beloved's knocking and God's call to return are invitations. Each can be delayed, dodged, or answered.

The two passages also show God's heart in a striking way. When the beloved puts his hand to the latch, the bride says, "my heart yearned for him" (Song 5:4). Just before Jeremiah's question, the Lord says of wayward Ephraim, "My heart yearns for him; I will surely have mercy on him" (Jer 31:20). In Hebrew the two lines use the same expression, which speaks of being stirred deep within. Ephraim has drifted, yet God's mercy toward him is stronger than his wandering.

Finally, Jeremiah shows that God's answer to human unfaithfulness is His own creative act. He does not answer the question "How long?" with a deadline. He answers it with a promise that He will create something new.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:2–8: The door opened too late. The scene begins at night. The bride is half-asleep when she hears "the voice of my beloved! He knocks, saying, 'Open for me, my sister, my love… For my head is covered with dew'" (5:2). He has come through the cold and wet to reach her. Her answer is a pair of small excuses: "I have taken off my robe; How can I put it on again? I have washed my feet; How can I defile them?" (5:3). She does love him. When his hand moves the latch, her heart yearns for him, and she rises. Her fingers drip with myrrh "on the handles of the lock" (5:5), as though he left a fragrance where his hand had been. But "my beloved had turned away and was gone" (5:6). Châmaq marks the moment the opportunity passed.

What follows is costly. "I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer" (5:6). The watchmen strike her and take her veil (5:7). She sends word that she is "lovesick" (5:8). Through all of it she still calls him "my beloved." The relationship has not ended. Her enjoyment of his presence has been lost for a time, and getting it back takes a hard search. The Song later brings them together again, with the bride able to say that she is her beloved's and he is hers (6:3).

Jeremiah 31:18–22: Wandering when the road is marked. The setting is tender. God has heard Ephraim's prayer, "Restore me, and I will return" (31:18), and his shame over "the reproach of my youth" (31:19). God answers with yearning and mercy (31:20). Then He gives directions: "Set up signposts, Make landmarks; Set your heart toward the highway… Turn back, O virgin of Israel" (31:21). The way home is marked and plain.

Against that clear road comes the question, "How long will you gad about, O you backsliding daughter?" (31:22). Israel's problem in this verse is indecision. She is not marching boldly in the wrong direction. She is turning one way and then another, putting off a clear return. Backsliding often looks like this. It is less a single act of defiance than a long habit of postponing, half-turning, and keeping options open, and it is exhausting.

The verse goes on: "For the LORD has created a new thing in the earth—A woman shall encompass a man." The final line is one of the hardest in the Old Testament, and interpreters have read it in many ways. One reading takes it to mean that the wandering woman, Israel, will at last cling to and embrace her husband. Whatever the precise sense, the flow of the verse is clear. God answers Israel's drifting with an act described in the language of creation, something only He can bring about.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The "new thing" of Jeremiah 31:22 is followed nine verses later by the new covenant. God says it will be "not according to the covenant that I made with their fathers… My covenant which they broke, though I was a husband to them" (31:32). The husband imagery continues from verse 22, and so does the problem of an unfaithful bride. The answer is deeper than better effort: "I will put My law in their minds, and write it on their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be My people" (31:33). It ends with forgiveness: "their sin I will remember no more" (31:34). Ezekiel promises the same inner change: "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you" (Ezek 36:26).

Jesus took up this promise at His last supper, when He called the cup the new covenant in His blood (Luke 22:20). Hebrews 8 quotes Jeremiah 31 at length as fulfilled in Him. The cure for the bride's gadding about is the work of Christ, who forgives her unfaithfulness and gives her a new heart that wants to stay home.

The Song's night scene also has a clear echo in the risen Lord's words: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Rev 3:20). Revelation does not quote the Song. Still, the picture of a loving visitor knocking and waiting for the door to open is the same. It lets Christians read the bride's hesitation as a warning about their own response to Christ.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride's excuses were small. She had put away her robe and washed her feet. She was comfortable, and getting up felt like too much trouble. Most delays in answering God look like this. They are rarely large rebellions. More often a believer senses the Lord's prompting to pray, to make something right, or to speak, and decides it can wait until morning. The Song shows that a moment of offered closeness can pass. This loss is a loss of fellowship. She remains his beloved throughout, and the Song brings them back together. But for a time she seeks without finding. A believer who has grown cold through repeated delays should not conclude that Christ has abandoned him. He should get up and go after the Lord.

Jeremiah addresses a different condition, a heart that keeps wandering. Many Christians recognize themselves in the restless turning of "gad about." They are not openly defiant, yet they are never settled, always drifting toward a new distraction. God's word to such a person is specific: "Set your heart toward the highway." The road home has signposts, and the way back is direct.

Jeremiah's deepest comfort is that the wandering heart is not left to fix itself. The verse that names the problem also announces God's new creation, and the chapter ends with a law written on the heart and sins remembered no more. The same Lord whose "heart yearns" for Ephraim is the One who promises to change him. A believer can therefore answer God's knock, and God's call to return, with confidence. The welcome is real, and the grace that brings the bride home is His.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Châmaq occurs only in Song of Solomon 5:6 and Jeremiah 31:22, and both verses are developed in Section 5. Two closely connected passages help in reading them:

  • Jeremiah 31:20. "My heart yearns for him; I will surely have mercy on him." This verse uses the same expression of inner yearning as Song 5:4 and shows God's tender heart just before His question about Israel's wandering.

  • Revelation 3:20. "Behold, I stand at the door and knock." This verse gives the New Testament form of the knocking image that frames the Song's use of châmaq.
