HAMATHITE - (Chămâthîy, Strong's H2577)

Summary

"Hamathite" is the name of one of the families descended from Canaan, the grandson of Noah through Ham. The word appears only twice in Scripture, both times in genealogies. Genesis 10:18 lists the Hamathite last among Canaan's sons, and 1 Chronicles 1:16 repeats the same list centuries later. The name comes from Hamath, a city on the Orontes River in what is now Syria. A Hamathite is someone who belongs to the people of that city.

The word is rare and plain, but where it stands gives it meaning. The Hamathite closes the list of Canaanite families just before Genesis says, "Afterward the families of the Canaanites were dispersed." These people settled at the far northern edge of the Canaanite world, and that region later became the northern marker of the land God promised Israel. When the Chronicler copied the name for Jews returning from exile, he was reminding a scattered and diminished people that the nations and their borders belong to God's ordering. The rise and fall of empires had not changed that.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chămâthîy is a Hebrew gentilic noun. A gentilic names a person by the people or place he belongs to, the way "Texan" or "Galilean" does in English. It is formed from Hamath (H2574), the name of an important city in the Orontes valley north of Lebanon. Hamath itself appears often in the Old Testament, in border lists, in the story of King David, and in the prophets. The gentilic form "Hamathite" appears only in the two genealogies that list the sons of Canaan.

Both settings are genealogies of nations. Genesis 10, often called the Table of Nations, describes how Noah's descendants spread across the earth after the flood. First Chronicles 1 condenses that record and makes it the opening of a long genealogy that leads to Israel, David, and the people who returned from Babylon.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

"Hamathite" means a member of the people of Hamath, a Canaanite family or clan named for the city where it lived. The word has one sense. It identifies a people group and makes no comment on their character, religion, or fate. Whatever the name Hamath may once have meant, that meaning is not carried into this word. It simply means "the people of Hamath."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The two uses are almost identical. In Genesis 10:18 the Hamathite is the eleventh and last name in the list that begins, "Canaan begot Sidon his firstborn, and Heth" (Gen. 10:15). In 1 Chronicles 1:16 the same three names close the same list: "the Arvadite, the Zemarite, and the Hamathite."

The one difference is what follows. Genesis adds a narrative note about dispersion and borders (Gen. 10:18–19). The Chronicler stops at the name and moves on to the line of Shem. Like the other names in the list, "Hamathite" is singular, with the article: "the Hamathite." It works as a collective, naming a whole people through one representative ancestor.

4. Theological Significance

The names in Genesis 10 are recorded to show that every nation traces back to the family God preserved through the flood. The God who saved Noah's household also knows where each branch of it went. Moses later puts this into song: "When the Most High divided their inheritance to the nations, When He separated the sons of Adam, He set the boundaries of the peoples" (Deut. 32:8). Paul tells the Athenians the same thing, that God "has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26).

The Hamathite is a small example of that truth. A clan from a cursed line (Gen. 9:25) still has its name written in God's record, along with its place on the map. Scripture does not begin with vague legends about faceless peoples. It begins with families, cities, and borders, real places where God would later act.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:15–19: the edge of the Canaanite world

The list of Canaan's sons moves roughly from the coast at Sidon outward to peoples of the interior and the north. The Arvadite, Zemarite, and Hamathite belonged to the northern coast and the Orontes valley. The Hamathite, the farthest north of all, is named last. Then comes the sentence that turns a list into a story: "Afterward the families of the Canaanites were dispersed" (Gen. 10:18).

Genesis 10:19 describes "the border of the Canaanites" from Sidon south toward Gaza and east toward Sodom and Gomorrah. That border describes the heartland that would later be called Canaan. The family list reaches farther, all the way up to Hamath. Genesis is marking out where Canaan's families lived, from the core of the land to its northern fringe.

This prepares for something later. When God gives Moses the borders of the land, the northern line runs "to the entrance of Hamath" (Num. 34:8). Joshua 13:5 uses the same marker for the land still to be possessed, "as far as the entrance to Hamath." The approach to the Hamathites' territory became the northern limit of Israel's inheritance. The people named at the end of Canaan's genealogy stand at the edge of the map God would draw for Abraham's descendants. Long before the conquest, Genesis had already outlined the territory God would deal with.

1 Chronicles 1:13–16: the same names for a returning remnant

The Chronicler wrote for Jews who had come back from Babylon to a small, battered province. By then the political landscape had changed under Assyria, Babylon, and Persia, and Hamath's independent kingdom had long since been overcome. The ancient name, however, remained in the record. The Chronicler still copies the list name by name, ending with "the Hamathite" (1 Chron. 1:16).

He begins Israel's story with Adam and the nations, not with the exile. That choice tells the returned community that their history did not begin with Persian permission or depend on which empire happened to rule. It goes back to God's first ordering of the peoples of the earth. A people holding on to a narrow strip of land around Jerusalem needed to hear that the One who set the boundaries of Canaan's sons was still the God of their inheritance.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word "Hamathite" does not appear again after these lists, but Hamath, the place, keeps returning in Israel's story, and it moves toward hope. Solomon's kingdom gathered worshipers "from the entrance of Hamath" (1 Kings 8:65). The prophets later announced judgment on Hamath along with the other nations. When the northern kingdom fell, the Assyrians resettled people from Hamath in Samaria, bringing their gods with them (2 Kings 17:24, 30). Yet Isaiah promised that the Lord would set His hand again to recover the remnant of His people from many lands, and he names Hamath among them (Isa. 11:11). Ezekiel's vision of the restored land again draws its northern line toward Hamath (Ezek. 47:15–17).

These connections are thematic and canonical. They are not stated fulfillments tied to the word Hamathite. Still, the movement is clear. Read across the canon, a border that first marked the spread of Canaan's family becomes the edge of promise, a place of exile and mixed worship, and finally a place from which God gathers His people. In Christ that gathering goes beyond any map. The Samaria resettled partly by people from Hamath is the same region where Jesus spoke with a Samaritan woman and where Philip later preached Christ (John 4; Acts 8). Paul's words in Acts 17:26 end with a purpose: God fixed the nations' times and boundaries "so that they should seek the Lord" (Acts 17:27, paraphrased). The Table of Nations is a record of the peoples the gospel would one day reach.

7. Spirit & Life

Few readers slow down for a name like "the Hamathite." God thought it worth recording twice. That shows how He regards people and places we pass over. A clan from a cursed line, at the far edge of the map, is still named and still located. The God who wrote that name knows the obscure corners of our own lives as well, and He is working out His purposes there.

The Chronicler's repetition speaks directly to believers who feel small and outmatched by the powers around them. The returned exiles had no independent Davidic kingdom, lived under imperial rule, and held little land, though they could still post armed guards when threatened (Nehemiah 4:13–23). What they had was a record reaching back to God's ordering of the nations. Our security rests on the same thing. The Lord who "set the boundaries of the peoples" (Deut. 32:8) has not given up the government of history to whatever power currently looks strongest.

Hamath's story also shows that the edges of things, where one people ended and another began, where exile happened, where false gods were brought in, are not beyond God's redeeming reach. The boundary of judgment became the boundary of promise, and in Christ the places of scattering become places of gathering.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word occurs only in Genesis 10:18 and 1 Chronicles 1:16, and both are discussed above. The other passages cited in this study, such as Numbers 34:8 and Joshua 13:5, use the related place-name Hamath (H2574) and supply context. They are not additional occurrences of "Hamathite."
