ONE, FIRST, EACH, ANOTHER - (ʼechâd, Strong's H0259)

Summary

ʼEchâd is the ordinary Hebrew word for "one." It counts things: one cubit, one ram, one silver platter. It also marks the first day of a month, singles out "each" curtain or "a certain" man, and pairs "one" with "the other." The NKJV renders it many ways, including "first," "each," "another," "once," "alone," and "same." Most of its uses are plain counting, and many cluster in the detailed instructions for Israel's tabernacle, sacrifices, and temple.

The same small word also carries some of the weightiest statements in Scripture. "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!" (Deuteronomy 6:4). God makes a man and woman "one flesh." He promises His scattered people "one heart" and a divided nation reunited under one king. The prophets look toward a day when "the LORD is one, and His name one" (Zechariah 14:9). Because the LORD is one, He asks for undivided love. Because people cannot produce that love on their own, He promises to give it.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼEchâd is the basic number "one" in Biblical Hebrew and one of the most common words in the Old Testament, appearing nearly a thousand times. It combines with "ten" to form "eleven" and with "twenty" to form "twenty-one," which is why those numbers appear among its translations.

The word is heavily concentrated in Exodus, Numbers, and Ezekiel. Exodus 25–40 uses it to measure the tabernacle. Numbers 7 repeats it again and again as each tribal leader brings an identical offering. Ezekiel 40–48 uses it to measure the visionary temple. It also appears in census lists, dates, battle reports, and everyday narrative. Against this ordinary background, its theological uses stand out sharply.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʼEchâd means "one." Context determines whether it counts, orders, singles out, or contrasts.

1. One (a number or single unit): a single item, person, or occurrence, including "once" and "alone." Numbers 7:13 lists "one silver platter... and one silver bowl."

2. First (in dates and sequence): the first day of a month or of a series. "So the evening and the morning were the first day" (Genesis 1:5).

3. Each, any, a certain one: an individual taken from a group. "The length of each curtain shall be twenty-eight cubits... every one of the curtains shall have the same measurements" (Exodus 26:2).

4. One... the other / another: a contrast within a pair, as when Aaron casts lots for two goats, "one lot for the LORD and the other lot for the scapegoat" (Leviticus 16:8).

The word can also mean "one and the same," as in "the same measurements" (Exodus 26:2). In passages about marriage, hearts, or nations, it describes things being joined into a single whole. The joining itself is taught by those passages; it is not a hidden meaning inside the number.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Counting in worship. Most uses count. The bronze pillars Huram cast for Solomon's temple are "each one eighteen cubits high" (1 Kings 7:13–15). Ezekiel's guide measures the temple wall: "the width of the wall structure, one rod; and the height, one rod" (Ezekiel 40:5). In Numbers 7 the twelve tribal leaders bring identical gifts, and the text records every item twelve times instead of summarizing. The repetition gives each tribe's gift equal honor before God.

Persons becoming one. When ʼechâd describes people, it often means they are united in purpose or bond. Husband and wife "become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Israel gathers "as one man" (Judges 20:1, 8, 11). The builders of Babel "are one" (Genesis 11:6). Whether a given unity is good depends entirely on what it is built around.

Singularity and uniqueness. ʼEchâd can describe something that stands alone in its class. The bridegroom in the Song calls his beloved the only one (Song of Solomon 6:9). Zechariah calls the coming day "one day which is known to the LORD" (Zechariah 14:7). In these places "one" means "unlike any other."

"Once." The word can mark a single occurrence. "Once a year he shall make atonement upon it" (Exodus 30:10). This use becomes important in Section 6.

4. Theological Significance

God's oneness calls for undivided loyalty. The Shema names the LORD as one and immediately calls Israel to love Him with all the heart, soul, and strength (Deuteronomy 6:5). Because there is no second god to share Israel's devotion, divided devotion is ruled out. Malachi draws the same line toward people: "Have we not all one Father? Has not one God created us? Why do we deal treacherously with one another" (Malachi 2:10). The one God who made His people expects them to treat one another faithfully.

God creates the unity worth having. Scripture sets two kinds of oneness side by side. God joins husband and wife, heals divided hearts, and reunites a broken nation. People also unite on their own terms, at Babel and in the "one purse" of the violent in Proverbs 1:14. Unity in itself is not the goal. Unity under God is.

God orders His own worship. Hundreds of measured "ones" in the tabernacle and temple chapters show that the LORD does not leave His worship to guesswork. He specifies the pattern, and Israel's obedience to the exact measure honored His holiness.

God owns time from first to last. Scripture's first numbered day is literally "day one" (Genesis 1:5), set apart when God speaks light into darkness. Zechariah's last great day is also "one day," known to the LORD, when "at evening time it shall happen that it will be light" (Zechariah 14:7). The God who began time with light will end this age with light.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 6:4 and Zechariah 14:9: The LORD is one. Moses speaks the Shema to a people about to enter a land full of gods, each with its own territory and claims. "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!" The statement is about who God is, and it also lays claim to Israel's whole life. A god among many could accept partial loyalty. The one LORD cannot. That is why the very next words command love with "all."

Israel did not live up to this, and the prophets tell the long story of divided worship. Zechariah, however, sees the Shema fulfilled on a worldwide scale: "And the LORD shall be King over all the earth. In that day it shall be, 'The LORD is one,' and His name one." What Israel was told to confess, the whole earth will one day acknowledge. No rival name will remain.

Genesis 2:24 and Genesis 11:6: Two kinds of oneness. In Eden, God brings the woman to the man, and Scripture concludes, "they shall become one flesh." Two distinct persons remain two persons, yet God makes them a single household and life. Their difference is preserved inside the union.

Babel is a counterfeit. "Indeed the people are one and they all have one language." Their unity serves self-glory: "let us make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:4). God scatters them because unity organized against Him only multiplies rebellion. Read together, these passages show that God values oneness He forms around Himself and opposes oneness formed to replace Him.

Jeremiah 32:39 and Ezekiel 11:19: The gift of one heart. Both prophets spoke to people whose hearts had split between the LORD and idols. Both use ʼechâd for what God will do. "I will give them one heart and one way, that they may fear Me forever" (Jeremiah 32:39). "I will give them one heart, and I will put a new spirit within them, and take the stony heart out of their flesh" (Ezekiel 11:19).

The verb in both is "give." God does not tell the exiles to pull themselves together. He promises surgery: the stone heart removed, a heart of flesh given, a new spirit placed within. "One heart" points both inward and outward. It means an undivided heart toward God, and it means a people sharing the same devotion. Jeremiah adds "one way," so a single heart produces a single direction of life. Ezekiel 36:26 repeats the promise of a new heart and new spirit for the whole nation.

Ezekiel 37:15–24: Two sticks become one. For centuries Israel and Judah had been rival kingdoms, sometimes at war. God tells Ezekiel to write "For Judah" on one stick and "For Joseph, the stick of Ephraim" on "another stick" (37:16), and then to hold them so they become one in his hand (37:17). The explanation repeats ʼechâd insistently: God will make them one nation, with one king over them all, and they will no longer be two nations (37:22). That king is "David My servant," and they will "all have one shepherd" (37:24). Ezekiel had already promised "one shepherd," God's servant David, in place of the many shepherds who fed themselves and scattered the flock (34:23). God answers the wound of division by placing His people under a single king of His choosing.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

When asked for the greatest commandment, Jesus begins with the Shema: the Lord our God is one Lord, so love Him with all your heart (Mark 12:29–30). He does not set aside Israel's central confession. He places it at the root of everything.

Ezekiel's "one shepherd" and "one king" find their answer in Jesus' words: "there will be one flock and one shepherd" (John 10:16). The "other sheep... not of this fold" show that the reunion Ezekiel pictured reaches beyond Israel and Judah to gather people from the nations under the Son of David.

Two smaller patterns run in the same direction. Fifty gold clasps join the tabernacle curtains "so that it may be one tabernacle" (Exodus 26:6; compare 36:13), many materials and pieces forming one dwelling for God. Paul describes the church as a building "fitted together" that grows into a holy temple, "a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:21–22). Numbers 15:15–16 gives "one ordinance" and "one law" for the Israelite and the stranger alike, showing God's heart for including the outsider on equal terms before Him; Christ makes "one new man" from Jew and Gentile (Ephesians 2:15). Neither Exodus nor Numbers predicts the church, and Ephesians does not name the tabernacle, but the picture of many joined into one dwelling and one people carries forward.

Jesus also quotes Genesis 2:24, "the two shall become one flesh" (Matthew 19:5), and draws the conclusion that what God has joined, no one should separate. Because the oneness of marriage is God's work, it carries God's authority.

The Day of Atonement adds another thread. The high priest entered the Most Holy Place "once a year" (Exodus 30:10; Leviticus 16:34). That single day was the high point of Israel's calendar, yet it had to come back every year. Hebrews builds on this pattern. The earthly priest entered once a year with blood not his own (Hebrews 9:7), while Christ entered the heavenly sanctuary once for all with His own blood, having obtained eternal redemption (Hebrews 9:12). The annual "once" of Leviticus gives way to the unrepeatable once of the cross.

Finally, Zechariah's "one day," when "the LORD is one, and His name one," points to the consummation of the kingdom. The confession that began in Moab becomes the confession of the whole earth.

7. Spirit & Life

The Shema exposes how easily the heart divides. James describes "a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways" (James 1:8). Most believers know that tension: loving God sincerely while keeping a second loyalty in reserve, whether money, reputation, or comfort. The one LORD does not accept a share. That can sound severe until we remember why. He is not one god among several offering competing goods. He is the only true good, and half-loyalty to Him is loyalty given to something lesser.

The prophets offer hope. God does not demand an undivided heart and then leave us to manufacture one. "I will give them one heart." A believer who feels divided does not have to treat that as a final verdict. It is a reason to ask God for what He promised to give, and the new spirit He places within is the means by which He keeps that promise.

The word also turns us toward each other. Malachi links one Father to how brothers treat one another, and Jesus links one Shepherd to one flock. People who confess one God and are gathered by one Shepherd cannot comfortably keep up rivalries that God has set out to end. The stick of Judah and the stick of Ephraim did not join because they finally agreed. They joined in the prophet's hand. Christians are made one the same way, in the hand of the Shepherd who holds us all.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 27:4. David names "one thing" he desires: to dwell in the LORD's house and behold His beauty. It is a single desire that organizes every other.

  • Exodus 12:46 and Psalm 34:20. The Passover lamb was to have no bone broken, and the psalm says of the righteous that not one of his bones is broken. John 19:36 sees this fulfilled at the cross.

  • Ecclesiastes 4:9–12. "Two are better than one," and woe to the one who falls alone. Here ʼechâd shows how vulnerable a person is in isolation.

  • 2 Chronicles 30:12. God's hand gave Judah "singleness of heart" to obey Hezekiah's call to Passover, a small historical picture of the heart God promises in Jeremiah and Ezekiel.

  • Zephaniah 3:9. God will purify the peoples' speech so that all may call on the LORD and serve Him with one accord, a reversal of Babel's scattering.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Some argue that ʼechâd itself implies plurality within God because it can describe compound unities such as "one flesh." The word does not carry that meaning on its own. It is equally at home counting a single ram. The Shema's force lies in God's exclusive claim against all rivals. Christian teaching about the Father, Son, and Spirit rests on the wider testimony of Scripture, not on the definition of this number.

Exploratory associations. Joshua 22:20 recalls that Achan did not perish alone in his iniquity; one man's sin brought wrath on the whole congregation. This anticipates, without stating, Paul's contrast between the one man Adam and the one Man Jesus Christ (Romans 5:12–19).

On the Day of Atonement, two goats together form the sin offering (Leviticus 16:5), with lots cast for "one" and "the other" (16:8). One goat dies, and the other carries the people's sins away. Christians have often seen both actions fulfilled in Christ's single work. That connection is a fruitful reflection rather than something the passage itself teaches.
