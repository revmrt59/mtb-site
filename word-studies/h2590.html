EMBALM, RIPEN - (chânaṭ, Strong's H2590)

Summary

The Hebrew verb chânaṭ is a rare word that describes two very different processes. It is usually connected with spices and aroma. In Genesis 50 it describes embalming, the careful treatment of a dead body so that it would not decay. In the Song of Solomon it describes the fig tree as it "puts forth her green figs" when spring arrives. The word is rare: it occurs 5 times in 4 verses of the Hebrew Bible (Genesis 50:2 has two). Four of those uses belong to the deaths of Jacob and Joseph in Egypt, and the fifth to an orchard coming to life.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

In Genesis the embalming of Jacob and Joseph is bound up with waiting on God's promise. Their bodies were kept until they could be carried out of Egypt for the land God swore to give Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Genesis ends with Joseph embalmed and laid in a coffin, still waiting. The Song of Solomon uses the same word when winter is past and new life swells from within the tree. Set side by side, the two uses picture the story of God's people from death held back in hope to life that rises up and calls the beloved to "come away."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chânaṭ is a Hebrew verb found only in Genesis 50 and Song of Solomon 2. Standard Hebrew dictionaries connect it with spicing or aromatic preparation. Embalming in the ancient world involved preservatives and fragrant materials applied to a body. That is the setting of Genesis 50, where Jacob and then Joseph die in Egypt, a land well known for preserving its dead.

The Song of Solomon setting is pastoral and poetic: an orchard in early spring, with figs forming and vines blossoming. The same verse that uses chânaṭ says the vines "give a good smell." Fragrance appears in both settings, which may help explain why one word could serve both.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chânaṭ has two principal senses:

1. To embalm: to prepare a dead body with spices and preservatives so that it is kept from decay. Example: "So the physicians embalmed Israel" (Genesis 50:2).

2. To ripen or put forth fruit: the natural swelling and sweetening of fruit in its season. Example: "The fig tree puts forth her green figs" (Song of Solomon 2:13).

The first sense describes something people do to the dead from the outside. The second describes something a living tree does from within.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Genesis, chânaṭ is always an action done to someone who has died. Joseph commands it for his father, and others carry it out for Joseph. Every use of this word for embalming takes place in Egypt and concerns a descendant of Abraham who died far from the land of promise.

In the Song, the subject of the verb is a fig tree. Nobody applies anything to it. The fruit develops because the season has turned. This difference in subject changes the picture completely. In Egypt, human hands work against decay. In the orchard, life comes on its own because winter has ended.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God whose promises reach beyond the grave. Jacob and Joseph died without possessing Canaan, yet neither treated death as the end of God's word to them. The embalming in Genesis is quiet evidence that God's people expected Him to keep His oath to Abraham even after they were dead.

Read within the whole of Scripture, the spring scene in the Song of Solomon shows the other side of God's ways. He governs the seasons and brings life after barrenness. "For lo, the winter is past, the rain is over and gone" (Song of Solomon 2:11). The fig tree does not force its fruit, and the gardener does not spice it. The sweetness comes because God has turned the year.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 50:2, 24–26: Embalming in the service of faith. In Egypt, embalming was closely tied to belief about the afterlife and the journey of the dead. Genesis keeps that religious world at a distance. "Joseph commanded his servants the physicians to embalm his father" (Genesis 50:2). The text names physicians, Joseph's own servants. Many interpreters see this as a deliberate choice to avoid the priests who usually handled embalming in Egypt. Whether or not that was Joseph's intention, Genesis never presents the practice as Egyptian worship. Genesis sets the embalming beside Jacob's request to be buried with his fathers in Canaan (Genesis 49:29–32; 50:5–13). His body was kept and then carried on that journey.

Joseph's own death makes the purpose plainer. He told his brothers, "God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land to the land of which He swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob" (Genesis 50:24). Then he made them swear: "you shall carry up my bones from here" (Genesis 50:25). Joseph's embalmed body became a standing promise in Egypt. His coffin remained a witness through the generations of bondage that Israel would one day leave. Centuries later "Moses took the bones of Joseph with him" (Exodus 13:19). Joshua 24:32 records their burial at Shechem. Hebrews treats the whole matter as faith: "By faith Joseph, when he was dying, made mention of the departure of the children of Israel, and gave instructions concerning his bones" (Hebrews 11:22). Joseph's instructions show that he looked past the embalming to the day God would act.

Genesis 50:26: How Genesis ends. Genesis opens with "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1). It moves to a garden full of life and to the blessing "be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28). It closes with these words: "they embalmed him, and he was put in a coffin in Egypt" (Genesis 50:26). The book begins with God breathing life into man and ends with a man embalmed in a foreign land. The patriarchs received the promises, but they died as strangers who had not yet received the land. This is the result of the fall: even the best of God's servants end in a coffin. Yet the coffin was not sealed in despair, because it rested on the oath Joseph had required of his brothers. Exodus picks up the story at that point: "the children of Israel were fruitful and increased abundantly" (Exodus 1:7). The blessing of Genesis 1 continues even while the coffin waits, and God's visitation is coming.

Song of Solomon 2:11–13: The fig tree ripens. The only other use of chânaṭ appears in a love song. The woman hears her beloved calling her out into the spring: "The flowers appear on the earth; the time of singing has come" (2:12). Then: "The fig tree puts forth her green figs, and the vines with the tender grapes give a good smell. Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away!" (2:13). The word that described spices applied to a corpse now describes sweetness rising from inside a living tree. The figs need no embalmer. The season is the reason: winter is over. The ripening fig is followed by a summons to rise and go, and Genesis 50 ended with a similar expectation of departure.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The two uses of chânaṭ point to a larger biblical movement from death preserved in hope to life that rises. The Gospels describe the same tension with different Greek words. After the crucifixion, Nicodemus brought a large quantity of myrrh and aloes, and Jesus' body was wrapped with spices according to Jewish burial custom (John 19:39–40). On the first day of the week the women came with more spices (Mark 16:1; Luke 24:1). Their spices were not needed, because the tomb was empty. Joseph's embalmed body waited centuries in Egypt for God to visit. Christ's body lay in the tomb only until the third day, when He rose (1 Corinthians 15:4), and Peter preached that God would not allow His Holy One to see corruption (Acts 2:27–31). Joseph's remains waited to be carried out; Jesus' body never saw corruption at all.

The Song's spring scene matches the hope of the gospel. Paul calls the risen Christ "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). The Song does not prophesy the resurrection, but it does supply its imagery: winter past, early fruit appearing, and the voice of the beloved saying, "Rise up... and come away." Jesus also used the fig tree as a sign of the season. When its branch becomes tender and puts out leaves, summer is near (Matthew 24:32). The God who kept Joseph's bones for the Exodus has brought the true spring in His Son. He will finish it when the dead in Christ are raised.

7. Spirit & Life

Joseph's coffin offers believers a pattern for how to die. His last request looked past Egypt's honors, though Egypt had mourned his father seventy days. He set his heart on what God had sworn, and he arranged his own burial as a confession that God would keep His word. A Christian who faces death can do the same. A believer's final instructions, funeral, and grave can all say that he or she expects God to visit.

The contrast between the two uses of chânaṭ also speaks to the inner life. Much religious effort works like embalming. It applies outward fragrance to something without life and tries to slow its decay. That is the most human hands can do. The fig tree in the Song shows another kind of sweetness, one that grows from inside because the season has changed. This is an analogy, not the teaching of the verse itself, but it fits the gospel well. The fragrance of a Christian life is not applied from outside. It grows from the life Christ gives. Where the believer's heart feels like winter, the Song gives the Bridegroom's word: "the winter is past." His call is not to preserve what is dead but to "rise up... and come away."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Every occurrence of chânaṭ is discussed above. One detail in Genesis 50:3 adds texture: "Forty days were required for him, for such are the days required for those who are embalmed; and the Egyptians mourned for him seventy days." The careful timing shows how fully Jacob was honored in Egypt. Even so, Jacob had already charged his sons to bury him with his fathers in Canaan (Genesis 49:29).
