LACK, WANT, DECREASE - (châçêr, Strong's H2637)

Summary

Châçêr is the Hebrew verb for lacking, falling short, or running low. It can describe floodwaters going down, a number coming up short, a jar of oil running dry, or a person going without bread. It names the gap between what is needed and what is on hand. Scripture uses this ordinary word to make some of its most comforting promises: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want" (Psalm 23:1).

The word's significance comes from what God does with lack. He prevents it. In the wilderness the manna left no one short, and Israel "lacked nothing" for forty years. At Zarephath a widow's oil did not run dry. He also sends lack as judgment on rebellion, and He commands His people to meet a poor brother's lack with an open hand. Psalm 8 uses the same word for humanity being made "a little lower" than the angels, and Hebrews applies that line to Jesus, who took the lower place so that His people would lack no good thing.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châçêr is a common Hebrew verb, and it appears in many kinds of writing: the flood narrative, Abraham's prayer, the wilderness story, the law, the Psalms, the Wisdom books, love poetry, and the prophets. The idea of falling short became so familiar in Hebrew that later Jewish scribes used a form of the same word for a "deficient" spelling, one written without an optional vowel letter. In Scripture, though, the word almost always concerns real life: food, water, oil, money, numbers, and the needs of people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to be short of something, to have less than is needed or expected. Context decides what is lacking and why.

1. To decrease or diminish, as when the floodwaters "decreased" after the rain stopped (Genesis 8:3, 5), or when Abraham asks about the city if the righteous are five "less" than fifty (Genesis 18:28).

2. To lack or be in want, to go without what life requires: "he who gathered little had no lack" (Exodus 16:18).

3. To fail or run out, as with the widow's jar of oil that would not "run dry" (1 Kings 17:14).

4. To cause someone to lack, to deprive or set lower. Ecclesiastes 4:8 uses it of a man depriving himself of good, and Psalm 8:5 uses it of humanity being made "a little lower than the angels."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses involve material need, and the setting changes what the lack means. In the wilderness and Psalms, the word usually appears with a negative: no lack, nothing lacking, shall not want. These are statements about God's care. In the prophets, lack is often the outcome of judgment. Ezekiel acts out the siege so that Jerusalem's people will lack bread and water (Ezekiel 4:17). In Wisdom literature, lack shows the difference between the righteous and the wicked, whose stomach will be in want (Proverbs 13:25).

The causative use is worth noticing. When someone makes another person lack something, the word points to a moral act. Isaiah's fool causes the drink of the thirsty to fail (Isaiah 32:6), which is cruelty that takes from the needy. In Psalm 8:5 it is God who places humanity a little lower. The same verb can describe either a wicked act of taking away or God's deliberate ordering of creation.

4. Theological Significance

Lack shows how dependent creatures are. Many uses of châçêr concern people who need bread, water, oil, and clothing and cannot always secure them. Scripture does not treat this need as shameful. It treats it as the place where God shows His character.

God is the one who keeps His people from lack. Moses promised a land "in which you will lack nothing" (Deuteronomy 8:9, paraphrased), and God's past provision in the wilderness stood behind that promise. Yet God also withholds and removes provision when His people turn from Him, as Ezekiel's siege shows. Lack therefore is never random in Scripture. It may be prevented by God's mercy or brought on by His judgment, and either way God is involved in it.

Because God supplies His people, He expects them to supply one another. In the law of release, Israel is told not to harden its heart or shut its hand against a poor brother, but to lend him what he needs, literally "his lack which is lacking to him" (Deuteronomy 15:8). The God who kept a whole nation from lack in the desert now gives His people a share in that work.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 16:18 — the manna. When Israel measured the manna, "he who gathered much had nothing left over, and he who gathered little had no lack. Every man had gathered according to each one's need." The miracle was exact sufficiency. On ordinary gathering days nobody could store up a surplus, and nobody went short; before the Sabbath God Himself commanded and preserved a double portion (Exodus 16:22–24). Through the daily portion God was teaching a nation to depend on Him one day at a time. Paul quotes this verse in 2 Corinthians 8:15 when he asks the Corinthians to give to the struggling believers in Judea: "your abundance may supply their lack, that their abundance also may supply your lack" (8:14). God once evened out the manna in the camp, and now He evens out need in the church through believers who give freely.

Deuteronomy 2:7 and Nehemiah 9:21 — forty years without lack. Moses reminds Israel, "He knows your trudging through this great wilderness. These forty years the LORD your God has been with you; you have lacked nothing." Those forty years were a judgment. A generation died in the desert because it refused to trust God at Kadesh. Even so, God was with them, and He knew how tired they were. Centuries later, Nehemiah's prayer of confession repeats the point: "They lacked nothing; Their clothes did not wear out And their feet did not swell." The confession is honest about Israel's sin, and it is just as honest about God's faithfulness. His discipline did not stop His provision.

Psalm 23:1 and Psalm 34:10 — the Shepherd's promise. "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want." The promise rests completely on who the Shepherd is. Sheep do not supply themselves, and the psalm makes no claim about the sheep's ability to provide. Psalm 34:10 sharpens the point with a contrast: "The young lions lack and suffer hunger; But those who seek the LORD shall not lack any good thing." The lion is the strongest hunter in the land, and it still goes hungry. Strength cannot guarantee supply. Those who seek the Lord will not lack, and the psalm specifies what they will not lack: any good thing. The Shepherd decides what is good, and He does not withhold it.

1 Kings 17:14 — the widow's jar. During a famine God Himself had sent, Elijah told a destitute widow in Zarephath, "The bin of flour shall not be used up, nor shall the jar of oil run dry, until the day the LORD sends rain on the earth." Here the word means running out, the ordinary result of using something up. God's word stopped that process for one household that received His prophet, and He kept it stopped until the drought ended. A Gentile widow who had almost nothing ended up with more security than Israel's king.

Jeremiah 44:18 — the wrong explanation. The Judeans who had fled to Egypt told Jeremiah, "since we stopped burning incense to the queen of heaven . . . we have lacked everything and have been consumed by the sword and by famine." Their lack was real, but they gave the wrong reason for it. Jeremiah answers that the disaster came because they had burned incense to other gods and refused to obey the Lord (Jeremiah 44:22–23, paraphrased). A heart set on idols will read its own suffering as a reason to go back to the sin that caused it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Psalm 8:5 marvels that God made man "a little lower than the angels" and "crowned him with glory and honor." The Hebrew literally speaks of being made to lack a little. Hebrews 2:8–9 admits that we do not yet see everything placed under humanity, "But we see Jesus, who was made a little lower than the angels, for the suffering of death crowned with glory and honor, that He, by the grace of God, might taste death for everyone." This is a stated New Testament application. The Son, "being in the form of God," made Himself of no reputation and humbled Himself to the death of the cross (Philippians 2:6–8). He accepted the lower place so that humanity's lost crown could be restored in Him.

The Shepherd psalm finds its fulfillment in Jesus' words: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The promise "I shall not want" now rests on the cross, and Paul draws the conclusion: "He who did not spare His own Son . . . how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things?" (Romans 8:32). Jesus also pointed to the widow of Zarephath in His first sermon at Nazareth as a sign that God's mercy reaches beyond Israel (Luke 4:25–26), and the flour and oil that never ran out foreshadow the One who fed thousands and called Himself the bread of life.

7. Spirit & Life

The manna teaches a kind of trust that many believers resist. On ordinary gathering days God gave enough for the day, teaching His people to trust Him for tomorrow rather than store up their own security. "I shall not want" does not mean a full storehouse. It means a Shepherd who is present every morning. Ecclesiastes 4:8 shows the opposite case: a man with no one to share with, toiling without end and depriving himself of good, never satisfied with his riches. A person can have plenty and still lack the good that matters.

The wilderness passages speak to anyone living under the consequences of their own sin. God did not stop feeding Israel while He disciplined them, and He knew how hard the journey was. That is a reason to stop running from Him and to confess honestly, as Nehemiah did, without despair.

Jeremiah 44 warns against reading hardship the wrong way. When things are lacking, the heart will look for someone to blame, and it often blames the very God it has left. The right response is to return to Him.

Deuteronomy 15:8 and 2 Corinthians 8 bring this down to ordinary life. The provision God gives us is partly meant for someone else's lack. Meeting a brother's need with an open hand is one of the ways the Shepherd keeps His promise to His flock.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 8:3, 5 — The floodwaters "decreased" steadily. The first uses of the word mark judgment receding and the earth being restored.

  • Genesis 18:28 — Abraham asks whether God will destroy the city if the righteous are five "less" than fifty. His intercession bargains over a shortfall, and God keeps answering in mercy.

  • Proverbs 13:25 — The righteous eat to their satisfaction, while the stomach of the wicked will "want." Godliness and sufficiency go together.

  • Proverbs 31:11 — The husband of the virtuous wife trusts her and will have no "lack" of gain. Her faithfulness keeps the household supplied.

  • Isaiah 51:14 — God promises the captive exile that he will be freed, will not die in the pit, and that his bread will not "fail."

  • Ezekiel 4:17 — The siege rations are meant so that the people "lack" bread and water, the lack itself becoming the judgment on their sin.
