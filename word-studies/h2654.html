DELIGHT, DESIRE, PLEASE - (châphêts, Strong's H2654)

Summary

The Hebrew verb châphêts describes a heart that leans toward something. It means to delight in, to desire, to be pleased with, or simply to want. The word can be ordinary. In Deuteronomy 25:7 it describes a man who does not want to marry his brother's widow. It can also express strong personal desire, as when Shechem delights in Jacob's daughter (Genesis 34:19). Its greatest weight in Scripture comes from how often God is the one who delights. In this word, what God wills and what God enjoys are held together.

David testifies that the LORD "delivered me because He delighted in me" (Psalm 18:19). The prophets use the same verb to say that God does not delight in sacrifices brought by unchanged hearts. He delights in mercy, and He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked. Most startling of all, Isaiah says, "it pleased the LORD to bruise Him" (Isaiah 53:10). The rest of the verse ties that pleasure to what the Servant's suffering would accomplish. The psalms also show the response this produces in people. When God turns a person's desires toward Himself, that person can say with Asaph that there is nothing on earth he desires besides God (Psalm 73:25).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Châphêts is a Hebrew verb found from Genesis to Malachi. Its largest concentrations are in Psalms and Isaiah, with smaller clusters in Esther and Job. It appears in four main settings:

  • legal decisions about what a person is willing to do (Deuteronomy 25:7–8; Ruth 3:13)

  • royal favor at a king's court (Esther 6)

  • prayer and worship in the Psalms

  • prophetic preaching about what God truly wants

The verb belongs to a word family that includes the noun chephets, meaning "delight" or "pleasure." That noun is the word behind Psalm 1:2: "his delight is in the law of the LORD." In one unusual passage, Job 40:17, the verb describes the great beast Behemoth moving his tail like a cedar. This is a rare physical use, and its exact sense there is debated. Elsewhere the verb always describes what someone wants or enjoys.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, châphêts means to be inclined toward something with both desire and will. The word joins choosing with enjoying. Context decides which side stands in front.

1. To want, be willing, or choose to do. The emphasis falls on decision. Examples include the redeemer who may or may not be willing to act (Ruth 3:13) and Solomon finishing "all Solomon's desire which he wanted to do" (1 Kings 9:1), where this verb stands behind the word "wanted." When God is the subject, this sense expresses His freedom: "He does whatever He pleases" (Psalm 115:3).

2. To delight in or be pleased with. The emphasis falls on affection and favor toward a person or thing. Examples include Saul's servants telling David that the king delights in him (1 Samuel 18:22) and the LORD delighting in David (Psalm 18:19).

3. To move or bend (rare, literal). This physical sense appears only in the description of Behemoth's tail (Job 40:17).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The verb's force depends on who is delighting and in what.

Human delight can be honorable or corrupt. Esther 6 repeats the phrase about the man the king delights to honor, and Haman assumes the phrase must describe him. Proverbs 18:2 says a fool has no delight in understanding, but only in expressing his own heart. In Isaiah 66:3, people's souls delight in their abominations.

When God is the subject, the verb carries great weight. Sometimes it expresses His freedom to act (Psalm 135:6; Proverbs 21:1). At other times it expresses His affection for someone (Psalm 18:19; Isaiah 62:4).

The prophets often use the word in the negative: "I do not delight in the blood of bulls" (Isaiah 1:11). These denials expose the gap between what worshipers assume God wants and what actually pleases Him. In Isaiah 65:12 and 66:4, Israel's tragedy is summed up as choosing exactly what God does not delight in.

4. Theological Significance

This verb shows that God's sovereignty is never cold. "Our God is in heaven; He does whatever He pleases" (Psalm 115:3). Psalm 135:6 extends that freedom to heaven, earth, the seas, and "all deep places." Even the pagan sailors in Jonah's storm confess, "You, O LORD, have done as it pleased You" (Jonah 1:14). These verses picture a God whom no outside pressure forces to act against His own desire. What He does flows from what He delights in. That is why His word "shall accomplish what I please" (Isaiah 55:11). God's purpose and His pleasure cannot be separated.

The same verb tells us what God's pleasure actually is. "I desire mercy and not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6). God tells the boaster to glory in knowing Him, the LORD who exercises "lovingkindness, judgment, and righteousness in the earth. For in these I delight" (Jeremiah 9:24). Micah asks, "Who is a God like You," and answers that He pardons iniquity "because He delights in mercy" (Micah 7:18).

God's forgiveness, then, does not come grudgingly. Mercy is what God enjoys doing. A sovereign who does whatever He pleases, and whose pleasure is mercy, is very good news for sinners.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 14:8 and Psalm 18:19 — rescue rooted in delight. At Kadesh, ten of the spies said Israel was not able to go up against the people of the land, and the congregation spoke of returning to Egypt. Joshua and Caleb staked everything on one condition: "If the LORD delights in us, then He will bring us into this land and give it to us." Centuries later, David looked back on his rescue from Saul and every enemy and gave the reason in the same terms: "He delivered me because He delighted in me" (Psalm 18:19; 2 Samuel 22:20).

What Joshua and Caleb hoped for on behalf of the nation, David confessed as personal experience. In both passages, deliverance is traced to God's pleasure in His people rather than to their own strength. David says the LORD brought him "out into a broad place." God's delight gave David room to breathe after years of being hunted.

Psalm 51 — the sacrifice God does want. David writes after his sin with Bathsheba, and the verb appears three times in the psalm.

  • "You desire truth in the inward parts" (Psalm 51:6).

  • "You do not desire sacrifice, or else I would give it" (Psalm 51:16).

  • "Then You shall be pleased with the sacrifices of righteousness" (Psalm 51:19).

These verses do not contradict each other. Between verses 16 and 19 stands the broken spirit and contrite heart of verse 17. Offerings brought to cover sin without repentance give God no pleasure. The same offerings, brought by a heart God has humbled and cleansed, please Him. The word "Then" in verse 19 marks the change.

Ezekiel 18 and 33 — no pleasure in death. The exiles complained that God's ways were unfair. God answered with a question: "Do I have any pleasure at all that the wicked should die? … and not that he should turn from his ways and live?" (Ezekiel 18:23). The chapter ends with the same truth and a direct appeal: "I have no pleasure in the death of one who dies … Therefore turn and live!" (Ezekiel 18:32). Ezekiel 33:11 repeats it again, with God swearing by His own life. God's justice is real, and the wicked who refuse to turn do die. Yet God's desire leans toward their return.

Isaiah 53:10 — the pleasure that bruised the Servant. After describing a Servant who was despised, wounded, and silent before His accusers, Isaiah writes: "Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise Him; He has put Him to grief." The rest of the verse explains what pleased the LORD:

  • the Servant's soul becomes "an offering for sin"

  • "He shall see His seed"

  • "He shall prolong His days"

  • "the pleasure of the LORD shall prosper in His hand"

That last phrase uses the related noun. God's pleasure looked through the suffering to what it would accomplish: a family of descendants, life beyond death, and God's saving purpose succeeding. Verse 11 continues the thought, saying that the Servant will see the result of His anguish and be satisfied, and will justify many. Isaiah does not present the pain as pleasing for its own sake; the verse ties the LORD's pleasure to the redemption the suffering would win.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The verb's story comes to its climax at the cross. Psalm 22:8 records the mockers' taunt that the LORD should rescue the sufferer, since He delights in Him. Matthew 27:41–43 shows the chief priests, scribes, and elders at Golgotha throwing nearly the same words at Jesus. They reasoned as Psalm 18 does: if God delights in someone, He delivers him.

The mockers were right that the Father delighted in His Son. They were wrong about what that delight would do. Read in the light of Isaiah 53, the Father's delight carried Jesus through death and out of the grave, so that He could "see His seed" (Isaiah 53:10).

The cross also holds together two statements that seem to pull apart. God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked (Ezekiel 18:32), yet it pleased Him to bruise His righteous Servant. The death God was pleased to appoint for His Servant was the death that opens the way for the wicked to turn and live. Peter echoes Ezekiel's heart when he writes that the Lord is longsuffering, not willing that any should perish (2 Peter 3:9).

Jesus also took up the prophets' use of this word. Twice He confronted the Pharisees with Hosea 6:6, telling them to go and learn what it means (Matthew 9:13) and saying that if they had known what it means, they would not have condemned the guiltless (Matthew 12:7). Hebrews 10:5–10 places the words of Psalm 40:6–8 in the mouth of Christ. In that psalm, God did not desire sacrifice and offering, and the speaker delights to do God's will. Christ is the obedient Son who offered God what the animal offerings could never supply. He gave His own body, wholly given to the Father's will.

7. Spirit & Life

When God turns a heart toward Himself, that heart begins to lean toward what He loves. The psalmist says he delights to do God's will (Psalm 40:8). He prays to be led in the path of God's commandments because he delights in it (Psalm 119:35). God's commands stop feeling like a fence and start feeling like a road the heart wants to travel.

Asaph shows how this happens in a real life. In Psalm 73 he confesses that his feet had almost slipped, because he envied the prosperity of the wicked. Their ease made his own faithfulness seem pointless. The turn came when he went into the sanctuary of God (Psalm 73:17). In God's presence he saw where the wicked were heading and who God was to him. Then he asked whom he had in heaven but God, and confessed that there was no one on earth he desired besides Him (Psalm 73:25).

Psalm 73 locates the healing of Asaph's desire in God's presence, where he saw God clearly, not in greater effort. The wicked in Job 21:14 tell God to depart, because they do not desire the knowledge of His ways. Asaph discovered the reverse: God Himself is the good worth wanting. His turn offers a picture of the Christian life as a heart learning to want God, the opposite of a list of duties carried out with gritted teeth.

The Song of Solomon adds a gentle warning. Three times the bride urges the daughters of Jerusalem not to stir up or awaken love until it pleases (Song of Solomon 2:7; 3:5; 8:4). Love cannot be hurried into being or forced on schedule. That is the bride's counsel about human love, but it also fits how God draws people. He does not want worship that is forced or faked. He wants a heart that freely delights in Him, and He is patient as He awakens it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 34:19 — Shechem delights in Dinah after violating her. This first use shows that human desire can be strong yet badly disordered.

  • Esther 6:6–11 — The king asks how to honor the man he delights in. Haman's assumption that he is the one becomes the turning point of his downfall.

  • Proverbs 21:1 — The king's heart is in the LORD's hand, and He turns it wherever He wishes. God's pleasure rules even over the rulers of the earth.

  • Isaiah 56:4 — Eunuchs who choose what pleases God are promised a place and a name in His house. Those once excluded are welcomed by sharing God's delight.

  • Isaiah 62:4 — Zion, once called Forsaken, receives a new name because the LORD delights in her. God's delight restores a people He had disciplined.

  • Jeremiah 6:10 — The people find God's word a reproach and have no delight in it. The verse sets their closed ears beside their lack of delight in His word.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2656 (chephets) — lexical relative. This noun means "delight" or "pleasure." It appears in Psalm 1:2 and in the closing words of Isaiah 53:10, "the pleasure of the LORD."

  • H2655 (chaphets) — lexical relative. This adjective describes someone who is pleased with or desires something.

  • H2617 (chesed) — canonical pairing. This word, God's steadfast love and mercy, is the object God delights in. It is paired with H2654 in Hosea 6:6 ("mercy"), Jeremiah 9:24 ("lovingkindness"), and Micah 7:18 ("mercy").
