HEPHER - (Chêpher, Strong's H2660)

Summary

Hepher is a Hebrew name that most likely means "pit" or "well." It comes from a root meaning "to dig" or "to search out." The Old Testament uses it for several unrelated people and places, and they should not be merged. The most important is Hepher the Manassite, a son of Gilead and the father of Zelophehad. When Zelophehad died in the wilderness with five daughters and no sons, his family line seemed headed for extinction. His daughters, Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah, stood at the doorway of the tabernacle and asked why their father's name should be removed from his family. The LORD answered, "The daughters of Zelophehad speak what is right" (Numbers 27:7). Because of that ruling, Zelophehad's branch of Hepher's family kept its inheritance in Manasseh. Years later, under Joshua, the daughters received land west of the Jordan (Joshua 17:3–4).

The name also belongs to a Canaanite royal city that Joshua defeated. Its region later became "all the land of Hepher" in one of Solomon's supply districts (Joshua 12:17; 1 Kings 4:10). It forms part of Gath Hepher, a town in Zebulun that was the hometown of the prophet Jonah (Joshua 19:13; 2 Kings 14:25). Two minor men also bear the name: a Judahite in the genealogies and one of David's mighty men. What unites these scattered records is not the name's meaning. It is the steady faithfulness of God that runs through them. He refuses to let a fatherless family's name disappear. He brings a hostile city's land into the service of Israel's king. He raises a prophet from a small Galilean town whose story Jesus later calls "the sign of the prophet Jonah."

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Hepher (Chêpher) is a Hebrew proper name derived from the root chaphar (Strong's H2658), "to dig, search out, explore." As a noun, chêpher is generally understood to mean "a pit" or "a well." That meaning is well supported by the root, though Scripture never comments on it or builds wordplay on it in any Hepher passage. The meaning helps explain how the word could name a place, perhaps one known for a well, and it could also serve as a personal name. It should not be read as a description of anyone's character or life.

The Strong's entry covers at least five separate referents:

  • Hepher the Manassite. He was a son of Gilead, grandson of Machir, and great-grandson of Manasseh son of Joseph. He was the father of Zelophehad, and his clan was called the Hepherites (Numbers 26:32–33; 27:1; Joshua 17:2–3). The clan name is indexed separately as H2662.

  • Hepher the Judahite. He was a son of Ashhur, "the father of Tekoa," by his wife Naarah (1 Chronicles 4:5–6).

  • Hepher the Mecherathite. He was one of David's mighty men (1 Chronicles 11:36). The meaning of "Mecherathite" is not known.

  • Hepher, the Canaanite city and region. Its king was defeated by Joshua (Joshua 12:17), and "all the land of Hepher" was later assigned to Solomon's officer Ben-Hesed (1 Kings 4:10). It is commonly placed in or near the Sharon plain, though its exact site is debated.

  • Gath Hepher. This was a town on the border of Zebulun (Joshua 19:13) and the home of Jonah (2 Kings 14:25). Gath means "winepress," so the compound is usually understood as "winepress of the well (or pit)." Tradition places it in Lower Galilee, a few miles northeast of Nazareth.

Manasseh's western territory reached toward the region associated with the land of Hepher. Because of this, some have wondered whether the Manassite clan and the place are connected. Scripture never draws that link, so the two should be kept distinct.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Hepher the Manassite and the daughters of Zelophehad. In the second wilderness census, Hepher appears as one of the six sons of Gilead, each heading a family within Manasseh (Numbers 26:30–32). The census then pauses on something unusual: "Zelophehad the son of Hepher had no sons, but daughters" (Numbers 26:33). In a system where land passed through sons, this meant Hepher's line through Zelophehad would lose its portion in Canaan.

The daughters did not accept that outcome. Numbers 27:1 traces their full descent: "the daughters of Zelophehad the son of Hepher, the son of Gilead, the son of Machir, the son of Manasseh." They "stood before Moses, before Eleazar the priest, and before the leaders and all the congregation, by the doorway of the tabernacle of meeting" (27:2). Their plea was honest and carefully reasoned. Their father "was not in the company of those who gathered together against the LORD, in company with Korah, but he died in his own sin; and he had no sons" (27:3). Then they asked, "Why should the name of our father be removed from among his family because he had no son? Give us a possession among our father's brothers" (27:4).

Moses did not decide the case himself: "So Moses brought their case before the LORD" (27:5). The LORD's answer vindicated them completely: "you shall surely give them a possession of inheritance among their father's brothers, and cause the inheritance of their father to pass to them" (27:7). God then turned their case into a standing law for all Israel. When a man died without sons, his inheritance would pass to his daughters, then to his nearest relatives (27:8–11).

The story continues in Numbers 36. The leaders of Gilead's family raised a fair concern. If the daughters married outside the tribe, Manasseh's land would pass to another tribe. The LORD again ruled through Moses that the daughters should marry within their father's tribe. They married their cousins, so the inheritance "remained in the tribe of their father's family" (Numbers 36:1–12).

The promise was kept in the land itself. Joshua 17:2 lists "the children of Hepher" among the families of Manasseh who received a lot. The daughters then came before Eleazar and Joshua and reminded them, "The LORD commanded Moses to give us an inheritance among our brothers." The text adds, "Therefore, according to the commandment of the LORD, he gave them an inheritance among their father's brothers" (Joshua 17:4).

Hepher the city and the land of Hepher. In the list of kings Joshua defeated west of the Jordan, the entry is brief: "the king of Tappuah, one; the king of Hepher, one" (Joshua 12:17). Hepher was a royal Canaanite city opposed to Israel. Centuries later, the name returns in a peaceful setting. Among Solomon's twelve officers who provided food for the royal household, "Ben-Hesed, in Arubboth; to him belonged Sochoh and all the land of Hepher" (1 Kings 4:10). A conquered enemy stronghold had become part of the ordered provision of Israel's kingdom.

Gath Hepher and Jonah. Gath Hepher first appears as a landmark on Zebulun's boundary (Joshua 19:13). Its lasting fame comes from 2 Kings 14:25. Jeroboam II "restored the territory of Israel from the entrance of Hamath to the Sea of the Arabah, according to the word of the LORD God of Israel, which He had spoken through His servant Jonah the son of Amittai, the prophet who was from Gath Hepher." This is the same Jonah whose book tells of his flight, his descent into the sea, and his mission to Nineveh.

Two minor figures. Hepher son of Ashhur appears only in Judah's genealogy, one of four sons born to Naarah (1 Chronicles 4:5–6). Hepher the Mecherathite is named among David's mighty men (1 Chronicles 11:36). The parallel list in 2 Samuel 23 reads differently at this point, and the relationship between the two lists is not certain. Nothing more is recorded about either man. Still, their names stand in Scripture: one in Judah's family records, the other among the warriors who stood with David.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Hepher's descendants show how God's promise of land worked in a family whose father died without sons. Scripture states the facts plainly. Zelophehad had no sons. His daughters appealed. The LORD declared them right and established a lasting law. The land was later given. From these facts a clear implication follows. The inheritance in Canaan was not just property. It was each family's share in what God had promised to Abraham's descendants. God provided for Zelophehad's daughters to receive their father's share. He also guarded the tribal portions, as the follow-up ruling in Numbers 36 shows. The same God who heard the daughters also heard the tribal leaders, and His answer protected both the family and the tribe.

The place called Hepher traces a different movement. Joshua 12:17 lists its king among the defeated, and 1 Kings 4:10 shows its land supplying Solomon's table. Scripture does not comment on this change. Even so, the two verses together show the promise of land being fulfilled in concrete terms. Territory once held against Israel now served the peace and order of the kingdom God established under David's son.

Gath Hepher reminds the reader that God's word came through a Galilean prophet in a dark period for the northern kingdom. The verses just after Jonah's prophecy explain why God gave Jeroboam success. The LORD saw Israel's bitter affliction, "and the LORD did not say that He would blot out the name of Israel from under heaven" (2 Kings 14:26–27, paraphrased). The Hepher passages repeatedly show God unwilling to let a name be erased: first a single family's name, then a nation's.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest connection to Christ runs through Gath Hepher. Jesus told those demanding a sign that none would be given "except the sign of the prophet Jonah." Jonah spent three days and nights in the great fish, and the Son of Man would be three days and nights in the heart of the earth (Matthew 12:39–40, paraphrased). The New Testament interprets this connection explicitly. Jesus Himself makes Jonah's descent and return a picture of His death and resurrection. Jonah's own prayer captures that rescue: "Yet You have brought up my life from the pit, O LORD, my God" (Jonah 2:6). The Hebrew word for "pit" in that verse is not chêpher, so the connection rests on Jonah's experience, not on the meaning of his hometown's name.

A Galilean detail adds a sharp irony. When Nicodemus urged a fair hearing for Jesus, the Pharisees replied, "Are you also from Galilee? Search and look, for no prophet has arisen out of Galilee" (John 7:52). Yet 2 Kings 14:25 records that Jonah came from Gath Hepher, a Galilean town traditionally located only a few miles from Nazareth, where Jesus grew up. The religious leaders dismissed Galilee, while the prophet Jesus named as His own sign had come from the same hills.

The inheritance story also connects with the wider biblical hope, though the New Testament never cites Zelophehad's daughters directly. Through Christ, believers become "heirs according to the promise" (Galatians 3:29). They receive "an inheritance incorruptible and undefiled" (1 Peter 1:4, paraphrased), an inheritance no gap in family history can cancel. The risen Lord promises the one who overcomes, "I will not blot out his name from the Book of Life; but I will confess his name before My Father and before His angels" (Revelation 3:5). This connection is a thematic link, not a declared fulfillment, but it follows naturally. The God who preserved Zelophehad's name and inheritance within Hepher's family is the God who secures His people's names forever in His Son.

5. Spirit & Life

The daughters of Zelophehad show what it looks like to bring a real need to God on the basis of His promise. They did not demand privilege or hide their father's sin. They came openly to the doorway of the tabernacle, the place where God met His people. They stated the truth and asked for a share in what God had pledged. Moses did not have an answer ready, so he took their case to the LORD. God did not treat their request as an inconvenience or an act of defiance. He called it right.

This tells us something about God's heart. He notices those whom the usual arrangements would leave out. He welcomes honest petitions, and He answers in ways that bless not only the person asking but everyone who comes after. The law given in Numbers 27:8–11 protected every Israelite family that would ever face the same loss.

The story also shows that God's answers continue to work out over time. The daughters had to come back years later and remind Joshua and Eleazar of what the LORD had commanded (Joshua 17:4). Believers do the same when they hold God to His word in prayer. They are not reminding Him because He forgets. They are standing on what He has said. To have one's name cut off was a mark of judgment in Israel (Psalm 109:13). Against that background, Christ's promise to confess His people's names before the Father carries real weight and comfort.

Finally, the land of Hepher, once ruled by a Canaanite king and later feeding Solomon's household, offers a fitting picture of grace. God takes what stood against His purposes and puts it to use in His kingdom. This picture does not come from a typology the text declares. It is a faithful reflection on how God works.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Numbers 26:30–33: Hepher is named among Gilead's sons, the Hepherite clan is recorded, and Zelophehad's lack of sons is noted.

  • Numbers 27:1–11: The daughters' appeal at the tabernacle, the LORD's ruling, and the general inheritance law.

  • Numbers 36:1–12: The follow-up ruling that kept the inheritance within the tribe.

  • Joshua 17:2–6: The children of Hepher receive a lot, and the daughters receive their promised inheritance.

  • Joshua 12:17: The king of Hepher is defeated.

  • 1 Kings 4:10: "All the land of Hepher" belongs to Solomon's district under Ben-Hesed.

  • Joshua 19:13: Gath Hepher marks Zebulun's boundary.

  • 2 Kings 14:25–27: Jonah of Gath Hepher prophesies Israel's restored territory, and God refuses to blot out Israel's name.

  • 1 Chronicles 4:6; 11:36: The Judahite Hepher and Hepher the Mecherathite.

  • Matthew 12:39–40; Jonah 2:6: Jesus' "sign of the prophet Jonah."

  • John 7:52: The Pharisees' claim that no prophet comes from Galilee.

  • Revelation 3:5: Christ's promise not to blot out the overcomer's name.
