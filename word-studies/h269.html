SISTER, ANOTHER - (ʼâchôwth, Strong's H0269)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼâchôwth means "sister." Most often it names an actual female sibling, such as Miriam watching over baby Moses, Rachel envying Leah, Dinah avenged by her brothers, or Tamar wronged by her half-brother Amnon. Hebrew also uses the word in an everyday phrase, literally "a woman to her sister," to say that things are joined "one to another." Scripture uses this phrase for the coupled curtains of the tabernacle and the touching wings of the cherubim in Ezekiel's vision.

The prophets and poets carry the word further. Jeremiah and Ezekiel portray Israel and Judah, and even Samaria and Sodom, as sisters who share the same unfaithfulness, and Ezekiel ends that grim picture with God's promise of restoration. Proverbs tells the young man to call wisdom his sister. The Song of Solomon gives the most striking use of all. There the bridegroom calls his bride "my sister, my spouse," joining a family word to a marriage word. That pairing speaks of a love that is both kinship and chosen devotion, which is the kind of love Christ has for His people. He became our Brother by sharing our flesh, and He gave Himself for us as His Bride.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼâchôwth is the feminine counterpart of the common Hebrew word for "brother." It appears throughout the Old Testament. It is most frequent in Genesis, with its family stories, and in Ezekiel, with its long prophetic allegories. It also appears in the family laws of Leviticus, the court history of 2 Samuel, the genealogies of Chronicles, and the poetry of Proverbs and the Song of Solomon.

In Israel's world a brother was expected to stand guard over his sister's honor and welfare. Laban speaks for Rebekah's marriage (Genesis 24:50–60), Simeon and Levi avenge Dinah, and Absalom takes Tamar into his house. Knowing this helps the reader feel the weight of the word in these stories, where loyalty and protection are at stake.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is plain: a female sibling. Context sets the range.

1. A sister by birth, whether full or half. Abraham says of Sarah that she is the daughter of his father but not of his mother (Genesis 20:12).

2. A close female relative, or a woman joined by kinship, love, or likeness. This sense appears in figurative speech. Examples include nations portrayed as sisters (Ezekiel 23:4), wisdom as a sister (Proverbs 7:4), and the bride as "my sister" (Song of Solomon 4:9).

3. In the reciprocal phrase "a woman to her sister," the meaning is "one to another." This describes things paired or joined together (Exodus 26:3; Ezekiel 1:9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In narrative, the word usually locates a woman within her family. Sometimes the family tie becomes the center of the story. Jehosheba, "sister of Ahaziah," hides the infant Joash and keeps David's royal line alive (2 Kings 11:2). Other times the tie is betrayed. Amnon pretends illness and asks for "my sister Tamar" (2 Samuel 13:6), turning the trust that belongs to family into a trap.

The law treats the bond as sacred. Leviticus 18 and 20 forbid sexual relations with a sister or half-sister. Leviticus 21:3 allows a priest to become ceremonially unclean to bury his unmarried sister, since she has no husband to do so.

The reciprocal phrase is ordinary Hebrew grammar. In Exodus 26 the NKJV renders it "another" or "together": "Five curtains shall be coupled to one another" (Exodus 26:3). In Ezekiel the same phrase describes the living creatures, whose wings touched one another (Ezekiel 1:9; 3:13). The idiom itself means only "one to another." Still, Hebrew chose a family word to describe parts joined into one whole, and it is fitting that the phrase gathers around God's dwelling and His throne. The tabernacle was one tent made of many curtains clasped together. The cherubim moved as one around the glory of God. Where God dwells, the parts belong to each other.

4. Theological Significance

Because "sister" names a bond nobody chooses and nobody can undo, the word lets Scripture speak with unusual force about loyalty and betrayal. God made family to protect. When brothers use the tie as an excuse for slaughter, as Simeon and Levi did at Shechem (Genesis 34:25–31), or as a cover for lust, as Amnon did, Scripture records the ruin that follows.

The prophets use the same bond to show how sin spreads among people who share a heritage. Judah watched her sister Israel fall and learned nothing from it. God's judgment is pointed: shared origin brings shared accountability. Yet the God who indicts these sisters also promises to gather them again. The family language that exposes the sin also holds the hope.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

"My sister, my spouse" (Song of Solomon 4:9–5:2). Four times in this section the bridegroom combines the two titles (4:9, 10, 12; 5:1): "You have ravished my heart, My sister, my spouse" (4:9). Each word says something the other cannot. A spouse is chosen and pledged. A sister belongs to you by birth and cannot stop being yours. By putting the words side by side, the bridegroom tells his bride that she is both the woman he has chosen and someone as near to him as his own family. The language reaches its height at the wedding feast: "I have come to my garden, my sister, my spouse" (5:1). The next verse keeps the family word with a new partner: "my sister, my love" (5:2). The Song closes with a different scene. The brothers speak protectively of "a little sister" who is not yet grown: "What shall we do for our sister In the day when she is spoken for?" (8:8). Within one book, "sister" covers both the guarded care of brothers and the delight of a husband.

The sister defense (Genesis 12:10–20; 20:1–18; 26:6–11). Three times a patriarch in a foreign land tells his hosts that his wife is his sister. In Abraham's case it was half true. Abraham's own explanation, though, shows his motive: he feared he would be killed for her sake (20:11). He used the family word to hide the marriage, and in doing so he left Sarah unprotected, first in Pharaoh's house and then in Abimelech's. Isaac later did the same with Rebekah. Each time God stepped in. He struck Pharaoh's house with plagues (12:17), warned Abimelech in a dream and kept him from touching Sarah (20:3–6), and exposed Isaac's lie before harm was done (26:8–11). The men who carried God's promise failed to trust Him with their wives. God guarded those wives and the promised line anyway.

Sisters in unfaithfulness (Jeremiah 3:6–10; Ezekiel 16:44–63; 23). Jeremiah calls Judah the "treacherous sister" who saw Israel sent away and still did not return to God with her whole heart (Jeremiah 3:7–10). In Ezekiel 23, Samaria and Jerusalem are Oholah and Oholibah. The younger sister watched the elder fall and became even more corrupt (23:11), so she must drink her sister's cup of judgment (23:31–33). Ezekiel 16 goes further. Jerusalem's sisters are Samaria and Sodom, and Sodom's sin is named plainly: pride, too much food, careless ease, and refusing to help the poor and needy (16:49). Jerusalem had made these sisters look righteous by comparison (16:51–52). Then the oracle turns to mercy. Jerusalem had despised the oath by breaking the covenant (16:59), yet God says, "Nevertheless I will remember My covenant with you in the days of your youth, and I will establish an everlasting covenant with you" (16:60). Jerusalem will remember her ways and be ashamed "when you receive your older and your younger sisters; for I will give them to you for daughters, but not because of My covenant with you" (16:61). Her broken obligations could not earn this restoration. It rests on God's faithfulness to what He began with her and on the everlasting covenant He Himself establishes, together with an atonement He provides (16:63). Former rivals in sin become a family under grace.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Paul quotes the marriage pattern of Genesis and then says, "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). He does not quote the Song. Still, the Song's double title fits the gospel closely. Hebrews says of Jesus that "both He who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all of one, for which reason He is not ashamed to call them brethren" (Hebrews 2:11). Christ took on our flesh to become our Brother, and He loves the church as His Bride. In Him the kinship of "sister" and the chosen love of "spouse" belong to the same Person and the same people.

The contrast with Genesis is striking. Afraid for his life, Abraham presented his bride as only a sister so that he would be spared. Christ did the opposite. He became one of our family and then laid down His life to win His Bride. The patriarchs hid their marriages in order to save themselves. Jesus openly claimed His people and did not save Himself.

Ezekiel 16:61 points the same direction. Samaria and Sodom, the sisters whose names stood for shame, are given to Jerusalem as daughters on the basis of a covenant God makes Himself. That anticipates a gospel in which outsiders and the notoriously sinful are brought into God's family by grace, as Hosea also foresaw when he told the people to call their sisters "Mercy is shown" (Hosea 2:1).

7. Spirit & Life

"Say to wisdom, 'You are my sister'" (Proverbs 7:4) is a command about closeness. In the stories of Dinah and Tamar, brothers showed fierce protective loyalty, though they twisted it into revenge. Proverbs takes that same loyalty and directs it toward God's wisdom, so that it may keep the young man from the immoral woman and her flattering words (7:5). A believer is to hold God's truth the way a good brother keeps a sister: near, watched over, and never handed over to strangers. Truth kept at a distance cannot protect anyone. Truth treated as family can.

The patriarchs' fear also warns us. When we stop trusting God's protection, we start managing outcomes ourselves, and the people closest to us usually pay for it. Sarah and Rebekah were put at risk by husbands who were protecting themselves. Yet God's faithfulness in those same chapters is real comfort. He kept His promise when His servants did not keep their nerve.

The Song lets the believer hear how Christ regards His people. He calls them His family and His beloved together. No one in His church is merely tolerated kin, and no one is a loved stranger with no belonging. We are both His own and His chosen.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 2:4, 7 – Moses' sister stands at a distance to watch the basket, then boldly offers Pharaoh's daughter a nurse. Her loyalty brings Moses back to his mother.

  • Exodus 15:20 – Miriam the prophetess, "the sister of Aaron," leads Israel's women in praise after the sea crossing.

  • Joshua 2:13 – Rahab asks the spies to spare her father, mother, brothers, and sisters. Faith in Israel's God reaches out to protect her whole household.

  • 2 Kings 11:2 – Jehosheba, Ahaziah's sister, rescues the infant Joash from Athaliah's massacre and preserves the line of David.

  • Job 17:14 – Job in despair calls the worm his mother and sister, a picture of being at home only with the grave.

  • Job 42:11 – After Job's restoration, his brothers and sisters come to comfort him and share a meal in his house.
