CHAINS, STRING OF JEWELS - (chârûwz, Strong's H2737)

Summary

The Hebrew word chârûwz means a string of beads or jewels worn around the neck. Scripture uses it only once, in Song of Solomon 1:10. There the bridegroom king looks at his bride and says, "Your cheeks are lovely with ornaments, Your neck with chains." The word names a real piece of jewelry: small precious pieces threaded together into one necklace.

Its single appearance comes at a revealing moment. The king notices the details of his bride's appearance and delights in them. In the next breath he promises more: "We will make you ornaments of gold With studs of silver" (Song 1:11). Read alongside the rest of Scripture, where God adorns His people and Christ prepares His bride, this small word opens a large truth. The Lord takes pleasure in the beauty His grace has already produced in His people, and that pleasure leads to a promise of still greater glory.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chârûwz is a Hebrew noun that occurs only once in the Old Testament. A word used only once is often called a hapax legomenon, a Greek phrase meaning "said once." Because no other biblical passage uses it, its meaning comes from its setting and from the related ancient vocabulary for jewelry. The word is commonly connected with a root meaning to pierce or thread. That connection fits the object well, since beads and stones had to be bored through before they could be strung.

The setting is the opening chapter of the Song of Solomon, a love poem between a bridegroom king and his bride, the Shulamite. In verses 9 to 11 the king praises her appearance. In the NKJV the Hebrew word stands behind the single English word "chains." The words "of gold" were added by the translators to complete the sense, which is why many editions print them in italics.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chârûwz has one plain meaning: a necklace made of beads, pearls, or precious stones threaded on a cord. The NKJV's "chains" should not suggest heavy forged links. This is ornamental jewelry made of many small pieces that together form one string. Since the word appears only once, there are no further senses to distinguish.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The only use is descriptive praise. The king observes his bride's adorned neck and names it as one reason she is lovely. The word comes in a pair: "ornaments" on the cheeks and "chains" on the neck. The two lines work together to describe a bride arrayed from face to neck. The surrounding verses then move from describing her present jewelry (v. 10) to promising new jewelry (v. 11). That movement gives the word its importance in the passage.

Other books describe necklaces with different Hebrew words. Proverbs 1:9 compares a parent's instruction to "chains about your neck." Ezekiel 16:11 speaks of God putting "a chain on your neck." These passages share the image, though not the vocabulary, and they help show what an adorned neck meant to an ancient reader: honor, favor, and value given by someone else.

4. Theological Significance

The passages that share this image reveal a God who adorns. He gives beauty and takes pleasure in it. In Ezekiel the Lord Himself dresses Israel in jewelry. In Proverbs wisdom received from a faithful parent becomes an ornament. In the Song of Solomon the king delights in the jewelry his bride wears and plans to give her more. Across these passages, adornment is something given, and the giver rejoices in seeing it worn.

This picture corrects a common distortion. Many believers think of God's attention mainly as inspection, an eye searching out flaws. Scripture does speak of God's searching holiness. But these passages show another side of His regard. He notices what is lovely in His people because He put it there, and He says so. Psalm 45:11, a royal wedding psalm, states it directly: "So the King will greatly desire your beauty."

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:9–11: present delight and promised glory. The king's words give the word its context. In verse 10 he admires what is already there: her cheeks lovely with ornaments, her neck with strings of jewels. He is not grudging or measuring. He has looked closely enough to notice the jewelry and wants her to know he finds it lovely.

Verse 11 then gives a promise: "We will make you ornaments of gold With studs of silver." The plural "we" most likely refers to the king speaking with his household or companions, those who will carry out the work. The order of the two verses matters. The king's delight in her present adornment does not mean he considers her finished. His delight becomes the starting point for something greater. The strung beads of verse 10 will be followed by crafted gold set with silver. The king's love accepts her as she is, and the same love invests in her further. His pleasure in her today and his plans for her tomorrow come from one heart.

Ezekiel 16:8–14: the adornment of the undeserving. Ezekiel 16 uses a different Hebrew word, but it is the Old Testament's fullest picture of God adorning His people. The chapter opens with Israel pictured as an abandoned newborn, unwashed and left to die (vv. 4–5). The Lord passes by, cares for her, enters into a covenant with her (v. 8), washes and clothes her, and then says, "I adorned you with ornaments, put bracelets on your wrists, and a chain on your neck" (v. 11). Every piece of jewelry she wears came from Him. Her beauty, God says, was "perfect through My splendor which I had bestowed on you" (v. 14).

This passage shows what a jeweled neck means in the Bible's larger story. The jewelry is a gift, and wearing it is a mark of honor that the wearer did not earn. Read beside Ezekiel, the bride's necklace in the Song suggests the same: she is wearing evidence of the king's favor.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song of Solomon is first of all a poem about love between a man and a woman. Scripture, however, repeatedly describes the Lord as a bridegroom and His people as a bride. That pattern makes it fitting to see in the king's delight a picture of Christ's love for His church, while recognizing that the Song itself does not name Christ.

The pattern runs from beginning to end. Psalm 45 celebrates a royal wedding, and Hebrews 1:8–9 applies that psalm to the Son. Isaiah 61:10 rejoices that God has clothed His people in salvation and righteousness, "as a bride adorns herself with her jewels" (paraphrased). Ephesians 5:25–27 says that Christ gave Himself for the church so that He might present her to Himself glorious, without spot or wrinkle. Revelation 21:2 gives the final picture: John sees the New Jerusalem "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband."

The movement in Song 1:10–11 from present jewels to promised gold matches this storyline. Paul describes the same direction of travel in 2 Corinthians 3:18: believers "are being transformed into the same image from glory to glory, just as by the Spirit of the Lord." The bride is already adorned and already delighted in, yet she is still being made ready. Her adornment will be complete when she stands before her Husband on the day Revelation describes.

7. Spirit & Life

A believer can take the king's words in Song 1:10 as a true window into how Christ regards His people. He sees what His grace has produced in them, and He enjoys it. That truth frees a Christian from living as though every glance from God were an audit. The same Lord who calls us to repentance also notices the faith, patience, and love His Spirit has formed in us, and He finds them lovely.

The nature of a chârûwz also offers a fitting illustration, though the word itself does not carry this meaning. A string of jewels is many small, separate pieces, each pierced and threaded on one cord until together they form a single ornament. The New Testament describes Christian character in a similar way. Peter urges believers to "add to your faith virtue, to virtue knowledge," and to keep adding self-control, perseverance, godliness, brotherly kindness, and love (2 Peter 1:5–7), one grace joined to the next. Paul names "the fruit of the Spirit" in the singular and then lists nine qualities (Galatians 5:22–23). In both passages the graces belong together and form one character, much as the beads form one necklace.

Ezekiel 16 adds a sober note. Soon after God adorned Israel, she trusted in her own beauty and gave His gifts to other lovers (vv. 15–17). The jewelry of grace is meant to be worn for the One who gave it. When a believer treats God's gifts as personal achievement or as a means of self-display, the adornment has been misused.

Finally, Song 1:11 gives every believer a reason for hope. Christ's pleasure in us now does not mean He is finished with us. He intends gold where there were beads. Whatever grace He has already worked into a life is the first piece of a larger design, and He has promised to complete it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Chârûwz occurs only in Song of Solomon 1:10, which is discussed above. The related passages on neck adornment that use other Hebrew words, Proverbs 1:9 and Ezekiel 16:11, are treated in Sections 3 and 5.
