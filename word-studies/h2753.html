HORI - (Chôrîy, Strong's H2753)

Summary

Hori is a Hebrew proper name connected with the ancient Horites, the people who lived in the rugged hill country of Mount Seir, south of the Dead Sea, before Esau's descendants took the land. Under this one Strong's entry stand three distinct realities: Hori the son of Lotan, a grandson of "Seir the Horite" (Genesis 36:22; 1 Chronicles 1:39); the Horite people and their chiefs as a group (Genesis 36:20, 30; Deuteronomy 2:22); and an unrelated Israelite of the tribe of Simeon named Hori, whose son Shaphat was one of the twelve spies (Numbers 13:5). The name is traditionally understood to mean "cave-dweller," from the Hebrew word for a hole or cave, which fits the cliffs and caves of Seir. Many scholars instead connect the Horites with the Hurrians, a well-known ancient people, so the meaning is probable rather than certain. For the Simeonite Hori, some suggest a different root meaning "noble" or "freeborn," though this is uncertain.

The Horites matter most in Deuteronomy 2. As Israel prepared to enter Canaan, Moses reminded them that the LORD "destroyed the Horites" before the descendants of Esau so they could live in Seir (Deuteronomy 2:22). God had given land to a people outside Israel's line, and He had removed long-established inhabitants to do it. That piece of history was meant to steady Israel's faith. It stands as an answer to the very fear that gripped the ten spies at Kadesh, one of whom was Hori's son Shaphat. The God who governs the boundaries of every nation (Acts 17:26) can keep every promise He makes to His people.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name חֹרִי (Chôrîy) appears six times under Strong's H2753. These passages name two men and also describe the Horite people; their histories should be distinguished:

  • Hori, son of Lotan: a member of the Horite ruling family of Seir. Lotan was the first-named son of "Seir the Horite," and Lotan's sons were "Hori and Hemam" (Genesis 36:20, 22). First Chronicles 1:39 repeats the line, spelling the second brother Homam.

  • The Horites as a people: the original inhabitants of Mount Seir, organized under tribal chiefs (Genesis 36:20, 30; Deuteronomy 2:22). Lexicons often index the gentilic "Horite" separately under H2752, but the proper name Hori and the people name Horite share the same Hebrew form, and these verses are counted with Hori in this study.

  • Hori the Simeonite: the father of Shaphat, who represented the tribe of Simeon among the twelve spies (Numbers 13:5). He lived centuries after the Seir genealogy and has no stated connection to it.

The traditional derivation links the name to the Hebrew chor (H2356), "hole" or "cave." On this reading, "Hori" and "Horite" mean "cave-dweller." That suits Mount Seir, the rocky, cave-filled region of Edom east of the Arabah. Many modern scholars propose instead that "Horite" reflects the name of the Hurrians, a people widely attested across the ancient Near East. The biblical text itself does not explain the name, so "cave-dweller" is best treated as the traditional and probable meaning. A link to the Hurrians is a scholarly proposal.

The Israelite Hori may carry a different sense. Some connect his name with chor (H2715), "noble" or "freeborn," which would make it a separate name that happens to share the same spelling. This is possible but uncertain. Scripture offers no wordplay on either meaning, and nothing in the text ties the Simeonite family to the Horites of Seir.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Horites of Seir. Genesis 36 sets two family records side by side. First come Esau's descendants, then "the sons of Seir the Horite who inhabited the land: Lotan, Shobal, Zibeon, Anah, Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan. These were the chiefs of the Horites, the sons of Seir, in the land of Edom" (Genesis 36:20–21). Hori, Lotan's son, belongs to the second generation of this ruling house, and the chapter adds that "Lotan's sister was Timna" (36:22). The list closes by summarizing the Horite chiefs "in the land of Seir" (36:29–30). These were an established people with their own leaders, living in Seir long before Esau arrived. A related, separately indexed reference shows them already there in Abraham's day, when eastern kings struck "the Horites in their mountain of Seir" (Genesis 14:6).

The two families became entangled. Esau moved to Mount Seir away from Jacob (Genesis 36:6–8). One of his wives, Aholibamah, was the daughter of Anah, the daughter of Zibeon (36:2), names that also appear in the Horite line. A woman named Timna became the concubine of Esau's son Eliphaz and bore Amalek (36:12). She is probably the same Timna named as Hori's aunt. Intermarriage was only part of the story, though. Deuteronomy records that the descendants of Esau eventually drove the Horites out and settled in their place (Deuteronomy 2:12, 22).

The retelling in Deuteronomy. On the plains of Moab, Moses reviewed Israel's wilderness travels. God told Israel not to provoke Edom, because He had given Mount Seir to Esau as a possession (Deuteronomy 2:4–5). Moses then explained how Esau came to hold it. The LORD had acted for Esau's descendants "when He destroyed the Horites from before them. They dispossessed them and dwelt in their place, even to this day" (Deuteronomy 2:22). The same chapter tells similar stories about Moab displacing the Emim and Ammon displacing the Zamzummim (2:10–11, 20–21).

The later genealogical record. First Chronicles 1:38–42 preserves the sons of Seir and the line of Lotan, including Hori, as part of its survey of the nations from Adam onward. By then the Horites had long since been dispossessed and replaced as Seir's ruling population, but their names remained in Israel's records.

Hori the Simeonite. At Kadesh, the LORD told Moses to send one leader from each tribe to scout Canaan. "From the tribe of Simeon, Shaphat the son of Hori" (Numbers 13:5) was among them. When the spies returned, ten of them, Shaphat included, gave a bad report and spread fear about the land's fortified cities and the descendants of Anak (13:28–33). Only Joshua and Caleb urged the people to trust the LORD (14:6–9). The ten died by plague (14:36–37), and that generation was turned back into the wilderness. Scripture says nothing more about Hori himself.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that the LORD "destroyed the Horites" before Esau's descendants (Deuteronomy 2:22). It also says the LORD gave Seir to Esau (2:5), as He gave Ar to Moab and the land of Ammon to Lot's descendants (2:9, 19). The point is clear. God's rule over land and nations is not confined to Israel. He assigned territories to peoples outside the line of promise and acted in their history to settle them there.

Moses records these events at the edge of the conquest, and the timing explains why. From this setting, a careful inference follows. The Horites were an old, organized people with chiefs and a long hold on hard mountain country. If the LORD could remove them for Esau, who was not the heir of Abraham's promise, Israel had no reason to doubt that He could do the same for the nation He had redeemed from Egypt. Deuteronomy draws the comparison itself when it says the Emim were tall "like the Anakim" (2:10–11). The giants the spies feared were not beyond God's reach. He had already dealt with peoples like them elsewhere.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No passage presents Hori or the Horites as a type of Christ, and none should be invented. The connection with the larger biblical story is one of thematic development rather than prediction. In Athens, Paul proclaimed that God "has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26). The Horites' rise and removal is one small example of exactly that truth. Paul goes on to give God's purpose in setting those boundaries: that people would seek Him (17:27). The God who governed Seir's history is the God who now calls all nations to repentance through the risen Christ (17:30–31).

5. Spirit & Life

Moses strengthened Israel's faith with something besides Israel's own miracles. He pointed to events in a neighbor's history: Esau's people living where the Horites once lived. God's hand had been at work in that neighboring people's history too. Believers can take real comfort from this. God is not only active where we can see His people praying. He governs the rise and fall of nations, and that wider rule is good news for those who belong to Him.

The two Horis also meet, without any family link, in the same moment of Israel's history. Hori's son Shaphat stood at Kadesh, saw the strength of Canaan, and decided God could not give it to Israel. A generation later Moses answered that unbelief by reminding Israel what God had done to the Horites. The name connects the two only by coincidence, but the lesson is real. Fear measures the enemy, while faith remembers what God has already done. The record of God's past faithfulness, in Scripture and in history, is meant to keep His people from repeating Kadesh.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:20–22, 29–30: identifies Seir the Horite, his sons as chiefs, and Hori son of Lotan; establishes the Horites as Seir's original inhabitants.

  • Genesis 14:6 (related, separately indexed): the Horites already living in Mount Seir in Abraham's time.

  • Deuteronomy 2:4–5, 12, 22: God gave Seir to Esau and destroyed the Horites before them; the central theological passage.

  • Deuteronomy 2:10–11, 20–21: parallel accounts of the Emim and Zamzummim, linked to the Anakim, framing Israel's coming conquest.

  • Numbers 13:5; 13:31–33; 14:36–37: Shaphat son of Hori among the spies whose fearful report brought judgment.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:38–39: the Horite genealogy preserved in Israel's later record, with Hemam spelled Homam.

  • Acts 17:26: God determines the times and boundaries of the nations, the broader truth illustrated by the Horites' history.
