LATTICE - (cherek, Strong's H2762)

Summary

The Hebrew word cherek means "lattice." It describes the crisscrossed grille set into a window opening in an ancient house. A lattice let in air and light and allowed people to see through it, yet it still kept a boundary between inside and outside. The word appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 2:9. There the beloved runs across the hills to the young woman's home and stands outside it: "Behold, he stands behind our wall; He is looking through the windows, Gazing through the lattice."

That single picture holds a great deal. The lover is close enough to be seen and heard, but the two are not yet together in the open. He does not stay at the lattice watching in silence. He speaks through it: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away" (Song 2:10). Christians have long heard in this love song an echo of Christ's love for His people. Read that way, the lattice pictures the believer's present life with Him. His presence is real and near, but it still comes to us partly veiled. He comes to us there and calls us out, and the scene stirs our longing for the day when "they shall see His face" (Revelation 22:4).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Cherek is a Hebrew noun found only in Song of Solomon 2:9. A word used only once in Scripture is often called a hapax legomenon, Greek for "said once." Because we have no other biblical uses to compare, Scripture alone cannot settle exactly how this kind of lattice was built. In general terms, it was a window covering of interlaced wood or similar material. It was neither a solid wall nor an empty hole. It let breeze, light, sight, and sound pass through while giving the household privacy and protection.

The Song is love poetry. Its images work through feeling and association rather than exact pictures. In chapter 2 the setting is a home at the turning of the seasons, with winter ending and spring arriving.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A lattice or grille: an open, interwoven covering across a window opening that lets a person see through it while a boundary remains. In Song of Solomon 2:9 the beloved gazes "through the lattice" from outside the house.

The word means only this physical object. What the lattice comes to suggest in the poem comes from the scene around it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In its one appearance, the lattice is the last stage in a movement that narrows step by step. The young woman first hears "the voice of my beloved" as he comes "leaping upon the mountains" (2:8). Then he "stands behind our wall." Next he is "looking through the windows." Finally he is "gazing through the lattice." The poem moves from a distant figure on the hills to a face at the grille. Each line brings him closer and makes his attention more focused.

The direction of his gaze is the key detail. He is outside looking in. The lattice does not hide him, and it does not stop him. The barrier still stands, yet it has openings, and he uses them.

4. Theological Significance

The scene shows a love that takes the first step. The young woman does not cross the mountains; the beloved does. He comes to her house and seeks her out before she has moved at all. Read as a picture of the Lord and His people, this matches what all of Scripture shows: God seeks His people before they seek Him.

The lattice also captures something true about how God makes Himself known: He reveals Himself while He conceals. When Moses asked to see God's glory, the LORD said, "You cannot see My face; for no man shall see Me, and live" (Exodus 33:20). He placed Moses "in the cleft of the rock" and covered him with His hand while His glory passed by (33:22). Moses then saw God's back, but not His face (33:23). The covering both shielded Moses and made the meeting possible. A lattice works the same way. It partly hides the one who stands outside, and that partial hiding is how real sight takes place. In the Song the young woman herself is pictured "in the clefts of the rock" (2:14). Whether or not the poet meant this as a deliberate echo, the two scenes say the same thing. The Lord's nearness to His people in this life is genuine, and it is also measured and veiled by His grace.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:8–14: the gaze that becomes a call. The beloved's coming to the lattice has a purpose. "My beloved spoke, and said to me: 'Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away'" (2:10). His voice carries through the lattice into the enclosed house. He then gives his reason: "For lo, the winter is past, The rain is over and gone. The flowers appear on the earth; The time of singing has come" (2:11–12). The fig tree is ripening and the vines are in blossom, and he repeats the call: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away!" (2:13). Then he speaks to her where she is hidden: "O my dove, in the clefts of the rock... Let me see your face, Let me hear your voice" (2:14).

The lattice, then, is a meeting point rather than the place where the story ends. The beloved sees her through it, but his own words show that he wants more than a glimpse. He wants her face and her voice in the open, out in a world that has come alive with spring.

Song of Solomon 2:17: light and shadow. Looking through a lattice means seeing through crossbars, so the view is real but broken up by shadow. The chapter closes with a longing that fits this picture: "Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, Turn, my beloved" (2:17). For now there are shadows. A day is coming when they will be gone.

The window-watchers of the Old Testament: a reversal. Other passages describe people looking through windows, using different Hebrew words, and these scenes are often dark. "The mother of Sisera looked through the window, And cried out through the lattice" for a son who would never come home (Judges 5:28). Michal "looked through a window and saw King David leaping and whirling before the LORD; and she despised him in her heart" (2 Samuel 6:16). Jezebel painted her eyes "and looked through a window" just before Jehu arrived and she died (2 Kings 9:30). Not every window scene is grim (compare Genesis 26:8), but the pattern is striking. In each of these scenes someone inside looks out in dread, contempt, or pride. Song 2:9 turns the picture around. Here someone outside looks in, and he looks with seeking love.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament calls Jesus the Bridegroom and His church the bride (John 3:29; Ephesians 5:31–32; Revelation 19:7). This is why believers have long read the Song as a picture of Christ's love, while recognizing that its first meaning is human love between a man and a woman. In that light, the lover leaping over the mountains suggests Christ coming to His people across every distance. Hebrews speaks of the "new and living way which He consecrated for us, through the veil, that is, His flesh" (Hebrews 10:20). In becoming human, the Son brought the glory of God near in a form people could look upon and live.

The lattice also fits the age we now live in. Paul writes, "For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12). Peter describes believers as those "whom having not seen you love. Though now you do not see Him, yet believing, you rejoice" (1 Peter 1:8). Christ is near. He speaks and makes Himself known, but He does not yet appear to us in the open. In Revelation the risen Lord still stands outside and calls: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice..." (Revelation 3:20). The story moves toward the day when the barrier is gone entirely: "They shall see His face" (Revelation 22:4). The lattice is the season of "now," and the open meeting face to face is the "then."

7. Spirit & Life

The first thing the lattice teaches is that grace comes first. Before the young woman stirs, the beloved has already crossed the hills and stands at her wall looking in. Whoever feels shut in, whether by sorrow, fear, or the narrow walls of a hard season, can know that Christ has come to the outside of that house, and His attention is fixed on the one within.

The second lesson is harder: we can grow too comfortable behind the lattice. It is possible to enjoy hearing about the Lord from a safe distance, inside familiar religious routines, while never answering when He calls. Yet His voice always comes with a summons: "Rise up... And come away." His purpose in revealing Himself is to draw us out into obedience, into a world where "the winter is past." To hear that voice and stay indoors is to choose the safety of the house over walking with the One who called.

The lattice can also serve as an image of Scripture, offered here as an analogy rather than the poem's own meaning. Like a lattice, the Bible is something carefully built: woven from human language, history, and poetry. We do not read it to admire the woodwork. We look through it to see the One who stands behind it, and His word reaches us through it.

Finally, the lattice teaches us to long for more. Every true glimpse of Christ, still crossed by shadow, is meant to awaken a holy dissatisfaction with seeing in part. It stirs the prayer of Song 2:17, a longing for the day to break and the shadows to flee.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Cherek occurs only in Song of Solomon 2:9, which is discussed fully above. The NKJV also uses the English word "lattice" in Judges 5:28, but there it translates a different Hebrew word, so that verse belongs to the wider window imagery of Scripture and is not another occurrence of cherek.
