HERMON - (Chermôwn, Strong's H2768)

Summary

Hermon is the great snow-capped mountain at the far northern edge of the land God gave Israel. It rises more than nine thousand feet at the southern end of the Anti-Lebanon range and towers over everything around it. Its name most likely comes from a Hebrew root meaning to set apart or to devote, so Hermon probably means something like sacred or set-apart mountain. That meaning is probable rather than certain. It fits the region's religious associations, for Scripture also mentions Mount Baal Hermon, a name meaning lord of Hermon that is tied to the Hermon area (Judges 3:3). Neighboring peoples called the same mountain Sirion and Senir, and Deuteronomy also calls it Sion.

In Scripture, Hermon first marks where Israel's conquest ended in the north, under Moses east of the Jordan and under Joshua in Canaan. The mountain stood as visible evidence that God had given His people the land He promised. Israel's poets then claimed the mountain for the Lord. In Psalm 89, Hermon rejoices in God's name. In Psalm 133, the heavy "dew of Hermon" pictures the blessing God pours on His people when they live together in unity. The Song of Solomon remembers its wild heights, home to lions and leopards. The New Testament never names Hermon, but Jesus' transfiguration on "a high mountain" soon after Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi, a town at Hermon's foot, has led many to see this mountain as a plausible place where the glory of God's Son was revealed. The Gospels do not name the mountain, and an older tradition points to Mount Tabor instead.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Hermon (Hebrew חֶרְמוֹן) is a single, well-defined place. It is the massive mountain, or mountain cluster, at the southern end of the Anti-Lebanon range, north of the region of Bashan and northeast of the Sea of Galilee. Its highest summit stands at roughly 9,200 feet, and its snows stay late into the year. The mountain formed a natural barrier between Israel, Aram (Syria), and Phoenicia. Its melting snow and springs feed the main headwaters of the Jordan River, so the river that runs through Israel's story begins at the foot of this mountain. The Hebrew name Hermon occurs 13 times in 13 different verses, and every one refers to the same mountain.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

The name most likely comes from the Hebrew root ḥaram, which means to set something apart, either as holy to God or as devoted to destruction. The same root lies behind the word Joshua's conquest narratives use for things devoted to the Lord. On that derivation, Hermon would mean something like the sacred mountain or the set-apart place. This is the most widely held explanation, and it fits what we know of the mountain as a religious high place. Still, ancient place names often predate Israel, and their exact origin cannot always be proven. The meaning sacred or set-apart mountain should therefore be held as probable rather than settled. Scripture itself never comments on the meaning of the name or plays on it.

The mountain went by several names. Deuteronomy 3:9 explains that the Sidonians call Hermon Sirion and the Amorites call it Senir. Deuteronomy 4:48 calls it "Mount Sion" and adds that this is Hermon. In Hebrew, "Sion" is a different word from "Zion," the hill of Jerusalem, even though the two look similar in English. These alternate names have their own Strong's entries: Sirion (H8303), Senir (H8149), and Sion (H7865). Their occurrences are not counted among the thirteen uses of Hermon. Senir sometimes appears beside Hermon as though it named a particular peak or part of the range (1 Chronicles 5:23; Song of Solomon 4:8). Another separately indexed name, Baal Hermon (H1179), lord of Hermon, is associated with the Hermon area. Judges 3:3 calls it "Mount Baal Hermon," and 1 Chronicles 5:23 uses it in a boundary description alongside Senir and Mount Hermon. Scripture does not say whether it named a separate peak, a town or a shrine. Psalm 42:6 also mentions "the heights of Hermon" using a plural form of the name that is indexed separately from this entry.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The northern limit of the land east of the Jordan. Hermon enters Scripture as Moses looks back on Israel's victories over Sihon and Og, the two Amorite kings east of the Jordan. Israel took the land "from the River Arnon to Mount Hermon" (Deuteronomy 3:8). Moses pauses to record the mountain's other names (Deuteronomy 3:9) and repeats the boundary in his summary of the land Israel held (Deuteronomy 4:48). Og, king of Bashan, had ruled over Mount Hermon and all Bashan (Joshua 12:5). In other words, the territory stretched from the gorge of the Arnon in the south to the giant mountain on the northern horizon.

The northern limit of Joshua's conquest. When the kings of northern Canaan gathered against Joshua, the list included "the Hivite below Hermon in the land of Mizpah" (Joshua 11:3). After God gave Israel victory at the waters of Merom, the narrator steps back to describe the whole campaign: "Thus Joshua took all this land: the mountain country, all the South, all the land of Goshen, the lowland, and the Jordan plain—the mountains of Israel and its lowlands, from Mount Halak and the ascent to Seir, even as far as Baal Gad in the Valley of Lebanon below Mount Hermon. He captured all their kings, and struck them down and killed them" (Joshua 11:16–17). Mount Halak in the barren south and Hermon in the snowy north frame the entire land. Joshua 12:1 repeats Hermon as the northern edge of the territory east of the Jordan.

Land still to be possessed. Joshua 13 adds an important note. When Joshua was old, the Lord told him that much land remained to be taken. Part of that remaining land reached "from Baal Gad below Mount Hermon as far as the entrance to Hamath" (Joshua 13:5), and the Lord promised that He Himself would drive out the inhabitants (Joshua 13:6). Hermon therefore marked both what Israel already held and the edge of what God still intended to give. The same chapter assigns "all Mount Hermon" with Bashan and Gilead to the tribes east of the Jordan (Joshua 13:11).

Manasseh's growth. Generations later, the half-tribe of Manasseh grew so numerous that it spread "from Bashan to Baal Hermon, that is, to Senir, or Mount Hermon" (1 Chronicles 5:23). The Chronicler's comment shows a tribe flourishing in the far north. The Chronicler then records that the tribes east of the Jordan "were unfaithful to the God of their fathers, and played the harlot after the gods of the peoples of the land," and that God sent Assyria to carry them into captivity (1 Chronicles 5:25–26). Their territory reached the area called Baal Hermon, lord of Hermon, a reminder that they lived among the names and gods of the land. The Chronicler, however, gives their unfaithfulness, not the place, as the reason for their exile.

Hermon in Israel's songs. In the psalms, Hermon becomes a witness to the Lord's greatness. Psalm 29 describes the thunderous voice of the Lord shaking the northern mountains: "He makes them also skip like a calf, Lebanon and Sirion like a young wild ox" (Psalm 29:6). Psalm 89 praises God as Creator of the whole earth: "The north and the south, You have created them; Tabor and Hermon rejoice in Your name" (Psalm 89:12). Psalm 133 turns to Hermon's famous dew to describe the blessing of brothers dwelling together in unity: "It is like the dew of Hermon, Descending upon the mountains of Zion; For there the LORD commanded the blessing— Life forevermore" (Psalm 133:3). In Psalm 42:6, a discouraged psalmist far from the temple says he will remember God "from the land of the Jordan, And from the heights of Hermon." That verse uses the separately indexed plural form of the name.

Wild heights in the Song of Solomon. In the Song, the bridegroom calls to his bride: "Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse, With me from Lebanon. Look from the top of Amana, From the top of Senir and Hermon, From the lions' dens, From the mountains of the leopards" (Song of Solomon 4:8). Hermon here is splendid and dangerous at the same time. It is a place of sweeping views and of predators, and the bridegroom wants his bride to come away with him from those heights.

The mountain near Caesarea Philippi. The New Testament never uses the name Hermon. Still, the mountain stands over a key turning point in the Gospels. Jesus took His disciples to the region of Caesarea Philippi, a town at Hermon's southwestern foot near one of the Jordan's springs. The town was known in that era for the worship of the Greek god Pan. There Peter confessed, "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God," and Jesus promised to build His church so that "the gates of Hades shall not prevail against it" (see Matthew 16:13–18). Days later ("after six days" in Matthew and Mark; "about eight days" in Luke) Jesus led Peter, James, and John "up on a high mountain by themselves," where He was transfigured before them, and Moses and Elijah appeared talking with Him (see Matthew 17:1–3; Mark 9:2–4; Luke 9:28–31). The Gospels give this sequence of events, but they do not name the mountain or trace the travel between the two scenes. Because Hermon is the high mountain beside Caesarea Philippi, its nearness makes it a plausible location. An older church tradition places the event on Mount Tabor in Galilee instead, so the identification remains possible rather than stated.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture plainly presents Hermon as a boundary of God's faithfulness. Joshua 11:16–17 spells out the land from south to north because God had promised that land to Abraham's descendants, and the list shows that He kept His word. People across much of northern Israel could look up and see Hermon's snow. Every time Israel saw that summit, they saw the edge of a gift they had not earned and could not have won without the Lord. Joshua 13 keeps the picture honest: some land beyond Hermon's foot still remained, and God promised to finish what He had begun. His faithfulness had a visible marker, and His purposes still reached beyond it.

Scripture also presents Hermon as a mountain under the Lord's rule. The compound name Baal Hermon, explained as lord of Hermon, suggests that the area was associated with Baal. The name Hermon itself probably means sacred mountain, and that probable meaning fits the same history. Psalm 89:12 says God created north and south and that Hermon rejoices in His name. Psalm 29:6 shows Sirion shaking at the Lord's voice. Both psalms depict the Lord's power over the northern mountains. They do not mention Baal, but read against the region's background the contrast is worth noticing: a mountain linked with the nations' gods is only a creature that answers to its Maker. These texts do not make Hermon a place of God's special dwelling the way Zion is. The poets took the most imposing landmark in the north and placed it under the Lord's rule.

One further connection is suggested by the name, if its probable derivation is correct. Hermon likely comes from the same root used for the devoting of Canaanite cities in Joshua's conquest, and Joshua's campaign ended at the mountain called Hermon. Scripture does not draw out this link, so it is best treated as an observation worth noticing rather than a point the text makes. Even so, it is striking that the land God gave His people in judgment on Canaan's corruption was bounded by a mountain whose name probably speaks of being set apart.

The history recorded in 1 Chronicles 5 adds a sober note. As the Chronicler tells it, the tribes who spread to Hermon's slopes did not stay faithful. A rich inheritance on the frontier did not protect them when their hearts turned to the gods of the land.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No New Testament writer names Hermon or calls it a type of anything. The connections here rest on geography, on the flow of the Gospel accounts, and on themes the Old Testament has already developed.

The strongest connection depends on a possible setting for the Transfiguration. If the "high mountain" of Matthew 17:1 is Hermon, the sequence carries great weight. At the foot of a mountain whose region bore Baal's name, in a town known for the worship of Pan, Peter confesses Jesus as the Son of the living God, and Jesus declares that the gates of Hades will not prevail against His church. Days later, on a high mountain that may have been Hermon, the Father speaks from the cloud: "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Hear Him!" (see Matthew 17:5). On this reading, a mountain whose region was linked with false worship witnessed the glory of God shining in the face of His Son. Peter later called the place "the holy mountain" (see 2 Peter 1:16–18). The location remains uncertain, and an older tradition favors Mount Tabor. The Gospels place the revelation on the mountain days after the confession ("after six days" in Matthew 17:1 and Mark 9:2; "about eight days" in Luke 9:28), but they do not name the mountain or trace the travel in between.

Some writers also point to later Jewish tradition. The book of 1 Enoch, a Jewish writing that is not part of Scripture, says that rebellious angels descended on the summit of Mount Hermon (1 Enoch 6:6). That documented tradition shows how some Jews of that era viewed the region. Nothing in Matthew suggests that Jesus' words about the gates of Hades allude to it, and the Bible itself makes no such claim about Hermon, so this belongs to background, not to biblical teaching.

Psalm 133 offers a different kind of connection, one of thematic resonance. The psalm says that where God's people dwell together in unity, "there the LORD commanded the blessing— Life forevermore." The New Testament shows that this life comes through Christ, who prayed that His people would be one (John 17:21) and who gives eternal life to all who believe in Him (John 3:16). The psalm does not predict Christ, but its picture of blessing that comes down from above and brings life finds its fullest meaning in the unity and life He gives His people.

Christian readers have long heard in Song of Solomon 4:8 the voice of Christ calling His people to come with Him. That is a devotional reading of a love poem rather than an explicit New Testament interpretation. Still, the call "Come with me" fits the way Jesus calls His followers to Himself throughout the Gospels.

5. Spirit & Life

Hermon speaks first of God's faithfulness that can be seen. Israel did not have to guess whether God had kept His promise; the snow on the northern horizon showed where the gift reached. Believers have their own markers: the cross, the empty tomb, and the answered prayers and preserved lives in their own history. Joshua 13 also reminds us that God's work is not finished at the point where we have already received. More land remained, and God said He would drive out the enemies Himself.

Hermon also teaches that nothing in creation belongs to false gods. The name Baal Hermon tied the area to Baal, yet Psalm 89 pictures Hermon rejoicing in the Lord's name. Whatever people around us treat as sacred, whether wealth, power, pleasure, or the self, still answers to its Creator. Christians do not need to fear the high places of their culture. They can confess, with the psalmist, that the north and the south belong to God.

Psalm 133 gives one of Scripture's clearest pictures of unity among God's people. The psalmist compares the blessing of brothers dwelling together in unity to Hermon's dew, "Descending upon the mountains of Zion." The comparison pictures unity as a blessing that comes down on God's people. Real unity among believers is not mainly something we manufacture by effort or agreement. It is a blessing the Lord commands, and where He commands it, He gives "Life forevermore." This should humble a divided church and also give it hope. We pursue peace with one another, but we also ask God to send what only He can give.

Psalm 42:6 adds a quieter word. A worshiper cut off from the temple, downcast and apparently far to the north in the land of the Jordan and the heights of Hermon, decides to remember God from exactly where he is. Distance from familiar places of worship did not put him beyond the reach of God's love. Many believers know what it is to feel spiritually far away. This psalmist shows that the right response is to remember the Lord and hope in Him, even before the feelings change.

Finally, the Song of Solomon shows Hermon's other side. Its heights were breathtaking, but they were also the haunt of lions and leopards. The bridegroom does not leave his bride there; he calls her to come with him. Impressive heights, whether achievements, influence, or dramatic spiritual experiences, are not where the soul is safest. Even at the Transfiguration, Peter wanted to build shelters and stay on the mountain, but Jesus led His disciples back down to ordinary obedience and toward the cross (see Matthew 17:4, 9). Our true home is not a peak experience but nearness to the One who calls us to Himself.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Deuteronomy 3:8–9 – Hermon as the northern limit of Israel's victories east of the Jordan, with its Sidonian and Amorite names, Sirion and Senir.

  • Deuteronomy 4:48 – Repeats the boundary and identifies "Mount Sion" as Hermon.

  • Joshua 11:16–17 – Hermon and Mount Halak frame the whole land Joshua took, a visible sign that God kept His promise.

  • Joshua 12:1, 5 – Hermon as the northern edge of the land east of the Jordan and part of Og's former kingdom.

  • Joshua 13:5–6, 11 – Land remaining below Hermon and God's promise to drive out its inhabitants; Mount Hermon assigned to the eastern tribes.

  • 1 Chronicles 5:23–26 – Manasseh's expansion to Baal Hermon and Senir, followed by unfaithfulness and exile.

  • Psalm 29:6 – Sirion (Hermon) shaking at the voice of the Lord.

  • Psalm 89:12 – Hermon rejoicing in God's name, a picture of the Creator's rule over the northern mountains.

  • Psalm 133:3 – The dew of Hermon as a picture of the blessing God commands on a united people.

  • Psalm 42:6 – Remembering God from the heights of Hermon (a related plural form of the name).

  • Song of Solomon 4:8 – The splendid but dangerous heights of Senir and Hermon, and the bridegroom's call to come with him.

  • Matthew 16:13–18; 17:1–9 (with Mark 9:2–8; Luke 9:28–36) – Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi near Hermon's foot and the Transfiguration on a "high mountain," plausibly Hermon, though the Gospels do not name it and an older tradition favors Mount Tabor.

  • 2 Peter 1:16–18 – Peter's memory of the glory he saw on "the holy mountain."
