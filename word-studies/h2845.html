HETH - (Chêth, Strong's H2845)

Summary

Heth is named in the Bible's family table of nations as a son of Canaan, listed right after Canaan's firstborn, Sidon (Genesis 10:15; 1 Chronicles 1:13). His name lives on mainly through his descendants, "the sons of Heth" and "the daughters of Heth," a Canaanite community living around Hebron in the days of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The name is often connected with a Hebrew root meaning "to be shattered" or "to be dismayed," which would give it a sense like "terror" or "dread." That derivation is commonly proposed but not certain, and Scripture itself never comments on the name's meaning or builds anything on it.

The sons of Heth stand at two turning points in the story of God's promises to Abraham. They sold Abraham the cave of Machpelah, where he buried Sarah. That field is the burial property whose purchase Genesis records in full, and it became the family grave of Abraham, Isaac, Rebekah, Leah, and Jacob. A generation later, the daughters of Heth whom Esau married brought grief to Isaac and Rebekah, and Rebekah's dread that Jacob might marry one of them helped send him away to find a wife from his own people. Together these scenes show a man of faith living honestly among gracious neighbors without being absorbed by them. Read with Hebrews 11, the family grave also stands within Abraham's hope that God would keep His word beyond death, a hope the New Testament ties to the resurrection.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Heth is first an individual: the son of Canaan, grandson of Ham, and great-grandson of Noah. Genesis lists him second among Canaan's sons: "Canaan begot Sidon his firstborn, and Heth" (Genesis 10:15). The Chronicler repeats the line almost word for word (1 Chronicles 1:13). Beyond these two genealogies, every occurrence of the name refers to Heth's descendants as a group. They are called "the sons of Heth" throughout Genesis 23 and in the later references to Machpelah, and "the daughters of Heth" in Genesis 27:46.

The name is commonly derived from the Hebrew root chathath, "to be shattered, dismayed, or terrified," which would yield a meaning like "terror" or "dread." This is a probable explanation, not a settled one. The Bible offers no wordplay on the name. The people who bear it in Genesis are courteous and generous rather than terrifying, so it would be a mistake to read the supposed meaning as a description of their character.

A related but separately indexed name needs careful distinction. The gentilic form Chittiy (H2850), "Hittite," describes a member of this people. Ephron, who sells Abraham the cave, is called "Ephron the Hittite" in the same verse that names "the sons of Heth" (Genesis 23:10). Esau's wives are also called Hittites (Genesis 26:34). Later Scripture speaks of the Hittite nation, Uriah the Hittite, and Ezekiel's "Hittite" mother of Jerusalem using this related term, not H2845 itself.

Historians still debate how the Hittites of Canaan relate to the great Hittite empire of Anatolia (modern Turkey). Some see a direct connection, and others think the Canaanite group was a local people with a similar name. The Bible's own concern is clear: it traces Heth through Canaan, which places his descendants among the native peoples of the land God promised to Abraham.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Origins. Heth appears in the Table of Nations among Canaan's sons (Genesis 10:15). Earlier, Noah had pronounced a curse on Canaan (Genesis 9:25). Genesis does not apply that curse to Heth by name, but it does place his family within the Canaanite world that later stories will set beside Abraham's line. The Chronicler's retelling, written for Israel after the exile, keeps Heth in the record of the nations from which God called His people (1 Chronicles 1:13).

The purchase of Machpelah. Genesis 23 is the main scene. Sarah has died at Hebron, and Abraham, after more than sixty years in Canaan, owns no ground in which to bury her. He "stood up from before his dead, and spoke to the sons of Heth," saying, "I am a foreigner and a visitor among you. Give me property for a burial place among you" (Genesis 23:3–4). Their answer honors him: "Hear us, my lord: You are a mighty prince among us; bury your dead in the choicest of our burial places" (23:6). The Hebrew phrase behind "mighty prince" includes the word for God, and some translations render it "prince of God." Genesis records that they honored this foreigner in these terms; it does not say what each of them worshiped or how fully they understood the phrase.

Abraham bows to them twice (23:7, 12) and asks for the cave of Machpelah belonging to Ephron, insisting on paying "the full price" (23:9). Ephron offers it as a gift: "I give you the field and the cave that is in it... I give it to you. Bury your dead!" (23:11). Abraham refuses that too: "I will give you money for the field; take it from me" (23:13). Ephron names a price, four hundred shekels of silver, as though it were nothing: "What is that between you and me?" (23:15). Abraham simply pays it. He "weighed out the silver for Ephron which he had named in the hearing of the sons of Heth" (23:16).

The text then records the deed with legal care. It lists the field, the cave, the trees, and the borders, "deeded to Abraham as a possession in the presence of the sons of Heth, before all who went in at the gate of his city" (23:17–18). The sons of Heth served as the public witnesses of the sale. Abraham buried Sarah there (23:19), and Genesis closes the chapter by stating that the sons of Heth confirmed the field and cave to Abraham as a burial possession (23:20).

The family grave. Machpelah kept being remembered by its origin. Isaac and Ishmael buried Abraham there, in the field "Abraham purchased from the sons of Heth" (Genesis 25:9–10). On his deathbed in Egypt, Jacob gave instructions to be carried home. He listed who lay there, Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, and Leah, and described the field as "purchased from the sons of Heth" (Genesis 49:29–32). His sons carried out his wishes (Genesis 50:13). A purchase made at Sarah's death became the burial place of three generations.

The daughters of Heth. The next generation met the sons of Heth in a more dangerous way. Esau married two Hittite women, Judith and Basemath, and Genesis says they were a grief of mind to Isaac and Rebekah (Genesis 26:34–35). Abraham had already made his servant swear not to take a wife for Isaac from the daughters of the Canaanites (Genesis 24:3). After Jacob received the blessing, Rebekah told Isaac she was weary of her life because of the daughters of Heth. If Jacob married one of them, she said, her life would no longer be worth living (Genesis 27:46). Her words also served a practical purpose, since she had just learned that Esau planned to kill Jacob (27:41–45). Her grief over Esau's wives was nonetheless real, and Isaac acted on it. He charged Jacob not to marry a Canaanite woman and sent him to Paddan Aram (Genesis 28:1–2).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The name Heth appears where God's two great promises to Abraham come under pressure: the land and the offspring.

On the land, Scripture states that God promised Canaan to Abraham's descendants and lists the Hittites among its peoples (Genesis 15:18–21, using the related name). Scripture also states that Abraham called himself "a foreigner and a visitor" (23:4) and gained this field only by paying full price before witnesses. What follows from that is a careful inference. Abraham did not seize the land, and he did not accept it as a favor that could later be held over him. Years earlier, he had refused even a thread from the king of Sodom so no one could say a man had made him rich (Genesis 14:22–23). The same instinct appears here. He wanted a clean, public title that no one could later dispute, and he left the rest of the promise in God's hands. God had also told him that "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Genesis 15:16). The peaceful dealings at Hebron fit that long patience toward the peoples of Canaan.

On the offspring, the daughters of Heth show where the greater danger lay. The sons of Heth were never hostile. In Genesis 23 their leaders dealt with Abraham courteously and publicly. Yet the next generation's closeness to them through marriage threatened the distinct family through whom God intended to bless the nations. Genesis does not present the danger as war. It presents it as gradual absorption into the surrounding culture.

Later Scripture confirms that this danger was real. Ezekiel, using the related term, indicts Jerusalem: "Your birth and your nativity are from the land of Canaan; your father was an Amorite and your mother a Hittite" (Ezekiel 16:3). Israel's unfaithfulness had made her look like the very people the patriarchs had kept their sons from marrying. The same pattern of foreign marriages pulling hearts away from the Lord recurs with Solomon, Ahab, and the returned exiles in Ezra and Nehemiah.

Grace also crosses this line. Uriah the Hittite, one of David's mighty men, shows more integrity than the king in 2 Samuel 11. The concern in Genesis was never ethnic contempt. It was loyalty to the Lord and His promise.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament speaks directly about the faith of the patriarchs who lie at Machpelah. Hebrews says Abraham lived in the land of promise as in a foreign country and looked for a city whose builder is God. It adds that these people all died in faith, not having received the promises, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth (Hebrews 11:9–10, 13–16). Abraham's words to the sons of Heth, "I am a foreigner and a visitor among you," are the historical ground of that confession. Peter uses similar language when he urges believers to live as sojourners and pilgrims (1 Peter 2:11). Stephen adds that God gave Abraham no inheritance in the land, not even a foot of ground, yet promised it to him and his descendants (Acts 7:5).

That is why the grave matters. The piece of the promised land whose purchase Genesis records was a tomb. A careful burial purchase does not by itself prove resurrection faith. Hebrews supplies that ground: Abraham, offering Isaac, concluded "that God was able to raise him up, even from the dead" (Hebrews 11:17–19), and he held God's promise of the land for his descendants (Genesis 13:15; Acts 7:5). Read within that wider hope, the tomb at Machpelah becomes a place of waiting rather than of loss. Jesus Himself argued for the resurrection from God's words, "I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." He drew the conclusion that God "is not the God of the dead, but of the living" (Matthew 22:31–32, paraphrasing the point). Every man He named was buried in the field bought from the sons of Heth.

One further connection is a thematic resonance rather than a stated type. Abraham bought a tomb to keep. The Son of Abraham was laid in a borrowed tomb and left it empty on the third day (Matthew 27:57–60; 28:6). The hope Abraham buried with Sarah finds its answer in that empty grave.

5. Spirit & Life

Genesis 23 shows a man of faith living among people outside the covenant family. Abraham grieved openly, bowed respectfully, spoke honestly, and paid what he owed. He could receive the honor of his neighbors without depending on their favor. God's people are called to that same kind of presence in the world: gracious and above reproach, yet clear about where their true home is.

The chapter also teaches us to hold God's promises across a longer horizon than our own lives. Abraham owned a grave, not a kingdom, and he did not count God unfaithful because of it. He planted his family's claim in the ground where his loved ones would wait for God to act. Christians bury their dead in the same hope, now made certain by the risen Christ.

Finally, the daughters of Heth are a sober warning. The friendliest world is not always the safest one. Rebekah's grief recognized something Esau ignored: the people we bind our lives to will influence whom we worship. Paul's command not to be unequally yoked with unbelievers (2 Corinthians 6:14) carries that concern into the church. The cure is not suspicion of our neighbors, since Abraham treated the sons of Heth with honor. It is a settled loyalty to the Lord that shapes our closest commitments.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:15; 1 Chronicles 1:13 — Heth's identity as a son of Canaan, listed after Sidon.

  • Genesis 23:3–20 — Abraham's purchase of Machpelah before the sons of Heth. The passage includes his confession as "a foreigner and a visitor" and their naming him a "mighty prince."

  • Genesis 25:9–10; 49:29–32; 50:13 — Machpelah, "purchased from the sons of Heth," becomes the burial place of the patriarchs.

  • Genesis 24:3; 26:34–35; 27:46; 28:1–2 — The concern over Canaanite and Hittite wives, and Jacob's departure.

  • Genesis 15:16, 18–21 — God's land promise and His patience toward Canaan's peoples.

  • Ezekiel 16:3 — Jerusalem's unfaithfulness described as Amorite and Hittite parentage (related name, H2850).

  • Hebrews 11:9–19; Acts 7:5; 1 Peter 2:11 — The patriarchs as strangers and pilgrims who died in faith and hoped in resurrection.

  • Matthew 22:31–32 — Jesus grounds the resurrection in God's relationship to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.
