SEAL, SEAL UP, MAKE AN END - (châtham, Strong's H2856)

Summary

The Hebrew verb châtham means to seal, to press a mark of authority onto something or to close it off so it stays secure. In Esther, a decree sealed with the king's ring could not be revoked. Jeremiah sealed a deed to a field. Prophets sealed visions until God's time to open them. The same verb can also mean simply to stop up or shut, as when a flow is stopped or stars are hidden from sight.

In Scripture, sealing shows the authority to make something final and the power to keep it safe. God seals the stars, seals revelation until its appointed hour, and in Daniel 9:24 promises a time "to seal up vision and prophecy." Job said his transgression was "sealed up in a bag," words that may voice fear that God was keeping evidence for judgment or hope that God would tie off his sin and put it away. The gospel answers that question with forgiveness: in Christ, God promises to remember His people's sins no more, like a record sealed and never reopened. In Christ, God also seals His people with the Holy Spirit as His own possession.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient world, a seal was usually an engraved ring or stone cylinder. Pressed into clay or wax, it left the owner's mark. A sealed document carried that person's authority. A sealed container could not be opened without the tampering being obvious. The Hebrew Old Testament uses châtham in royal courts (1 Kings, Esther), in legal transactions (Jeremiah), in public commitments (Nehemiah), in poetry (Job, Song of Solomon), and in prophecy (Isaiah, Daniel). The related noun for a signet ring and the Aramaic verb used in Daniel 6 are separate words, though they belong to the same family of ideas.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To seal with authority: to affix a mark that makes a document official and binding, as with the king's signet ring in Esther 8:8.

2. To seal up for safekeeping or concealment: to close something so its contents are preserved and kept from view until the right time, as with the "book that is sealed" in Isaiah 29:11.

3. To close off or stop: to block a flow or shut something away from use, as when God "seals off the stars" (Job 9:7) or a bodily discharge is "stopped" (Leviticus 15:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning changes with the thing being sealed. When the object is a letter or decree, sealing makes it binding. When it is a scroll or vision, sealing keeps it closed, either as judgment on people who will not see (Isaiah 29) or as protection for a later day (Daniel 12). When the object is something in creation, such as stars or human hands, sealing becomes a picture of God's power to restrain. When the object is sin, Job 14:17 raises two possibilities: God may be storing sin up for judgment, or closing it off for good. Daniel 9:24 describes God dealing with sin in the seventy weeks, bringing it to an end. Deuteronomy 32:34 speaks of what God keeps sealed up in store for the day of vengeance.

4. Theological Significance

Sealing belongs to whoever holds the authority. Scripture places that authority with God over everything. Job says God "commands the sun, and it does not rise; He seals off the stars" (Job 9:7). Job uses the language of a royal seal for the heavens. The lights of the sky shine because God allows them to shine, and they stop when He says so.

The word also shows that God preserves what He seals. He keeps His word secure through generations of unbelief (Isaiah 8:16; Daniel 12:4). In Deuteronomy 32:34–35 the LORD speaks of something "laid up in store with Me, Sealed up among My treasures," kept for the time when "Vengeance is Mine." The verse does not say exactly what is stored, but it is held for the judgment God has appointed. In these passages, what God seals is not lost, misplaced, or altered. That truth is a comfort to the faithful and a warning to the rebellious.

Ezekiel 28:12 gives the word a striking turn. The king of Tyre is called "the seal of perfection, full of wisdom and perfect in beauty." He was a finished, stamped example of splendor. The lament that follows describes how pride corrupted him. The greater the stamp of glory, the deeper the ruin when a creature turns that glory against its Maker.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Esther 3:12; 8:8 and 1 Kings 21:8 — the authority of the king's seal. Haman's decree to destroy the Jews was "written, and sealed with the king's signet ring" (Esther 3:12). Esther 8:8 explains why that was so terrible: "whatever is written in the king's name and sealed with the king's signet ring no one can revoke." The king did not cancel the first decree. Instead, a second decree was written at Mordecai's command in the king's name and sealed with the same ring, and it permitted the Jews to defend themselves (Esther 8:9–11). The first decree still stood, but a second sealed word overcame its intended result. The story of Naboth in 1 Kings 21:8 shows the other side of this power. Jezebel "wrote letters in Ahab's name, sealed them with his seal," and used them to arrange Naboth's murder. The seal carried and presented the king's authority, and Jezebel used that outward mark to give her letters the appearance of royal authorization. A seal can present authority, but it cannot make the one who uses it good.

Jeremiah 32:10–44 — sealing a deed under siege. While Babylon's armies surrounded Jerusalem, God told Jeremiah to buy his cousin's field at Anathoth. Jeremiah writes, "I signed the deed and sealed it, took witnesses, and weighed the money on the scales" (Jeremiah 32:10). By any ordinary measure this was a foolish purchase, since the land was about to fall to the enemy. Jeremiah's seal made his own purchase legally secure, with witnesses, a sealed copy and an open copy (Jeremiah 32:9–12). The deeds were placed in a clay jar "that they may last many days" (Jeremiah 32:14). The restoration that purchase pointed to was still future. God promised, "Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land" (Jeremiah 32:15), and that men would again "sign deeds and seal them" when He caused the captives to return (Jeremiah 32:44). Jeremiah completed a real purchase while the city was under siege, trusting a restoration that lay years ahead. Faith acts on what God has said before circumstances agree.

Isaiah 29:11 and Daniel 12:4, 9 — two kinds of sealed book. In Isaiah, God's vision had become "like the words of a book that is sealed." The literate man says, "I cannot, for it is sealed." The words were there, but a people who had closed their hearts to God found His word closed to them. That sealing was judgment. Daniel received the opposite command: "shut up the words, and seal the book until the time of the end" (Daniel 12:4). There the seal protected true revelation and kept it safe until God's appointed time (12:9). In Isaiah, the seal shuts out the rebellious. In Daniel, it guards the truth for those who will wait.

Song of Solomon 4:12 — the fountain sealed. The bridegroom calls his bride "a garden enclosed... a spring shut up, a fountain sealed." A sealed spring was kept from contamination and from strangers. It belonged to its owner. Here the seal speaks of a love kept whole and reserved for one beloved. Sealing language can describe tenderness and belonging as well as legal force.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Job's cry in Job 14:17 is "My transgression is sealed up in a bag, And You cover my iniquity." Readers have taken it in two ways. One reading hears fear that God is keeping evidence for the day of judgment, much as Deuteronomy 32:34–35 speaks of what God keeps in store for vengeance. Another, drawing on Job's hope in the surrounding verses, hears a longing that God would tie off his sin and put it away. The verse can bear either reading. On the first, the sealed bag is evidence kept; on the second, sealing and covering mean putting the sin away.

Daniel 9:24 offers a comparison, for it describes God dealing with sin in its promise of the seventy weeks: "To finish the transgression, To make an end of sins, To make reconciliation for iniquity, To bring in everlasting righteousness, To seal up vision and prophecy, And to anoint the Most Holy." In the written Hebrew text, the verb behind "make an end of sins" is châtham. The Jewish scribes' traditional reading pronounces it as a similar word meaning finish, and the NKJV follows that sense. Both readings speak of sin brought to an end. That end comes alongside reconciliation for iniquity and everlasting righteousness, which Christians see accomplished by Christ at the cross.

The same verse seals up "vision and prophecy." That likely means confirming them and bringing them to their completion. Revelation 5 picks up the image of the sealed book. When no one in heaven or on earth could open the sealed scroll, only the slain Lamb was found worthy. The One who dealt with sin is the One who opens God's sealed purposes.

The New Testament uses a different Greek word for sealing, but the Old Testament meanings carry over. Believers "were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise" (Ephesians 1:13). God "has sealed us and given us the Spirit in our hearts as a guarantee" (2 Corinthians 1:22). The authority of Esther's ring, the promised future behind Jeremiah's deed, the safekeeping in Daniel, and the belonging pictured in the Song all come together in the Spirit's seal. Unlike Ahab's seal in Jezebel's hand, this seal is used by a God whose character is wholly good. His sealed word for His people is a word no one can revoke.

7. Spirit & Life

Job 37:7 says, "He seals the hand of every man, That all men may know His work." In context, Elihu is describing snow and heavy rain that stop work in the fields, and he gives the purpose of that stopping: "that all men may know His work." Believers can apply this to their own seasons of interruption, whether an illness, a closed door, or a loss. Such a season can become an occasion to notice God and His work, even when we cannot know His particular reason for what has happened.

Jeremiah's clay jar invites a similar trust. Some of God's promises are signed, sealed, and stored for a day we cannot yet see. A sealed promise is not a broken one. Daniel was told to close the book and wait. Jeremiah was told to buy the field and wait. Both men acted on God's word and left the timing with Him.

For the one burdened by sin, the gospel speaks to Job's fear. Daniel 9:24 speaks of reconciliation for iniquity, and sins forgiven in Christ are not reopened against the one who trusts Him: "Their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more" (Hebrews 10:17). A record sealed shut is a fitting picture of that forgiveness. It is a different use of sealing from the visions Daniel sealed until their appointed time and the scroll the Lamb opens. Those were sealed in order to be opened. In this picture, the record of forgiven sin is sealed to stay closed. The believer who carries the Spirit's seal can live as a "fountain sealed": kept, guarded, and reserved for the Lord who bought him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 15:3 — A discharge that is "stopped" shows the plain physical sense of closing off a flow.

  • Nehemiah 9:38; 10:1 — The leaders, Levites and priests of the returned exiles put their seal on a written commitment to obey God, publicly binding themselves to His law.

  • Isaiah 8:16 — The command to "seal the law among my disciples" pictures guarding God's word within a faithful remnant during a time of unbelief.

  • Job 33:16 — Elihu says God speaks in dreams and seals instruction to people. The image suggests God pressing His warning firmly onto the heart to turn a person from pride.

  • Job 24:16 — Thieves "break into houses which they marked for themselves in the daytime." Here the verb, rendered "marked" in the NKJV, describes what thieves do by day to the houses they will enter after dark, a dark use of the word's ordinary sense.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2368 (chôwthâm) — The noun for a seal or signet ring, a lexical relative of châtham. It names the instrument used for the sealing this verb describes.

  • H2857 (chătham, Aramaic) — The Aramaic counterpart. It is used when the king seals the lions' den "with his own signet ring" so "the purpose concerning Daniel might not be changed" (Daniel 6:17). The verse is a clear picture of a seal making a decision final.
