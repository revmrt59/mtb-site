WEDDING, MARRIAGE - (chăthunnâh, Strong's H2861)

Summary

The Hebrew word chăthunnâh means "wedding," the day a marriage is celebrated. It appears only once in the Old Testament, in Song of Solomon 3:11. There the daughters of Zion are called to come out and see King Solomon wearing the crown his mother placed on him "on the day of his wedding, the day of the gladness of his heart." In this single use, Scripture defines the wedding day as the king's day of joy. The wedding is a public celebration, it brings honor, and at its center is a glad heart.

Because the word is so rare, its full weight comes from the scene it describes and from the passages that pick up the same picture. The royal wedding in Psalm 45, the prophets' promise that God rejoices over His people as a bridegroom over his bride, Jesus' description of Himself as the Bridegroom, and the marriage supper of the Lamb in Revelation all develop this one verse's portrait. Song of Solomon 3:11 lets us see a King whose happiest day is the day He is joined to His bride. It also invites us to go out and look at Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Chăthunnâh is a Hebrew noun connected to the verb chathan, which describes forming a marriage relationship, giving someone in marriage, or becoming a son-in-law. The noun names the event itself, the wedding.

The word appears only in the Song of Solomon, a collection of love poetry. Its one verse closes a royal procession (Song 3:6–11). Solomon's couch is carried "out of the wilderness," perfumed with myrrh and frankincense. Sixty armed men guard it, and the palanquin is built of cedar from Lebanon, silver, gold, and purple, its interior "paved with love." The word "wedding" is the last thing that procession shows us.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chăthunnâh has one sense: a wedding, the day and celebration of a marriage. Older English versions render it "espousals." The NKJV translates it "wedding." The verse does not tell us anything about the ceremony itself. It places the word within a day of royal honor and personal joy.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The poet uses parallel lines, a common pattern in Hebrew poetry. In this pattern the second line restates and expands the first:

"On the day of his wedding,

The day of the gladness of his heart."

The parallel tells us how the poet wants the reader to understand the wedding. He does not describe its legal terms or its political advantages. He describes the bridegroom's inner delight. For Solomon, "the day of his wedding" and "the day of the gladness of his heart" are the same day.

4. Theological Significance

This single verse brings together three things that belong together in God's design for marriage. The first is public witness, because the daughters of Zion are summoned to look. The second is honor, because the king wears a crown. The third is deep joy, because the day is called the gladness of his heart. Taken together, these three keep marriage from shrinking into either a private feeling or a cold contract. A wedding is joy made public and honored before others.

The verse also shows what Scripture considers fitting to a wedding. It is a day for gladness. The same expectation lies behind Jesus' question, "Can the friends of the bridegroom fast while the bridegroom is with them?" (Mark 2:19). Mourning does not belong at a wedding. When Scripture later describes God's relationship with His people as a marriage, it carries this joy along with it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 3:1–11: from the night search to the public procession. Chapter 3 opens in darkness and anxiety. "By night on my bed I sought the one I love; I sought him, but I did not find him" (3:1). The woman rises, searches the streets, and questions the watchmen. When she finds him, "I held him and would not let him go" (3:4). Everything in those verses is private, hidden, and urgent. Then the scene changes. A procession comes "out of the wilderness like pillars of smoke" (3:6), and the chapter ends in broad daylight with a call to everyone: "Go forth, O daughters of Zion, and see" (3:11). Love that began as a lonely search at night ends in a celebration the whole city is invited to watch. The wedding is where hidden longing becomes public joy.

The crown his mother gave him. The crown Solomon wears in 3:11 is described in a specific way. It is the crown "with which his mother crowned him on the day of his wedding." Solomon did not win it in battle, and the verse does not present it as a symbol of his rule. It comes from family blessing and belongs to this day. Among all the gold and armed guards of the procession, the honor the poet chooses to highlight is the honor of the marriage. Proverbs states the same truth from the other side: "An excellent wife is the crown of her husband" (Prov. 12:4). In God's design, faithful love is itself a crowning honor.

Psalm 45: the companion royal wedding song. Psalm 45 is the other royal wedding song in Scripture, and it fills in what Song 3:11 compresses into one line. Psalm 45 describes the king being anointed with "the oil of gladness" and dressed in garments fragrant with myrrh and aloes (Ps. 45:7–8). The bride is "all glorious within the palace; her clothing is woven with gold" (45:13). The psalm ends with a procession of its own: "With gladness and rejoicing they shall be brought; they shall enter the King's palace" (45:15). Both poems describe fragrance, splendor, a public procession, and above all gladness. Read together, they show the royal wedding as Scripture's picture of joy at its fullest.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets take the joy of the royal bridegroom and apply it to God Himself. "As the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you" (Isa. 62:5). Zephaniah says the LORD "will rejoice over you with gladness... He will rejoice over you with singing" (Zeph. 3:17). The gladness of heart that marked Solomon's wedding day becomes a promise about the heart of God toward His people.

The procession "out of the wilderness" also fits a larger pattern in Scripture, although the Song does not make the connection itself. God told Jerusalem, "I remember you, the kindness of your youth, the love of your betrothal, when you went after Me in the wilderness" (Jer. 2:2). Some readers also see the meal of Israel's elders before God on Mount Sinai (Ex. 24:9–11) as a kind of wedding feast that followed the wilderness journey. In this pattern the wilderness is the place of preparation and the wedding is the destination.

Jesus calls Himself the Bridegroom (Mark 2:19). He also describes the kingdom as "a certain king who arranged a marriage for his son" (Matt. 22:2). Paul says Christ "loved the church and gave Himself for her... that He might present her to Himself a glorious church" (Eph. 5:25–27). Hebrews gives the reason Jesus endured: "for the joy that was set before Him endured the cross" (Heb. 12:2). Hebrews does not quote Song 3:11. Still, the two passages resonate with each other. The King's wedding day was the gladness of his heart, and the joy set before Christ included the bride He came to purchase and present to Himself.

The whole story ends at a wedding. "Let us be glad and rejoice and give Him glory, for the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready" (Rev. 19:7). The call to the daughters of Zion to go out and see becomes a blessing on everyone who is invited: "Blessed are those who are called to the marriage supper of the Lamb!" (19:9). History moves toward a public royal wedding where mourning and fasting will be over for good.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers think about salvation mainly from their own side: rescue, pardon, relief. Song 3:11 asks us to look at it from the King's side. "Go forth... and see King Solomon." What the daughters of Zion are called to see is a king on the day of his greatest gladness. The gospel extends the same invitation. Come and look at Christ. He is not reluctant about His people. Their union with Him is His joy. A believer who grasps this can stop approaching God as someone grudgingly tolerated and start living as someone whose presence brings Him gladness.

This joy does not make holiness less important. It gives believers a reason for holiness. The bride in Revelation "has made herself ready," and "to her it was granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright" (Rev. 19:7–8). In Jesus' parable, the man who came without a wedding garment was found out the moment the king came in to see the guests (Matt. 22:11–12). A believer pursues holiness the way a bride prepares for a wedding she longs for. The motive is love and expectation, and fear is not the driving force. Isaiah 61:10 puts both truths together: "He has clothed me with the garments of salvation... as a bride adorns herself with her jewels." God supplies the garment, and the bride gladly wears it.

The movement of Song of Solomon 3 also speaks to seasons of spiritual night. The woman's anxious searching did not have the last word in that chapter. Her search ended in a procession and a wedding. Believers who feel they are seeking and not finding can remember that the story God is telling ends in daylight, in public, and with a glad King.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Song of Solomon 3:11 is the only occurrence of chăthunnâh, and it is discussed above. The passages cited elsewhere in this study use other Hebrew and Greek words for weddings, bridegrooms, and marriage. They support the study as related biblical imagery, not as further uses of this word.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2859 (chathan), lexical relative: the verb meaning to form a marriage relationship or become a son-in-law. Chăthunnâh is the noun connected to this verb.

  • Canonical pairing: Song of Solomon 3:11 and Psalm 45 are Scripture's two royal wedding songs. They share the themes of procession, splendor, and gladness, and each passage helps explain the other.
