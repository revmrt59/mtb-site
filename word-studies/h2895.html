BE PLEASING, GO WELL, DO GOOD - (ṭôwb, Strong's H2895)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ṭôwb means to be good in action or effect: to be pleasing, to go well, or to do good to someone. It describes goodness in motion. A plan seems good to a king, life goes well for a people, a heart is refreshed, or God sets Himself to bless. English translations render it in many ways, including "well," "pleases," "good," "better," "lovely," and even "embellished."

The word follows a thread through Scripture. Moses tells Israel to walk in God's ways "that it may be well with you" (Deuteronomy 5:33). Esther and Nehemiah plead before Persian kings with "if it pleases the king." Job asks in anguish whether it seems good to God to oppress him. Then the prophets give the word its fullest voice. In Jeremiah 32:41 God says, "I will rejoice over them to do them good." Doing good to His people is something God delights in with all His heart.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Ṭôwb is a Hebrew verb closely related to the far more common adjective ṭôb (H2896), the ordinary word for "good." The adjective usually describes what something is. The verb usually describes what something does or how something turns out: whether a matter pleases someone, whether life goes well, whether a person acts kindly.

The word turns up in very ordinary settings, such as Moses inviting his father-in-law on the journey, servants seeking music to calm a troubled king, and courtiers framing requests to a Persian emperor. It also appears in some of the most important promises in the prophets. Its everyday character is part of its value. The same word that describes ordinary human kindness describes God's own resolve to bless.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core idea is being or doing what is good for someone, or being agreeable in someone's eyes. Context shows which emphasis is meant.

1. To go well or prosper. This sense describes life turning out well, as in "that it may be well with you" (Deuteronomy 5:33) or a servant who "prospers" in his master's house (Deuteronomy 15:16).

2. To be pleasing or acceptable to someone. This is the language of the royal court, as in "If it pleases the king" (Nehemiah 2:5; Esther 1:19).

3. To do good or act well toward someone. Moses promises Hobab, "we will treat you well" (Numbers 10:29), and God promises "to do them good" (Jeremiah 32:41).

4. To be fine, lovely, or impressive. Balaam marvels at how lovely Israel's tents are (Numbers 24:5), and Proverbs speaks of creatures "majestic" in their stride (Proverbs 30:29).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning shifts with the subject of the verb. When circumstances are the subject, ṭôwb describes flourishing: things "go well." When a person's judgment is in view, the word describes approval: something "pleases" or "seems good." When someone acts toward another person, it describes kindness or benefit, as when Jonathan watches for "good toward David" from Saul (1 Samuel 20:12).

The setting matters as well. In 1 Samuel 16:16 and 16:23, Saul's servants expect David's harp to make Saul "well," and when David played, the distressing spirit left him and Saul was refreshed. The word can describe relief and restored well-being, without any reference to moral virtue.

The word can also be turned against itself. In Hosea 10:1, Israel grows prosperous and pours its bounty into idolatry: "They have embellished his sacred pillars." The people did "good" work on false worship and made it more beautiful while they themselves drifted further from God.

4. Theological Significance

Running through these passages is a question about where real well-being comes from. Deuteronomy ties it to the Lord: "You shall walk in all the ways which the LORD your God has commanded you, that you may live and that it may be well with you" (Deuteronomy 5:33). Removing innocent blood from the land is commanded for the same reason (Deuteronomy 19:13). God's commands describe the conditions under which His people can flourish.

Israel's memory told a different story. In the wilderness they complained, "For it was well with us in Egypt" (Numbers 11:18). Their nostalgia used the same word as Moses' promise. Slaves remembered the fish and the vegetables and forgot the whip, and they called that life good. Scripture shows the human heart misjudging what is "well," and Hosea's embellished pillars show the same error later in the nation's history. People pursue what seems good to them and decorate it lavishly, while the One who actually does them good stands outside the picture.

Against that background, Scripture shows that God's own pleasure is steady. When Balaam "saw that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel" (Numbers 24:1), he stopped looking for omens to curse them. The hired prophet could not change what pleased God. The same verb shows up again and again in the Persian court. A plan to destroy the Jews is put to Ahasuerus as something that might please the king (Esther 3:9). Later Esther uses the same formula to plead for her people's lives (Esther 7:3). An earthly king's pleasure could swing either way. God is never named in Esther, yet the whole book shows Him overruling that unstable pleasure to preserve His people. Nehemiah stands before Artaxerxes with the same words, "If it pleases the king" (Nehemiah 2:5), and later credits the king's favor to "the good hand of my God" upon him (Nehemiah 2:8).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Jeremiah 32:40–41 — God rejoices to do good. Jeremiah speaks these words while Jerusalem is under siege and its fall is certain. Even so, God tells him to buy a field as a pledge that the land will be inhabited again. Then comes the promise: "I will make an everlasting covenant with them, that I will not turn away from doing them good; but I will put My fear in their hearts so that they will not depart from Me" (Jeremiah 32:40). Verse 41 uses ṭôwb: "Yes, I will rejoice over them to do them good, and I will assuredly plant them in this land, with all My heart and with all My soul."

Each detail carries weight. God does not grant this good grudgingly; He rejoices in it. The phrase "with all My heart and with all My soul" echoes the wholehearted love God asked of Israel in Deuteronomy 6:5, and here God gives that same wholehearted devotion to His people. He also deals with the problem that had always ruined Israel's well-being. Deuteronomy said it would go well if they walked in His ways. In Jeremiah, God promises to place His fear in their hearts so they will not turn away. The good God does for His people now includes changing them from within. Ezekiel 36:11 carries the same resolve: God will "do better for you than at your beginnings." Restoration will surpass even the beginning. Psalm 119:68 puts the confession plainly: "You are good, and do good." God's goodness acts.

1 Kings 8:18 (2 Chronicles 6:8) — A desire God calls good. At the temple's dedication, Solomon recalls what God told David: "Whereas it was in your heart to build a temple for My name, you did well that it was in your heart." God had refused David's request to build the house, yet He did not dismiss the desire behind it. He called it doing well. The Lord weighs what is in the heart. A faithful longing that God redirects or postpones is not wasted in His sight, even when another person completes the work.

Job 10:3 — When God's pleasure seems dark. Job uses the word in the form of a terrible question: "Does it seem good to You that You should oppress, That You should despise the work of Your hands, And smile on the counsel of the wicked?" Job is asking whether God behaves like a Persian king who finds pleasure in crushing his subjects. Scripture keeps this question in the canon. Job brings it to God rather than walking away from Him. Later Job turns the word on his friends, asking whether it would go "well" with them when God searched them out (Job 13:9). The book does not answer the question with a formula. God's own answer, and Job's restoration at the end of the book, show that God had not taken pleasure in oppressing His servant.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of ṭôwb moves from conditional well-being in Deuteronomy, through the unstable favor of earthly kings, to God's wholehearted promise in Jeremiah and Ezekiel. The New Testament announces that the promised new covenant has arrived in Jesus. At the Last Supper He calls the cup the new covenant in His blood (Luke 22:20), and Hebrews 8 applies Jeremiah's new-covenant promise to Christ's ministry. The God who said He would not turn away from doing His people good fulfilled that promise in His Son.

Peter summarized Jesus' life by saying that He went about doing good and healing those oppressed by the devil (Acts 10:38). Luke wrote in Greek, so this is a different word, but it describes the same reality. In Jesus, God's rejoicing to do good walked among people and healed them. Jesus also answers the court language of Esther with a better King. He tells His small, fearful flock that it is the Father's good pleasure to give them the kingdom (Luke 12:32). Believers no longer wait anxiously to learn whether it will please the King to spare them. The Father has already said what pleases Him.

7. Spirit & Life

This word helps believers see God's attitude toward them. Many Christians picture God as a reluctant monarch who must be approached carefully, like a Persian king who might extend the scepter or might not. Jeremiah 32:41 corrects that picture. God rejoices to do good, and He commits all His heart and soul to it. Prayer can begin there, with the confidence of Psalm 125:4, "Do good, O LORD," made by people who know that is what He loves to do.

The word also exposes our own judgments of what is good. Israel called Egypt "well" and decorated its idols. Every believer is capable of the same distortion: missing the old slavery, or pouring energy and resources into whatever seems good while neglecting God. The remedy is the one Jeremiah promised. God places His fear in the heart so that we no longer want to leave Him.

The passages also bring comfort in two kinds of disappointment. Some believers carry unfulfilled callings. Like David, they desired to do something for God that He gave to someone else. God says of such a desire, "you did well that it was in your heart." Others carry suffering they cannot explain. Like Job, they may honestly ask whether God takes pleasure in their pain. Scripture allows that question to be asked, and the larger story answers it. God's settled pleasure is to bless, and Christ is His proof.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 10:29 — "Come with us, and we will treat you well; for the LORD has promised good things to Israel." Moses' kindness to Hobab overflows from God's promised goodness to Israel.

  • Numbers 24:5 — Balaam, hired to curse, instead exclaims how lovely Jacob's tents are. God's pleasure to bless (24:1) shapes even what Balaam sees.

  • Deuteronomy 15:16 — A freed servant may choose to stay because he loves his master's house and "prospers" there. Some service is good to stay in.

  • 2 Kings 10:30 — The Lord tells Jehu he has done well in carrying out judgment, though the next verse records that Jehu did not walk in God's law with all his heart.

  • Song of Solomon 4:10 — The beloved's love is "better" than wine. Here the word describes a delight that surpasses other pleasures.

  • Ezekiel 33:32 — The people treat Ezekiel like a singer who plays "well." They enjoy the prophet's words but do not obey them.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2896 (ṭôb), "good" — The closely related adjective, the Old Testament's common word for goodness. Ṭôwb describes goodness in action or effect, as Psalm 119:68 brings God's being good and His doing good together.
