GOOD, BETTER, WELL - (ṭôwb, Strong's H2896)

Summary

Ṭôwb is the Old Testament's ordinary word for "good." It covers a wide range: a ripe fig, a handsome young man, a merry heart, a fertile land, wise advice, a righteous life, and the character of God Himself. At its heart the word describes something that is as it ought to be, whether useful, pleasing, beneficial, or right. Context decides which of these is meant. The same word that calls Rebekah "very beautiful to behold" also says of the LORD, "You are good, and do good."

The word carries much of the Bible's story. God calls His creation good, and humanity falls by deciding for itself what is good. Israel's worship rests on the confession "the LORD is good." The wisdom books use "better" to set right priorities, and the prophets define the good God requires. The deepest discovery along the way is that God Himself is the highest good. Nearness to Him is worth more than every gift He gives. His good Spirit leads His people into that life.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Ṭôwb is a common Hebrew word that works as an adjective ("good"), a noun ("a good thing," "goods," "prosperity"), and an adverb ("well"). It appears in nearly every book of the Old Testament. It is most frequent in Psalms, Proverbs, and Ecclesiastes, followed by Genesis and the history books.

The NKJV translates it in dozens of ways, including "good," "better," "best," "beautiful," "merry," "pleasing," "precious," "prosperity," and "kind." This variety does not mean the word is vague. Hebrew used one ordinary word where English reaches for many. A reader who knows that the "good" of Genesis 1, the "better" of Proverbs, and the "goodness" of Psalm 23:6 are the same word will hear connections that English translations hide.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ṭôwb describes what is right, fitting, and beneficial for its purpose. Its main senses are:

1. Pleasing or beautiful in appearance or experience. Rebekah "was very beautiful to behold" (Genesis 24:16). David was "ruddy, with bright eyes, and good-looking" (1 Samuel 16:12). The Philistines' hearts were "merry" (Judges 16:25).

2. Beneficial, favorable, or prosperous. Fertile land, good news, and material well-being belong here: those who obey God "shall spend their days in prosperity" (Job 36:11).

3. Morally right or upright. This is good conduct as God defines it: "He has shown you, O man, what is good" (Micah 6:8).

4. Good in character, especially of God. This is the goodness that belongs to God's nature and shows in His actions: "For the LORD is good" (Psalm 100:5).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the word largely determines its weight. A good fig is good to eat. A good king acts justly. When the subject is the LORD, the word describes His character.

Two patterns stand out. First, Hebrew expresses comparison by placing "good" alongside "than," which English renders "better." This construction drives the wisdom books and turns ṭôwb into a tool for weighing one thing against another. Second, "good in someone's eyes" is the ordinary way of saying "pleasing to someone." Esther's petition, "If it pleases the king... and I am pleasing in his eyes" (Esther 8:5), uses ṭôwb twice in this sense. The same idiom shapes Genesis 3, where the question becomes who decides what is good in whose eyes.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture does not present God as merely having good things or doing good on occasion. Goodness is what He is. Psalm 119:68 joins His being and His action in one breath: "You are good, and do good." Because His goodness comes from His nature, it does not change with circumstances. That is why Israel's most repeated confession pairs His goodness with His lasting mercy: "Oh, give thanks to the LORD, for He is good! For His mercy endures forever" (Psalm 136:1; see also Psalm 100:5). This refrain was sung at the temple's dedication (2 Chronicles 5:13; 7:3) and when the foundation of the second temple was laid (Ezra 3:11). Each generation that saw God act returned to the same sentence.

This goodness is meant to be known by experience as well as believed. "Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good; Blessed is the man who trusts in Him!" (Psalm 34:8). Tasting is personal. No one can do it for you, and no description replaces it. The psalm joins the tasting to trust, because the person who leans on God finds out what He is like.

God also has the authority to define the good. In Genesis 1 He looks at what He has made and pronounces it good. That right belongs to Him, and the rest of the Bible's use of the word depends on it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 1–3: good declared and good redefined. Seven times God surveys His work and calls it good, beginning with light (Genesis 1:4). The sequence ends when humanity is in place: "Then God saw everything that He had made, and indeed it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). The physical world is not a prison for the soul or a lesser reality. In its original design it was exactly what God intended, a fitting home where He would walk with people He made in His image. Even Genesis 2:18, "It is not good that man should be alone," uses the word to mark something unfinished that God then completes.

Then the serpent promises that eating will make them "like God, knowing good and evil" (Genesis 3:5). The next verse shows the result: "the woman saw that the tree was good for food." Throughout chapter 1 it was God who "saw" that things were good. Now a human being looks and makes the judgment herself, based on appetite and appearance. The sin lay in taking over God's role as the one who declares what is good. Isaiah later pronounces woe on those who "call evil good, and good evil" (Isaiah 5:20). Once people make themselves the judges of good, they eventually lose the ability to tell good from evil at all.

Psalm 73 and Lamentations 3: God Himself as the good. Psalm 73 opens by affirming that God is good to Israel (73:1). The writer then nearly loses his footing as he watches the wicked prosper. His crisis breaks when he enters God's sanctuary. He comes out with a new definition of the good, saying in essence that for him it is good to draw near to God (73:28). Nothing in his outward situation has changed. What changed is what he counts as good.

Lamentations 3:25 reaches the same place in far worse conditions. Jerusalem lies in ruins, and the temple, the land, and the king are gone. In that setting the poet affirms that the LORD is good to those who wait for Him and to the soul who seeks Him. He goes on to say it is good to hope and wait quietly for the LORD's salvation (3:26). When every earthly good has been stripped away, God remains, and He proves to be the good that cannot be lost.

Jeremiah 24: exile "for their own good." Jeremiah sees two baskets of figs, one very good and one inedible. God explains: "Like these good figs, so will I acknowledge those who are carried away captive from Judah, whom I have sent out of this place for their own good" (Jeremiah 24:5). The captives were the ones who lost everything, yet God calls them the good figs and says He sent them away for their good. Later He promises to give them one heart and one way, so that they will fear Him for their own good and the good of their children (Jeremiah 32:39). God's idea of good aims at a changed heart and a restored relationship, and He will use loss to bring it about. The psalmist learned the same thing on a personal scale. He confessed that his affliction had been good for him, because it taught him God's statutes (Psalm 119:71).

Micah 6:8: the good God requires. Israel asks what offering would satisfy God: thousands of rams, rivers of oil, even a firstborn child (6:6–7). Micah answers, "He has shown you, O man, what is good; And what does the LORD require of you But to do justly, To love mercy, And to walk humbly with your God?" The good was never hidden or expensive. God had already shown it. It consists of how one treats others and how one walks with God, reflecting His own justice and mercy in daily life.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word traces the Bible's whole movement. Creation begins good, humanity grasps at good on its own terms and loses it, and God works through promise, discipline, and exile to bring His people back. Joshua 21:45 and 1 Kings 8:56 record that not one word of God's good promises failed. The prophets then look ahead: "I will perform that good thing which I have promised to the house of Israel and to the house of Judah" (Jeremiah 33:14). The verses that follow name what that good thing is, a righteous Branch from David's line. Isaiah's messenger brings "glad tidings of good things" (Isaiah 52:7), a verse Paul applies to gospel preaching in Romans 10:15. Isaiah also describes the child Immanuel as one who knows to refuse evil and choose good (Isaiah 7:15). He is the reverse of Eden, a human being who judges good rightly.

In Mark 10:18 Jesus asks the rich young ruler, "Why do you call Me good? No one is good but One, that is, God." The man had called Jesus good without thinking. Jesus presses the question so that he will consider what he has said. If only God is good, and Jesus is good, then the man is standing before God. Christ is the full showing of the goodness Israel praised in Psalm 136. Through Him "every good gift and every perfect gift" comes down from the Father (James 1:17), and above all the gift of Himself.

7. Spirit & Life

God does not leave His people to work out goodness by willpower. Nehemiah, retelling the wilderness years, says God gave His good Spirit to instruct Israel (Nehemiah 9:20). David makes it a personal prayer: he asks God to teach him to do His will, confesses that God's Spirit is good, and asks to be led on level ground (Psalm 143:10). The Spirit is called good because He shares God's own character, and He shows that goodness by teaching and leading. A believer who wants to live the good of Micah 6:8 has a Teacher who is goodness Himself and who walks beside him.

This changes how one looks at both blessing and loss. The wisdom books keep weighing things with the word "better." "Better" is a little with the fear of the LORD than great treasure with trouble (Proverbs 15:16). A dinner of herbs where love is outweighs a fattened ox with hatred (15:17). Getting wisdom is better than gold (16:16), and two are better than one (Ecclesiastes 4:9). Each proverb trains the reader to stop valuing things the way the world does. Psalm 73 and Lamentations 3 show where that training leads. The person who has learned to say "it is good for me to draw near to God" can lose a great deal and still have the good that matters most. Then the invitation of Psalm 34:8 becomes daily practice: taste, trust, and find again that the LORD is good.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 23:6 — Goodness and mercy follow the one the LORD shepherds all his days. God's goodness pursues His people.

  • Psalm 133:1 — How good and pleasant it is for brothers to dwell together in unity. Here the word describes the shared life of God's people.

  • Nahum 1:7 — The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble, and He knows those who trust in Him. This is spoken in a book about judgment on Nineveh.

  • Ezekiel 34:14 — God promises to feed His scattered flock in good pasture, taking over the care the false shepherds neglected.

  • Psalm 84:11 — The LORD withholds no good thing from those who walk uprightly.

  • Proverbs 18:22 — Whoever finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2617 (chesed, "mercy, steadfast love") — canonical pairing. Israel's central confession joins God's goodness with His enduring mercy: "For the LORD is good; His mercy is everlasting" (Psalm 100:5; also Psalm 136:1).
