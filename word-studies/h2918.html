ENCAMPMENT, SETTLEMENT, BATTLEMENT, ROW - (ṭîyrâh, Strong's H2918)

Summary

The Hebrew word ṭîyrâh names an enclosure: a ring of stones or tents around a camp, a settlement where a clan kept its flocks, or a course of masonry built around or on top of something. The NKJV translates it as "settlements," "encampments," "forts," "dwelling place," "battlement," and "rows." The word is rare. It occurs only seven times in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible, but those seven uses cover a wide range of life. It describes the settlements of Ishmael's princes, Midian's cities and forts, the towns given by lot to Aaron's family, the dwelling of the wicked in a psalm of suffering, foreign encampments overrunning Ammon, a silver crown on a wall in the Song of Solomon, and the stone kitchens of Ezekiel's temple.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

Taken together, these passages raise a searching question: what makes a dwelling secure? Midian's cities and forts burned. The enemies in Psalm 69 have their dwelling left desolate, and the apostles apply that verse to Judas. The Levites, who had no single territory of their own, received settlements by lot from the Lord, who was Himself their portion. In God's house, cooking hearths are built under rows of stone, where the people's sacrifices are boiled. The walls are not what make a dwelling safe. Safety comes from who has given the place and who is welcome in it.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Ṭîyrâh is a Hebrew noun connected to the word for a row, such as a row of stones. The basic picture is something set in a course or ring around a space. In the ancient Near East, herding peoples often enclosed their camps with a circle of stones or tents. These enclosures protected people and flocks, but they were lighter and less permanent than walled cities.

One possible reconstruction pictures the NKJV's "forts" in Numbers 31:10 as fortified camps rather than castles, though the verse does not describe them. In two later passages the same idea of an enclosing course of stone is applied to building work: a battlement along the top of a wall, and stone rows lining a temple courtyard.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. An encampment or settlement. This is a settlement or encampment where a clan or tribe lived with its flocks, perhaps ringed with stones or tents, as in "their towns and their settlements" (Genesis 25:16). The poetic use in Psalm 69:25 ("dwelling place") belongs here as well.

2. A built course or tier of stone. This is an architectural enclosure. It can be a battlement on top of a wall (Song of Solomon 8:9) or the rows of stones under which the temple's cooking hearths were made (Ezekiel 46:23).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses describe where people live. They are often non-Israelite peoples: Ishmael's twelve princes, Midian, and "the men of the East." In these passages the settlements usually stand beside a second dwelling word, as in "their towns and their settlements" (Genesis 25:16), "the cities where they dwelt, and all their forts" (Numbers 31:10), or "dwelling place" alongside "tents" (Psalm 69:25). In these pairings the ṭîyrâh appears to be the more camp-like dwelling.

What happens to the enclosure depends on its owner. Midian's cities and forts are burned, Ammon's land fills with foreign camps as a judgment, and the persecutors' dwelling is left empty. When God assigns the place, as with the Levites, or builds it, as in Ezekiel's temple, the same word describes something ordered and holy.

4. Theological Significance

The word quietly exposes the limits of self-made security. Numbers 31:10 does not say why Midian built its cities and forts. It reports only that when Israel went to war against Midian, "they also burned with fire all the cities where they dwelt, and all their forts." Whatever their builders intended, a ring of stones can turn away a wolf or a raider, but it cannot stand when God brings judgment. Ezekiel 25:4 makes the same point from the other side. Ammon had rejoiced when God's sanctuary was profaned (Ezekiel 25:3, paraphrased), so God let desert peoples "set their encampments among you" and consume Ammon's fruit and milk.

Against this background, Scripture shows that God Himself assigns His people's dwelling and is Himself their security. The same word that names Midian's burned settlements also names the Levites' settlements, and there it describes a gift from His own hand. In the Song it becomes an image of honor for a steadfast sister, and in Ezekiel a place of fellowship inside God's house. The difference lies in God's relationship to the place and its people.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 69:25 and Acts 1:20: the dwelling that is forfeited. Psalm 69 is the cry of a righteous sufferer who is hated without cause. The New Testament repeatedly applies this psalm to Jesus: zeal for God's house consumed Him, and He was given vinegar to drink. Near the end of his prayer the psalmist asks, "Let their dwelling place be desolate; Let no one live in their tents." After Judas's death, Peter stood before the believers and said, "For it is written in the Book of Psalms: 'Let his dwelling place be desolate, And let no one live in it'; and, 'Let another take his office.'" Peter changes the psalm's plural "their" to "his" and focuses it on the one man who betrayed the Son. He then joins it to Psalm 109:8 about another taking his office. The desolate ṭîyrâh therefore means more than an empty campsite. Judas had been numbered among the Twelve and shared in their ministry. By betraying Christ, he fell from his part in this ministry and apostleship, and another took his office (Acts 1:17, 20, 25).

1 Chronicles 6:54: settlements given by lot. The other tribes received large territories, but the Lord told Aaron that he would have no inheritance in the land because the Lord Himself was his portion and inheritance (Numbers 18:20, paraphrased). The Levites were therefore scattered through the other tribes in assigned towns. First Chronicles records "their dwelling places throughout their settlements in their territory, for they were given by lot to the sons of Aaron." The Hebrew word that named the settlements of Ishmael and Midian is used here for the Levites' settlements, but the word itself does not tell us how they were built. The towns listed next are established cities, Hebron and Libnah among them, each given "with its common-lands" (1 Chronicles 6:55–57; compare Joshua 21:3, 10–13). 1 Chronicles 6:54 tells how Aaron's family received its settlements: by lot. Numbers 31:10 tells what happened to Midian's cities and forts: they were burned in judgment. Set side by side, the two passages show the same word used for places with very different ends. The Levites lived scattered among the other tribes. Their deepest security rested on the God who placed them there and who was Himself their inheritance. That was the security of belonging to Him, not a promise of physical protection or that the towns could never be lost.

Song of Solomon 8:9: a battlement of silver. The brothers plan for their young sister: "If she is a wall, We will build upon her A battlement of silver; And if she is a door, We will enclose her With boards of cedar." A wall stands closed and firm. A door swings open. On a common reading, if she proves steadfast and chaste, they will crown her with a silver battlement, and if she proves open, they will board her up to protect her. Here the enclosure rises off the ground and becomes adornment. In the brothers' proposal, her steadfastness would be met with protection that is also honor. In the next verse she answers, "I am a wall" (Song of Solomon 8:10). Silver was the metal of the sanctuary's ransom money (Exodus 30:11–16), and some readers hear redemption in the image. The plainer point of the brothers' image is enough: steadfastness would be adorned with something precious.

Ezekiel 46:23–24: rows of stone around the hearths. In the corners of the outer court of Ezekiel's visionary temple, "there was a row of building stones all around... and cooking hearths were made under the rows of stones all around." The phrase "the rows of stones" is where ṭîyrâh stands. The opening "row of building stones" translates a different, related Hebrew word, ṭûr (H2905). Both describe courses of masonry, and together they picture stone boundaries built around and above the cooking places. The guide explains, "These are the kitchens where the ministers of the temple shall boil the sacrifices of the people." The kitchens stand in the corners of the outer court, and they are for boiling the people's sacrifices (Ezekiel 46:21–24). The stone rows mark off a space where those offerings are prepared rightly. The passage does not describe a meal, but Israel's sacrificial practice suggests one: worshipers ate before the LORD with their households from the sacrifices they brought (Deuteronomy 12:6–7). In this vision God's house has kitchens built into its plan.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Psalm 69 places this word inside the story of Christ's sufferings. The Righteous One endured hatred and betrayal, and Peter's use of the psalm shows that betraying Jesus cost Judas his place in the apostles' ministry. Christ Himself had nowhere to lay His head (Matthew 8:20, paraphrased). He suffered "outside the gate" (Hebrews 13:12), and Hebrews calls believers to "go forth to Him, outside the camp, bearing His reproach. For here we have no continuing city, but we seek the one to come" (Hebrews 13:13–14). Hebrews uses a Greek word for "camp" that differs from ṭîyrâh, but the canonical picture fits well. Like the Levites, believers live as sojourners while their true inheritance is God Himself. Peter calls the church a royal priesthood (1 Peter 2:9, paraphrased), and a priesthood lives on the Lord's allotment.

Ezekiel's kitchens point forward in a similar way. They picture a sanctuary where the people's sacrifices are prepared, and by inference from Israel's sacrificial meals, a place where worshipers would eat. This resonance is found in the passage's details rather than stated as a fulfillment. As a Christian application, it fits the gospel: Christ's once-for-all offering brings His people to eat at the Lord's table.

7. Spirit & Life

The same Hebrew word names Midian's settlements and the Levite settlements. Numbers 31:10 records that Midian's cities and forts were burned, and 1 Chronicles 6:54 records that Aaron's descendants received their settlements by lot. Setting those two records side by side is this study's comparison, not a contrast either passage draws. Christians build their own enclosures too, such as savings, reputations, and carefully guarded plans. As an illustration, these passages ask whether we are living in Midian's fort or in the Lord's lot. A place received from God, with God Himself as our portion, is more secure than a strong place we have built for ourselves, because its security rests in Him rather than in its walls. That is the security of belonging to Him, not a promise that nothing we hold will ever be lost.

Judas is a sober warning that being close to Jesus does not guarantee a place with Him. He walked with the Lord, held an office, and handled the common purse, yet his dwelling became desolate. We belong to Christ through faith in Him, and faithfulness is the fruit of that faith. Outward nearness to Jesus, and even an office among His people, is not enough.

The Song and Ezekiel also show that God's boundaries are not cages. In the Song, the brothers' silver battlement honors the sister who stands firm, and in Ezekiel the stone rows guard the hearths where the people's sacrifices are boiled. As an application, limits that God places around a life can become a setting for honor and fellowship. Standing firm as "a wall" in a compromising world is costly, but the Song's image of a silver battlement offers an encouraging picture of honor given to steadfastness.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 25:16: Ishmael's twelve princes are listed "by their towns and their settlements." This fulfills God's promise that Ishmael would become a great nation. The verse lists towns and settlements; it does not say whether any of them were fortified.

  • Numbers 31:10: "All their forts" were burned. The verse reports the destruction of Midian's cities and forts without saying who built them or why.

  • Ezekiel 25:4: "They shall set their encampments among you." Foreign camps spreading across Ammon's land become the form God's judgment takes.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2905 (ṭûr), row: a lexical relative. It describes a course of stones, the same basic picture behind ṭîyrâh. Ezekiel 46:23 uses both words in one verse: ṭûr for the opening "row of building stones" and ṭîyrâh for "the rows of stones" under which the hearths were made.

  • H4264 (maḥăneh), camp: a thematic connection. It is the common Hebrew word for a camp, used widely for Israel's wilderness encampment, but it is a different word from ṭîyrâh.

  • G3925 (parembolē), camp: a thematic connection. It is the Greek word behind "outside the camp" in Hebrews 13:13, which supplies the New Testament picture of pilgrim life.
