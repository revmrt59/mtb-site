DEW - (ṭal, Strong's H2919)

Summary

The Hebrew word ṭal means dew, the moisture that settles on grass, fields and fleece during the night. In Israel's long, rainless summers, dew was a source of moisture during the dry months that could benefit plants. For that reason Scripture treats it as more than a pleasant feature of the morning. When Isaac blesses Jacob, his first request is "the dew of heaven" (Genesis 27:28). When the returned exiles leave God's house in ruins, He tells them that the heavens "withhold the dew" (Haggai 1:10). In passages like these, dew becomes a visible sign of God's blessing on the land or, where His word says so, of His judgment.

The prophets and poets turn this ordinary moisture into a picture of God's ways. Moses prays that his teaching will "distill as the dew" (Deuteronomy 32:2), and the manna came to Israel on a bed of morning dew. Hosea compares Israel's shallow loyalty to dew that burns off by midmorning, and then records God's promise that He Himself will be like the dew to Israel. Isaiah gives the image its fullest meaning: he compares God's dew to "the dew of herbs" and declares that "the earth shall cast out the dead" (Isaiah 26:19). Together these passages picture life coming down from God, quietly and faithfully, in a form no one can manufacture.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

From late spring to early autumn, the land of Israel receives almost no rain. In those months, dew typically forms at night when water vapor in moist air from the Mediterranean condenses on surfaces that have cooled enough for condensation. That nightly moisture could benefit plants during the dry months. Rain and dew are often named together in Scripture, and the passages below show, each in its own setting, how Israel's harvests depended on God's gifts and how withholding them could come as judgment.

Ṭal appears throughout the Old Testament: in patriarchal blessings, wilderness narratives, the Gideon story, wisdom poetry, the Song of Solomon and the prophets. The NKJV translates it as "dew" every time.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ṭal has one plain meaning: the moisture that forms on the ground and on plants overnight. The word does not mean grace, Spirit or resurrection. Its range of use comes from the role dew played in daily life. Something that arrives silently, settles over the ground, refreshes what is dry and disappears in the sun's heat lends itself naturally to comparison. Most of the word's richest uses are similes built on those familiar qualities.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Several patterns stand out.

In blessing and judgment, dew is tied to God's favor on the land. It is promised to Jacob (Genesis 27:28) and to Joseph's territory (Deuteronomy 33:13). Elijah announces that it will be withheld in Ahab's day (1 Kings 17:1), and Haggai tells the returned exiles that the heavens withhold it because God's house lies in ruins (Haggai 1:9–10). Zechariah then brings a promise to the people of the rebuilding, spoken after the foundation of the temple was laid: "the heavens shall give their dew" (Zechariah 8:9, 12). The common phrase "dew of heaven" presents dew as provision from God: the land receives it as His gift.

In provision, dew lies under the manna in the wilderness (Exodus 16:13–14; Numbers 11:9).

In similes, the point depends on which quality of dew the writer has in mind. Its gentleness pictures teaching (Deuteronomy 32:2) and a king's favor (Proverbs 19:12). Its short life pictures fickle loyalty (Hosea 6:4) and, in a warning of judgment, a people who will soon pass away (Hosea 13:3). Its settling over the ground pictures, in Hushai's advice, an attack by all Israel's assembled forces that would leave none of David's company alive (2 Samuel 17:11–13). This last use shows that the image is not automatically a symbol of blessing. Context decides what dew means in each passage.

4. Theological Significance

Dew belongs to God. In His answer to Job, He asks, "Has the rain a father? Or who has begotten the drops of dew?" (Job 38:28). No farmer could summon dew or hurry it. Scripture therefore treats dew as a gift from God's own hand, and His blessings on the land name it among the good things He gives (Genesis 27:28; Deuteronomy 33:13, 28).

Some passages go further and present the withholding of dew as God's judgment. Elijah announces to Ahab, "there shall not be dew nor rain these years, except at my word" (1 Kings 17:1). The announcement comes right after the record that Ahab served Baal and provoked the LORD to anger (1 Kings 16:31–33). Baal was credited with the weather, and the drought ends only when the LORD says, "I will send rain on the earth" (1 Kings 18:1). Haggai states the connection most directly. The returned exiles left God's house in ruins, "therefore the heavens above you withhold the dew" (Haggai 1:10), and the LORD adds, "I called for a drought" (Haggai 1:11). David's words over Gilboa are a different kind of speech. In his lament for Saul and Jonathan he calls on the mountains to have "no dew nor rain" because "the shield of the mighty is cast away there" (2 Samuel 1:21). That is a poetic curse spoken in grief, not a report of God's judgment or a verdict on the people's standing with Him.

Dew also reveals something about how God gives. Moses prays, "My speech distill as the dew, as raindrops on the tender herb" (Deuteronomy 32:2). God can speak in thunder, as He did at Sinai, but much of His sustaining work comes quietly and in the dark, one morning at a time.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Manna on the dew (Exodus 16:13–14; Numbers 11:9). "When the dew fell on the camp in the night, the manna fell on it" (Numbers 11:9). The dew came first. When it lifted in the morning, the bread from heaven lay on the ground, and Israel gathered it fresh each day. The Bible's picture of daily provision therefore includes dew as the layer on which God set Israel's food. The provision was quiet and dependable. Ordinary daily portions could not be kept overnight; what was left until morning "bred worms and stank" (Exodus 16:20). The one exception was God's own provision for the Sabbath. On the sixth day Israel gathered twice as much, and the portion laid up for the Sabbath "did not stink, nor were there any worms in it" (Exodus 16:22–24). Even that stored portion was kept by God, so Israel still lived on what He supplied, one day at a time.

Gideon's fleece (Judges 6:37–40). Gideon asks first for dew on the fleece alone, and he wrings out "a bowlful of water" while the ground stays dry. Then he asks for the reverse: "let it now be dry only on the fleece, but on all the ground let there be dew." The sign lay in the contrast between the fleece and the ground on two successive mornings. The first morning the fleece was wet and the ground dry; the next morning the fleece was dry and the ground wet with dew. God had promised to save Israel through Gideon, and Gideon asked for confirmation of that promise: "If You will save Israel by my hand as You have said" (Judges 6:36). The sign confirmed the promise of deliverance through him. A frightened man learned that God directs His favor exactly where He chooses.

Hosea's two kinds of dew (Hosea 6:4; 13:3; 14:5). God grieves over His people: "your faithfulness is like a morning cloud, and like the early dew it goes away" (6:4). Their repentance looked real at dawn and was gone by midmorning. Hosea 13:3 uses the same image for the people themselves, who will pass away "like the early dew," as a warning of judgment. Then, in the book's final chapter, God reverses the image and promises that He Himself will be like the dew to Israel, so that Israel will blossom like the lily (14:5). Israel's devotion evaporated, but God's faithfulness to them would remain. Their renewal would depend on Him.

Dew on the dust (Isaiah 26:19). "Your dead shall live; together with my dead body they shall arise. Awake and sing, you who dwell in dust; for your dew is like the dew of herbs, and the earth shall cast out the dead." Isaiah compares the dew God gives to the dew of herbs, and he declares that the earth will give up the dead. Picturing that dew falling on a grave is one way to illustrate his comparison: as dew refreshes plants, God's life-giving power reaches those who lie in the dust. This is one of the clearest Old Testament promises of bodily resurrection. Its imagery presents resurrection life as coming from God to those who can do nothing for themselves.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Psalm 110, the psalm the New Testament quotes most often about Jesus, says of David's Lord, "from the womb of the morning, You have the dew of Your youth" (Psalm 110:3). Interpreters debate whether the phrase describes the King's own vigor or the young volunteers who come to Him. Either way, the image is of fresh, morning strength that comes from God and does not wear out. That fits the Priest-King who serves "according to the order of Melchizedek" (Psalm 110:4) and who, as Hebrews says, holds His office "according to the power of an endless life" (Hebrews 7:16).

The other dew passages point toward the same Christ. Jesus called Himself the true bread from heaven, greater than the manna that lay on the dew (John 6:31–35). Christians see Isaiah's dew on the dust fulfilled first in Christ's own resurrection, the firstfruits of all who sleep (1 Corinthians 15:20), and it will be completed when the dead in Christ rise. Hosea's promise that God will be dew to His people is kept in the gospel, where life is given rather than produced by the people themselves.

Many readers also see in dew a picture of the Holy Spirit's quiet, life-giving work. Scripture never states that equation, but the resemblance is real. The Spirit gives life to mortal bodies (Romans 8:11), much as dew refreshes dry ground. The Song of Solomon offers a more tender association. The beloved knocks at the door and says, "my head is covered with dew, my locks with the drops of the night" (Song 5:2). He has stood outside through the night. Christians have long heard in that verse the patient Bridegroom who endures the night to win His bride. This is an analogy rather than the text's stated meaning, but it is a fitting one.

7. Spirit & Life

Hosea's warning should search the heart. A tearful response at a meeting or a resolution made in a hard moment can look like dew at dawn and be gone by noon. Hosea's remedy is not to try harder at producing moisture of our own. It is to live under the One who promised, "I will be like the dew." Lasting faithfulness grows where God keeps giving Himself, so the believer returns to Him each morning as Israel went out to gather manna.

Psalm 133 speaks of brothers dwelling together, and its picture applies readily to the church. Unity among brothers is "like the dew of Hermon, descending upon the mountains of Zion; for there the LORD commanded the blessing—life forevermore." Hermon was known for heavy dew, and the psalmist pictures it falling on Zion where God's people gathered. Read in that light, Christian unity is not mainly a matter of negotiated agreement. It comes down from God, and where it rests, He commands life. Believers can pray for that unity as farmers prayed for dew.

Micah's picture of Israel's remnant also speaks to the church's place in the world. The remnant of Jacob will be "like dew from the LORD... that tarry for no man nor wait for the sons of men" (Micah 5:7). The image pictures God's people as a refreshing presence among the nations, yet one that does not depend on human permission. Isaiah 18:4 shows the same God watching the restless nations, "like a cloud of dew in the heat of harvest," calm and unhurried, until He acts at the time He has chosen. No empire controls the dew.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 27:39 — Isaac's word to Esau echoes "the dew of heaven." The Hebrew can also be read away from the dew, which would set Esau's portion against Jacob's.

  • Deuteronomy 33:28 — Moses closes his blessing with Jacob's land secure under heavens that drop down dew, a picture of settled peace.

  • 2 Samuel 17:12 — Hushai advises Absalom, "we will fall on him as the dew falls on the ground," in a plan to gather all Israel and attack with forces so numerous that "there shall not be left so much as one" of David's company (2 Samuel 17:11–12).

  • Job 29:19 — Job remembers his prosperous days as a tree whose branch received dew all night long.

  • Proverbs 3:20 — By His knowledge the clouds drop down dew, linking the daily moisture to God's wisdom in creation.

  • Proverbs 19:12 — "his favor is like dew on the grass": a good king's kindness is gentle and refreshing, unlike his wrath, which is like a lion's roar.

  • Zechariah 8:12 — God promises the people of the rebuilding that "the heavens shall give their dew," a word spoken after the temple's foundation was laid (Zechariah 8:9). It answers Haggai's drought with a promise of restored dew.
