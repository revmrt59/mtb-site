DEFILE, SOIL - (ṭânaph, Strong's H2936)

Summary

The Hebrew word ṭânaph means "to soil" or "to get dirty." It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 5:3, where the NKJV translates it "defile." It describes ordinary physical dirt, the kind feet pick up when they touch the ground. It does not describe sin, religious uncleanness, or moral corruption. The bride in the Song has gone to bed. Her beloved knocks at the door, and she answers with an excuse: "I have washed my feet; How can I defile them?"

That small, practical excuse costs her a great deal. By the time she gets up and opens the door, her beloved has gone. The Bible's only use of this word marks a moment when a minor inconvenience outweighed the presence of the one she loved. The scene speaks plainly to anyone who has heard the Lord's call and answered with a reason to stay comfortable. Clean feet can be washed again in a moment. A missed meeting with the beloved sent her searching through the night.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Ṭânaph is a Hebrew verb used only once in the Old Testament. A word used only once is called a hapax legomenon. Because it has no other biblical uses to compare, its meaning comes from its one setting and from the general sense recorded in the lexicons, "to soil."

That setting is the Song of Solomon, a book of love poetry between a bride and her beloved. Chapter 5 opens a night scene in which the bride is in bed, hears her beloved at the door, and hesitates before responding (Song 5:2–8). The word belongs to the household language of that scene. It does not come from the vocabulary of the temple or the law.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To soil or make dirty, in the plain physical sense. In Song 5:3 the bride has washed her feet before bed, and stepping onto the floor would dirty them again.

The NKJV word "defile" can suggest religious impurity to modern readers, so the distinction matters. The common Hebrew word for ceremonial or moral uncleanness is a different word, ṭāmēʾ (H2930), which governs the laws of clean and unclean in passages like Leviticus. Ṭânaph carries none of that weight. Nothing forbidden or sinful is in view. The bride faces dusty feet, nothing more serious.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's single use comes as a question: "How can I defile them?" The bride is not asking for information. She is explaining why she would rather not get up. She pairs it with a matching complaint: "I have taken off my robe; How can I put it on again?" (Song 5:3). The two complaints work together. Each points to something she has already done to settle in for the night, and each turns that preparation into a reason to stay where she is.

This is what gives the plain word its force. The obstacle she names is real but small. Feet that touch the floor do get dirty. The poem lets the reader feel how slight that cost is compared with what she is putting off.

4. Theological Significance

Since ṭânaph names ordinary dirt, the passage shows something sobering about how love grows cold. The bride is not tempted by anything wicked. She is not rejecting her beloved. She loves him, and when his hand touches the latch, "my heart yearned for him" (Song 5:4). What holds her back is the plain wish not to undo her comfort.

Scripture often shows that devotion can be quietly smothered by ordinary things as easily as it can be openly attacked. Jesus spoke of the word being choked by the cares and pleasures of life (Luke 8:14, paraphrased). The bride's dusty feet belong to that same category. They are a legitimate concern that she allows to sit in the place where love should sit. The passage asks the reader to weigh what she weighed: her own undisturbed rest against the presence of the one who came for her.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:2–8: The knock, the excuse, and the empty doorway. The scene opens with the bride half-asleep: "I sleep, but my heart is awake; It is the voice of my beloved!" (Song 5:2). He calls her by every tender name, "my sister, my love, My dove, my perfect one," and he gives his reason for urgency: "my head is covered with dew, My locks with the drops of the night."

That detail sharpens the contrast. He has been outside in the cold and wet, and he stands drenched at her door. She, warm in bed, is unwilling to get the soles of her feet dusty. His willingness to bear the night set beside her reluctance to cross the floor is the heart of the scene. The only use of ṭânaph in Scripture sits at the point where love waits outside and comfort holds the door shut.

She does stir. His hand at the latch moves her heart, and she rises with "my hands dripped with myrrh, My fingers with liquid myrrh, On the handles of the lock" (Song 5:5). But the delay has cost her: "I opened for my beloved, But my beloved had turned away and was gone... I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer" (Song 5:6).

What follows shows how lopsided her trade was. The dust she wanted to avoid would have washed off in moments. Instead she goes out into the city streets at night. The watchmen strike her and wound her, and they take her veil (Song 5:7). She ends up pleading with the daughters of Jerusalem to tell him "I am lovesick!" (Song 5:8). She refused the small cost of answering at once, and she paid a much heavier one searching for him afterward.

Her clean feet and the purpose of feet. The bride treats her clean state as something to protect by staying in bed. Feet are made for walking, though, and cleanliness that can only be kept by never moving has lost touch with what it is for. Her washing was meant to serve her life with her beloved. Instead it became the very thing that kept her from him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song is love poetry first. Yet Scripture repeatedly describes God's people as a bride and the Lord as her husband, and Christian readers have long heard the voice of Christ in the beloved at the door. The New Testament does not quote Song 5. Even so, the resemblance to Revelation 3:20 is hard to miss: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me." Jesus speaks those words to the church at Laodicea, a church He describes as comfortable and convinced it needed nothing (Rev 3:17, paraphrased). The danger in Song 5 and in Revelation 3 is the same: a settled people hear a knock and are slow to rise.

The Gospels carry the picture further. In John 13 Jesus kneels and washes His disciples' dusty feet. When Peter protests, Jesus answers, "He who is bathed needs only to wash his feet, but is completely clean" (John 13:10). Those words treat dusty feet as part of ordinary walking, and the Lord Himself provides for their cleansing. The bride feared getting her feet dirty. In Christ, the One who knocks is also the One who washes dirty feet. Walking toward Him is never a loss that cannot be restored.

Luke 7 shows what the opposite of the bride's hesitation looks like. At Simon's table, a woman known as a sinner weeps over Jesus' feet. He tells Simon, "you gave Me no water for My feet, but she has washed My feet with her tears and wiped them with the hair of her head" (Luke 7:44). She also "has not ceased to kiss My feet" and "has anointed My feet with fragrant oil" (Luke 7:45–46). She set aside dignity, reputation, and every concern about mess so that she could be near Him. Simon kept his distance politely. She came close with nothing held back.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride's excuse grew out of her preparations. She had washed and undressed for rest, and those good acts became her reasons for not answering. Believers can do the same thing. A settled devotional routine, a well-ordered schedule, or a sense of being spiritually "finished for the day" can turn the Lord's unexpected prompting into an interruption. He may call us to pray at an inconvenient hour, go to someone in need, or deal with something we would rather leave alone. The temptation is to answer as she did: I have already done my part; how can I get up now?

Paul writes that "we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:10). The cleansing Christ gives is meant for walking. A purity that stays in bed to keep its feet clean has misunderstood what it was given for. Answering Him will sometimes mean stepping into the dust of service, conversation, or plain obedience.

The passage also gives honest comfort. The bride's heart did yearn, and she did rise and search. The Lord who knocks at Laodicea's door still knocks, and the Christ of John 13 still washes feet. The lesson of Song 5:3 is not to seek dirt. It is to hold His presence more dearly than our undisturbed comfort, and to open the door while His voice can still be heard.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Ṭânaph occurs only in Song of Solomon 5:3, which is discussed fully in Section 5. The verses that surround it (Song 5:2–8) supply its whole setting, and the canonical connections in Revelation 3, Luke 7, and John 13 are developed in Section 6.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H2930 (ṭāmēʾ), "to be unclean."** This is a distinct word and not a relative of ṭânaph. It is the Old Testament's main term for ceremonial and moral uncleanness, as in the laws of Leviticus. The contrast helps readers see that Song 5:3 is about ordinary dirt rather than sin or ritual impurity.
