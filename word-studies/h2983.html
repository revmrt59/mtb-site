JEBUSITE - (Yᵉbûwçîy, Strong's H2983)

Summary

The Hebrew word translated "Jebusite" names a Canaanite people who lived in the central hill country and held the city of Jebus, later known as Jerusalem. It is a people name and carries no hidden meaning. The Jebusites first appear in the table of nations, then in God's repeated lists of peoples whose land He promised to Abraham and his descendants. Long after Israel entered Canaan, they still held Jerusalem. Judah and Benjamin could not drive them out, and though Judah once captured and burned Jerusalem (Judges 1:8), the Jebusites kept living there until David took their stronghold and made it his capital.

The word matters because of where the Jebusites lived. They occupied the very hill God would choose for His name. Scripture follows them from enemies on God's promised ground, to a conquered city under the Lord's anointed king, to a single Jebusite, Araunah, whose threshing floor became the place where God stopped a plague and where Solomon later built the temple. Zechariah then uses the Jebusite as a picture of a former enemy brought inside God's people. Along the way, the name records both God's kept promises and Israel's recurring compromise with what God had already defeated.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yᵉbûwçîy is a gentilic noun, a word that identifies someone by people or tribe, much as "Texan" or "Scot" does in English. Genesis 10:16 places "the Jebusite" among the descendants of Canaan, so from the start the Jebusites belong to the peoples of the land God would give to Abraham. Their name is tied to their city. Joshua 18:28 identifies Jebus as Jerusalem, and in that verse the NKJV renders this word as "Jebus."

Most occurrences fall in three settings. The first is the lists of nations in the Law and Joshua. The second is the accounts of Jerusalem's conquest and of the purchase of Araunah's (or Ornan's) threshing floor. The third is a few later passages that look back on Israel's history.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means a member of the Jebusite people, or the Jebusites as a group. In a few verses it stands for their city: Joshua 15:8 and 18:16 refer to "the Jebusite city," and in Joshua 18:28 the same form is rendered "Jebus." Context tells whether the people, an individual such as Araunah, or the place is in view. The word does not carry the sense "enemy" or "outsider." Those associations come from how the Jebusites figure in Israel's story.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most often the Jebusites appear in a stock list of Canaanite nations: Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, Girgashites, and Jebusites, in varying order and number. These lists come with God's promise to give the land (Gen 15:21; Exod 3:8, 17) and His command not to make treaties with its peoples or adopt their gods (Exod 34:11; Deut 7:1; 20:17). In these settings the word represents the whole hostile, idolatrous world God would remove before His people.

A second use is specific to Jerusalem. Here the Jebusites are a particular population in a particular fortress, and the narratives focus on who holds the city (Josh 15:8, 63; Judg 1:21; 19:11).

A third use is personal. In 2 Samuel 24 and 1 Chronicles 21, "the Jebusite" becomes the title of one man, Araunah or Ornan. The name that once marked an enemy nation now identifies a landowner who deals respectfully with Israel's king.

4. Theological Significance

The Jebusites show that God keeps His word across centuries. He named them in His promise to Abraham (Gen 15:21), and Nehemiah's prayer centuries later praises God for giving the land of the Jebusites to Abraham's descendants and for performing His words (Neh 9:8). Once listed as a promise, the name came to be cited as proof that the promise was fulfilled.

The same name also marks a persistent danger. After the exile the Jebusites were no longer a military force, but Ezra's leaders report that the people "have not separated themselves from the peoples of the lands, with respect to the abominations of the Canaanites, the Hittites, the Perizzites, the Jebusites" (Ezra 9:1). God's people were drifting back toward ways He had long since overthrown. His faithfulness had not failed. Their resolve had.

Under both lines lies a larger truth. God chose to place His name in a city that began in enemy hands. Zion did not start out holy ground, and God made it His dwelling by conquest and by mercy.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The stronghold the tribes could not take (Josh 15:63; Judg 1:21; 2 Sam 5:6–8; 1 Chr 11:4–6). Joshua records that the people of Judah could not drive out the Jebusites of Jerusalem. Judges says the same of Benjamin and adds that the Jebusites lived among them. The city sat on the border between two tribes, and neither could secure it. Judges 1:8 does record that Judah "fought against Jerusalem and took it" and set the city on fire, yet the same chapter says the Jebusites still lived there with Benjamin (Judg 1:21). An early capture did not bring lasting control. Judges 19 shows how foreign it remained. When a weary servant urged his master to "lodge in" the "city of the Jebusites," the Levite refused: "We will not turn aside here into a city of foreigners, who are not of the children of Israel" (Judg 19:11–12). He went on to Gibeah instead, and what happened there exposed that Israel's own towns could be as dark as any Canaanite city.

Lasting possession came only when David became king over all Israel. The Jebusites were confident enough to taunt him that even the blind and the lame could keep him out (2 Sam 5:6). David took the stronghold of Zion anyway, and the account says it became the City of David. In Chronicles, Joab goes up first and wins the command (1 Chr 11:6). The narrative invites a contrast. The tribes' possession stayed incomplete, and the city fell under the Lord's anointed king leading a united people.

The threshing floor where judgment stopped (2 Sam 24:16–25; 1 Chr 21:15–28; 2 Chr 3:1). This is the word's most remarkable setting. David's census brought a plague on Israel. As the angel stretched out his hand toward Jerusalem, the Lord relented and halted him beside the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite (2 Sam 24:16). The prophet Gad sent David to build an altar there. Araunah offered the land, the oxen, and the wood as a gift, but David insisted on paying, unwilling to offer the Lord something that cost him nothing. In Chronicles, God answered with fire from heaven on the altar. When David saw that the Lord had answered him at the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite, he went on sacrificing there (1 Chr 21:26, 28).

Second Chronicles 3:1 then names this exact spot as the site of Solomon's temple on Mount Moriah, where the Lord had appeared to David. The center of Israel's worship and sacrifice did not stand on a site that honored Israel's goodness. It stood where Israel's king had sinned, where God's judgment had fallen, and where His mercy stopped it. The ground came from a Jebusite. The shared name "Moriah" also suggests a connection, long held in tradition, with the land of Moriah where Abraham was told to offer Isaac on "one of the mountains" (Gen 22:2). Genesis does not identify that mountain with the later temple hill, but read canonically the shared name links this place with the memory of God providing in place of death.

"Like a Jebusite" (Zech 9:7). Zechariah 9 announces judgment on the Philistine cities. Then, unexpectedly, God says He will take the blood from the Philistine's mouth and the abominations from between his teeth. A remnant will belong to God, will be like a leader in Judah, and Ekron will be like a Jebusite. The likely sense is that the Jebusites, never wholly wiped out, ended up living within Israel under David's house, Araunah among them, and that Philistines would one day be absorbed into God's people the same way. That inclusion was not always gentle. Solomon conscripted the remaining Jebusites for forced labor (1 Kgs 9:20–21). Zechariah's emphasis falls on God cleansing an old enemy and claiming it for Himself.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Jebusite story follows a line that the gospel completes. The tribes could not secure Zion, and David the anointed king did. Psalm 2 celebrates God's decree: "Yet I have set My King On My holy hill of Zion" (Ps 2:6). Jesus, the Son of David, is that King, and what He secures is greater than a hill fortress. He takes back human hearts and peoples that have long been held against God.

The temple built on Araunah's threshing floor was the place where sacrifice met judgment and mercy prevailed. Jesus spoke of His own body as the temple (John 2:19–21). At the cross, God's judgment on sin and His mercy toward sinners meet in one Person. The temple's tie to a Jebusite also anticipates the purpose Jesus declared for God's house, a house of prayer for all nations (Mark 11:17).

Read across the canon, Zechariah's vision of Ekron "like a Jebusite" is fulfilled in Gentiles brought into God's people through Christ. Paul does not cite Zechariah 9:7, but he describes the same movement. Paul tells Gentile believers they were once "aliens from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers from the covenants of promise," but "now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (Eph 2:12–13). He compares them to a wild olive branch "grafted in" to share "the root and fatness of the olive tree" (Rom 11:17).

7. Spirit & Life

Believers will recognize the Jebusite stronghold. Some sins and fears stay entrenched, close to the very place where God wants to dwell, and they mock every effort to remove them, much as the Jebusites taunted David. Scripture does not say the tribes could not win until a king came. God had promised the land and commanded the conquest, and faith, obedience, and His enabling were the way to take it. Read across the whole story, though, the contrast between the tribes' unfinished possession and David's victory points beyond both to Christ. What resists our resolve gives way to Christ's rule, and the Christian life is more a matter of surrendering the stronghold to Him than of fighting harder alone.

The threshing floor shows how God builds His house. He chose a place marked by sin and stopped judgment, not a place of human success. A believer's fellowship with God rests on the same ground. It is where He relented and provided a sacrifice, and David's refusal to offer what cost him nothing shows the right response to mercy received.

Ezra 9:1 warns that defeated enemies can still return as habits we never fully separated from. God's people can be rescued from a nation's power and still take on its ways. Nehemiah 9:8 answers that warning: the God who dispossessed the Jebusites keeps His word, and He can be trusted to finish what He began.

Zechariah 9:7 gives reason for hope about people who seem impossibly far from God. If Philistines could become "like a Jebusite," no enemy is beyond His cleansing and claim.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 15:21. The Jebusites close the list of nations whose land God pledges to Abraham's descendants, so their name stands inside the original promise.

Exodus 3:8. At the burning bush, God names the Jebusites among the peoples whose land He will give to Israel, a land flowing with milk and honey, as He prepares to deliver His people from Egypt.

Deuteronomy 7:1. Moses lists the Jebusites among seven nations "greater and mightier" than Israel. Victory would depend on the Lord, not on Israel's strength.

Joshua 24:11. In his farewell, Joshua recalls that the Jebusites fought against Israel at Jericho and that God delivered them into Israel's hand. The conquest is retold as God's gift.

2 Chronicles 8:7–8. Solomon conscripts the Jebusites who remained for labor. The once-defiant people of Jerusalem now serve the kingdom of David's son.
