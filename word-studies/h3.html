GREENERY, GREEN, VERDURE - (ʼêb, Strong's H0003)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼêb means freshness or fresh green growth: the shoots and greenery of living plants. The word occurs 2 times in 2 verses of the Hebrew Bible, both in poetry. Because the word is so rare, the two verses speak with special force when read side by side. In Job 8:12, a papyrus plant stands green and uncut in the marsh, yet "it withers before any other plant." In Song of Solomon 6:11, someone goes down to a garden "to see the verdure of the valley," eager to learn whether the vines have budded and the pomegranates have bloomed.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

Read side by side, the two verses show fresh growth in two very different scenes: in Job the green papyrus withers when its water fails, and in the Song the fresh growth of a tended garden is something a person goes out to see. Placed alongside Psalm 1, Jeremiah 17 and John 15, which set life drawn from a lasting source against life that dries up, the comparison teaches that early freshness proves nothing by itself. What matters is where a life draws its water and who is caring for it. That truth reaches its fullest meaning in Christ, the true Vine, who rejoices over the first signs of life in His people and keeps that life from withering.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼêb is a Hebrew noun found only in Job 8:12 and Song of Solomon 6:11. The NKJV translates it "green" in Job and "verdure" in the Song. "Verdure" is an older English word for lush, fresh vegetation.

The word belongs to the same family as abib (H0024), which describes fresh young ears of grain and gives its name to the spring month of the Exodus: "On this day you are going out, in the month Abib" (Exodus 13:4). This family connection does not define ʼêb, but it fits the word's place in the vocabulary of spring and fresh growth.

Both settings are linked with water. Job's papyrus grows in a marsh. The Song's verdure grows in a "valley," and the Hebrew word there commonly describes a stream valley or wadi, where green growth springs up along the watercourse.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʼêb means freshness or fresh green growth: shoots, greenery and new vegetation. A single definition covers both uses. The word does not simply name a color. It describes living plant growth in its fresh state, and nothing in the word itself says whether that growth will end in fruit or in withering. The phrase "not cut down" in Job 8:12 belongs to the sentence, not to the word.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears in two different settings, and those settings determine how it reads.

In Job 8:12 the setting is a wisdom proverb, and "green" means the plant is at its prime: "While it is yet green and not cut down." The phrase "not cut down" stresses that no harvester has touched it. Its ruin will come without any outside blow.

In Song of Solomon 6:11 the setting is a love poem, and the verdure is something a person goes out to see. It stands beside two other signs of spring, the budding vine and the blooming pomegranates. In this verse the fresh growth is a sign of what is coming.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats green growth as something God gives and sustains. Elsewhere the Bible draws a sharp line between the person whose leaf "shall not wither" because he is planted "by the rivers of water" (Psalm 1:3) and the person who "trusts in man" and is "like a shrub in the desert" (Jeremiah 17:5–6). Again and again in Scripture, a plant's freshness or withering pictures the favor or the judgment of God.

The two uses of ʼêb fit inside this larger teaching. Life cannot sustain itself. Freshness that does not come from God has an expiration date, even while it looks its best. God is also not indifferent to small beginnings. In the Song, the first green shoots are the very thing someone goes out to look for. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, the same tender stage that exposes the fragility of a false life becomes, in a garden that is loved and tended, an occasion for watchful delight.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Job 8:11–13: green, uncut, and already dying. Bildad asks, "Can the papyrus grow up without a marsh? Can the reeds flourish without water?" (Job 8:11). The papyrus is one of the lushest plants in the ancient world, and it is also one of the most dependent. It flourishes only while the marsh holds water. When that supply fails, the plant that looked strongest is the first to collapse: "While it is yet green and not cut down, It withers before any other plant" (v. 12). No one has to harvest it. The plant dies because the source it relied on disappeared. Bildad then names his meaning: "So are the paths of all who forget God; And the hope of the hypocrite shall perish" (v. 13). A life that forgets God can look vigorous, even enviable, while it rests on something that cannot last.

The proverb is true, but Bildad used it against the wrong man. Job had not forgotten God. His withered circumstances were not proof of a hollow faith. Bildad's lesson belongs to the hypocrite. It gives no basis for passing judgment on a suffering believer.

Song of Solomon 6:11: going down to see the buds. "I went down to the garden of nuts To see the verdure of the valley, To see whether the vine had budded And the pomegranates had bloomed." The speaker goes down deliberately, and the phrase "to see" is repeated as the speaker looks closely at each plant. Many readers hear the bridegroom's voice here, though the speaker is debated. In Song of Solomon 7:12 the bride uses nearly the same words, inviting her beloved to go out early and see whether the vine has budded. Either way, both lovers in the Song share this eager attention to new growth. It echoes the bridegroom's earlier call: "For lo, the winter is past... The fig tree puts forth her green figs, And the vines with the tender grapes Give a good smell" (Song of Solomon 2:11, 13). In that spring song new growth looks ahead to fruit, and the verdure of 6:11 is worth seeing for the same reason.

The distinction between the two passages. Both verses describe fresh, promising growth in different scenes. Job's papyrus withers when the marsh water fails. The Song's verdure grows in a valley garden that someone comes to see with eager interest; the Song does not say how that garden is watered or promise that its growth will bear fruit. The contrast becomes a lesson when the scenes are read beside Psalm 1:3, Jeremiah 17:5–8 and John 15:1–5, where Scripture itself sets life rooted in a lasting source against life that dries up.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus takes up the language of plants that live by their source: "I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing" (John 15:5). The papyrus in Job pictures life cut off from its supply, and the vine in John pictures life that cannot be cut off as long as the branch remains in Him. The Father is "the vinedresser" (John 15:1), and He tends the branches so that they bear more fruit.

On the road to the cross, Jesus told the women mourning Him, "Daughters of Jerusalem, do not weep for Me, but weep for yourselves and for your children" (Luke 23:28), because days of judgment were coming. Within that warning He used green-wood imagery, with a different word: "For if they do these things in the green wood, what will be done in the dry?" (Luke 23:31). The green wood most naturally stands for Jesus Himself, innocent and full of life, yet suffering; if such things were done to Him, the dry wood of a people ripe for judgment had far more to fear. The wider gospel adds what this warning does not state: the One cut down in innocence died to give life, as Jesus said of the grain of wheat, "if it dies, it produces much grain" (John 12:24).

The link between ʼêb and abib adds a quieter echo. The month of fresh grain was the month of Passover. Paul calls Christ "our Passover" and "the firstfruits" of those raised from the dead (1 Corinthians 5:7; 15:20). This is a thematic resonance rather than a stated fulfillment, but it fits the pattern: in Christ, spring follows death, and the first green shoots of new life are the beginning of a harvest.

7. Spirit & Life

A fresh beginning with God is like a season of ʼêb. New faith, renewed devotion, and an early season of ministry are all tender, green, and full of promise. Bildad's picture in Job 8 is aimed at those who forget God, but it also shows that freshness does not sustain itself. Enthusiasm fed by favorable circumstances, the approval of others, or a passing emotional high is like papyrus in a marsh, and it can look strongest just before it fails. Israel learned a similar lesson in Deuteronomy 11:10–11. Egypt, home of the papyrus, watered its crops by human labor. The Promised Land "drinks water from the rain of heaven." The comparison is an analogy, but it fits well: the important question is whether a life is watered by its own effort or by God.

The Song of Solomon gives the other half of the picture. The Lord does not despise the day of small things (compare Zechariah 4:10). The Song pictures love that looks for the first buds. A believer who sees only faint signs of growth can take heart that those signs are exactly what the Song describes someone going out to find. Fruit has not yet appeared, but the vine has budded, and that is worth seeing.

This word also counsels humility. Bildad saw withering and assumed hypocrisy. Many green shoots wither under pressure, but some believers lose everything outwardly while remaining rooted in God. Discernment means asking what someone is drawing life from before judging what their circumstances seem to show.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

ʼêb occurs only in Job 8:12 and Song of Solomon 6:11, and both are discussed above. The following passages use different Hebrew words, but they develop the same imagery:

  • Psalm 1:3 – The righteous person is a tree "planted by the rivers of water," whose leaf "shall not wither." This is the stable counterpart to Bildad's papyrus.

  • Jeremiah 17:7–8 – The one "whose hope is the LORD" has a leaf that "will be green" and "will not be anxious in the year of drought."

  • Psalm 92:13–14 – Those "planted in the house of the LORD" remain "fresh and flourishing" and "still bear fruit in old age." Their greenness lasts beyond youth.

  • Isaiah 19:6–7 – When God judges Egypt, "the papyrus reeds by the River" wither and "be no more."

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H0024 (abib) – A lexical relative meaning fresh young ears of grain. It is also the name of the month of the Exodus (Exodus 13:4).

  • Thematic connections – Psalm 1:3, Jeremiah 17:8, and Luke 23:31 share the image of green, living growth but use different Hebrew and Greek words.
