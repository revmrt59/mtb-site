HAND, POWER, AUTHORITY - (yâd, Strong's H3027)

Summary

The Hebrew word yâd means hand. It can be the hand that holds a staff, wears a bracelet, or grips a sword. Because the hand is how a person acts, Hebrew also used yâd for what a person can do and what a person controls: power, authority, custody, and agency. To fall "into the hand" of an enemy is to come under his control. A word spoken "by the hand of" a prophet is God's word carried through a human messenger. Hebrew expressions built on this word also describe ordaining a priest, which is filling the hand, and swearing an oath, which is lifting the hand.

The word's greatest weight comes when Scripture speaks of the hand of the LORD. God's hand brought Israel out of Egypt and judged Pharaoh. It came upon Elijah and Ezekiel, and it is never too short to save. God raised that hand to swear that He would keep His promises, and the psalmists rested their lives in it: "My times are in Your hand." The study ends at the cross and the empty tomb. The Psalms speak of hands that were pierced, and the risen Jesus invited Thomas to look at His hands. Those wounded hands are the same hands from which no one can snatch His sheep.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâd is one of the most common nouns in the Hebrew Old Testament. The word occurs 1,618 times in 1,446 verses of the Hebrew Bible. It appears in every part of the canon: in law, narrative, poetry, wisdom, and prophecy. Most often the NKJV simply translates it "hand" or "hands." The Hebrew speaker also stretched the word well beyond the body. The NKJV renders it "wrists," "paw," "side," "banks" of a river, "tenons" (the projecting pegs on the tabernacle boards), "armrests" on Solomon's throne, a "monument," and even portions or shares ("five times as much," Genesis 43:34; "four-fifths," Genesis 47:24).

This wide range is not a puzzle once we see how Hebrew thought about the hand. A hand reaches, holds, works, gives, and strikes. Whatever lay within a person's reach and grip came to be described as being "in his hand."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The physical hand of a person or animal, including the wrist or paw. For example, Rebekah received bracelets for her wrists (Genesis 24:22), and David was delivered from the paw of the lion (1 Samuel 17:37).

2. Power, control, or custody. For example, the spies reported, "Truly the LORD has delivered all the land into our hands" (Joshua 2:24). Joseph was placed in authority over Potiphar's house (Genesis 39:4), and Esther was taken into the custody of the king's officer (Esther 2:8).

3. Agency or means, in the phrase "by the hand of," meaning "through" someone. For example, the LORD commanded "by the hand of Moses" (Numbers 27:23), and His word came by Haggai and by Malachi (Haggai 1:1; Malachi 1:1).

4. Extended physical senses: a side, a riverbank, a structural support, or a marker or monument. For example, Absalom set up a pillar that came to be called "Absalom's Monument" (2 Samuel 18:18).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Several fixed expressions carry meanings that a word-for-word English rendering would hide:

  • "Fill the hand" means to ordain a priest. The NKJV translates it "consecrate" (Exodus 28:41; 29:9). Judges 17:5 and 12 show the same expression used for Micah's self-appointed household priest, so the phrase names the act of installing someone, faithful or not.

  • "Lift the hand" means to swear an oath. Abram told the king of Sodom that he had raised his hand to the LORD (Genesis 14:22). God speaks the same way of Himself (Numbers 14:30; Ezekiel 20:5).

  • "The hand of the LORD was upon" someone describes God taking hold of a prophet for His purposes (1 Kings 18:46; Ezekiel 1:3).

  • "The work of men's hands" is the prophets' scornful label for idols (Psalm 115:4; Isaiah 2:8; Micah 5:13).

  • Laying the hand on a sacrifice or a person marks identification or commissioning (Leviticus 1:4; Numbers 27:18).

The context also shows whose power is at stake. A human hand may be generous, guilty, weary, or strong. With God's hand, the context tells what it is doing. In the Exodus one act of His hand rescued Israel and judged Egypt. His hand can also discipline His own people, as David felt when it was heavy upon him (Psalm 32:4), and it can empower His servants, as it did Elijah and Ezekiel (1 Kings 18:46; Ezekiel 1:3). The famous "right hand" passages, such as Exodus 15:6 and Psalm 110:1, use a different Hebrew word that specifically means "right hand." They belong to the same family of images, but they are not occurrences of yâd.

4. Theological Significance

Above all, the hand of the LORD shows that God is personally present and active in human history. He does not stand at a distance from it. Before the plagues, God told Moses that He would lay His hand on Egypt so that "the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD" (Exodus 7:4–5). Israel was to remember that "by strength of hand the LORD brought you out of this place" (Exodus 13:3), and they were to tell their children the same thing (Exodus 13:14). After the sea closed over Pharaoh's army, the people saw what the NKJV calls "the great work" the LORD had done. The Hebrew there is literally the great hand (Exodus 14:31). A single act of God's hand rescued Israel and judged Egypt, and Scripture keeps the two together. God's power is never neutral. It always acts for a purpose.

That hand did not grow weak with time. When Moses doubted whether God could feed the people, the LORD asked whether His arm (literally hand) had been shortened (Numbers 11:23). Centuries later Isaiah gave a discouraged nation the same answer: the LORD's hand is not shortened so that it cannot save, and the problem lay in their sins (Isaiah 59:1–2). The idols around Israel had carved hands that could not move (Psalm 115:4, 7). Israel's God had a living hand.

That same hand could weigh heavily or open wide. David felt God's hand "heavy upon me" day and night while he hid his sin (Psalm 32:4). Elsewhere God opens His hand and satisfies the desire of every living thing (Psalm 145:16). In David's case the heaviness came while he kept silent about his sin and gave way to forgiveness when he confessed it (Psalm 32:3–5). God Himself had not changed.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Filling the hand: priests installed by God's provision (Exodus 28–29; Leviticus 8). God commanded Moses to anoint Aaron and his sons and to fill their hand, which the NKJV translates "consecrate" (Exodus 28:41). The ordination ritual in Leviticus 8:25–28 shows what that filling looked like. Moses placed the fat, the right thigh, and the unleavened bread in the hands of Aaron and his sons and waved them as a wave offering before the LORD. Then Moses took them from their hands and burned them on the altar (Leviticus 8:27–28). The rite pictures the priests' hands being filled with what God provided before they began to serve. A priest did not bring his own offering to qualify himself. As an application of the rite, he served with what God had put in his hands. Isaiah later shows the tragic reversal of this picture. Israel lifted its hands in worship, but those hands were full of blood (Isaiah 1:15).

Laying on the hand: identification, sin-bearing, and commissioning (Leviticus 1:4; 16:21; Numbers 27:18–23). The worshiper "shall put his hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it will be accepted on his behalf to make atonement for him" (Leviticus 1:4). The verse ties the gesture to the animal's being accepted on the worshiper's behalf, so the animal stood, in effect, in his place. On the Day of Atonement, Aaron laid both hands on the live goat and confessed Israel's sins, "putting them on the head of the goat" (Leviticus 16:21). The same gesture also passed on leadership. Moses "laid his hands on him and inaugurated him, just as the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses" (Numbers 27:23). Here the laying on of hands was part of Joshua's public commissioning before Eleazar the priest and the whole congregation, in which Moses was to give him some of his authority (Numbers 27:19–20). One verse uses the word twice: Moses' hands rest on Joshua, and God's command comes by Moses' hand.

The lifted hand: God binds Himself by oath (Exodus 6:8; Ezekiel 20:5; Hebrews 6:13). In Exodus 6:8, God promises, "I will bring you into the land which I swore to give to Abraham." The word "swore" stands for the Hebrew lifted My hand. Ezekiel repeats the image twice in one sentence: "I raised My hand in an oath to them, saying, 'I am the LORD your God'" (Ezekiel 20:5). People raise their hands to call on someone greater as a witness. God has no one greater to call on. Hebrews draws the conclusion: "because He could swear by no one greater, He swore by Himself" (Hebrews 6:13). God took up a human gesture so that weak people could know His promise is settled.

The hand of the LORD upon His servants (1 Kings 18:46; Ezekiel 1:3; 3:14, 22; 8:1; Haggai 1:1; Zechariah 7:12). When the hand of the LORD came on Elijah, he ran ahead of Ahab's chariot all the way to Jezreel. Ezekiel's ministry is marked again and again by the same experience. God's hand came upon him by the river Chebar (Ezekiel 1:3). When the Spirit lifted him up and took him away, "the hand of the LORD was strong upon me" (Ezekiel 3:14). Later, as he sat in his house with the elders of Judah sitting before him, "the hand of the Lord GOD fell upon me there" (Ezekiel 8:1), and the Spirit brought him "in visions of God to Jerusalem" (Ezekiel 8:3) to show him the city's corruption. This was God's strength taking over a man for God's purpose, far beyond human ability. The related phrase "by the hand of" shows God's word arriving through human messengers. Zechariah says the LORD sent His words "by His Spirit through the former prophets," and refusing those words brought "great wrath" (Zechariah 7:12). The messenger was human, but the authority belonged to God.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of God's hand moves toward hands that were wounded. In Psalm 22, a righteous sufferer surrounded by enemies cries, "They pierced My hands and My feet" (Psalm 22:16). The psalm describes more than anything recorded of David's life. The Gospels explicitly apply other lines of Psalm 22 to the cross, such as the dividing of His garments (John 19:24). They do not quote the line about hands and feet as a fulfillment, but their account of Jesus' wounds corresponds to it: the risen Lord showed Thomas the nail marks in His hands (John 20:25–27), and Christians have long read verse 16 in that light. Zechariah 13:6 asks, "What are these wounds between your arms?" and hears the answer, "Those with which I was wounded in the house of my friends." That verse sits in a difficult passage about false prophets, so its connection to Christ is a resonance and should not be pressed as a direct prediction. Even so, the image of a man wounded by his own people fits the One who "came to His own" and was rejected.

After the resurrection, Jesus pointed to His hands as proof of who He was. "Behold My hands and My feet, that it is I Myself" (Luke 24:39). He invited the doubting disciple: "Reach your finger here, and look at My hands" (John 20:27). The hands of the Son, through whom the heavens were made, bore the marks of human hands.

The sacrificial gestures of Leviticus also find their meaning in Him. The sins that Aaron laid on the goat's head were finally laid on the Lamb of God. Isaiah says that through the suffering Servant, "the pleasure of the LORD shall prosper in His hand" (Isaiah 53:10). Pilate tried to declare himself innocent by washing his hands (Matthew 27:24). His gesture recalls the rite of Deuteronomy 21:6–7, where the elders washed their hands and declared, "Our hands have not shed this blood." Pilate washed his hands while condemning the only truly innocent man. Jesus Himself prayed in the words of Psalm 31:5, committing His spirit into His Father's hand as He died (Luke 23:46). The Son who entrusted Himself to the Father's hand is the same Shepherd who had already said of His sheep that "neither shall anyone snatch them out of My hand" (John 10:28).

7. Spirit & Life

"My times are in Your hand; Deliver me from the hand of my enemies" (Psalm 31:15). In one verse David names two hands. One is the hand of his enemies, which is real and dangerous. The other is God's hand, which governs his times: every season, delay, and outcome of his life. The believer's security does not depend on pretending enemies have no hands. It depends on knowing whose hand holds the calendar. When Jesus prayed the same psalm from the cross, He showed us how far that trust can reach.

Our own hands respond to His. "Lift up your hands in the sanctuary, And bless the LORD" (Psalm 134:2). "I spread out my hands to You; My soul longs for You like a thirsty land" (Psalm 143:6). Open, lifted hands say that we have nothing to bring and are waiting to receive. That posture matches what God did when He ordained priests: He filled their hands with what He had provided for them to offer.

What our hands do with others matters just as much. God told Israel to open its hand wide to a poor brother (Deuteronomy 15:8). The psalmist links "clean hands and a pure heart" with drawing near to God (Psalm 24:4; a different Hebrew word for hand, describing the same moral picture). Job adds, "he who has clean hands will be stronger and stronger" (Job 17:9). The condition of a person's hands shows the condition of the life behind them.

The word even speaks to our longing to be remembered. Absalom set up a pillar for himself because, as he said, "I have no son to keep my name in remembrance," and it came to be called Absalom's Monument, literally his hand (2 Samuel 18:18). Saul did the same after disobeying God (1 Samuel 15:12). God offers the faithful eunuch, who could have no descendants, something better. He promises "in My house and within My walls a place and a name" (Isaiah 56:5). The word "place" there is yâd. A lasting legacy cannot be built with our own hands. God gives it, and He gives it in His house.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 39:3–4, 22–23 — Joseph's master saw that the LORD made all he did prosper in his hand and put everything under his authority. God's favor showed itself in what came under Joseph's hand, even in a prison.

  • Exodus 17:11–12 — Moses planned to stand on the hill "with the rod of God in my hand" (Exodus 17:9). When Moses held up his hand, Israel prevailed against Amalek. When his hands grew heavy, Aaron and Hur supported them. The account links Israel's success to Moses' raised hand, and those hands needed the help of friends.

  • 1 Samuel 17:37 — David trusted that the LORD who rescued him from the paw of the lion and the bear would rescue him from the hand of the Philistine. Past deliverances gave him confidence for the next one.

  • Ezra 7:6, 9; Nehemiah 2:8, 18 — Ezra's success is credited to the hand of his God upon him, and Nehemiah credits his own to the good hand of his God upon him. In each case, God's kind help came through the decisions of a pagan king.

  • Proverbs 31:20 — The worthy woman reaches out her hands to the needy. Her skilled hands are also open hands.

  • Ecclesiastes 9:10 — Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might. Ordinary work is to be done with full effort, because life is short.
