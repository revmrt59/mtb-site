KNOW, MAKE KNOWN - (yâdaʻ, Strong's H3045)

Summary

The Hebrew verb yâdaʻ means to know, and the setting decides what kind of knowing is meant. A person can know a fact, and can also know another person, know how to do something with skilled hands, or be known by God with chosen and caring attention. The same word describes Adam knowing Eve, Pharaoh's officials realizing Egypt was ruined, craftsmen who knew how to build the tabernacle, and God telling Israel, "You only have I known of all the families of the earth" (Amos 3:2). When the Old Testament speaks of knowing God, the word is especially personal and practical. To know the LORD is more than holding facts about Him. It means that who He is has become part of your experience and your life.

That is why this word sits at the center of the Bible's story. Humanity's first sin was a grab for a kind of knowledge apart from God, and it left Adam and Eve knowing their own shame. God's great acts in Exodus and Ezekiel are repeatedly aimed at one result: that people will know that He is the LORD. The prophets grieve over a people who did not know Him, and Jeremiah looks ahead to a day when "they all shall know Me" (Jeremiah 31:34). Underneath all of it is God's complete knowledge of every person. For those who belong to Him, that knowledge becomes a steady comfort: He knew them before they knew Him, and in His covenant and His forgiveness He does not turn away from them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâdaʻ is a common Hebrew verb, found from Genesis to Malachi. The word occurs 945 times in 873 verses of the Hebrew Bible. It appears in ordinary conversation ("Do you know Laban?" Genesis 29:5), in family life, in courtrooms and battlefields, in worship, and most heavily in the prophets. Ezekiel alone uses it about a hundred times, mostly in his repeated refrain that people will know that God is the LORD.

The NKJV translates it with a wide range of English words: know, realize, perceive, notice, acknowledge, understand, be skillful, and, in some forms, show, teach, declare, or make known. That variety in English reflects how much ground one Hebrew word covers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, yâdaʻ simply means to know. Context determines what kind of knowing is meant: plain awareness of a fact, discernment, acquaintance with a person, firsthand experience, or practical skill.

1. To perceive, learn, or realize something: to become aware of a fact or situation. Samson woke up and did not know that the LORD had departed from him (Judges 16:20).

2. To know a person by acquaintance and relationship, including the closest relationship of sexual union. "Now Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived" (Genesis 4:1). The word can also describe someone's acquaintances or friends, the people they know (Psalm 55:13).

3. To recognize someone with attention and care, to take notice of them or acknowledge them as one's own. When God saw Israel's misery in Egypt, He "acknowledged" them (Exodus 2:25), and "the LORD knows the way of the righteous" (Psalm 1:6).

4. To know how: to have practical skill. Hiram's workers knew how to cut timber (1 Kings 5:6), and David was "skillful" on the harp (1 Samuel 16:16, 18). God filled Bezalel and the other craftsmen with wisdom to know how to do all the work of the sanctuary (Exodus 36:1).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word changes color depending on who is doing the knowing. When people are the subject, it ranges from simple awareness to trusting relationship. When God is the subject, it often means both full awareness and personal commitment.

A striking pattern is the phrase "did not know," which covers more than one kind of failure. A new king arose "who did not know Joseph" (Exodus 1:8). The verse does not describe his thoughts, but his harsh treatment of Israel that follows suggests he felt no tie to Joseph or his people. Pharaoh's not knowing was open defiance: "Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice to let Israel go? I do not know the LORD, nor will I let Israel go" (Exodus 5:2). After Joshua, a generation grew up "who did not know the LORD nor the work which He had done for Israel" (Judges 2:10). For them, the memory of God's works had been lost along with the relationship. Eli's sons are also said not to know the LORD (1 Samuel 2:12), yet they served as priests and handled the sacrifices. Their failure was moral and relational, shown in how they treated the people's offerings (1 Samuel 2:13–17): they had no living loyalty to the God whose altar they served.

The word also has forms that mean to cause someone else to know, which is why the NKJV often renders it "show," "teach," or "make known." David prays, "Show me Your ways, O LORD" (Psalm 25:4), and God shows His people the path of life (Psalm 16:11). When knowledge of God comes, it comes because God makes Himself known.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats knowing God as the purpose of human life and the goal of God's work in history. Jeremiah strips away every other boast: let the wise, the strong, and the rich stop glorying in what they have, and "let him who glories glory in this, That he understands and knows Me, That I am the LORD, exercising lovingkindness, judgment, and righteousness in the earth. For in these I delight" (Jeremiah 9:24). This knowledge has content. It is knowing what kind of God He is, and what He delights in.

God is known because He chooses to reveal Himself, in His deeds, His words, and His dealings with His people. Scripture does not present knowing Him as a human achievement. The order matters throughout the Old Testament: God's knowing comes first. He knew Abraham (Genesis 18:19), Moses by name (Exodus 33:17), and Jeremiah before birth. Human knowing of God is a response to having been known.

Knowing God is lived and practical. In the same way a craftsman's knowledge shows up in his finished work, knowledge of God shows itself in trust, worship, and obedience. Scripture does not treat someone who ignores His ways as truly knowing the LORD (compare Jeremiah 22:16).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 3: the knowledge that was grasped. The first crisis in Scripture turns on this word. The serpent promised that if Eve ate, her eyes would be opened and she would be like God, knowing good and evil (Genesis 3:5). In effect, the offer was to decide good and evil for herself, apart from trusting the Creator's word. The result is described with bitter irony: "the eyes of both of them were opened, and they knew that they were naked" (Genesis 3:7). They got the knowledge they reached for, and what they knew first was shame. God's verdict confirms it: "Behold, the man has become like one of Us, to know good and evil" (Genesis 3:22). Then comes the exile from the garden. Knowledge seized apart from God did not lift them up. It left them hiding from the One they were made to know.

Exodus and Ezekiel: "they shall know that I am the LORD." Again and again God states why He acts. The plagues fall on Egypt so that the Egyptians will know that He is the LORD (Exodus 7:5). At the Red Sea He says Egypt will know Him when He gains honor over Pharaoh (Exodus 14:18). Israel, too, is to know Him through the manna and quail (Exodus 16:12). Ezekiel turns this into the refrain of his book. After judgment on idolatry, the people will know that He is the LORD (Ezekiel 6:7). After restoration, the ruined cities filled with people like flocks for a feast, they will know it again (Ezekiel 36:38). God acts to vindicate His holy name before the nations so they will know Him (Ezekiel 36:23). Pharaoh began by saying he did not know the LORD (Exodus 5:2) and ended up meeting Him in power. This kind of knowing is an inescapable encounter with who God is, through judgment or through rescue. History has a purpose, and the purpose is that God be known.

Amos 3:2 and Jeremiah 1:5: knowing as choosing. Sometimes God is the one who "knows," and each setting shows what that knowing involves. Through Amos, God says, "You only have I known of all the families of the earth." God was obviously aware of every nation. He means that Israel alone was His in a close, chosen relationship. The next line is startling: "Therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities." Being known by God brings privilege and accountability together. Jeremiah hears the same kind of knowing applied to one life: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you; Before you were born I sanctified you; I ordained you a prophet to the nations" (Jeremiah 1:5). "Knew" stands alongside "sanctified" and "ordained." God's knowledge of Jeremiah was a setting apart for a calling, decided before the prophet drew breath. It was more than advance information about him.

Psalm 139 and Job 23:10: fully known. David opens with "O LORD, You have searched me and known me" (Psalm 139:1). God knows his "sitting down and my rising up" (Psalm 139:2), and "there is not a word on my tongue, But behold, O LORD, You know it altogether" (Psalm 139:4). Nothing in David is hidden. Yet the psalm does not end in fear. It ends in invitation: "Search me, O God, and know my heart; Try me, and know my anxieties" (Psalm 139:23). David asks to be known even more deeply, because he trusts the One who knows him. Job, in the dark and unable to find God, holds on to the same truth: "But He knows the way that I take; When He has tested me, I shall come forth as gold" (Job 23:10). Psalm 1:6 shows the other side: "the LORD knows the way of the righteous, But the way of the ungodly shall perish." God's knowledge watches over the righteous and exposes the wicked.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story that starts with stolen knowledge in Eden moves toward knowledge freely given. Moses pleaded with God to show him His way "that I may know You" (Exodus 33:13). Hosea urged a wayward people to press on to know the LORD (Hosea 6:3) and grieved that the land lacked knowledge of God (Hosea 4:1). Israel's history showed how often God's people failed to know Him. God promised to do what they could not: give them a heart to know Him (Jeremiah 24:7).

Jeremiah's promise of a new covenant brings this to its high point: "No more shall every man teach his neighbor... saying, 'Know the LORD,' for they all shall know Me, from the least of them to the greatest of them" (Jeremiah 31:34). Notice the word that follows: "For I will forgive their iniquity, and their sin I will remember no more." Forgiveness is the ground of this knowledge. The shame that entered when Adam and Eve "knew that they were naked" is answered by a God who removes sin so His people can know Him without hiding.

Jesus Christ stands at the center of that fulfillment. Isaiah describes the Servant as acquainted with grief (Isaiah 53:3, using this same verb). He came to know human sorrow by experience, so that we could know God. Jesus Himself defines eternal life in these terms: "this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent" (John 17:3). The New Testament uses Greek words, but it carries forward the same personal, life-giving meaning. Habakkuk's vision of the earth filled with the knowledge of the LORD's glory (Habakkuk 2:14) shows where the story is heading.

7. Spirit & Life

There is great relief in the order Scripture keeps. We are not trying to get the attention of a distant God. He knew Jeremiah before the womb, Moses by name, and Job on a road Job himself could not understand. Paul later put it the same way when he wrote of believers who have come to know God, or rather are known by God (Galatians 4:9). Our seeking is a response to His knowing.

Genesis 3 and Hebrews 4 together draw a sharp picture. Adam and Eve knew they were naked and sewed fig leaves to cover themselves (Genesis 3:7). When they heard God, they hid among the trees (Genesis 3:8). Hebrews says that before God "all things are naked and open to the eyes of Him to whom we must give account" (Hebrews 4:13). Every fig leaf fails. But Psalm 139 shows what happens when a person stops hiding: being fully known becomes the safest place to stand. A believer can pray David's prayer, "Search me, O God, and know my heart," with honesty instead of dread, because the God who sees everything is also the God who forgives (Jeremiah 31:34).

The skilled hands of Bezalel's craftsmen show what this knowing looks like in practice. Their knowledge was visible in the gold and wood they shaped. In the same way, knowing God shows itself in a life that reflects His lovingkindness, justice, and righteousness, the very things Jeremiah says He delights in (Jeremiah 9:24). Paul counted everything else as loss "for the excellence of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord" (Philippians 3:8). That is the Old Testament's way of knowing God, carried all the way through: a relationship worth more than anything a person could grasp for on their own.

Psalm 46:10 — God calls His people to be still and know that He is God. Knowing Him begins with ceasing from frantic striving.

Psalm 103:14 — God knows our frame and remembers we are dust. His knowledge of our weakness moves Him to compassion.

Proverbs 3:6 — In all your ways acknowledge Him, and He will direct your paths. "Acknowledge" is this same verb: knowing God as a daily, practical recognition of Him.

Nahum 1:7 — The LORD is good, a stronghold in trouble, and He knows those who trust in Him. His knowing is protective care.
