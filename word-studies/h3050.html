YAH - (Yâhh, Strong's H3050)

Summary

Yah is a shortened form of the personal name of the God of Israel, YHWH, usually rendered "the LORD" in English Bibles (Strong's H3068). The name has no separate meaning of its own. It carries the meaning of the full name it abbreviates. Hebrew scholarship recognizes Yah either as a contraction of YHWH or as an older poetic form of it, and the evidence does not settle which. Scripture ties the full name to God's own words to Moses at the burning bush, where He revealed Himself as "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14–15). Yah is the God who is, who remains, and who acts for His people. It is a name of the one true God, not a lesser deity or a separate title, and it appears almost entirely in song, poetry, and prayer.

The name first appears in the song Israel sang after the Red Sea: "The LORD is my strength and song, and He has become my salvation" (Exodus 15:2). From there it fills the Psalms, especially the closing praises of the Psalter. It is found most often inside the word Hallelujah, "Praise Yah," which most English Bibles translate as "Praise the LORD!" Isaiah takes up the Red Sea song for a future salvation (Isaiah 12:2), and Hezekiah grieves that death would keep him from seeing Yah (Isaiah 38:11). In the New Testament the same praise sounds as "Alleluia!" when heaven celebrates God's final victory (Revelation 19:1–6). Yah is the name God's people use when He has rescued them. It belongs to the songs of redeemed people, and it points ahead to Christ's victory over sin and death, which gives the church its own reason to sing.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Yah (יָהּ) refers to one person only, the LORD, the God of Israel. Because this is a divine name, there is no biography to trace and no namesakes to separate. Instead, the study follows how God made Himself known through this name and how His people used it.

The meaning of Yah depends on its relationship to YHWH. Hebrew lexicons treat it as a short form of God's personal name. Whether it was shortened from YHWH or was an older poetic form used alongside it is debated, so this study does not claim more than the evidence allows. What is certain is that Yah is not a separate name with its own etymology. It means what YHWH means. Scripture itself explains that name. When Moses asked what name to give Israel, God answered "I AM WHO I AM" and declared that "the LORD" was His name forever (Exodus 3:14–15). The name speaks of God's self-existence and faithfulness: He is the living God who keeps His word and acts on behalf of His people. Yah carries that whole meaning in a single syllable.

What sets Yah apart from YHWH is where it appears, not what it means. YHWH occurs thousands of times across law, narrative, and prophecy. Yah is concentrated in Exodus 15 and 17, the Psalms, and three passages in Isaiah, almost always in song or prayer. It is the name of God as His people call out to Him and praise Him.

Two features keep Yah hidden from many English readers. First, most English translations, including the NKJV, render it "LORD," the same as the full name. The NKJV shows "YAH" in only a few places, such as Isaiah 12:2. Second, many of its occurrences sit inside the word Hallelujah, a combination of the Hebrew verb for praise (H1984) and Yah. When the NKJV says "Praise the LORD!" at the opening and close of many psalms, the Hebrew is calling the congregation to praise Yah by name. The same name also appears within many Hebrew personal names, such as Elijah, Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Josiah. Those names are indexed separately, but they show how deeply the name of God was woven into the life of Israelite families.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Red Sea: Where the Name First Becomes a Song

Yah first appears on the far shore of the Red Sea. Pharaoh's army has drowned, and Moses leads Israel in song: "The LORD is my strength and song, and He has become my salvation; He is my God, and I will praise Him; My father's God, and I will exalt Him" (Exodus 15:2). The Hebrew name here is Yah. Israel did not learn this name in a classroom. They sang it the day God brought them out of slavery and stopped an enemy they could never have stopped themselves. The song closes by declaring that the LORD will reign forever and ever (Exodus 15:18), which sets up a thread that runs to the last book of the Bible.

Later, after Israel defeats Amalek at Rephidim, Moses builds an altar named "The-LORD-Is-My-Banner" and speaks of the LORD's war with Amalek "from generation to generation" (Exodus 17:15–16). The Hebrew of verse 16 is difficult. It speaks literally of a hand upon the throne of Yah, which the NKJV renders as the LORD having sworn. Either way, Yah is the God who takes up His people's cause against an enemy that attacked them when they were weakest (Deuteronomy 25:17–18).

The Psalms: The Name at the Heart of Worship

Nearly all the occurrences of Yah are in the Psalms, and they cover the full range of Israel's life with God.

Praise for His greatness. Psalm 68 calls Israel to sing to the One who rides on the clouds, whose name is Yah (68:4). Psalm 89 asks who is mighty like Yah, whose faithfulness surrounds Him (89:8). These verses praise God for who He is, apart from any single rescue.

Remembering in the dark. In Psalm 77 the psalmist cannot sleep and fears God has forgotten him. He turns his mind deliberately: he will remember the works of Yah and His wonders of old (77:11). The memory he reaches for is the Exodus, when God led His people "through the sea" (77:19–20). The name first sung at the Red Sea becomes the name a troubled believer holds onto when God seems silent.

Confidence when the wicked mock. In Psalm 94 the wicked crush the widow and the orphan and say that Yah does not see (94:7). The psalmist answers that the God who made the eye certainly sees (94:9). He goes on to call the person whom Yah disciplines and teaches from His law blessed (94:12). The same name the wicked dismiss is the name under which God corrects and teaches His own.

Mercy for sinners. Psalm 130 cries from the depths: "If You, LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand?" (130:3). The first "LORD" is Yah. The psalmist knows that no one could survive if God kept a record of sin, and his hope rests on the next verse: there is forgiveness with Him (130:4).

Hallelujah and the closing praises of the Psalter. The call "Hallelujah" first appears at the end of Psalm 104 (104:35) and then marks the beginning or end of many psalms that follow (105:45; 106:1, 48; 111:1; 112:1; 113:1, 9; 116:19; 117:2; 135:1, 3, 21). The Psalter ends with five psalms that begin and end with "Hallelujah" (Psalms 146–150). Their reasons for praise are concrete. Yah reigns forever (146:10). He has dealt with Israel as with no other nation by giving them His word (147:19–20). He has raised up a people who are near to Him (148:14). He has chosen Jacob for Himself (135:4). The final psalm opens, "Praise the LORD! Praise God in His sanctuary; Praise Him in His mighty firmament!" (150:1). It ends by calling everything that has breath to praise Yah (150:6). The book that began with a single blessed man meditating on God's law (Psalm 1) ends with every breathing creature praising God by name.

The Hallel and Psalm 118. Psalms 113–118, called the Egyptian Hallel in Jewish tradition, were sung at the great festivals, including Passover. Psalm 118 uses Yah more than any other psalm. The worshiper recalls distress: "I called on the LORD in distress; The LORD answered me and set me in a broad place" (118:5), where both occurrences are Yah. Surrounded by nations, he declares, "in the name of the LORD I will destroy them" (118:10), here using the full name. Then he sings the Red Sea words again: "The LORD is my strength and song, and He has become my salvation" (118:14). He declares that he will not die but live and tell the works of Yah. Yah has disciplined him severely but has not given him over to death (118:17–18). He asks for the gates of righteousness to be opened so he can go in and praise Yah (118:19). In this psalm the private rescue of one worshiper becomes the public praise of the whole congregation.

Pilgrimage and future generations. Psalm 122 describes Jerusalem as the place where the tribes of Yah (NKJV, "the tribes of the LORD") go up to give thanks to the name of the LORD (122:4). Psalm 102 looks beyond its own time: these things will be written down so that a people not yet created may praise Yah (102:18). Praise of this name was never meant to end with one generation.

Praise and the living. Psalm 115 notes that the dead do not praise Yah, and it answers that "we will bless" Yah from this time forth and forevermore (115:17–18). Hezekiah takes up the same concern.

Isaiah: A New Exodus and a King Facing Death

Isaiah 11 promises a day when the LORD will gather His scattered people and prepare a highway for them, "as it was for Israel in the day that he came up from the land of Egypt" (Isaiah 11:16). Isaiah 12 gives the song for that day, and it repeats the Red Sea song: "Behold, God is my salvation, I will trust and not be afraid; 'For YAH, the LORD, is my strength and song; He also has become my salvation'" (Isaiah 12:2). Isaiah places both names side by side, Yah and YHWH. The prophet takes Moses' song and gives it to a people God has not yet gathered. The first Exodus becomes the pattern for a greater one still to come.

Isaiah 26 belongs to another song of that future day. It calls God's people to trust the LORD forever, because in Yah, the LORD, is lasting strength. The Hebrew image is of an everlasting rock (26:4). Once again the doubled name, Yah and YHWH together, stands behind a call to trust God completely.

Isaiah 38 sounds a different note. King Hezekiah, sick to the point of death, writes down his lament. He thought he would never again see Yah in the land of the living (38:11, where the name appears twice). He knew that the grave cannot praise God, but the living can (38:18–19). When God added fifteen years to his life, Hezekiah promised to sing in the house of the LORD all the days of his life (38:20).

Revelation: The Final Hallelujah

The Greek Alleluia (G239), a transliteration of Hallelujah, appears in the New Testament only in Revelation 19. When God judges the great city Babylon, a great multitude in heaven cries out, "Alleluia! Salvation and glory and honor and power belong to the Lord our God!" (19:1). They praise Him because "true and righteous are His judgments" (19:2). The cry is repeated (19:3), the elders and living creatures answer "Amen! Alleluia!" (19:4), and the chapter's praise peaks with a voice "as the sound of many waters and as the sound of mighty thunderings, saying, 'Alleluia! For the Lord God Omnipotent reigns!'" (19:6). The short form of God's name that Moses sang on the shore of the Red Sea is the last word of praise heaven sings before the marriage supper of the Lamb (19:7–9).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The plain facts of Scripture show that Yah is God's own name, first sung in response to the Exodus. The Red Sea formula, "my strength and song, and He has become my salvation," is repeated in three places: Exodus 15:2, Psalm 118:14, and Isaiah 12:2. Isaiah adds the full name beside Yah and varies the wording slightly. This repetition is explicit in the text and forms one of the clearest threads connecting the Law, the Writings, and the Prophets. Each time it is sung, God's people are saying that the God who saved at the Red Sea has saved again.

The three words in the formula fit together. God is the strength His people do not have themselves. He becomes their song, so that their joy is centered on Him rather than on their own achievement. And He has become their salvation, a rescue accomplished in history, not just a feeling. Israel did not split the sea, and the song reflects that. God acted, and Israel responded in praise.

It is reasonable to draw a further conclusion from where the name appears. Since Yah is used almost entirely in poetry and prayer, it seems to have been the name Israel used in worship. It is the name of God spoken directly to Him, in thanksgiving, crying out, or praising. The fact that this short form carried the Red Sea song through centuries of worship suggests that Israel remembered its rescue largely by singing it.

The name also connects worship with life itself. Psalm 115:17–18, Psalm 118:17, and Isaiah 38:11–19 together present a consistent view: being alive before God means praising Him, and the worst thing about death, from that Old Testament vantage point, was silence before Yah. Scripture states this outright in Hezekiah's prayer. The implication is that God created and redeemed His people so they would know Him and praise Him.

Finally, the name follows God's purposes from beginning to end. Yah is first sung when Pharaoh falls and is last sung, as Alleluia, when Babylon falls. Revelation itself draws the connection, since the victors earlier in the book sing "the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb" (Revelation 15:3). The pattern of judgment on oppressors, rescue for God's people, and praise to Yah frames the whole story of redemption.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Several passages connect Yah to Christ, and they carry different levels of certainty.

The clearest is Psalm 68:18. The psalm celebrates God ascending on high, leading captives, and receiving gifts so that Yah God might dwell among His people. Paul quotes this verse in Ephesians 4:8 and applies it to Christ's ascension, when He gave gifts to His church. This is an explicit New Testament interpretation. The God who rides on the clouds and is praised as Yah has come down, risen, and ascended in the person of Jesus, and He now lives among His people by His Spirit (Ephesians 2:22).

Psalm 118, the psalm with the most uses of Yah, is quoted more than once in the Gospels. The crowds greeted Jesus with its words, "Blessed is He who comes in the name of the LORD" (118:26; Matthew 21:9). Jesus applied its rejected stone that became the chief cornerstone to Himself (118:22; Matthew 21:42). Verse 26 uses the full name, and verse 22 contains no divine name at all, but both belong to the same song that declares Yah to be strength, song, and salvation.

The connection to the Last Supper is strongly supported but rests partly on tradition. Matthew records that after the meal, when Jesus and His disciples "had sung a hymn," they went out to the Mount of Olives (Matthew 26:30; Mark 14:26). Matthew does not name the hymn. However, the Passover setting and Jewish custom make it very likely that this was the Hallel, Psalms 113–118. If so, on the way to Gethsemane Jesus sang that Yah had become His salvation, and sang the lines of Psalm 118:17–18 about not dying but living and not being given over to death. The psalmist spoke of rescue from the edge of death. Jesus would pass through death itself and rise. The fit is striking, though no apostle explicitly draws this particular connection.

Hezekiah's lament gains new depth in light of Christ. Hezekiah feared the grave would end his praise. Paul writes that Christ "has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Timothy 1:10, paraphrased). The fear that death would silence the praise of Yah is answered in the resurrection. That is why the final Alleluias of Revelation are sung by a great multitude in heaven, including people who had died in faith.

5. Spirit & Life

Yah is not a name Israel made up to describe an idea about God. God attached it to His own acts and His people learned it by experiencing them. The first time anyone in Scripture sings this name, they are standing safe on the far shore with the waters closed behind them. That order matters for worship today. Praise in the Bible is a response to what God has done. Christians sing Hallelujah because God has acted in Christ, bringing them out of a slavery worse than Egypt's (Colossians 1:13).

The Psalms also show that praising Yah is not only for good days. The same name appears in the sleepless night of Psalm 77, the injustice of Psalm 94, and the depths of Psalm 130. In those psalms, believers say God's name before they feel any relief, holding onto what He has done in the past while waiting for Him to act again. Psalm 130:3 is honest about why we need Him: if Yah kept a record of our sins, no one could stand. Our praise rests on God's forgiveness, not on our worthiness.

Hezekiah's prayer asks us what we think life is for. He did not grieve mainly over lost years or a lost throne. He grieved that he would no longer see Yah or praise Him. For anyone who belongs to Christ, that fear is gone, because the One who conquered death has made praise eternal. The present has its own call, though. Psalm 118:17 is a good purpose for any day God gives: to live and declare the works of the LORD.

Finally, the closing word of the Psalter applies to every reader. "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD" (Psalm 150:6, paraphrased). Breath is God's gift, and Scripture expects it to be used to praise Him. The heavenly crowd of Revelation 19 is singing a song that started on the shore of the Red Sea, and the church on earth is invited to join in now.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Exodus 15:2: The first occurrence. Yah is sung as Israel's strength, song, and salvation after the Red Sea.

  • Exodus 17:16: Yah in connection with God's lasting war against Amalek; the Hebrew is difficult.

  • Psalm 68:4, 18: Yah who rides on the clouds and who ascends to dwell with His people; quoted in Ephesians 4:8 and applied to Christ's ascension.

  • Psalm 77:11: Remembering the works of Yah in a time of distress, with the Exodus in view.

  • Psalm 94:7, 12: The wicked deny that Yah sees; the righteous are blessed by His discipline.

  • Psalm 115:17–18: The dead do not praise Yah, but the living will bless Him forever.

  • Psalm 118:5, 14, 17–19: Distress, rescue, the repeated Red Sea formula, life instead of death, and the gates of righteousness.

  • Psalm 130:3: If Yah kept a record of sins, no one could stand; the basis for hope in His forgiveness.

  • Psalms 146–150: The closing Hallelujah psalms that end the Psalter, finishing with 150:1 and 150:6.

  • Isaiah 12:2: "YAH, the LORD" in the song of a new Exodus, repeating Exodus 15:2.

  • Isaiah 26:4: Trust in Yah, the LORD, as an everlasting rock.

  • Isaiah 38:11, 18–20: Hezekiah fears that death would keep him from seeing and praising Yah.

  • Matthew 26:30: Jesus and the disciples sing a hymn after the Passover meal, most likely the Hallel.

  • Revelation 19:1–6: The four New Testament Alleluias, celebrating God's righteous judgment and His reign.
