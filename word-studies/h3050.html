YAH, LORD - (Yâhh, Strong's H3050)

Summary

Yâhh (Yah) is a short, poetic form of God's personal name, Yahweh, the name God revealed to Israel as the One who rescues and stays with His people. In most English Bibles it is hidden behind the capitalized word "LORD." The NKJV prints "YAH" in a few places, such as Psalm 68:4, Isaiah 12:2, Isaiah 26:4, and Isaiah 38:11. The shortened name appears mainly in songs and prayers. It first appears in Israel's song at the Red Sea, gathers in the closing psalms of the Psalter, and returns in Isaiah's songs of future salvation. Every "Hallelujah" in the Old Testament ends with it, because the word means "Praise Yah."

The name shows up at both extremes of life. It rings through Psalm 150's final shout, and it is also the name a man calls on from "the depths" of guilt (Psalm 130) or from a deathbed (Isaiah 38). One confession repeats across centuries: "The LORD is my strength and song, and He has become my salvation." That line moves from Moses to the psalmist to Isaiah, and Revelation develops the same pattern of deliverance and song when the redeemed sing "the song of Moses... and the song of the Lamb" in new words of praise. Yah is the God who saves, disciplines, defends, and receives the praise of everything that breathes.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâhh is the Hebrew short form of the divine name YHWH. It belongs almost entirely to poetry. Its uses fall in Exodus (the Song of the Sea and the altar after the battle with Amalek), in the Psalms, and in Isaiah. Hebrew poetry often favored short, intense forms, and this name has that quality. It is the name as a worshiper would cry it out or sing it.

The name also lives inside a word nearly every believer knows. Hallelu-yah joins the plural command "praise!" to "Yah." When the NKJV prints "Praise the LORD!" at the beginning or end of a psalm, the Hebrew behind it is usually this exclamation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Yah is a proper name, so its meaning is the Person it names. It identifies the God of Israel, the same God called Yahweh, in a shortened form suited to song, praise, and urgent prayer. It does not refer to a second deity or to a lesser title. It also carries no separate definition apart from the full name. One sense covers every occurrence.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The name is used in three main ways.

  • Praise. Most occurrences are calls to worship, especially "Praise Yah" at the opening or close of a psalm (Psalm 104:35; 106:1; 113:1; 135:1; 150:1, 6). Psalm 68:4 tells worshipers to extol God "by His name YAH."

  • Personal prayer and testimony. The psalmist calls on Yah in distress and reports what Yah did (Psalm 118:5, 17–18). The guilty pray to Him for mercy (Psalm 130:3), and the dying grieve at losing His presence (Isaiah 38:11).

  • Confession of trust. Isaiah joins the short and full names together, "YAH, the LORD" (Isaiah 12:2; 26:4), and so names God twice in a single phrase.

4. Theological Significance

The God of the Bible wants to be known by name and called on by name. Psalm 68:4 calls worshipers to rejoice before Him "by His name YAH," and the next verse says who this is: "A father of the fatherless, a defender of widows, Is God in His holy habitation" (68:5). The God who "rides on the clouds" is also the God who notices the orphan. The name carries His majesty and His care together.

The name also carries holiness, and the guilty feel it. "If You, LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand?" (Psalm 130:3). The psalmist speaks Yah's name with no illusions about himself, and he holds on to the next line: "But there is forgiveness with You, That You may be feared" (130:4). Forgiveness from Yah does not make people careless. It produces reverence, because a forgiven person knows whom he has been dealing with.

The "Hallelujah" psalms show that biblical praise is aimed at a particular Person with a particular history. Psalm 135 calls the people to "Praise the LORD, for the LORD is good," and grounds that call in His choice of His people: "For the LORD has chosen Jacob for Himself, Israel for His special treasure" (135:3–4). Praising Yah means remembering who He is and what He has done for His people.

Isaiah 26:4 gives that trust its footing: "Trust in the LORD forever, For in YAH, the LORD, is everlasting strength." The Hebrew pictures an everlasting rock. The verse comes right after the promise of perfect peace to the one whose mind is stayed on God (26:3). The command to trust forever rests on a God named twice over and described as a rock that does not wear away.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 15:2; Psalm 118:14; Isaiah 12:2 — one song across the centuries. On the far shore of the Red Sea, Moses and Israel sing, "The LORD is my strength and song, And He has become my salvation" (Exodus 15:2). The song celebrates what God did with the horse and rider (15:1) and declares, "The LORD is His name" (15:3). Centuries later, a worshiper surrounded by enemies uses the same words for his own rescue (Psalm 118:14). Isaiah then places them in the mouth of God's people on the future day of restoration: "For YAH, the LORD, is my strength and song; He also has become my salvation" (Isaiah 12:2). The same confession therefore covers the founding rescue of Israel, one believer's deliverance, and the hope of the age to come. In each case the singers do not say that God gave them strength or provided salvation. They say God has become those things to them. Yah Himself is the strength, the reason for the song, and the salvation.

Psalm 118:5, 17–18 — the God who rescues and disciplines. The psalmist says he called on Yah in distress and Yah answered and set him in a broad place (118:5). The same psalm also admits that Yah chastened him severely but did not give him over to death (118:18). The hand that disciplined him also held him back from the grave, and the discipline had a purpose. He declares that he will live and declare the works of Yah (118:17). The pain was measured, and it led to life and testimony. Hebrews 12:5–6 teaches the same truth in the language of a father's discipline.

Exodus 17:16 — Yah's throne and the defense of His people. After Amalek attacked Israel, Moses built an altar and spoke a solemn word. The NKJV renders it as the LORD having "sworn" that He will war with Amalek from generation to generation. The Hebrew behind it is literally "a hand upon the throne of Yah." The exact sense of that phrase is debated, but it plainly ties the attack on Israel to God's throne. Deuteronomy 25:17–19 adds that Amalek struck the weak and weary at the rear of the march. Yah treats an assault on the helpless among His people as an issue for His own throne.

Psalm 115:17–18 and Isaiah 38:11 — the God of the living. Psalm 115 says that the dead do not praise Yah, nor anyone who goes down into silence, but "we" will bless Him from this time forth and forevermore. Hezekiah, told he would die, felt this as personal grief: "I shall not see YAH, The LORD in the land of the living" (Isaiah 38:11). He grieved that he was "deprived of the remainder of my years" (38:10), and he grieved not only a shortened life but the loss of seeing Yah and praising Him among the living (38:11, 18–20). For him, life meant being in God's presence and praising Him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The anthem of Exodus 15 does not end in the Old Testament. In Revelation 15:3 the victors stand beside a sea of glass and "sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb." Their words in verses 3–4 do not repeat Exodus 15:2; they praise God's "great and marvelous" works and His "just and true" ways. Yet the pattern is the same: deliverance through judgment, answered by a song, and now the song belongs to the Lamb as well. In the end, the One who has "become my salvation" is known in Jesus Christ.

Psalm 118, which repeats that confession, runs straight into the Gospels. Jesus applies its "stone which the builders rejected" to Himself, and the crowds greet Him with its words about the One who comes in the name of the LORD. The psalmist was disciplined but not given over to death, and he entered the gates to give thanks to Yah (118:18–19). Read as an interpretive comparison, his rescue points ahead to the greater Son, who needed no correction for sin, yet went into death and came out of it alive. Psalm 68:18, another verse that uses this name, speaks of ascending on high and leading captivity captive, and Paul applies it to Christ's ascension in Ephesians 4:8.

The only "Hallelujahs" in the New Testament come in Revelation 19, where heaven rejoices over God's final judgment and the marriage of the Lamb. The Psalter closes with "Praise the LORD!" (Psalm 150:6), and that same cry reappears at the close of the whole biblical story.

7. Spirit & Life

Saying "Hallelujah" is an act of remembering. The word calls on a specific God: the One who threw the horse and rider into the sea, who answered a cry from a narrow place, who chastened without destroying, and who forgives so that He may be feared. Believers can let each "Praise the LORD!" in the Psalms send them back to those works of God. Praise that remembers in this way has real content.

The same short name fits Psalm 150 and Psalm 130. A believer may pray it at the peak of joy or from the bottom of guilt and fear. Yah is great enough to receive the praise of "everything that has breath" (150:6), and near enough that one forgiven sinner can wait for Him "more than those who watch for the morning" (130:6).

Psalm 118 also helps believers read their hardest seasons. The God who disciplines is not working against the God who delivers, because He is the same Yah. The psalmist's testimony was that Yah chastened him severely but did not give him over to death, so he lived to declare His works (118:17–18). That was his rescue, not a promise that every hard season ends in preserved earthly life; Scripture also records discipline that ended in death (1 Corinthians 11:30–32), and not every hardship is named as correction. What holds for every believer is that God governs His discipline and aims it at our good, "that we may be partakers of His holiness" (Hebrews 12:10).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 77:11 — In a sleepless season of doubt, the psalmist resolves to remember the works of Yah and His wonders of old. Memory becomes his way back to faith.

  • Psalm 89:8 — Yah is addressed as the LORD God of hosts, mighty beyond comparison and surrounded by His faithfulness.

  • Psalm 94:7, 12 — The wicked claim that Yah does not see. A few lines later, the psalm calls the man whom Yah instructs blessed. Some deny His attention, and others are trained by it.

  • Psalm 102:18 — The psalmist's deliverance is written down so that a people yet to be created will praise Yah. God intends praise to pass from one generation to the next.

  • Psalm 122:4 — The tribes go up to Jerusalem, the tribes of Yah, to give thanks to the name of the LORD.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3068 (YHWH, Yahweh) — Lexical relative. Yah is its shortened poetic form, and the two are joined in "YAH, the LORD" (Isaiah 12:2; 26:4).

  • H1984 (*halal*, "praise") — Lexical companion. Its plural command combines with Yah to form "Hallelujah" (Psalm 113:1; 150:6).
