AFTER, BEHIND, FOLLOW - (ʼachar, Strong's H0310)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼachar means "behind" or "after." In its simplest use it tells where someone stands, at the rear of another, or when something happens: "after these things," "afterward." Scripture uses it hundreds of times to move its stories forward, from the genealogies of Genesis to the reigns of the kings. Because the Bible so often pictures life as a walk, "behind" naturally comes to mean following. To walk after someone is to go where that person leads.

That simple picture carries great spiritual weight. Israel's faithfulness is described as following the Lord, and its unfaithfulness as going after other gods. Caleb followed the Lord fully, and Elisha left his oxen to walk behind Elijah. Two uses of "behind the back" reveal opposite hearts. Rebellious people cast God behind their backs, while God casts the sins of His people behind His own. The prophets also give "afterward" a hopeful edge. After judgment and restoration, God will pour out His Spirit on all flesh. The word keeps asking every reader the same question: whom are you walking behind?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼAchar is one of the Old Testament's most common words of position and time. It works as a preposition ("behind," "after"), as an adverb ("afterward"), and occasionally as a connecting word that the NKJV renders "inasmuch," "since," or "because" (Genesis 41:39; Ruth 2:11). Most of its uses are plain narrative sequencing, such as the repeated "After he begot..." of Genesis 5 or the "After" that opens many sections of Nehemiah 3. The theologically rich uses cluster in Deuteronomy, the books of Kings, and Jeremiah. There the language of walking becomes the language of loyalty.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Behind, at the rear. This sense describes physical position. Lot's wife looked back from behind Lot (Genesis 19:26), and the Angel of God moved behind Israel's camp (Exodus 14:19).

2. After, afterward. This sense describes sequence in time. For example, "after the death of Moses" (Joshua 1:1).

3. Following, going after. When the word is joined to verbs of walking or going, it describes one person following another, whether as a companion, a servant, a pursuer, or a worshiper (1 Kings 19:20; Deuteronomy 6:14).

4. What comes after, descendants. Sometimes the word points to the family that remains after a person. The NKJV renders it "posterity" or "remnant" (1 Kings 16:3; 14:10).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The most important pattern is the pairing of ʼachar with verbs such as "walk," "go," or "follow." Grammatically the phrase only says go behind. In context, however, it describes allegiance. When Elijah challenges the crowd on Mount Carmel, he puts the choice in these terms: if the Lord is God, follow Him, and if Baal, follow him (1 Kings 18:21). Israel could not walk behind two leaders at once.

The object of the word changes its tone. Going after the Lord is covenant faithfulness, a promise kept to the God who bound Himself to Israel (Deuteronomy 13:4). Going after "other gods" is betrayal (Deuteronomy 6:14). Going after an enemy is pursuit (2 Samuel 2:19). The phrase "behind the back" adds a further nuance. It describes deliberately putting something out of sight. Finally, "after you" often looks ahead to coming generations, as when God promises to be God to Abraham "and your descendants after you" (Genesis 17:7).

4. Theological Significance

Because the word measures both position and time, it shows two truths about God. The first is that God is owed the lead position. In Isaiah, the Lord declares that before Him no god was formed and none shall be after Him (Isaiah 43:10). No one comes before Him or after Him. Every rival that people follow is a pretender walking in a place that belongs to God alone.

The second truth is that God alone rules what comes after. Ecclesiastes feels the pain of this keenly. The Preacher hates his labor because he must leave it to the man who will come after him, and he cannot know whether that man will be wise or a fool (Ecclesiastes 2:18–19). He also asks who can tell a man what will happen after him under the sun (Ecclesiastes 6:12). Human beings act in the present, but the "after" lies outside their control.

Judgment makes this sobering. Against Jeroboam, God says, "I will take away the remnant of the house of Jeroboam, as one takes away refuse until it is all gone" (1 Kings 14:10). The very legacy a king labors to secure, the family that would follow him, is what God removes. Yet the same God binds Himself in grace to the generations after Abraham (Genesis 17:7) and to David's seed after him (2 Samuel 7:12). Whether a person's "afterward" holds hope or ruin rests in the Lord's hands.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Following fully or going after other gods (Numbers 14:24; Deuteronomy 13:4; Jeremiah 2:25). When ten spies spread fear, the Lord singled out Caleb. Caleb had a different spirit and had followed the Lord fully, so he would enter the land (Numbers 14:24). The phrase returns as a refrain in Caleb's story (Deuteronomy 1:36; Joshua 14:8, 9, 14). Moses later commands Israel to walk after the Lord their God, to fear Him, keep His commandments, and hold fast to Him (Deuteronomy 13:4). The same construction then describes the opposite. Solomon's fall is told in the very words of following: "For Solomon went after Ashtoreth the goddess of the Sidonians, and after Milcom the abomination of the Ammonites" (1 Kings 11:5). By Jeremiah's day the people say bluntly that they have loved foreign gods and will go after them (Jeremiah 2:25). In these passages no one stands still. Each person is walking behind something, and unfaithfulness begins when the heart changes whom it follows.

Two backs (1 Kings 14:9; Nehemiah 9:26; Ezekiel 23:35; Isaiah 38:17). God's charge against Jeroboam is that he made other gods "and have cast Me behind your back" (1 Kings 14:9). Nehemiah's prayer confesses that Israel cast God's law behind their backs (Nehemiah 9:26), and Ezekiel brings the same accusation (Ezekiel 23:35). To put God behind you is to remove Him from your sight so that you can look at something else. Hezekiah, delivered from death, uses the same image in the opposite direction: "You have lovingly delivered my soul from the pit of corruption, For You have cast all my sins behind Your back" (Isaiah 38:17). The contrast is striking. Rebellious people throw the glorious God behind them and chase their sins. God, in mercy, throws those sins behind Himself and turns His face toward the person.

Elisha's call (1 Kings 19:20–21). When Elijah threw his mantle on him, Elisha "left the oxen and ran after Elijah." He asked only to say goodbye to his parents, and then he slaughtered the oxen and burned the equipment to cook them, ending his old livelihood. "Then he arose and followed Elijah, and became his servant." Walking behind the prophet meant serving and learning from him, and eventually succeeding him. Following here describes an entire life given over to another's leading.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets turn "afterward" into a promise. Hosea foresees Israel returning: afterward the children of Israel will seek the Lord their God and David their king in the latter days (Hosea 3:5). Jeremiah's promise of the new covenant is set "after those days." In it, God will write His law on His people's hearts (Jeremiah 31:33). Joel crowns the pattern: "And it shall come to pass afterward That I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh" (Joel 2:28). Israel's long record of going after other gods does not have the last word. God's "afterward" does.

At Pentecost, Peter announced that Joel's afterward had arrived, rendering it "in the last days" (Acts 2:17). Hebrews applies Jeremiah's "after those days" to the covenant Christ mediates (Hebrews 8:8–12). The seed God promised to raise up after David (2 Samuel 7:12) is fulfilled in Jesus, the Son of David.

The picture of following reaches its fullness in Jesus as well. Elisha ran after Elijah. Jesus calls fishermen with the words "Follow Me, and I will make you become fishers of men" (Mark 1:17). Mark's Greek wording literally means come after Me. Like Elisha, they leave their livelihood on the spot. What Caleb did wholeheartedly and Israel failed to do, the Spirit poured out "afterward" now makes possible in those who walk behind Christ.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah gives a tender picture of what following the Lord feels like from the inside: "Your ears shall hear a word behind you, saying, 'This is the way, walk in it,' Whenever you turn to the right hand Or whenever you turn to the left" (Isaiah 30:21). The verse places God's voice behind the one walking. A believer does not always see the road ahead, but the Lord is close enough to correct the next step. Guidance comes as the believer walks, and often at the very moment of turning aside.

David describes the same nearness in Psalm 63:8. His soul follows close behind God, and God's right hand holds him up. Following brings the follower near to the One he follows.

The two backs of Section 5 ask something searching of every heart. Is there anything I keep out of sight so I can look at something else? Some people put God's word behind them because it unsettles them. The gospel answers that God has already put behind His back the very sins that make us want to hide. People run from a God whose face they fear, and the person who knows his sins are forgiven no longer needs to.

Finally, the word teaches how to live with an unknown future. The Preacher could not see what would come after him. The psalmist, who had nearly stumbled over the prosperity of the wicked, could say that God would guide him with His counsel and afterward receive him to glory (Psalm 73:24). The believer may not know tomorrow, but he knows who owns the afterward.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 19:26 — Lot's wife looked back from behind him and became a pillar of salt. Jesus later told His hearers to remember her (Luke 17:32).

  • Exodus 14:19 — The Angel of God and the pillar of cloud moved from the front of Israel's camp to the rear. God stood between His people and the pursuing Egyptians.

  • Ruth 1:16 — Ruth refused to turn back from following Naomi. Her loyalty to Naomi became loyalty to Naomi's God.

  • 1 Kings 18:21 — Elijah demands that Israel decide whom it will follow, the Lord or Baal. No one can walk behind both.

  • Psalm 73:24 — God guides with His counsel and "afterward" receives His servant to glory.

  • Genesis 17:7 — God promises to be God to Abraham and to his descendants after him. This is a great promise that reaches into the "afterward."
