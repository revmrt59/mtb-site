JOBAB - (Yôwbâb, Strong's H3103)

Summary

Jobab is a Hebrew name shared by at least five different men in the Old Testament. One is a son of Joktan in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:29). Another is an early king of Edom from the city of Bozrah (Genesis 36:33–34). A third is the king of Madon who joined Jabin of Hazor against Joshua (Joshua 11:1). The last two are Benjamites named in the Chronicler's family records (1 Chronicles 8:9, 18). The meaning of the name is uncertain. Some lexicons connect it with a root meaning "to call loudly" or "cry out," and others suggest an Arabic word related to "desert." Neither proposal is secure enough to build on, and none of these men is explained by his name.

The two most developed figures show God at work among the nations. Jobab of Edom ruled "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). Esau's family had kings and capitals before Jacob's line had any king, though God had promised that kings would come from Jacob. Jobab's city of Bozrah later became the prophets' picture of God's judgment on proud Edom. Jobab of Madon stood in a northern coalition "as many people as the sand that is on the seashore," yet the LORD told Joshua, "Do not be afraid because of them" (Joshua 11:4, 6). An ancient Greek tradition went further and identified the Edomite Jobab with Job. That tradition is part of how the name was later read, but Scripture itself does not make the identification.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Strong's number H3103 names a lemma, not a single person. The nine Hebrew occurrences describe five separate men who should not be merged:

1. Jobab son of Joktan, a descendant of Shem through Eber (Genesis 10:29; 1 Chronicles 1:23).

2. Jobab son of Zerah of Bozrah, the second named king of Edom (Genesis 36:33–34; 1 Chronicles 1:44–45).

3. Jobab king of Madon, a Canaanite ruler in the north during Joshua's conquest (Joshua 11:1).

4. Jobab son of Shaharaim, a Benjamite born to Hodesh in the country of Moab (1 Chronicles 8:8–9).

5. Jobab son of Elpaal, a Benjamite in the family groups that lived in Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 8:18, 28).

The meaning of Yôwbâb is uncertain. One proposal connects it with a Hebrew root for calling out loudly or shrilly. Another links it to an Arabic word associated with desert land. Both are scholarly suggestions, not settled facts, and the Bible offers no wordplay or explanation of the name. It is wiser to say plainly that we do not know what the name meant than to treat "howling" or "desert" as the key to any of these lives.

Two related names need to be kept separate. Job (H347) is a different Hebrew name with its own Strong's entry, even though an ancient tradition linked him to Jobab (see Section 4). Madon and Bozrah are place names attached to two of these men. They are indexed separately and are not further occurrences of Jobab.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Jobab of Edom (Genesis 36:31–34; 1 Chronicles 1:44–45). Genesis 36 traces Esau's descendants and then lists "the kings who reigned in the land of Edom before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). First came Bela son of Beor, whose city was Dinhabah. Then, "when Bela died, Jobab the son of Zerah of Bozrah reigned in his place" (36:33). After him, "Husham of the land of the Temanites reigned in his place" (36:34). The kings come from different towns, and none is said to be the son of the king before him, so Edom's throne does not seem to have passed from father to son. Genesis also names a Zerah among Esau's grandsons (36:13, 17). The text never says whether he is Jobab's father, so that link should stay open. Jobab's city, Bozrah, was an important town in Edom. It returns much later in the prophets.

Jobab of Madon (Joshua 11:1–12). After Joshua's victories in the south, "when Jabin king of Hazor heard these things, … he sent to Jobab king of Madon, to the king of Shimron, to the king of Achshaph" (Joshua 11:1), and to many other peoples of the north (11:2–3). Madon was a royal city in northern Canaan. Its exact site is uncertain, though it was probably in the region west of the Sea of Galilee. The allied armies gathered "at the waters of Merom to fight against Israel" (11:5). They came "as many people as the sand that is on the seashore in multitude, with very many horses and chariots" (11:4). Against this force the LORD gave Joshua a promise: "Do not be afraid because of them, for tomorrow about this time I will deliver all of them slain before Israel. You shall hamstring their horses and burn their chariots with fire" (11:6). Israel struck the coalition at Merom (11:7–9). Joshua then took the cities of these kings and struck their kings with the sword (11:12). The king of Madon appears again in the list of defeated kings (12:19).

Jobab son of Joktan (Genesis 10:29; 1 Chronicles 1:23). Jobab is the last of Joktan's thirteen sons. Their territory stretched "from Mesha as you go toward Sephar, the mountain of the east" (Genesis 10:30), usually understood as the Arabian region. Joktan's brother Peleg carried the line that leads to Abraham (Genesis 11:16–26). This Jobab belongs to a related branch of Shem's family that the story then leaves behind.

The two Benjamites (1 Chronicles 8:8–18). The Chronicler records that Shaharaim "had children in the country of Moab, after he had sent away Hushim and Baara his wives," and that by Hodesh he fathered Jobab and his brothers. These sons are counted as "heads of their fathers' houses" (8:8–10). Scripture does not say when or why the family lived in Moab. Elimelech's family in Ruth 1 shows that Israelites sometimes did settle there. A second Jobab is listed among the sons of Elpaal (8:18), and Elpaal is named as Shaharaim's son by Hushim (8:11–12). This Jobab belongs to the family heads who "dwelt in Jerusalem" (8:28). The relationships in this chapter are hard to untangle, so the family connection between the two Benjamite Jobabs should be held loosely.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Genesis says plainly that Edom had kings before Israel did (36:31). That sentence sits in a book where God had promised Abraham and Jacob that kings would come from them (Genesis 17:6; 35:11), and where the LORD had said before the twins were born that the older would serve the younger (25:23). Read in that setting, Jobab's reign is a quiet reminder that the line God had chosen did not get its throne first. Esau's family settled, organized, and crowned kings in Seir. Jacob's line would wait much longer for its own king. Genesis does not say exactly when Jobab reigned, only that it was before Israel had a king. Scripture never treats this as a failure of God's word. Edom received what God gave it, and Israel's kingship came on God's schedule, eventually in David. It may also reflect Isaac's words that Esau would live by his sword (27:40), though Genesis does not draw that connection itself.

Joshua 11 states directly that the LORD gave the northern kings into Israel's hand (11:6, 8). It adds a striking explanation: "it was of the LORD to harden their hearts, that they should come against Israel in battle" (11:20). From this it is fair to infer that the alliance Jobab joined, which was meant to overwhelm Israel, became the means of its own downfall. By marching out of their walled cities together, these kings brought the battle to one place. What looked like the enemy's strength served God's purpose, though the victory at Merom did not end the campaign: Joshua still took the cities, and he "made war a long time with all those kings" (11:18).

The Benjamite Jobabs are small entries, but they matter. After the exile the Chronicler carefully preserved Benjamin's families, including a household with roots in Moab. These records show that God kept His people as named families, even when their histories were scattered and complicated.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Bozrah and the Divine Warrior. Jobab is the first person Scripture connects with Bozrah, and the city becomes a significant prophetic name. Isaiah declares, "the LORD has a sacrifice in Bozrah, and a great slaughter in the land of Edom" (Isaiah 34:6). Jeremiah also announces Bozrah's ruin (Jeremiah 49:13, 22). Isaiah 63:1 then pictures One coming from Edom, "with dyed garments from Bozrah," who has trodden the winepress of judgment alone. Revelation draws on this imagery when it describes Christ returning in a robe dipped in blood and treading the winepress of God's wrath (Revelation 19:13–15). Jobab himself is not a type of anything. The city where Edom's early royal power took root became, in the prophets, the place where God's final judgment on proud human power is pictured. Obadiah's closing line states where that story ends: the kingdom will be the LORD's (Obadiah 21).

Kings against the LORD. The coalition at Merom fits a pattern Scripture states openly in Psalm 2, where the kings of the earth set themselves against the LORD and His Anointed and God laughs at their plans. The early church applied that psalm to Herod, Pilate, and the nations gathered against Jesus (Acts 4:25–28). Joshua 11 is not quoted in those passages, so the link is a thematic resonance rather than an explicit fulfillment. Still, the resemblance is real: a great alliance, horses and chariots, and a word from God that makes it powerless. It is also worth noting that Joshua and Jesus carry the same name.

The Septuagint and Job. The Greek translation of Job adds a postscript after Job 42:17 that is not found in the Hebrew text. The NKJV ends simply, "So Job died, old and full of days." The Greek addition claims that Job was formerly called Jobab, lived in the land of Ausitis on the borders of Edom and Arabia, and was the son of Zare (Zerah). This identifies him with the Edomite king of Genesis 36. Ancient readers probably reasoned from real details in Scripture: Job's home was Uz, which Lamentations 4:21 connects with Edom, and his friend Eliphaz was a Temanite (Job 2:11), while Jobab's successor came from the land of the Temanites. The Hebrew names are different, however, and the Hebrew Bible never makes the connection. It is best treated as an early piece of interpretation, not as biblical history.

5. Spirit & Life

Genesis 36 can look like a list of names to skip, but it records something believers recognize. Others get their kingdom early. Esau's family had crowns and cities while Jacob's family held only a promise. God did not hurry to fix that, and He did not forget His word either. When the world seems settled and successful while God's promises feel slow, the brief notice of Jobab's reign is a reminder that coming first is not the same as lasting. The throne God promised came through David's line to Christ, whose kingdom has no end, while Edom's kings are remembered mainly as a list.

Joshua 11 speaks to fear. Israel faced numbers they could not count and weapons they did not have. God's answer was not a strategy session but a promise and a command: do not be afraid, and burn the chariots. Israel was not even allowed to keep the horses and chariots for itself, so it could not trust them (compare Psalm 20:7). The same God still calls His people to measure threats by His word rather than by the size of what is coming against them.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:29–30; 1 Chronicles 1:23 — Jobab son of Joktan in the Table of Nations and his people's territory.

  • Genesis 36:31–34; 1 Chronicles 1:44–45 — Jobab son of Zerah of Bozrah, king of Edom before Israel had a king.

  • Genesis 25:23; 35:11 — The promises that set Edom's early kingship in context.

  • Joshua 11:1–12, 20; 12:19 — Jobab of Madon in Jabin's coalition, the LORD's promise, and the defeat at Merom.

  • 1 Chronicles 8:8–10 — Jobab son of Shaharaim, born in Moab, head of a father's house.

  • 1 Chronicles 8:18, 28 — Jobab son of Elpaal among Benjamite families in Jerusalem.

  • Isaiah 34:6; 63:1–6; Jeremiah 49:13, 22 — Bozrah as the prophetic scene of God's judgment on Edom.

  • Revelation 19:13–15; Psalm 2; Acts 4:25–28 — Later canonical development of the Divine Warrior and of nations gathered against the LORD's Anointed.

  • Job 42:17 — The end of Job in the Hebrew text, where the Greek Septuagint adds its Jobab tradition.
