DAY, TODAY, DAYS - (yôwm, Strong's H3117)

Summary

The Hebrew word yôwm means "day." It can name a full day, evening and morning together, the hours of daylight, or "today." It can also stretch to cover a lifetime ("all the days of my life"), an era ("in the days of the judges"), or a habit ("day by day"). Context decides which one is meant, and the Old Testament uses the word in every one of its books.

Scripture uses this ordinary word to show that time belongs to God. He named the first Day, set apart the seventh, and appointed days for meeting Him and for cleansing from sin. Through Moses He pressed His people to answer Him "today." The prophets warned of a coming "day of the LORD" when He would judge pride and save His people. Human days are short, but they are held inside God's purposes, and they move toward His appointed Day. That is why every believer's "today" carries weight.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yôwm is a common Hebrew noun. It appears in creation, law, worship calendars, royal histories, laments, proverbs, and prophecy. Some of its English renderings in the NKJV, such as "chronicles," "always," "forever," "daily," and "lifetime," come from Hebrew idioms built on the word. "The book of the chronicles of the kings" is literally a record of "the events of the days." The English reader often meets yôwm without seeing the word "day" at all.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is a day. Its principal senses are:

1. A day or daylight: the evening-and-morning cycle, or the light as distinct from night. "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night" (Genesis 1:5).

2. Today, this day: the present moment of speaking or acting, as in "Today, if you will hear His voice" (Psalm 95:7).

3. A period, era, or lifespan: usually in the plural, as in a person's "days," "the days of" a king, or the whole creation period summarized as "the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens" (Genesis 2:4).

4. An appointed or decisive day: a set occasion such as the Day of Atonement, or the prophetic "day of the LORD."

Idioms add further shades. "Day by day" means daily, and "all the days" means always.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Law, yôwm sets the calendar: six days of work, the seventh for rest, feast days, and set periods of purification (Leviticus 23). In the historical books it measures reigns and eras. Wisdom literature uses the plural "days" to describe how short life is (Job 14:1; Psalm 39:4–5).

Two constructions matter for interpretation. The first is "today." Deuteronomy uses it again and again to bring the hearers into the present moment of decision. The second is "the day of the LORD." The prophets use it for a specific divine intervention, so the expression points to an event rather than to a calendar date. The related phrase "in that day" is ordinary time language. In prophetic contexts it often points back to the intervention just described, but it does not always mean the final Day. The prophets' historical judgments and God's last Day stay distinct, even as the earlier ones foreshadow the last.

4. Theological Significance

God is Lord of time. Genesis 1 pictures Him ordering the days before filling them, and He gave time a shape: days, Sabbaths, seasons, and appointed feasts. He did not leave time as an endless, meaningless loop. It runs in a direction He has chosen.

He also built grace into the calendar. On the Day of Atonement, the high priest brought sacrifice so that "on that day" the people would be cleansed from all their sins before the LORD (Leviticus 16:30; 23:27–28). God did not wait for Israel to think of a way back to Him. He fixed a day and supplied the means.

Scripture sets the shortness of human days beside God's permanence. Man is born of woman, Job says, short-lived and full of trouble. God has set the number of his days (Job 14:1, 5). The psalmist describes his days as a shadow that lengthens and as grass that withers (Psalm 102:11; 103:15). That frailty leads to a prayer: "So teach us to number our days, That we may gain a heart of wisdom" (Psalm 90:12). Wisdom starts with admitting that our days are limited and that God is not.

Against that frailty stands God's faithfulness across "all the days." He promised Joshua that no one would stand against him all the days of his life (Joshua 1:5). David expected goodness and mercy to follow him all the days of his life, and he expected to dwell in the LORD's house for length of days (Psalm 23:6). God does not attend to His people only in certain seasons.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 1–2 and Exodus 20: the shape of time. The first thing God names is the light, and He calls it "Day" (Genesis 1:5). The account then counts days until "on the seventh day God ended His work... and He rested" (Genesis 2:2). God "blessed the seventh day and sanctified it" (Genesis 2:3). Sinai ties Israel's week directly to this pattern: "For in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth... and rested the seventh day. Therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day and hallowed it" (Exodus 20:11). The command reaches servants, foreigners, and even cattle (Exodus 20:10). God wove rest and worship into the week itself, and no one in the household was left out of it.

Exodus 16: day by day. God sent manna and told the people to gather a certain quota every day "that I may test them, whether they will walk in My law or not" (Exodus 16:4, paraphrased). The phrase is literally "the portion of a day in its day." Manna kept overnight spoiled, except on the eve of the Sabbath. God could have stocked Israel for a year. He chose to feed them one morning at a time, so that each day began with trust in Him.

Deuteronomy and Psalm 95: the urgency of "today." On the plains of Moab, Moses keeps returning to one word. He tells the people that those who held fast to the LORD "are alive today, every one of you" (Deuteronomy 4:4). He summons them to hear the statutes he speaks today (5:1). Near the end, he says he has set before them today life and good, death and evil, and he urges them to choose life (30:15–19, paraphrased). The people's response to God could not be postponed to some later generation. Psalm 95 picks up the same word for later worshipers: "Today, if you will hear His voice" (Psalm 95:7). It then warns them by recalling the wilderness generation, which hardened its heart.

The prophets: the day of the LORD. In the prophets, yôwm comes to name God's decisive intervention in history. Isaiah writes, "For the day of the LORD of hosts Shall come upon everything proud and lofty... And it shall be brought low" (Isaiah 2:12). Joel cries, "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is at hand; It shall come as destruction from the Almighty" (Joel 1:15). Amos corrects those who looked forward to that day as if it would only vindicate Israel. He tells them it will be darkness and not light (Amos 5:18–20). Belonging to God's people gave no one immunity. Zephaniah calls for hushed awe, "Be silent in the presence of the Lord GOD; For the day of the LORD is at hand" (Zephaniah 1:7). He then piles up "day" after "day" in a single verse: a day of wrath, trouble, distress, devastation, darkness, and clouds (1:15).

The same Day also holds salvation. Zechariah sees the LORD Himself going out to fight for Jerusalem and becoming King over all the earth "in that day" (Zechariah 14:1–9). Malachi describes the coming day "burning like an oven" for the proud (Malachi 4:1). In the next breath, for those who fear God's name, he promises that the Sun of Righteousness will rise with healing in His wings (4:2). He also promises that Elijah will be sent before the great and dreadful day (4:5). Whether that Day brings ruin or rescue depends on where a person stands with God. History is moving toward a Day God has set, and pride will not survive it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament takes up all of these threads. Jesus read Isaiah 61 in Nazareth and stopped after "the acceptable year of the LORD," leaving out "the day of vengeance of our God" (Luke 4:18–19). His first coming opened an age of favor, and the day of reckoning still lies ahead. Paul quotes Isaiah 49:8 and announces that now is the day of salvation (2 Corinthians 6:2). The apostles also kept the prophets' warning: "the day of the Lord so comes as a thief in the night" (1 Thessalonians 5:2; 2 Peter 3:10).

The annual Day of Atonement had to be repeated every year. Hebrews sets it beside Christ, who offered Himself once at the end of the ages to put away sin (Hebrews 9:25–28; 10:11–14). Jeremiah's "the days are coming" (Jeremiah 23:5; 31:31) looked ahead to a righteous Branch from David and a new covenant, and Hebrews 8:8 declares that promise taken up in Christ. Psalm 2:7's royal "Today I have begotten You" is applied to Jesus' resurrection and sonship (Acts 13:33; Hebrews 1:5). Even the Sabbath rest and the "all the days" of God's presence point forward. Jesus promised, "I am with you always, even to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20), and the city of Revelation has "no night there" (Revelation 21:25).

7. Spirit & Life

The "today" of Deuteronomy and the "that day" of the prophets belong together. God has a Day coming, so how we answer Him now cannot be treated as small. Hebrews 3 quotes Psalm 95, "Today, if you will hear His voice, Do not harden your hearts" (Hebrews 3:7–8). It then tells believers to exhort one another daily while it is still called "Today" (3:13). The Holy Spirit speaks in the present tense. Yesterday's responsiveness cannot stand in for today's, and no one is promised tomorrow (Proverbs 27:1).

Numbering our days keeps us from both despair and presumption. A believer who knows his days are few lets go of the idea that he controls them. He finds rest in the God who has counted them (Psalm 139:16). The manna shows how that trust works in practice. God still gives grace the way He gave bread, enough for this day. Jesus taught His disciples to ask for daily bread and called them to take up the cross daily (Matthew 6:11; Luke 9:23). The Lord "daily loads us with benefits" (Psalm 68:19), and each morning is a fresh chance to receive them and to obey.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:17: God warns that death would follow "in the day" Adam ate of the forbidden tree. From the start, a single day carries the weight of a moral choice.

  • Deuteronomy 33:25: Moses blesses Asher with strength that will match his days. It is first a tribal blessing, and believers have rightly drawn comfort from it as a picture of God's sufficiency for every stage of life.

  • Psalm 84:10: One day in God's courts is better than a thousand elsewhere. The value of a day is measured by nearness to God.

  • Psalm 118:24: The psalmist rejoices in the day the LORD has made. He receives the day of deliverance as God's own work.

  • Hosea 6:2: Israel hopes that God will revive them after two days and raise them up on the third. The hope is fixed on God's timing of restoration.
