JAVAN - (Yâvân, Strong's H3120)

Summary

Javan is first a man and then a people. Genesis names him as the fourth-listed son of Japheth and a grandson of Noah. His descendants spread through the "coastlands," the islands and shores of the Mediterranean, and in later Scripture his name stands for the Greek world. The name is the Hebrew equivalent of the Greek word for the Ionians, the Greeks of the Aegean coast and islands who were the first Greeks most Near Eastern peoples met. Scripture does not explain what the name originally meant, and its underlying meaning is unknown. What is clear is that the name identifies the Greek people. The NKJV therefore usually reads "Javan" in the genealogies and the prophets, but "Greece" in Daniel and Zechariah.

Across the Old Testament, Javan appears in four roles. It is a distant branch of the human family in Genesis and a merchant nation that bartered "human lives" with Tyre in Ezekiel. Daniel shows it as a coming empire that would shatter Persia, and Zechariah pictures it as an enemy against whom God would raise up the sons of Zion. Yet Isaiah's final chapter promises that God will send messengers to "Tubal and Javan," far-off peoples who "have not heard My fame nor seen My glory." The nation that once trafficked in God's people is named as a destination for the news of His glory. That promise begins to unfold in the New Testament, when the gospel of "Christ crucified" goes out to "both Jews and Greeks" (1 Corinthians 1:23–24).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The lemma Yâvân (יָוָן) names a man and the people descended from him. The individual Javan is a son of Japheth, listed with his brothers Gomer, Magog, Madai, Tubal, Meshech, and Tiras (Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5). His sons are Elishah, Tarshish, Kittim, and Dodanim (Genesis 10:4). First Chronicles 1:7 has Rodanim in place of Dodanim, a common spelling variation in the genealogies. In later occurrences, Javan usually means the people and lands descended from this ancestor, which is the Greek world. Ezekiel 27:19 is a debated exception, discussed below.

The name corresponds to the Greek Iaōn, "Ionian." The Ionians settled the western coast of Asia Minor and the Aegean islands, and their name came to stand for Greeks generally among Israel's neighbors. Assyrian and Persian sources used similar forms. So "Javan" is a genuine ethnic and geographic name. It was not a symbolic label invented by the biblical writers. The Bible gives no wordplay or explanation of the name, and no reliable meaning for the word itself has been established. Readers should treat the name's meaning as unknown and its identification with the Ionians and Greeks as secure.

One occurrence raises a separate question. Ezekiel 27:19 lists "Dan and Javan" among Tyre's trading partners, together with goods such as cassia and cane that suggest Arabian trade routes. Some translations read the first name differently, and some interpreters think this Javan may be a trading settlement in Arabia rather than Greece itself. The text does not settle the matter.

A related name is indexed separately. Yevani (Strong's H3125), "Greeks" or "sons of the Javanites," appears in Joel 3:6. In the New Testament, the Greek words for "Greek" (G1672) and "Greece" (G1671) carry the same people forward. These are connected names. They are not additional occurrences of H3120.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The coastland peoples. In the Table of Nations, Javan's line becomes the family from which the coastland peoples of the Gentiles spread out by land, language, and clan (Genesis 10:4–5). Several of his sons reappear in later Scripture. Tarshish becomes a byword for the farthest western seaport, the place Jonah tried to flee to (Jonah 1:3). Kittim, usually linked with Cyprus, shows up in Balaam's oracle that "ships shall come from the coasts of Cyprus" (Numbers 24:24) and again in Daniel 11:30. From Israel's point of view, Javan's family lived at the edges of the known world, across the sea.

Merchants of human lives. By Ezekiel's day, Javan was a commercial partner of Tyre: "Javan, Tubal, and Meshech were your traders. They bartered human lives and vessels of bronze for your merchandise" (Ezekiel 27:13). The phrase "human lives" means slaves. Joel makes the cost painfully personal. Using the related name Yevani, he charges Tyre, Sidon, and Philistia: "Also the people of Judah and the people of Jerusalem You have sold to the Greeks, That you may remove them far from their borders" (Joel 3:6). Sons and daughters of Jerusalem were shipped to Greek markets as merchandise, and God promised to repay the sellers (Joel 3:7–8).

The coming empire. Daniel lifts Javan onto the stage of world empires. In the vision of the ram and the goat, a male goat races from the west without touching the ground and shatters the two-horned ram (Daniel 8:5–7). Gabriel explains it plainly: "And the male goat is the kingdom of Greece. The large horn that is between its eyes is the first king" (Daniel 8:21). That horn is broken at the height of its strength, and four lesser horns rise in its place (Daniel 8:8, 22). History matches this closely with Alexander the Great and the division of his empire among his successors, though Daniel does not name him. Daniel 11:2 adds that a wealthy fourth Persian king "shall stir up all against the realm of Greece," and a mighty Greek king follows (11:3–4). Many interpreters also see Greece as the third kingdom in Nebuchadnezzar's statue, the kingdom of bronze (Daniel 2:39), and in the leopard of Daniel 7:6. This is a reasonable reading, but those chapters do not name Javan.

The prince of Greece. Daniel 10 pulls back the curtain further. A heavenly messenger tells Daniel he was delayed by "the prince of the kingdom of Persia" (10:13) and adds: "And now I must return to fight with the prince of Persia; and when I have gone forth, indeed the prince of Greece will come" (Daniel 10:20). These princes are not human kings. They are spiritual powers hostile to God's messenger, connected in some way with the empires that bore their names.

Sons of Zion against sons of Greece. After the exile, Zechariah hears God declare: "For I have bent Judah, My bow, Fitted the bow with Ephraim, And raised up your sons, O Zion, Against your sons, O Greece, And made you like the sword of a mighty man" (Zechariah 9:13). The Lord Himself appears over His people and defends them (9:14–15). Many interpreters see a fulfillment in the second-century BC Jewish struggle against the Greek Seleucid kings. The passage also looks beyond any single battle.

Javan hears of God's glory. Isaiah's closing vision has God gathering "all nations and tongues" to see His glory (Isaiah 66:18). He then sends survivors outward: "and those among them who escape I will send to the nations: to Tarshish and Pul and Lud, who draw the bow, and Tubal and Javan, to the coastlands afar off who have not heard My fame nor seen My glory. And they shall declare My glory among the Gentiles" (Isaiah 66:19).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Several things are stated plainly in Scripture. Javan belongs to the one human family descended from Noah. God foretold Greece's rise, its conquest of Persia, and the breaking of its first king's power long before these events happened (Daniel 8:21–22; 11:2–4). God sees and judges the selling of His people (Joel 3:6–8). He will defend Zion against the sons of Greece (Zechariah 9:13–15). And He intends His glory to be declared to Javan (Isaiah 66:19).

From these statements, a larger picture can be drawn with care. Javan never stands outside God's rule. Even at its most powerful, its empire is a goat whose great horn snaps "when he became strong" (paraphrasing Daniel 8:8). Daniel teaches that God sets the rise and fall of kingdoms, and the book records Greece's glory in a way that also shows its limits.

Daniel 10:13 and 20 describe the messenger's struggle with the prince of Persia, and verse 20 announces that "the prince of Greece will come." Reading Greece's prince as another hostile spiritual power is well grounded in the passage, including verse 21, but it is an inference; the text does not narrate a past attack by Greece. Scripture does not describe how they operate or give a detailed map of territorial spirits, so it is wise not to build more on this verse than it says. What it plainly shows is that the conflicts of nations have an unseen dimension, and that heaven's messengers keep working while the struggle goes on.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never quotes the Javan passages directly, but Javan's story flows naturally into the gospel. The Greeks are the heirs of Javan, and the New Testament is full of them, though there "Greek" can mean any Greek-speaking Gentile, not only someone of Greek descent.

Zechariah 9 is an important link. The same chapter that sets the sons of Zion against the sons of Greece opens with Zion's King coming "lowly and riding on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9), a prophecy the Gospels apply to Jesus' entry into Jerusalem (Matthew 21:4–5). The victory Zechariah promises is tied to that humble King. In the New Testament, the conflict with the Greek world becomes a contest of message rather than of swords: "For Jews request a sign, and Greeks seek after wisdom; but we preach Christ crucified, to the Jews a stumbling block and to the Greeks foolishness" (1 Corinthians 1:22–23). Paul is not reading Zechariah allegorically here. Still, the connection fits the canon well. Javan's long confidence in human wisdom meets a crucified Messiah, and the wisdom of God wins. The opposition does not end in destruction, either: "to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24).

Isaiah 66:19 comes to life as the apostles carry the message to the Greek world. Greeks come to Philip asking to see Jesus (John 12:20–21). Paul is called over to Macedonia (Acts 16:9) and preaches in Athens (Acts 17). He describes the gospel as power "for the Jew first and also for the Greek" (Romans 1:16), and he declares that in Christ "there is neither Jew nor Greek" (Galatians 3:28). People who "have not heard My fame nor seen My glory" now see God's glory in the face of Christ.

History adds a further observation that Scripture does not make directly. The Greek conquests spread a common Greek language across the eastern Mediterranean, and that language became the language of the New Testament. By the apostles' day Alexander's empire had long since divided and Rome ruled, but the Greek-speaking world that empire created became a setting for the gospel.

5. Spirit & Life

Javan's story moves from trafficking to grace. Ezekiel remembers Javan bartering "human lives," and Joel remembers Judah's children sold to Greek buyers. Yet when Isaiah lists the far nations God will reach, Javan is named. However one identifies the messengers in Isaiah 66:19, God's answer to a people who treated human beings as cargo includes sending them the news of His glory. Joel shows that God does not ignore injustice, and He repays it (Joel 3:7–8). Isaiah shows that His purposes for the nations reach beyond repayment to mercy. The gospel later went out exactly this way, to people who had nothing in their record to recommend them.

Daniel also gives perspective to anyone who feels small before powerful forces. The Greek empire looked unstoppable, and an unseen prince stood behind it. Yet God had already described its rise and its fall, and His messenger was still at work on Daniel's behalf (Daniel 10:12, 20). Believers today face both visible and spiritual opposition (Ephesians 6:12). They are not left to guess how the story ends.

Zechariah adds that God works through His people. He bends Judah as His bow and makes Zion "like the sword of a mighty man." Under the New Testament, that strength takes the form of preaching Christ crucified, a message the wise call foolishness and God uses to save Greeks as well as Jews.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:2, 4–5; 1 Chronicles 1:5, 7 – Javan as son of Japheth and father of the coastland peoples. These verses establish his place in the human family.

  • Ezekiel 27:13, 19 – Javan as a merchant nation trading "human lives" and bronze with Tyre. Interpreters differ over which people or place verse 19 names.

  • Joel 3:6 (related name, H3125) – Judeans sold "to the Greeks," and God's promise of repayment.

  • Daniel 8:21–22; 11:2–4 – Greece named as the goat-kingdom whose first king's power is broken and divided, in prophecy given before the events.

  • Daniel 10:20 – the "prince of Greece," revealing spiritual opposition behind earthly empires.

  • Zechariah 9:9, 13 – Zion's humble King and Zion's sons raised against the sons of Greece.

  • Isaiah 66:19 – God's glory to be declared to Javan and the distant coastlands.

  • 1 Corinthians 1:22–24; John 12:20–21; Romans 1:16 – Greeks meeting the crucified Christ as God's power and wisdom.
