DOVE, PIGEON - (yôwnâh, Strong's H3123)

Summary

The Hebrew word yôwnâh simply means dove or pigeon. It names the gentle, ordinary bird that Israelites saw nesting in rock cliffs, heard cooing in the valleys, and brought to the tabernacle when they could not afford a lamb. Scripture draws on what everyone knew about doves: their soft mourning call, their quick and nervous flight, their habit of hiding in clefts of rock, and their instinct to fly home.

The bird carries a great deal of meaning in the Bible. Noah's dove first came back without finding rest, then returned with an olive leaf that told Noah the waters had receded, and on her third release did not return at all. In Leviticus, God allowed pigeons in place of a lamb so that the poor could still come near Him. The Song of Solomon uses "my dove" as a lover's name for undivided devotion. The prophets used the dove's call to describe sorrow over sin. Hosea used its flight in two ways: first for Israel's foolish fluttering between nations, then for its trembling return home when God calls. These threads come together in the New Testament. When Mary and Joseph presented the infant Jesus at the temple, they brought the poor family's offering of birds, turtledoves or young pigeons, and at His baptism the Spirit of God descended "like a dove" and rested on Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yôwnâh is the ordinary Hebrew name for the dove or pigeon. These birds were common in the land of Israel. Wild rock doves nested in cliffs and caves, and pigeons were easy to raise and cheap to buy. Because people knew them so well, the dove became a natural image in poetry and prophecy.

The word appears in four principal settings: the flood story, the laws of offering, Israel's songs, and the prophets. A related compound also appears in a siege narrative. The prophet Jonah's name, Yônâh, is spelled the same way, but it is listed as a separate proper name.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The bird itself, a dove or pigeon. This is the literal sense, as in Noah's dove (Genesis 8:8) and the "young pigeons" offered at the altar (Leviticus 1:14).

2. A figure of comparison or a term of affection. Writers compare people to doves because of how doves look, sound, or behave. In the Song of Solomon the word becomes a tender name, "O my dove" (Song of Songs 2:14).

Second Kings 6:25 uses a related compound (H1686), rather than a direct occurrence of H3123, in the phrase the NKJV translates "dove droppings." Some interpreters think the phrase may be a popular name for a cheap plant food. Either way, the verse describes something almost worthless being sold at a ruinous price.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of a dove image depends on which trait of the bird the writer has in mind.

Its sound. The dove's low, repeated call sounded like grief. Facing death, Hezekiah said, "I mourned like a dove" (Isaiah 38:14). Ezekiel described survivors of judgment as "doves of the valleys, all of them mourning, each for his iniquity" (Ezekiel 7:16). In Isaiah 59:11 the people confess, "We… moan sadly like doves; we look for justice, but there is none." Nahum 2:7 pictures captive women of Nineveh moaning "as with the voice of doves, beating their breasts." In these passages the sound expresses grief over death, over guilt, or over salvation that seems far off.

Its flight and refuge. Doves fly fast and hide in places predators cannot reach. Jeremiah told Moab to "dwell in the rock, and be like the dove which makes her nest in the sides of the cave's mouth" (Jeremiah 48:28).

Its gentleness and vulnerability. The superscription of Psalm 56 sets the song to the tune "The Silent Dove in Distant Lands." The psalm itself never calls David a dove, but the heading's image fits the situation the superscription describes, David captured by the Philistines in Gath, far from home. A different Hebrew word expresses the same idea in Psalm 74:19: "Oh, do not deliver the life of Your turtledove to the wild beast! Do not forget the life of Your poor forever." Here the helpless bird stands for God's own poor.

In the offering laws, yôwnâh is usually paired with "turtledoves," which translates a separate Hebrew word.

4. Theological Significance

The dove passages show God's attention to people who are small and easily overlooked. The dove was the poor family's offering, the image in the heading over a hunted psalmist's song, and the bird whose voice sounded like quiet weeping. In Psalm 74:19 the psalmist uses the vulnerable turtledove, a different Hebrew word, to picture God's afflicted people, and he pleads for them: "Do not forget the life of Your poor forever." God is not drawn only to strength and grandeur. He notices the defenseless creature and the person with nothing to offer.

The dove passages also show that God takes sin seriously and also makes a way back to Him. The mourning doves of Ezekiel 7:16 grieve "each for his iniquity." The famine passage using the related compound in 2 Kings 6:25 shows how far a people who abandon the Lord can fall. Yet the same bird carries an olive leaf after the flood, supplies a sin offering that a poor person can afford, and pictures exiles flying home. Judgment is real in these passages, but the dove usually appears at the point where God is showing mercy.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 8:7–12: the raven and the dove. Noah first released a raven, "which kept going to and fro until the waters had dried up from the earth" (8:7). Scripture tells us nothing more about the raven. The dove's story is different, because each of her returns is told. "The dove found no resting place for the sole of her foot, and she returned into the ark to him" (8:9). Noah "put out his hand and took her, and drew her into the ark to himself." That small act of care is one of the tender moments in a story of judgment. Seven days later the dove returned "in the evening, and behold, a freshly plucked olive leaf was in her mouth" (8:11). From that leaf "Noah knew that the waters had receded from the earth" (8:11). After another seven days she "did not return again to him anymore" (8:12). The next verse reports that the waters were dried up from the earth (8:13).

The three releases give three results: the dove returns without finding rest, returns with an olive leaf that tells Noah the waters have receded, and does not return at all. As an application, readers have seen in this sequence the beginning of a cleansed earth. Scripture does not explicitly call this a picture of the Spirit. Still, it is fitting that the Spirit, who was "hovering over the face of the waters" at creation (Genesis 1:2), later descends "like a dove" at the start of Jesus' ministry. Later Jewish tradition also compared the Spirit's hovering in Genesis 1:2 to a dove.

Leviticus 1, 5 and 12: the offering of the poor. God's law expected a lamb for many offerings, but the sin offering of Leviticus 5 made provision for people of limited means: "If he is not able to bring a lamb, then he shall bring to the LORD… two turtledoves or two young pigeons" (Leviticus 5:7). The law did not stop there. If someone could not afford even two birds, a small portion of fine flour was accepted (5:11). In this provision God reduced the requirement step by step so that poverty did not keep anyone from the atonement it offered. A mother after childbirth who could not afford a lamb could likewise bring two birds (Leviticus 12:8). A healed leper of limited means still brought one lamb, but birds took the place of the other two animals (Leviticus 14:10, 21–22). In other rites the law simply prescribes birds, with no mention of poverty: the purification after a bodily discharge (Leviticus 15:14, 29) and the renewal of a Nazirite vow interrupted by contact with a dead body (Numbers 6:10–11).

The poor person's offering was not treated as second-class. When a bird was offered as a burnt sacrifice, the priest "shall split it at its wings, but shall not divide it completely" (Leviticus 1:17). Even so, the verse calls this small offering "a sweet aroma to the LORD," the same description given to the costly bull and ram earlier in the chapter.

Hosea 7:11 and 11:11: the foolish dove and the homing dove. Hosea used the dove twice, with opposite meanings. First he gave a diagnosis: "Ephraim also is like a silly dove, without sense—they call to Egypt, they go to Assyria" (7:11). Ephraim, the northern kingdom of Israel, was like a frightened bird flying from one great power to another. It looked for safety everywhere except in the Lord, and God warned that wherever they went He would spread His net on them (7:12).

Later Hosea gave a promise. The LORD "will roar like a lion" (11:10), and then His children "shall come trembling like a bird from Egypt, like a dove from the land of Assyria. And I will let them dwell in their houses" (11:11). The same two nations appear in both verses. Egypt and Assyria were the places Ephraim foolishly ran to, and they become the places God calls His people home from. The dove's restless flight becomes the dove's return home. The people still tremble, but now they tremble on the way back to God. Hosea's message is that God's call can do what Israel's own plans could not: it brings His scattered people home.

Song of Solomon 1:15 and 2:14: the beloved dove. "You have dove's eyes" (1:15) is the bridegroom's praise of his bride's gentle beauty. She returns the image when she says his eyes are "like doves" (5:12). Later he calls her "my dove, my perfect one," the only one for him (6:9). In 2:14 he calls out to her: "O my dove, in the clefts of the rock, in the secret places of the cliff, let me see your face, let me hear your voice." The bride is shy and hidden, like a dove in a crevice, and the bridegroom wants her to come out and be with him. Love here is gentle, set on one person, and eager for the other's presence. The church has long read the Song as a picture of the devotion between Christ and His people.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The provision in Leviticus 12:8 reappears at the beginning of the Gospel. When Mary and Joseph presented Jesus at the temple, they offered "a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons" (Luke 2:24). They gave the offering of a family that could not afford a lamb. Paul's words describe what was happening: "though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor" (2 Corinthians 8:9). The Son of God entered the world in the same economic position as the people the dove offering was meant for. Years later He overturned "the seats of those who sold doves" in the temple (Matthew 21:12). His stated reason was Scripture: "My house shall be called a house of prayer," but they had made it a "den of thieves" (Matthew 21:13). The text does not single out how the dove sellers treated the poor. Still, because doves were the poor family's offering, their place in that trade implies that even the offering of the poor had been drawn into the market.

At the Jordan, "He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and alighting upon Him" (Matthew 3:16). The Gospels do not explain why the Spirit came in this form. Yet readers who know the Old Testament may recall earlier doves. Noah's dove at first found no resting place. At the Jordan the Spirit descends like a dove and alights on Jesus, and Christians have seen in this the beginning of a new creation. The bird associated with gentleness, purity and the offering of the poor marks the Messiah, whose strength is shown through meekness.

Jesus also gave His disciples a new use for the dove image: "Be wise as serpents and harmless as doves" (Matthew 10:16). Hosea's dove was "without sense" because it trusted the wrong powers. The disciples' dove-likeness is innocence. Paul expresses the same idea: "wise in what is good, and simple concerning evil" (Romans 16:19).

7. Spirit & Life

David's cry in Psalm 55:6 is an honest one: "Oh, that I had wings like a dove! I would fly away and be at rest." He had been betrayed by a close friend, and violence filled his city. Many believers have felt that same longing to escape. The psalm does not shame David for it, but it does not leave him there. By the end David has found a better refuge than the wilderness: "Cast your burden on the LORD, and He shall sustain you" (Psalm 55:22). He does not fly away. Instead, the Lord carries him and his burden where he is.

The two doves in Hosea work as a warning and as hope. A believer can live like the "silly dove," running from one source of security to another. Yet the God who calls Israel home from Egypt and Assyria also calls His children back from wherever they have wandered. Even a trembling return is a real return.

The mourning doves show that quiet grief over sin has a place before God. Not every prayer is loud. Recalling his sickness, Hezekiah wrote that he could only moan and say, "O LORD, I am oppressed; undertake for me!" (Isaiah 38:14). The Lord had heard his prayer and seen his tears, and added fifteen years to his life (Isaiah 38:5).

The bride hidden "in the clefts of the rock" is also being invited out: "Let me see your face, let me hear your voice." The Lord does not merely tolerate His people. He wants to hear them and see them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Related compound H1686 — 2 Kings 6:25 – During the siege of Samaria, "one-fourth of a kab of dove droppings" sold "for five shekels of silver." This shows how desperate the nation had become under judgment.

  • Psalm 68:13 – "You will be like the wings of a dove covered with silver, and her feathers with yellow gold." The details of the Hebrew are debated, but the picture of a people raised from lying "among the sheepfolds" to shining glory is clear.

  • Isaiah 60:8 – In Zion's coming glory, Isaiah asks who these are who fly like a cloud and like doves to their roosts. It is a vision of God's people flying home, which echoes Hosea's promise.

  • Song of Solomon 5:2 – The bride hears her beloved knocking and calling her "my dove, my perfect one."

  • Numbers 6:10 – A Nazirite whose vow had been broken by contact with a dead body brought two birds, so he could begin his vow again.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H3124 (Yônâh, Jonah) is the prophet's name. It is spelled the same as yôwnâh.

  • Canonical counterpart: G4058 (peristera) is the Greek word for dove. It is used for the Spirit descending on Jesus (Matthew 3:16) and for the purification offering in Luke 2:24.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The prophet Jonah carries the dove's name, and his story resembles Hosea's "silly dove." Like Ephraim, Jonah ran from the Lord and headed for Tarshish. God then brought him back. Scripture does not explicitly make this connection, but it fits Jonah's story well. The God who calls doves home did not give up on a prophet named Dove.
