JETUR - (Yᵉṭûwr, Strong's H3195)

Summary

Jetur was a son of Ishmael and a grandson of Abraham through Hagar. He is listed tenth among Ishmael's twelve sons (Genesis 25:15; 1 Chronicles 1:31), and his name later identifies a people descended from him who lived east of the Jordan. The name probably means "enclosure" or "encampment," perhaps the circular camp of a herding people, though the derivation is not certain. Scripture records no words or deeds of Jetur himself. His importance lies in what his line shows about God's word to Abraham and in one vivid battle where his descendants faced Israel.

That battle is told in 1 Chronicles 5:18–22. The tribes of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh fought "the Hagrites, Jetur, Naphish, and Nodab," and they won "for they cried out to God in the battle. He heeded their prayer, because they put their trust in Him." The Chronicler credits the victory to God, not to Israel's skilled archers. Many interpreters connect Jetur's people with the Itureans of later history, and Luke names Iturea as part of Philip's territory when the word of God came to John the Baptist (Luke 3:1). Jetur's story begins with God keeping a promise to Ishmael. It ends near the place and time where God's greater promise in Christ was being announced.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Jetur is first an individual, one of the twelve sons of Ishmael. In order they are Nebajoth, Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam, Mishma, Dumah, Massa, Hadar, Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah (Genesis 25:13–15). The Chronicler repeats the list in his great genealogy of the nations (1 Chronicles 1:29–31). By the time of 1 Chronicles 5:19, "Jetur" names a people group descended from him and living on Israel's eastern frontier. These are not two different Jeturs. A clan carries its founder's name, as often happens in Scripture.

The Hebrew name probably comes from a root meaning "enclosure" or "encampment." It could suggest a ring-shaped camp or a sheep enclosure, which fits a herding, tent-dwelling people. Lexicons such as BDB support "enclosure," and some scholars have proposed a link with the Aramaic ṭûr, "mountain" or "rock." Neither meaning is certain, and Scripture builds no wordplay on the name. It should not be treated as a key to Jetur's spiritual character.

The Itureans of Luke 3:1 are indexed separately in Greek (Itouraia, G2484). They are widely thought to descend from Jetur and to have taken his name. That identification is reasonable and commonly held, but Scripture never states it directly. Iturea is therefore treated here as a related name, not as an additional occurrence of H3195.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Among Ishmael's princes. Genesis opens Ishmael's line by reminding us of its origin: "Now this is the genealogy of Ishmael, Abraham's son, whom Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah's maidservant, bore to Abraham" (Genesis 25:12). After naming the sons, Moses adds, "These were the sons of Ishmael and these were their names, by their towns and their settlements, twelve princes according to their nations" (Genesis 25:16). Jetur takes his place as one of those princes. Each son heads a people, and the list carries the story of Abraham's family outward into the desert regions.

War on the eastern frontier. Centuries later the Chronicler records a war between the Transjordan tribes and a coalition: "They made war with the Hagrites, Jetur, Naphish, and Nodab" (1 Chronicles 5:19). Israel's force numbered "forty-four thousand seven hundred and sixty valiant men, men able to bear shield and sword, to shoot with the bow, and skillful in war" (5:18). The spoils were huge: "fifty thousand of their camels, two hundred and fifty thousand of their sheep, and two thousand of their donkeys—also one hundred thousand of their men" (5:21). The camels and flocks match the herding life the name Jetur may describe. The Chronicler adds that "many fell dead, because the war was God's. And they dwelt in their place until the captivity" (5:22). An earlier note places a Reubenite war with the Hagrites "in the days of Saul" (5:10), but the date of this larger campaign is not stated precisely.

Jetur and his brother Naphish appear here beside the Hagrites, whose name many connect with Hagar. If that connection is right, Ishmael's extended family stood together in this conflict. Psalm 83:6 lists Hagrites and Ishmaelites separately, however, so the relationship should not be pressed.

Loss of the land. The victory did not last forever. Later these same tribes "were unfaithful to the God of their fathers," and God stirred up the Assyrian king, who carried Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh into exile (1 Chronicles 5:25–26, paraphrased). The land taken from Jetur's people was among the first that Israel lost.

Iturea in the Gospel era. Luke dates the beginning of John the Baptist's ministry with a list of rulers, including "his brother Philip tetrarch of Iturea and the region of Trachonitis" (Luke 3:1). Josephus reports that the Hasmonean ruler Aristobulus I conquered Itureans about a century earlier and required them to be circumcised. If these Itureans descend from Jetur, his people's history reaches into the world of the New Testament.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God promised to bless Ishmael, make him fruitful, and make him the father of twelve princes and a great nation (Genesis 17:20; 21:13, 18). Genesis 25:16 records that promise kept, and Jetur is part of the evidence. God kept His word even to the son who was not the heir of the promise. The line of promise ran through Isaac (Genesis 17:21), yet God did not forget the boy He had heard crying in the wilderness (Genesis 21:17).

The war in 1 Chronicles 5 teaches what Scripture says outright. The decisive factor was not Israel's training but their cry to God and their trust in Him. The Chronicler, writing for a small community after the exile, wanted his readers to see that victory belongs to God. He also wanted them to see that blessings received through faith can be lost through unfaithfulness. Verses 20 and 25 stand only a few lines apart, and the contrast is deliberate.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Paul treats Hagar and Ishmael as a picture of those "born according to the flesh," contrasted with Isaac, born by promise (Galatians 4:22–31). This is explicit New Testament interpretation, though Paul does not mention Jetur. The fact that a son of Ishmael later fought against Isaac's descendants fits the pattern Paul describes, where the child of the flesh opposes the child of promise (Galatians 4:29). Abraham's attempt to secure God's promise through Hagar had consequences that lasted for generations. This connection is a thematic resonance and should not be read as a claim that Jetur is a designed type.

Scripture also leaves room for hope toward Ishmael's descendants. Isaiah foresees the flocks of Kedar and the rams of Nebaioth, two of Jetur's brothers, brought to the Lord's altar in the day of His glory (Isaiah 60:7). Luke places Iturea on the map at the very moment "the word of God came to John" (Luke 3:2, paraphrased). Jesus later brought His disciples to the region of Caesarea Philippi, in Philip's territory, where Peter confessed Him as the Christ (Matthew 16:13–16). These are connections of setting and theme. They are not prophecies about Jetur, but they show the gospel advancing into the old borderlands of Abraham's wider family.

5. Spirit & Life

The clearest window into God's heart in Jetur's story is 1 Chronicles 5:20. Trained soldiers were in the middle of a battle, and they "cried out to God." He "heeded their prayer, because they put their trust in Him." Their skill was real, but they did not rely on it. God answers people who lean their full weight on Him when the enemy is large and the odds are uncertain.

The sequel is sobering. The same tribes later drifted into idolatry and lost what God had given them. Faith that cries out in a crisis must continue in steady faithfulness when the crisis has passed.

Jetur's line also shows God's kindness toward those outside the chosen line. God kept His promise to Ishmael's sons, and in Christ the door is opened to the nations, including the peoples of the desert edge.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:20; 21:13, 18 — God's promise to make Ishmael fruitful, the father of twelve princes and a great nation.

  • Genesis 25:12–16 — Jetur named among Ishmael's twelve sons, "twelve princes according to their nations."

  • 1 Chronicles 1:29–31 — The Chronicler's repetition of Ishmael's line.

  • 1 Chronicles 5:18–22 — War with Jetur's people, and victory credited to prayer and trust because "the war was God's."

  • 1 Chronicles 5:25–26 — The Transjordan tribes' unfaithfulness and exile.

  • Galatians 4:22–31 — Paul's interpretation of Hagar's and Sarah's sons.

  • Isaiah 60:7 — Ishmael's descendants Kedar and Nebaioth gathered to the Lord's worship.

  • Luke 3:1 — Iturea under Philip at the start of John's ministry (related, separately indexed name G2484).
