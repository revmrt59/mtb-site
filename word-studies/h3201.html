ABLE, PREVAIL, ENDURE - (yâkôl, Strong's H3201)

Summary

The Hebrew word yâkôl means to be able. It measures what a person can or cannot do, bear, or overcome. In the NKJV it appears as "can," "could," "able," "prevail," "endure," and sometimes "may" or "must." The word is ordinary. It describes a land too small for two herds, eyes too dim to see, and a riddle no one could solve. Scripture also uses it at the decisive edges of life with God, where human strength runs out.

Most of the time the word appears with a negative: someone cannot. Moses could not enter the tabernacle when God's glory filled it, and the priests could not stand to minister in Solomon's temple. Ten spies insisted Israel was not able to take the land, while Caleb, using the same word, said they were. Jacob, crippled at Peniel, "prevailed" by clinging to God. Habakkuk says that God Himself "cannot look on wickedness." Read together, these passages present the limits of human ability as the place where God's ability is revealed, and God's own "cannot" as an expression of His perfect holiness.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâkôl is a common Hebrew verb found from Genesis to Zephaniah. It appears most often in narrative, where people meet real limits: famine, enemies, old age, grief, and the presence of God. In the poetic books it often describes what a person can endure. In the prophets it describes what Israel cannot do, what its enemies will not do, and what God will not tolerate. Jeremiah uses it more than any other prophet, which fits a prophet whose ministry was marked by struggle.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, yâkôl describes having the capacity to do something. Context decides what kind of capacity is meant.

1. To be able or capable. The land could not support both Abram and Lot (Genesis 13:6), and in his old age the eyes of Israel (Jacob) were so dim that he could not see (Genesis 48:10).

2. To prevail or overcome against someone. Rachel says she has "prevailed" in her struggle with her sister (Genesis 30:8), and Goliath's challenge depends on whether Israel's champion can prevail against him (1 Samuel 17:9).

3. To endure or bear. God says, "I cannot endure iniquity and the sacred meeting" (Isaiah 1:13).

4. To be permitted, in legal settings. In Deuteronomy the word marks what a person may or may not lawfully do, such as eating the tithe at home (Deuteronomy 12:17) or offering the Passover in any town one chooses (Deuteronomy 16:5).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject and object of yâkôl determine its weight. When the subject is a person and the object is a task, the word simply measures capacity: Moses could not carry the people alone (Numbers 11:14; Deuteronomy 1:9). When the object is an opponent, the word becomes a contest, and the English shifts to "prevail," "overcome," or "defeat." When the object is something hard to bear, such as grief, hypocrisy, or arrogance, the sense moves toward "endure" (Esther 8:6; Psalm 101:5).

The negative use matters most. Many key passages turn on could not. Scripture uses this word to mark limits, and those limits are rarely just physical. They reveal what flesh cannot bear, what unbelief cannot attempt, and what sin cannot accomplish.

4. Theological Significance

The word draws a clear line around human ability, especially in moral matters. Jeremiah asks, "Can the Ethiopian change his skin or the leopard its spots? Then may you also do good who are accustomed to do evil" (Jeremiah 13:23). The "may" there translates yâkôl. The prophet's point is that long practice in evil leaves a person as unable to change himself as a leopard is to remove its spots. Joshua gives a similar warning when Israel quickly promises loyalty. He tells them they cannot serve the LORD, because He is holy (Joshua 24:19). Joshua does not turn their commitment away. When they insist, he makes them witnesses against themselves and tells them to put away their foreign gods (Joshua 24:21–23), pressing them to see what that commitment requires. Ecclesiastes adds that what is crooked cannot be made straight (Ecclesiastes 1:15; compare 7:13).

Sin also takes away strength God's people once had. After Achan hid stolen goods, God said Israel could not stand before its enemies (Joshua 7:12). The problem was not Israel's fighting strength. God told them He would not be with them anymore unless the accursed things were destroyed from among them. Isaiah describes a whole society in the same condition: "truth is fallen in the street, And equity cannot enter" (Isaiah 59:14). Where sin is tolerated, righteousness cannot even find a way in.

Against these human limits stands the God who can. Job's final confession, made after the whirlwind, uses this word: he knows God can do everything (Job 42:2).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Glory too heavy to bear (Exodus 40:35; 1 Kings 8:11; 2 Chronicles 5:14; 7:2). When the cloud covered the finished tabernacle, Moses could not enter. This was the same Moses who had spoken with God on the mountain. Centuries later, when the cloud filled Solomon's temple, the priests could not continue ministering, and later they could not even enter the house. These were consecrated men in a sanctuary God Himself had designed, yet they were not able to remain there. God had already told Moses that he could not see His face and live (Exodus 33:20). The pattern is consistent: the best earthly preparation cannot make a person able to stand in God's full glory.

Kadesh: the same word, two verdicts (Numbers 13:30–31; 14:16). Caleb looked at the same walls and giants the others saw and said Israel was well able to overcome them. The ten spies answered with the same verb, saying they were not able. Both sides used yâkôl. Caleb measured the task against God's promise, while the ten measured it against themselves. Moses then pleaded with God. If God killed Israel in the wilderness, he said, the nations would conclude that the LORD was not able to bring this people to the land He had sworn to give them (Numbers 14:15–16). In Moses' plea, Israel's unbelief threatened to make God's name look weak among the nations. Nearly forty years later, in his address to the next generation in the land of Moab (Deuteronomy 1:3, 5), Moses spoke to that same fear with the same verb: if they asked how they could dispossess nations greater than they, they were to remember what the LORD had done to Pharaoh (Deuteronomy 7:17–18).

Peniel: prevailing by holding on (Genesis 32:25, 28; Hosea 12:4). The Man who wrestled Jacob saw that He did not prevail against him. With one touch He put Jacob's hip out of joint. Then He named Jacob Israel, because he had struggled with God and with men and "prevailed." The Man who could disable Jacob with a touch was clearly not outmatched. The best reading is that God limited His strength so that Jacob could prevail in the only way open to him, which was to cling to God and refuse to let go without a blessing. Hosea shows what that prevailing looked like: "he struggled with the Angel and prevailed; He wept, and sought favor from Him" (Hosea 12:4). Jacob won with tears and pleading, not with strength.

God's holy "cannot" (Habakkuk 1:13; Isaiah 1:13). Habakkuk says, "You are of purer eyes than to behold evil, And cannot look on wickedness." He immediately asks, "Why do You look on those who deal treacherously?" The prophet knows God sees everything. His "cannot" means that God cannot look on evil with approval or treat it as acceptable. Isaiah records God's own words: "I cannot endure iniquity and the sacred meeting." This "cannot" does not mean God is weak. It means His holiness will not allow Him to accept evil. Paul expresses a related truth when he speaks of "God, who cannot lie" (Titus 1:2).

The fire Jeremiah could not hold in (Jeremiah 20:7–11). In one chapter the word appears four times. Jeremiah says God was stronger than he and prevailed. He resolved never to speak in God's name again, but the word burned like fire shut up in his bones. He grew weary of holding it back, and he could not (20:9). His enemies watched for him to fall so they could prevail against him (20:10), but he ends this part of his complaint with confidence: the LORD was with him "as a mighty, awesome One," and his persecutors would "stumble, and will not prevail" (20:11). The LORD had prevailed over His prophet (20:7), and the prophet, carrying that word, trusted that his enemies would not prevail over him. Amos describes the same pressure: "The Lord GOD has spoken! Who can but prophesy?" (Amos 3:8).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Each limit shown by yâkôl points ahead to what God would do in Christ. The tabernacle and temple scenes show people who could not enter God's glory. Hebrews announces what those priests never had: "boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way which He consecrated for us, through the veil, that is, His flesh" (Hebrews 10:19–20). Under the old order the way in was tightly restricted. Only the high priest entered the Most Holy Place, "once a year, not without blood" (Hebrews 9:7), and by this the Holy Spirit was showing "that the way into the Holiest of All was not yet made manifest" (Hebrews 9:8). Christ has now opened that way for everyone who comes to God through Him, not once a year for one man, but with boldness for all His people.

Kadesh is also taken up in the New Testament: "So we see that they could not enter in because of unbelief" (Hebrews 3:19). The writer applies Israel's failure to everyone who hears the gospel. The inability Jeremiah described in Jeremiah 13:23 appears again in Paul's words: "those who are in the flesh cannot please God" (Romans 8:8). The gospel answer is that God did what the law could not do by sending His own Son (Romans 8:3–4). Jacob's limp leads to Paul's thorn and Christ's word: "My strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). God's holy "cannot" from Habakkuk is satisfied at the cross, where sin is judged instead of overlooked.

7. Spirit & Life

These passages tell the truth about our limits, and that truth is good news. The priests who could not stand did nothing wrong. Their inability simply showed that God is greater than any building. When a believer finally admits, "I cannot change myself," he is in the same place as the leopard in Jeremiah 13. That is also where grace begins.

Kadesh shows that faith and unbelief can describe the same giants accurately. The difference is whose ability they count on. Caleb did not deny the walls. He trusted God's promise more than his fear of those walls.

Peniel and Jeremiah 20 belong together. In one, God allows Himself to be held. In the other, God holds His servant fast. Prevailing with God looks like Jacob weeping and refusing to let go. Faithfulness under pressure looks like Jeremiah, too tired to speak and too full of God's word to stay silent. Neither man prevailed because he was strong. Each found that God's word and God's blessing were stronger than his weakness.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ruth 4:6 – Once Boaz explains that whoever buys Naomi's field must also take Ruth, the dead man's widow, as his wife to preserve the dead man's name (Ruth 4:5, 10), the closer relative says he cannot redeem the property without ruining his own inheritance. His refusal opens the way for Boaz, the redeemer who is both willing and able.

  • 1 Kings 3:9 – Solomon asks who is able to judge so great a people. Wise leadership begins with knowing one's own limits.

  • Song of Solomon 8:7 – Many waters cannot quench love. Here the word describes what love can withstand.

  • Psalm 129:2 – Israel looks back on generations of affliction and says its enemies have not prevailed.

  • Jeremiah 1:19 – At his call, Jeremiah is told that his enemies will fight against him but will not prevail, because God is with him.

  • Ezekiel 47:5 – The river from the temple becomes too deep to cross, waters a person could not pass over. The life flowing from God's house is more than anyone can measure.
