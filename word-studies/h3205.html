BEGOT, BORE, BORN - (yâlad, Strong's H3205)

Summary

Yâlad is the ordinary Hebrew verb for bringing new life into the world. A mother bears a child, a father begets a son, a child is born, and a midwife helps bring the baby out. Because every family line runs through birth, this is the word that drives the genealogies of Genesis and Chronicles. Its long chains of "begot" record how God kept His promise alive from Adam to David, generation after generation of people who lived and died.

Scripture also uses the word in pictures. The wicked "bring forth" lies they have carried inside. Nations in judgment writhe like a woman in labor. God speaks of Himself as the Rock who begot Israel. At the height of its use, the LORD says to His anointed King, "You are My Son, Today I have begotten You" (Psalm 2:7), and the apostles hear that decree fulfilled when God raised Jesus from the dead. Birth in the Bible carries pain because of the Fall, but it also carries hope, because the God who gives life finishes what He begins.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâlad is one of the most common verbs in the Old Testament, used nearly five hundred times. More than half of those uses fall in two books, Genesis and 1 Chronicles, where it forms the backbone of family records. It also appears in birth stories, in laws about childbirth (Leviticus 12), in the poetry of Job and the Psalms, and often in the prophets, who draw on the experience of labor to describe both judgment and renewal.

Hebrew uses this one verb for the father's part and the mother's part. The NKJV shows the difference by context: "begot" when a man is the subject, "bore" when a woman is, and "born" for the child. The same verb also supplies the Hebrew word for "midwife," the woman who helps another give birth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, yâlad means to bring forth offspring. Context decides whose part in the process is in view.

1. To bear or give birth, said of a mother: "Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son" (Genesis 21:2). This sense can focus on labor itself, as with Rachel's hard labor in Genesis 35:16–17.

2. To beget or father, said of a man: the repeated "begot" of Genesis 5 and 11 and of 1 Chronicles 1–9.

3. To be born, said of the child: "Man who is born of woman / Is of few days and full of trouble" (Job 14:1).

4. To bring forth in a figurative sense, where something other than a child comes into being: the wicked "conceives trouble and brings forth falsehood" (Psalm 7:14).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses are plainly literal: genealogies, birth announcements, and the stories of barren women whose wombs God opened. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Samson's mother, and Hannah all bear children only after God intervenes. In these stories, the subject who "bears" is a woman, but the one who makes the birth possible is the LORD.

The prophets turn labor into an image of distress. Micah tells Jerusalem, "pangs have seized you like a woman in labor" (Micah 4:9). Jeremiah asks whether a man can ever be in labor with a child, and then pictures warriors gripping their sides like women giving birth (Jeremiah 30:6). In these prophetic contexts the image conveys overwhelming, inescapable distress: when God's judgment arrives, there is no escaping its pain.

When God is the subject, the word stops being biological. He "begot" Israel (Deuteronomy 32:18) and "begot" the King (Psalm 2:7). In these passages the verb expresses origin, relationship, and public standing. It says nothing about physical reproduction.

4. Theological Significance

The first use of yâlad comes immediately after sin enters the world: "In pain you shall bring forth children" (Genesis 3:16). The very act through which God promised a Seed who would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15) now carries sorrow. Every later birth continues a fallen race. Amid the wider human family, God kept the promise of rescue alive through a particular line, so birth in Scripture carries both sorrow and hope. That tension never disappears from the word, which is why labor pain can later stand for judgment and also for the coming of deliverance (Micah 4:10).

The genealogies make the same point on a larger scale. Genesis 5 repeats one pattern: a man lived, he begot a son, and "he died." The refrain sounds like a funeral bell (Enoch, whom God took, is the one break in it), yet the chain keeps going. Death claims each generation, but it never breaks the line God is tracing. Ruth closes her book with a line of ten named generations, from Perez to David, joined by "begot" (Ruth 4:18–22), and 1 Chronicles, written for a people returned from exile, starts again with Adam and walks forward. Those long lists are a record of God's faithfulness, written in names. He was watching over the line of promise when no one else was counting.

The barren mothers teach a related truth. God's people never survived on natural vitality. Again and again the line hangs on a closed womb, and the next generation arrives only because the LORD gives it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 2:7 — The King declared Son. The nations rage against the LORD and His Anointed, and God answers by installing His King on Zion. Then the King speaks: "I will declare the decree: The LORD has said to Me, 'You are My Son, Today I have begotten You.'" This is a royal decree. Many interpreters read it against the background of a king's coronation, so that "today" marks a public installation, and the verses that follow grant the Son the nations as His inheritance. The begetting here confers status and authority. It does not mark the beginning of the Son's existence.

The New Testament applies the decree to Jesus' resurrection. Paul preaches that God "has raised up Jesus. As it is also written in the second Psalm: 'You are My Son, Today I have begotten You'" (Acts 13:33). Romans 1:4 says Jesus was "declared to be the Son of God with power... by the resurrection from the dead." Hebrews 1:5 uses the same words to show that no angel ever received such a title. The New Testament does not tie every sense of "today" to one calendar date, and the Son did not begin to exist when He rose; the resurrection publicly vindicated the One who was already God's Son. The empty tomb was God's public answer to the world that crucified His Son: this is My King.

Isaiah 66:7–9 — God finishes what He begins. Isaiah describes an impossible birth: "Before she was in labor, she gave birth; Before her pain came, She delivered a male child" (66:7). The prophet asks who has ever heard of a land or a nation being born in one day, yet Zion's children come all at once (66:8). Then the LORD asks a question that carries the whole passage: would He bring a child to the moment of birth and then withhold delivery? Would He who causes birth shut the womb (66:9)? The question answers itself. God does not carry His redemptive purpose to the edge of fulfillment and abandon it there. The restoration of His people is as certain as His own character.

Exodus 1:15–21 — The midwives who feared God. Pharaoh ordered "the Hebrew midwives, of whom the name of one was Shiphrah and the name of the other Puah" (1:15) to kill every Hebrew boy as he was born. The account keeps using the birth word: midwives, women giving birth, children born. Their whole calling was to help life come into the world, and the king demanded they turn it into a calling of death. They refused because they feared God, and they let the boys live (1:17). God dealt well with them and gave them households of their own (1:20–21). Fearing the Creator meant protecting the life He brings forth, even against the most powerful man in the world. Moses himself was born into this danger (Exodus 2:2).

Deuteronomy 32:18 — Forgetting the One who gave you life. In the Song of Moses, God's charge against Israel is personal: "Of the Rock who begot you, you are unmindful, And have forgotten the God who fathered you." Moses joins two unlikely images, a Rock and a parent, to say that Israel's very existence came from God. They did not form themselves into a nation. Their idolatry was therefore a child's forgetting of the One who gave them life. Exodus 4:22 had already named Israel God's "firstborn" son.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word carries the story from Eden toward Bethlehem. Amid the many family lines recorded in Genesis, Ruth, and Chronicles, God preserved the particular line through which the promised Seed of Genesis 3 would come. Matthew opens his Gospel with the same kind of record, tracing the line to "Jesus who is called Christ," and showing that God's long genealogy has reached its goal.

The prophets narrowed the hope to a specific birth. Isaiah announced that a virgin would bear a Son called Immanuel (Isaiah 7:14) and that a Child would be born to rule on David's throne (Isaiah 9:6). Micah said God would give His people up "until the time that she who is in labor has given birth" (Micah 5:3, paraphrased), linking the birth pains of Israel's suffering with the arrival of the Ruler from Bethlehem.

Psalm 2:7 then carries the word past the manger to the resurrection. The Son born of a woman was installed as King when God raised Him from the dead. And Isaiah 66 looks toward the gathering of a people from the nations, a family brought forth by God's power. The New Testament's language of being born of God continues that hope, as people who were "born of woman" receive a new origin from God Himself.

7. Spirit & Life

Scripture uses birth to show how sin grows. "They conceive trouble and bring forth futility; Their womb prepares deceit" (Job 15:35). Isaiah 59:4 says the same: "They conceive evil and bring forth iniquity." Sin has a pregnancy. It is welcomed, carried, and nourished inside before it comes out as a lie or an act of violence. James uses the same picture: "when desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death" (James 1:15). The hidden thought you keep feeding is not harmless. The time to deal with it is at conception, before it reaches full term.

Zechariah 13:3 shows how deep loyalty to God must go. In the day of cleansing, if a man prophesies lies in the LORD's name, "his father and mother who begot him" will refuse to protect him. The phrase appears twice, pressing on the strongest natural bond there is. Jesus spoke with the same seriousness: "He who loves father or mother more than Me is not worthy of Me" (Matthew 10:37). The bonds of birth are God's gift, but they were never meant to outrank Him.

Isaiah 66:9 gives a steady word to anyone waiting on God. The promise concerns God's redemptive purpose and the restoration of Zion, and what He has truly promised He will complete. It does not guarantee that every plan we hope He is behind, in a life, a family, or a church, will turn out as we expect. But believers can rest in this: God does not abandon His own work halfway. Labor hurts, and Scripture never pretends otherwise. The suffering of His people belongs to a purpose the LORD has begun, and He is the One who causes delivery.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 21:2–3. Sarah bears Isaac at the time God had spoken. The child of laughter arrives on God's schedule, long after natural hope had died.

1 Samuel 1:20. Hannah bears Samuel and names him because she asked him of the LORD. Her song soon celebrates the barren woman who has borne seven (1 Samuel 2:5).

Psalm 87:4–6. God enrolls people from Egypt, Babylon, Philistia, and Tyre as though they were born in Zion. Birth becomes a picture of belonging to the city of God, extended even to the nations.

Psalm 90:2. Moses praises the God who was there before the mountains were brought forth. The birth image reaches back before creation to the eternal God.

Proverbs 17:17. A brother is born for adversity. Family ties exist for the hard days, when loyalty is tested.

Jeremiah 20:14. The prophet curses the day he was born, a raw cry from a faithful man that Scripture preserves without editing.
