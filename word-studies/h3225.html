RIGHT, RIGHT HAND, SOUTH - (yâmîyn, Strong's H3225)

Summary

The Hebrew word yâmîyn means right, as in the right hand, the right side, or the direction to one's right. For someone facing east toward the sunrise, the right also pointed south, so the same word can name a direction. Because most people do their strongest and most skillful work with the right hand, the word naturally came to stand for strength, favor, and honor. Jacob blesses with his right hand. A king seats an honored guest at his right hand. The Psalms celebrate the Lord's right hand as the power that rescues His people.

Through this ordinary word, Scripture shows us a great deal about God. His right hand shattered Pharaoh's army at the sea. Yet the same God promises Israel, His servant, to uphold them with His right hand and to hold their right hand, saying, "Fear not" (Isaiah 41:10, 13). Believers rightly take courage from that promise as an application of the passage. He stands at the right hand of the poor, the place where an accuser would stand, in order to defend them. In Psalm 110 the Lord invites David's Lord to sit at His right hand, and the New Testament proclaims that Jesus Christ, having purged our sins, now sits there and reigns. The word finally points to the place where God's people find their joy. The psalmist says pleasures are found at God's right hand forever.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâmîyn is the common Hebrew word for the right side of a person or object. It appears throughout the Old Testament, in family blessings, battle stories, temple building plans, priestly instructions, the Law, wisdom sayings, and above all the Psalms.

Ancient Israel oriented itself toward the east, where the sun rises. A person facing east has the south on the right, so yâmîyn can also mean south. Psalm 89:12 uses it this way: "The north and the south, You have created them." When Solomon set the bronze Sea "on the right side of the house, toward the southeast" (1 Kings 7:39), the right side of the east-facing temple was its southern side.

The tribe of Benjamin carries this word in its name. Jacob renamed his last son Benjamin, son of the right hand (Genesis 35:18). That background becomes important in Judges.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The right hand or right side, in a literal, physical sense: Jacob "stretched out his right hand and laid it on Ephraim's head" (Genesis 48:14).

2. The south, as the direction on a person's right when facing east (1 Samuel 23:19; Psalm 89:12).

3. The right hand as the hand of strength, skill, and favor. In this sense "at the right hand" means a place of honor or protection, and "the right hand of the LORD" means His active power (Psalm 98:1; Psalm 110:1).

Judges also uses a distinctive phrase that literally reads restricted in his right hand. The NKJV translates it "left-handed" (Judges 3:15; 20:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word changes with whose right hand is in view and what that hand is doing.

When God's right hand acts, the word describes His personal intervention. The right hand of the Lord fights, saves, upholds, and plants His people in their land (Exodus 15:6, 12; Psalm 78:54; Psalm 80:15).

When someone stands or sits at the right hand of another, the setting decides the meaning. At a royal court this position signals honor: Solomon has a throne set for his mother at his right hand (1 Kings 2:19), and the queen stands at the king's right hand in Psalm 45:9. In a courtroom it is where an accuser or a defender takes his place (Zechariah 3:1; Psalm 109:31). On the battlefield it is where a protector stands beside a warrior.

"To the right hand or to the left" works as a fixed expression for wandering off a path. Moses uses it of departing from God's commands (Deuteronomy 5:32). Psalm 91:7 uses the word differently. It speaks of a thousand falling at a person's side and ten thousand at his right hand, yet the danger "shall not come near."

In the priestly laws, the right thigh of the peace offering belongs to the priest (Leviticus 7:32–33). The right side is the choice portion.

4. Theological Significance

The Psalms keep returning to one conviction: God saves His people with His own right hand. Israel's history taught them this. Psalm 44:3 looks back on the conquest of Canaan: "For they did not gain possession of the land by their own sword, Nor did their own arm save them; But it was Your right hand, Your arm, and the light of Your countenance, Because You favored them." The psalmist sets the people's sword and arm beside God's right hand and gives the credit entirely to God. The verse ends with the reason God acted: "Because You favored them." The victory began in His favor toward them.

The pattern begins at the Red Sea. In Exodus 15:6 Moses sings that the Lord's right hand is glorious in power and has dashed the enemy in pieces. In verse 12 he says that God stretched out His right hand and the earth swallowed them. Later psalms take up the same song: "His right hand and His holy arm have gained Him the victory" (Psalm 98:1). David says the same of his own life: "Your right hand has held me up, Your gentleness has made me great" (Psalm 18:35). David places God's right hand beside His gentleness. The power that topples armies stoops to lift up one man.

This saving hand also marks out a path. Moses commanded Israel to do what the Lord commanded and "not turn aside to the right hand or to the left" (Deuteronomy 5:32). Joshua received the same charge about the Book of the Law (Joshua 1:7; 23:6), and even Israel's king was to keep this rule (Deuteronomy 17:20). Notice that turning to the right is as much a departure as turning to the left. God's Word is the road itself, and drifting from it in either direction is still drifting. Proverbs 4:27 brings the same image into daily life.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 48 and Judges 3: God Turns the Expected Right Hand. Joseph positioned his sons with care. He brought "Manasseh with his left hand toward Israel's right hand" (Genesis 48:13), so that the firstborn would receive the primary blessing. Jacob crossed his arms, "guiding his hands knowingly, for Manasseh was the firstborn" (Genesis 48:14). Joseph tried to move his father's hand and protested, "Not so, my father, for this one is the firstborn; put your right hand on his head" (Genesis 48:18). Jacob refused. Joseph's protest shows what the right hand meant, and that is exactly why the crossing mattered. God's blessing would follow His purpose, not the order of birth. Paul draws the same lesson from the earlier choice of Jacob over Esau, "that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11).

Judges shows a similar surprise. The tribe named son of the right hand produces Ehud, "a left-handed man" (Judges 3:15), literally a man restricted in his right hand. Interpreters debate whether the phrase describes an impairment or a trained skill. The Benjamite slingers of Judges 20:16, who "could sling a stone at a hair's breadth and not miss," suggest a deadly advantage. Either way, the irony is plain. God delivered Israel through the son of the right hand who did not use his right hand. Ehud fastened his dagger "under his clothes on his right thigh" (Judges 3:16), and when he was alone with Eglon, he "reached with his left hand, took the dagger from his right thigh" (Judges 3:21). The text does not say whether anyone checked him for weapons. A plausible tactical reading is that a blade on the right thigh was less likely to be expected, since a right-handed man would normally wear it on his left side. Whatever the tactic, the text is clear that the LORD raised up this deliverer (Judges 3:15), and He did so through an unexpected hand.

Psalm 110:1 and Psalm 89: The Seat of Shared Rule. "The LORD said to my Lord, 'Sit at My right hand, Till I make Your enemies Your footstool'" (Psalm 110:1). David hears the Lord speak to someone David himself calls Lord, and the Lord gives that figure the place of highest honor and shared authority. The enemies are not yet subdued, but the throne is already given. Psalm 89 shows that God's promise to David's line rests on God's own strength: "high is Your right hand" (Psalm 89:13). God then promises to set the king's right hand over the rivers (Psalm 89:25). The king's reach extends only because God's right hand stands behind it. Read in the NKJV wording, Psalm 110 also seems to reverse the picture. In verse 1 the king sits at God's right hand, and verse 5 says, "The Lord is at Your right hand; He shall execute kings in the day of His wrath." On that reading, the God who seats the king beside Him is also with the king in battle. Translators and interpreters take the pronouns and the one addressed in verse 5 in more than one way, so this reversal is a reading of the verse rather than a certainty. The New Testament's use of verse 1 is not in doubt.

Isaiah 41 and the Psalms of Trust: Holding and Standing Beside. If Psalm 110:5 is read as above, it pictures God at His king's right hand. Other passages show God at the side of His people, in two different ways. In Isaiah 41 the servant is Israel, "Jacob whom I have chosen" (Isaiah 41:8). God promises to uphold His servant with His righteous right hand (41:10). In 41:13 He goes further and says that He, the Lord, will hold His servant's right hand, telling him not to fear because He will help him. Here God takes hold of the very hand a person works and fights with. Believers may take this promise to themselves as an application of what God said to Israel. Elsewhere God stands at the believer's right side as a guard. David says that because the Lord is at his right hand, he will not be moved (Psalm 16:8). Psalm 121:5 calls the Lord a shade at your right hand. Asaph, after nearly losing his footing over the prosperity of the wicked, confesses that God has held him by his right hand (Psalm 73:23). In Psalm 63:8 the order is reversed: David's soul follows close behind, and God's right hand upholds him. God grips the hand and also guards the side. The believer is held from within and shielded from without.

Zechariah 3:1 and Psalm 109: The Courtroom. Joshua the high priest stands before the Angel of the Lord, and Satan stands at his right hand to oppose him (Zechariah 3:1). In a court, that was the accuser's position. Joshua offers no defense. He is clothed in filthy garments, and the Lord Himself rebukes Satan, takes away Joshua's iniquity, and clothes him afresh (Zechariah 3:2–4). Psalm 109 uses the same setting. Psalm 109:6 asks that "an accuser stand at his right hand." Interpreters read the verse in two ways: as the psalmist quoting his enemies' wish against him, or as the psalmist's own plea concerning an enemy. The Hebrew has no marker that settles it. Either way, the psalm closes by saying that the Lord stands at the right hand of the poor to save him from those who condemn his soul (Psalm 109:31). God takes the place where an accuser would stand, and He stands there to defend.

Psalm 16:11: Joy at God's Right Hand. David ends Psalm 16 by saying that fullness of joy is in God's presence and that pleasures are at His right hand forevermore. The hand of power and the seat of authority turn out to be a place of delight. All of God's saving strength aims to bring His people near Him, where their joy will never end.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus used Psalm 110:1 to show that David's son is also David's Lord (Matthew 22:44). After the resurrection, the apostles treated the psalm as fulfilled. On Pentecost, Peter first quotes Psalm 16, where the Lord is at David's right hand and joy fills His presence (Acts 2:25–28). He then announces that Jesus is "exalted to the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, He poured out this which you now see and hear" (Acts 2:33). He quotes Psalm 110:1 to finish the point (Acts 2:34–35). In a single sermon, both right-hand psalms meet in the risen Christ. The poured-out Spirit is the evidence that Jesus now reigns.

Hebrews explains what this seat means. Jesus "sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" only "when He had by Himself purged our sins" (Hebrews 1:3). In the tabernacle, priests stood day after day offering the same sacrifices (Hebrews 10:11). "But this Man, after He had offered one sacrifice for sins forever, sat down at the right hand of God" (Hebrews 10:12). He sits because His sacrifice is complete, and He waits there until His enemies are made His footstool (Hebrews 10:13).

This also answers Zechariah's courtroom. Paul asks who can condemn God's people, since Christ is at God's right hand interceding for us (Romans 8:34). The accuser once stood at the high priest's right hand. Now our High Priest is at the Father's right hand, and the case has been settled in our favor.

7. Spirit & Life

The Bible never gives us two right hands of God, one for armies and another for frightened people. The hand that brought the sea down on Pharaoh is the hand Isaiah describes taking hold of His servant's hand. Applied to a believer who feels small, this means he is not being held by a lesser power. That is why "Fear not" in Isaiah 41 carries real weight. God's strength stands behind His tenderness.

The courtroom passages speak to anyone burdened by accusation, whether it comes from others, from a guilty conscience, or from the enemy himself. Joshua did not win his case by arguing. The Lord rebuked the accuser and clothed him. Our confidence rests on who stands at the Father's right hand and on whose side He stands.

The priestly consecration uses a closely related Hebrew form for right. Blood was placed "on the tip of Aaron's right ear, on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot" (Leviticus 8:23). The hearing, the working, and the walking of the priest's dominant side were all claimed for God. The cleansed leper received the very same marks (Leviticus 14:14). The man who had lived outside the camp received the same signs as the high priest. God claims our best strength, and He gives that dignity even to those who were once excluded.

Wisdom asks for the same orientation in daily life. "A wise man's heart is at his right hand, But a fool's heart at his left" (Ecclesiastes 10:2). Wisdom offers her best gift in her right hand: "Length of days is in her right hand, In her left hand riches and honor" (Proverbs 3:16). And the road of obedience runs straight ahead, with no turning to the right or to the left. A heart set in the right direction walks that road toward the place where pleasures are found forever.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 77:10. In his anguish, Asaph resolves to remember the years of the right hand of the Most High. Remembering what God's hand has done becomes his way out of despair.

  • Psalm 74:11. The psalmist asks why God withdraws His hand, even His right hand, and pleads with Him to act. Lament can honestly ask God to put His strength to work.

  • Psalm 118:15–16. The tents of the righteous resound with the cry that the right hand of the Lord does valiantly and is exalted. Rescued people sing about God's right hand.

  • Psalm 139:10. Even at the farthest edge of the sea, David says, God's hand would lead him and His right hand would hold him. No distance puts a believer beyond God's grip.

  • 2 Chronicles 3:17. Solomon set up two pillars before the temple, "one on the right hand and the other on the left," naming the one on the right Jachin. The name is commonly understood to mean He shall establish.

  • Jonah 4:11. God pities Nineveh's people, who cannot tell their right hand from their left. They were helpless and confused, and God showed them compassion.
