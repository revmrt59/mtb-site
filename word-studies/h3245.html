FOUND, LAY A FOUNDATION, ESTABLISH, APPOINT - (yâçad, Strong's H3245)

Summary

The Hebrew verb yâçad means to found something: to lay a foundation, to establish, or to appoint. Builders use it for the costly stones beneath Solomon's temple and for the foundation laid when the exiles returned to rebuild the temple. Poets and prophets use it for God laying the foundations of the earth, a picture of His authority over everything that stands on it. The same verb describes appointing people to an office or setting a royal order in place, and once it describes God ordaining strength from the mouths of infants.

The word's biblical significance rests on a sharp contrast. People try to found cities and refuges of their own and to plot together against God, and those efforts fail. What God founds holds: the earth, His testimonies, Zion as a refuge for the poor. When Jerusalem's leaders trusted lies, God promised, "Behold, I lay in Zion a stone for a foundation… a sure foundation" (Isaiah 28:16). The New Testament names that stone Jesus Christ. The God who set the earth in place has laid His Son as the one foundation His people can rest their whole weight on.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâçad is a Hebrew verb drawn from the world of building. In the ancient Near East a large structure began with leveled ground and heavy squared stones, and those stones decided whether the walls would stand. Solomon "commanded them to quarry large stones, costly stones, and hewn stones, to lay the foundation of the temple" (1 Kings 5:17).

The word appears in several settings: city-building (Egypt, Jericho), the first and second temples, the creation poetry of Job, Psalms, and Proverbs, and the prophecies of Isaiah, Habakkuk, Haggai, and Zechariah. It also moves beyond stone. A royal order can be "founded," as when King Ahasuerus "had ordered all the officers of his household" (Esther 1:8). Gatekeepers can be "appointed" to their posts (1 Chronicles 9:22).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its center, yâçad means to set something on a firm base so that it can stand and last. Context brings out several distinct senses:

1. To lay the physical foundation of a building or city, as with Solomon's temple (1 Kings 6:37) and the rebuilt temple in Ezra 3:10.

2. To found or establish the earth and the created order, said only of God: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (Job 38:4).

3. To appoint, ordain, or set in place by decision, whether people in an office (1 Chronicles 9:22), a king's order (Esther 1:8), or strength from infants' mouths (Psalm 8:2).

4. In a reflexive form, to gather or "take counsel together" (Psalm 2:2; 31:13). Some lexicons trace this sense to a separate Hebrew root meaning to sit together or to conspire, so the link to foundation-laying is less certain here.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The most important variable in this word is who does the founding. When God is the subject, the word carries permanence. He founded the earth "upon the seas" (Psalm 24:2), founded "His strata in the earth" (Amos 9:6), and founded His testimonies forever (Psalm 119:152). When human beings are the subject, the outcome depends on whether they build with God or against Him. Solomon and Zerubbabel lay foundations for God's house. Hiel lays a foundation God had forbidden.

The passive phrase "the foundation… was laid" becomes a fixed point in time in the post-exilic books. Ezra 3:6 notes that burnt offerings began on the first day of the seventh month, "although the foundation of the temple of the LORD had not been laid," and Haggai and Zechariah look back to the day the foundation was laid (Haggai 2:18; Zechariah 8:9). The laying of a foundation marks a beginning that God treats as decisive.

In Psalm 2:2, the form translated "take counsel together" describes rulers taking counsel together against the LORD. Psalm 31:13 uses it of enemies scheming against David's life.

4. Theological Significance

The creation uses of yâçad establish God's right to rule. In Job 38, God answers Job's questions with questions of His own. He asks who measured the earth, who fastened its foundations, and who "laid its cornerstone, when the morning stars sang together" (Job 38:6–7). The Builder of the world does not answer to anyone standing on it. Psalm 24 draws the conclusion of ownership: "The earth is the LORD's… For He has founded it upon the seas" (Psalm 24:1–2).

The prophets turn this truth into comfort. Isaiah rebukes a frightened people because "you forget the LORD your Maker, Who stretched out the heavens And laid the foundations of the earth; You have feared continually every day Because of the fury of the oppressor" (Isaiah 51:13). The verse ends by asking, "And where is the fury of the oppressor?" An oppressor stands on ground God laid, and Isaiah treats fear of man as a symptom of forgetting who the Maker is.

Scripture uses the same verb for God founding the earth and for His founding of Zion and His sanctuary. Psalm 78:69 says He "built His sanctuary like the heights, Like the earth which He has established forever." Isaiah 51:16 joins the two acts in one sentence: God lays "the foundations of the earth" and says "to Zion, 'You are My people.'" Earth and Zion are different things, but they have one Architect, and His redeeming work is as deliberate and lasting as creation. Isaiah 14:32 says the LORD has founded Zion, and the poor of His people take refuge in it.

God's foundations also include His decrees, including hard ones. After Jericho fell, Joshua pronounced that whoever laid its foundation would lose his firstborn (Joshua 6:26). Centuries later, under Ahab, "Hiel of Bethel built Jericho. He laid its foundation with Abiram his firstborn… according to the word of the LORD, which He had spoken through Joshua" (1 Kings 16:34). Building on ground God had placed under a curse did not escape His word. Habakkuk faces an even harder appointment. Addressing God as his Rock, he confesses that the LORD has marked or appointed the ruthless Chaldeans to correct His people (Habakkuk 1:12). God appoints even a wicked empire as an instrument of discipline, and Habakkuk goes on to confess that the Holy One, whose eyes are too pure to look on evil, does not share its cruelty (Habakkuk 1:13).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 28:16: the sure foundation in Zion. Jerusalem's rulers had boasted of an agreement with death and the grave and had made lies their shelter (Isaiah 28:14–15). God's answer was to lay something new rather than repair their refuge: "Behold, I lay in Zion a stone for a foundation, A tried stone, a precious cornerstone, a sure foundation; Whoever believes will not act hastily." Yâçad appears twice in this one verse, once in "I lay" and again in "sure foundation." The repetition places all the weight on God's act. He lays the stone, and He has already tested it. The one who trusts it "will not act hastily," which means that person will not panic or run. God promises stability to the one who believes instead of to the one who builds cleverly. The coming hail and flood would sweep away the refuge of lies (Isaiah 28:17–18), and only the stone God laid would remain.

Ezra 3, Haggai 2, and Zechariah 4: a foundation that guarantees completion. When the returned exiles laid the second temple's foundation, the priests and Levites sang, "For He is good, For His mercy endures forever toward Israel" (Ezra 3:11). Old men who had seen Solomon's temple "wept with a loud voice," while others shouted for joy, "so that the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping" (Ezra 3:12–13). That was an earlier scene. Years later, after the work had stalled and then resumed, Haggai brought God's word on the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month. He called the people to "consider" from that day, "from the day that the foundation of the LORD's temple was laid," and asked, "Is the seed still in the barn?" He went on, "As yet the vine, the fig tree, the pomegranate, and the olive tree have not yielded fruit. But from this day I will bless you" (Haggai 2:18–19). Haggai invokes the laying of the foundation and promises blessing "from this day," while the trees still had no fruit to show. The passage does not fix the date of the earlier foundation scene in Ezra 3. The blessing was declared before any harvest could prove it. Zechariah adds that Zerubbabel's hands had laid the foundation and his hands would finish it (Zechariah 4:9), and that the work would be done by God's Spirit rather than by might or power (Zechariah 4:6). What God founds, He completes.

Psalm 2:2 and Psalm 8:2: rulers' plotting and God's ordaining. In Psalm 2, "the kings of the earth set themselves, And the rulers take counsel together, Against the LORD and against His Anointed." Whatever the exact origin of this sense of yâçad, the verse pictures rulers plotting together. Heaven laughs, because God has already installed His King on Zion (Psalm 2:4–6). Psalm 8 reverses the scale: "Out of the mouth of babes and nursing infants You have ordained strength… That You may silence the enemy and the avenger." The God who founds the earth builds His defense from the weakest voices in the human race. Read side by side as a literary comparison, the kings take counsel together and fail, while God ordains strength from infants' praise and silences His enemies.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament identifies Isaiah's foundation stone directly. In Romans 9:33 Paul combines two passages from Isaiah. From Isaiah 28:16 he takes the stone God lays in Zion and the promise for whoever believes; from Isaiah 8:14 he takes the language of "a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense." The result reads, "Behold, I lay in Zion a stumbling stone and rock of offense, And whoever believes on Him will not be put to shame" (Romans 9:33). Peter applies Isaiah 28:16 to Christ as the chosen, precious cornerstone (1 Peter 2:6). Paul states the conclusion plainly: "For no other foundation can anyone lay than that which is laid, which is Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 3:11). The church is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ Himself being the chief cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20). These passages use Greek words, so they are not occurrences of yâçad. Romans and 1 Peter explicitly apply its most important Hebrew use to Christ, and Paul's foundation language builds on the same picture.

The other strands of the word also move toward Christ. In Acts 4:25–28, the church reads Psalm 2 as the plotting of Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles, and Israel's leaders against Jesus. Their plan accomplished only what God had determined beforehand. When children shouted praise to Jesus in the temple, He defended them with Psalm 8:2 (Matthew 21:16). The temple foundations of Ezra and Haggai looked forward to a greater dwelling, and that dwelling rests on Christ. The Creator who laid the earth's foundations entered His own creation and was laid in Zion as the tested stone. He was rejected by the builders, and He is now the foundation of a new people.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 51:13 explains why fear can grip a believer. Fear grows when the oppressor looks larger than the Maker. The remedy Isaiah gives is to remember rather than to feel brave: remember who stretched out the heavens and laid the earth's foundations. Every threat stands on ground God laid.

Isaiah 28:16 promises that the one who believes "will not act hastily." People who build on lies have to keep scrambling to hold their structures up. A person resting on Christ can stand still, because the stone has already been tested and holds.

Haggai offers a quiet encouragement to anyone at a small beginning. God said "from this day I will bless you" while the vine, the fig tree, the pomegranate, and the olive tree had not yet yielded fruit (Haggai 2:19). He does not wait for visible success before committing Himself to what He has founded. Ezra 3 shows that such beginnings can be tearful. When God restores a life, a marriage, or a church, grief over what sin destroyed and joy at His mercy can rise together until the two sounds cannot be told apart. Both belong in the praise "His mercy endures forever."

One further picture comes from the physical nature of foundations, and it is offered as an analogy. Solomon's costly stones ended up buried and out of sight, carrying the whole temple. The believer's life is likewise "hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:3). What holds us up is mostly unseen. Jesus described the one who comes to Him, "hears My sayings and does them": he is like a man who "dug deep and laid the foundation on the rock," so that when the flood arose, "the stream beat vehemently against that house, and could not shake it, for it was founded on the rock" (Luke 6:47–48). The hidden foundation in the parable is doing what Jesus says, set against the one who hears and does not do (Luke 6:49).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 9:18 — The coming hail is described as unlike anything seen in Egypt since its founding. The plague would shake a nation that seemed fixed and permanent.

  • Psalm 104:5, 8 — God laid the earth's foundations so that it should never be moved, and He sent the waters to the place He had founded for them. Even the boundaries of the seas are His appointment.

  • Proverbs 3:19 — The LORD founded the earth by wisdom. The wisdom that holds creation together is the same wisdom the father in Proverbs urges his son to seek.

  • Song of Solomon 5:15 — "His legs are pillars of marble Set on bases of fine gold." The bride admires her beloved's strength and worth in the imagery of a well-founded building.

  • Isaiah 44:28 — God says of Cyrus that he will declare to the temple that its foundation shall be laid. God names the rebuilding before the exile has even ended.

  • Isaiah 54:11 — God promises the afflicted, storm-tossed city that He will lay her foundations with sapphires. He restores her and makes her more beautiful than before.

  • Zechariah 12:1 — The LORD lays the foundation of the earth and forms the spirit of man within him. The Creator of the earth is also the Maker of each human life.
