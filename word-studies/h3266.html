JEUSH - (Yᵉʻûwsh, Strong's H3266)

Summary

Jeush is the name of four different men in the Bible, and they should not be confused with one another. The first is an Edomite chief, a son of Esau by his wife Aholibamah (Genesis 36:18; 1 Chronicles 1:35). The second is a Gershonite Levite, a son of Shimei, listed in David's organization of the Levites (1 Chronicles 23:10–11). The third is a Benjaminite from King Saul's family line through Jonathan (1 Chronicles 8:39). The fourth is a prince of Judah, a son of King Rehoboam by his wife Mahalath (2 Chronicles 11:19). The meaning of the name is uncertain. The Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon tentatively suggests "He comes to help," comparing an Arabic root, while Strong's traditional derivation gives "hasty." Neither meaning is settled. The name's meaning should not be read into the lives of the men who carried it.

None of these men speaks a recorded word, yet each appears at a point where God's purposes can be seen at work. Esau's son becomes a chief in Seir while Jacob's family holds only a burial ground and a parcel at Shechem (Genesis 33:19), its national inheritance still unfulfilled. The Levite and his brother have few sons, so they are counted together as one family for service in God's house. Saul's descendant shows that Jonathan's family line continued long after Saul's throne was gone. Rehoboam's son is passed over when his father makes Abijah crown prince, and Abijah is the son through whom the royal line toward Christ continues (Matthew 1:7). Taken together, these brief entries show God keeping His word in quiet places, through families that ordinary history would have forgotten.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H3266 covers six occurrences of the name Jeush. They refer to four separate men from different tribes and centuries.

1. Jeush son of Esau, an Edomite chief (Genesis 36:18; 1 Chronicles 1:35). His mother was Aholibamah, the daughter of Anah. Genesis 36:5 and 36:14 also name this same son of Esau, but in those two verses the written Hebrew form is spelled slightly differently, and this study follows the indexing that places them under a separate Strong's entry, H3274. Some lexicons group all of these spellings under one name, Jeush. They describe the same man, but they are not counted among the six H3266 occurrences.

2. Jeush son of Shimei, a Levite of the Gershonite clan in David's time (1 Chronicles 23:10–11).

3. Jeush son of Eshek, a Benjaminite descended from Saul through Jonathan, and the second of Eshek's three sons (1 Chronicles 8:39).

4. Jeush son of Rehoboam, a prince of Judah born to Mahalath (2 Chronicles 11:19).

Another Jeush, a Benjaminite son of Bilhan (1 Chronicles 7:10), is also indexed under H3274. He would be a fifth man with this name, separately indexed and not part of this entry.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. The Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon tentatively proposes "He comes to help," comparing an Arabic root meaning to give help. Strong's traditional derivation, from a root meaning to hasten (H5789), gives "hasty." This is a scholarly proposal rather than a settled meaning, and Scripture never comments on the name or plays on it. The name was evidently common enough to appear among Edomites, Levites, Benjaminites, and Judah's royal family. That fact alone warns against reading any one Jeush's story through the name's possible meaning.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Jeush the Edomite chief. Genesis 36:5 records that "Aholibamah bore Jeush, Jaalam, and Korah. These were the sons of Esau who were born to him in the land of Canaan." Genesis 36:2 lists Aholibamah among the wives Esau took "from the daughters of Canaan." Genesis 26:34–35 names two other wives, Judith and Basemath, whose marriages grieved Isaac and Rebekah. The two lists do not match exactly, and any identification between them is a harmonization. Later Esau moved his household to Mount Seir (Genesis 36:6–8). There his sons became tribal leaders: "Chief Jeush, Chief Jaalam, and Chief Korah. These were the chiefs who descended from Aholibamah, Esau's wife, the daughter of Anah" (Genesis 36:18). This Korah is an Edomite and is not the Levite Korah of Numbers 16. The Chronicler repeats Jeush's name centuries later in his opening survey of the nations (1 Chronicles 1:35). The chiefs of Edom reappear as a group in Exodus 15:15, trembling at the news of what God had done at the Red Sea.

Jeush the Levite. As David prepared for the temple his son would build, he counted the Levites and arranged them by families (1 Chronicles 23:1–6). Within the Gershonite branch, "the sons of Shimei: Jahath, Zina, Jeush, and Beriah. These were the four sons of Shimei" (1 Chronicles 23:10). The next verse adds an unusual detail: "Jahath was the first and Zizah the second. But Jeush and Beriah did not have many sons; therefore they were assigned as one father's house" (23:11). (Zina and Zizah are evidently the same son with a variant spelling.) Jeush and his brother were not left out. Their two small families were counted together as one unit so they could take their place in the work of the house of God (23:24).

Jeush the Benjaminite. First Chronicles 8:33–40 traces Saul's descendants through Jonathan and Jonathan's son Merib-Baal, who is called Mephibosheth in 2 Samuel. The list runs many generations down to Azel and his brother Eshek. Eshek's sons were Ulam the firstborn, Jeush the second, and Eliphelet the third (8:39). The chapter closes by describing Ulam's descendants as mighty archers with many sons and grandsons (8:40). Scripture records nothing else about Jeush himself.

Jeush the prince of Judah. "Rehoboam took for himself as wife Mahalath the daughter of Jerimoth the son of David… And she bore him children: Jeush, Shamariah, and Zaham" (2 Chronicles 11:18–19). Her father Jerimoth was David's son, and her mother Abihail was the daughter of Eliah, the son of Jesse, so she was related to David's family through both parents. "After her," Rehoboam married Maachah, Absalom's granddaughter, and "loved Maachah… more than all his wives and his concubines" (11:20–21). As a result, "Rehoboam appointed Abijah the son of Maachah as chief, to be leader among his brothers; for he intended to make him king" (11:22). The text then says Rehoboam "dealt wisely, and dispersed some of his sons throughout all the territories of Judah and Benjamin, to every fortified city" (11:23). Scripture does not say whether Jeush was one of the sons sent to these cities.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Each Jeush stands where a larger purpose of God becomes visible.

The Edomite chief belongs to the line God did not choose for the promise. Even so, God had told Rebekah that two nations were in her womb (Genesis 25:23), and Genesis 36 shows that word coming true. Genesis 36:31 notes that kings reigned in Edom "before any king reigned over the children of Israel." It is a fair inference that the contrast is intended. Esau's sons became chiefs with territory, while Jacob's descendants would wait a long time, through Egypt and slavery, for their national inheritance. Edom's quick success did not mean God had favored it. The promise to Abraham took longer and passed through suffering.

The Levite shows how God's worship was ordered. Scripture states plainly that Jeush and Beriah had few sons and were counted as one father's house (1 Chronicles 23:11). The implication is that a small family was combined with another rather than set aside. Belonging to Levi carried the privilege of service, and having few descendants did not take it away.

The Benjaminite matters because of what happened to Saul's house. God took the kingdom from Saul (1 Samuel 15:28), but David had sworn to Jonathan that he would not cut off his kindness from Jonathan's house (1 Samuel 20:14–17, 42). David kept that covenant by showing kindness to Mephibosheth "for Jonathan's sake" (2 Samuel 9:1, 7). Jeush appears many generations down that very line. Judgment on Saul's throne did not mean the end of his family.

The prince of Judah shows God's promise to David continuing through a flawed family. The Chronicler presents Abijah's appointment as the result of Rehoboam's preference for Maachah and makes no claim that God directed it. The order of the account suggests Mahalath's sons were born before Abijah, but the text does not call Jeush the firstborn. If he was, Rehoboam's choice sits uneasily beside the law against favoring the son of a loved wife over the firstborn (Deuteronomy 21:15–17). Even so, the line from Rehoboam to Abijah is the line Matthew traces to Christ (Matthew 1:7).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No passage of Scripture interprets the name Jeush prophetically or in the New Testament. Two of these men, however, are collateral relatives of lines the gospel story takes up, though neither is a named ancestor of Jesus.

Esau's son belongs to the family Paul uses to explain God's freedom in mercy. Before the twins were born, "that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works but of Him who calls… it was said to her, 'The older shall serve the younger'" (Romans 9:11–12). Jeush the Edomite chief is part of that older brother's line, outwardly established and successful, yet not the line through which the Messiah would come. The promise depended on God's call, not on who prospered first.

Rehoboam's son stands beside the royal line itself. Christ's genealogy passes through Abijah rather than Jeush (Matthew 1:7). This is less a case of divine election overturning birth order than of God keeping His promise to David through a father's partiality. The connection is thematic: the line to Christ was preserved by God's faithfulness, not by the virtue of the kings who carried it.

David's kindness to Jonathan's descendants also resembles, at the level of theme, the gospel's welcome of people who have no claim of their own. Mephibosheth ate at the king's table for another man's sake (2 Samuel 9:7). Scripture does not present this as a type of Christ, but the likeness is real and worth noticing.

5. Spirit & Life

Jeush the Levite offers the clearest encouragement among these entries. His family was small and could not stand alone in David's arrangement. Instead of being dropped, he and his brother were counted together and served together (1 Chronicles 23:11). God's house has always had room for small families and limited resources. What one household lacked, its joining with another supplied, and the work of worship went on. Many believers feel too few, too weak, or too unremarkable to matter in God's service. This verse quietly answers that fear.

Jeush the Benjaminite shows that God remembers promises after people have stopped thinking about them. Saul's failure was public and final, yet a vow of kindness made between two friends was kept, and Jonathan's family line went on for generations. A promise that God takes up is not lost when the people who made it are gone.

The Edomite and the Judahite prince bring a sober reminder. Early prominence, a royal mother, and a high place in a family list did not decide either man's significance. Esau's line rose quickly and Jacob's line waited. Rehoboam's choice was driven by preference, and God still kept His word to David. God's purposes are not hostage to human advantage or human favoritism.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:5, 18 – Jeush, son of Esau and Aholibamah, born in Canaan and later a chief of Edom (36:5 indexed separately under H3274).

  • Genesis 36:31 – Kings in Edom before Israel had a king, which highlights the contrast between the two lines.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:35 – The Chronicler includes Esau's son in his survey of the nations.

  • 1 Chronicles 23:10–11 – Jeush the Gershonite Levite; his small family is joined with his brother's for service.

  • 1 Chronicles 8:33–40 – Jeush, son of Eshek, in Saul's line through Jonathan and Merib-Baal.

  • 1 Samuel 20:14–17; 2 Samuel 9:1, 7 – David's covenant with Jonathan and the kindness he showed to Jonathan's house.

  • 2 Chronicles 11:18–23 – Jeush, son of Rehoboam and Mahalath; Abijah is elevated over his brothers.

  • Matthew 1:7 – The royal line to Christ continues through Abijah.

  • Romans 9:11–12 – God's purpose in election, illustrated by Jacob and Esau.
