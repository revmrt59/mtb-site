FOREST, WOODS, HONEYCOMB - (yaʻar, Strong's H3293)

Summary

The Hebrew word yaʻar means a forest, woods, or thicket. It describes the dense, uncultivated land beyond the farmer's fields and the city walls, where trees grow thick, paths disappear, and lions and bears make their home. The same Hebrew spelling, indexed under the same Strong's number, also includes the word the NKJV translates "honeycomb" in Song of Solomon 5:1. Scripture uses yaʻar for real places, such as the woods of Ephraim where Absalom's army perished. It also uses the forest as a picture of proud empires that God cuts down, of holy places that sin turns back into wilderness, and of untamed creation singing before its Maker.

The forest's most hopeful moments come in the prophets. In Ezekiel, God promises that under His shepherd His flock "will dwell safely in the wilderness and sleep in the woods" (Ezekiel 34:25). In Isaiah, the barren land waits only "until the Spirit is poured upon us from on high" (Isaiah 32:15). Then ordinary fields grow so lush that they look like forests. The woods that once meant danger and judgment become a place of rest and Spirit-given abundance. The wild places of the earth have never been outside the Lord's rule.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yaʻar is a Hebrew noun used across the historical books, Psalms, the Song of Solomon, and especially the prophets. The word occurs 59 times in 59 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and Isaiah alone has fourteen of them. The NKJV usually translates it "forest" or "woods," and occasionally "wooded," "woodland," or "honeycomb."
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

For an Israelite, the forest was the opposite of the settled world. The field, the vineyard, and the city were cleared, planted, and protected. The forest was dark and pathless, and dangerous animals lived there. Men went into it to cut timber with an axe (Deuteronomy 19:5). New land had to be cleared out of it (Joshua 17:15, 18). Fighting soldiers could lose their way in it. That sharp contrast between cultivated land and wild woods is the key to nearly every symbolic use of the word.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A forest, woods, or thicket: uncultivated land thick with trees and undergrowth. Examples include "the woods of Ephraim" (2 Samuel 18:6) and the bears that "came out of the woods" (2 Kings 2:24).

2. Honeycomb. The bridegroom says, "I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey" (Song of Solomon 5:1). In the NKJV this is the one place the word is translated "honeycomb." Some suggest a link with wild bees hiving in woodland, but that is only a possibility. Hebrew lexicons treat "honeycomb" as a separate entry and discusses 1 Samuel 14:25–26 under it, although the NKJV says "forest" and "woods" there.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Many uses are plain geography: the forest of Hereth where David hid (1 Samuel 22:5), or, as the NKJV renders 1 Samuel 14:25–26, the woods where honey dripped for Saul's army. Even these literal forests carry the sense of untamed ground. In the forest a lion roars over its prey (Amos 3:4), a boar uproots the vine (Psalm 80:13), and bears come out to attack (2 Kings 2:24). In Absalom's revolt the terrain itself became deadly: "the woods devoured more people that day than the sword devoured" (2 Samuel 18:8). David's enemies fell to the land God had made as much as to David's soldiers.

Solomon's great hall, "the House of the Forest of Lebanon" (1 Kings 7:2), took its name from its "four rows of cedar pillars." The name suggests that its rows of cedar columns recalled the forests of Lebanon, and Solomon stored his three hundred gold shields there (1 Kings 10:17).

In poetry and prophecy the forest becomes a picture. It can stand for towering national power, for fuel ready to burn (Psalm 83:14; Isaiah 9:18), for the wilderness that judgment brings back, or for a fruitful world restored. Context decides which picture is in view. A forest in a historical narrative is simply a forest.

4. Theological Significance

The forest shows that no part of creation lies outside God's authority. The wildest ground, which no farmer could tame, obeys Him. The narratives report striking events. Two bears came out of the woods after Elisha "pronounced a curse on them in the name of the LORD" (2 Kings 2:24). In Absalom's battle, "the woods devoured more people that day than the sword devoured" (2 Samuel 18:8). Neither text says God spoke a command to the bears or named the terrain as His judgment, but read theologically, both show wild creation within the reach of God's rule. Its thickets burn when He kindles the fire: "I will kindle a fire in its forest, and it shall devour all things around it" (Jeremiah 21:14). Isaiah adds a sobering twist. Human "wickedness burns as the fire" and will "kindle in the thickets of the forest" (Isaiah 9:18), so sin itself becomes the fire that consumes a people.

The same untamed creation also worships. "Then all the trees of the woods will rejoice before the LORD" (Psalm 96:12). David's song when the ark came to Jerusalem gives the reason: "For He is coming to judge the earth" (1 Chronicles 16:33). Isaiah calls on the forest to "break forth into singing… For the LORD has redeemed Jacob" (Isaiah 44:23). Creation that humans cannot control knows its Creator. The coming of His righteous rule is good news even to the trees.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 10 and Zechariah 11 — The proud forest felled. Assyria boasted that it had stripped nations bare by its own strength (Isaiah 10:13–14). God answers with the image of a logger at work. Fire "will consume the glory of his forest" (Isaiah 10:18). What remains will be "so few in number that a child may write them" (Isaiah 10:19). The Lord will cut down "the thickets of the forest" with iron, and Lebanon will fall (Isaiah 10:34). The empire that looked impenetrable, a forest no army could clear, falls until a child could count the survivors. Zechariah uses the same picture for doomed leadership: "Wail, O oaks of Bashan, for the thick forest has come down" (Zechariah 11:2). However deeply rooted a power seems, it cannot stand against the God who made the trees.

Micah 3:12 — The temple mount turned to wild ground. Micah's sentence on Jerusalem's corrupt rulers reverses all that God had built: "the mountain of the temple like the bare hills of the forest." The place where God set His name, the most cultivated and holy ground in Israel, would go back to scrub and wilderness. Hosea warns of the same undoing when God says of Israel's vines and fig trees, "I will make them a forest" (Hosea 2:12). Sin does more than break rules. It undoes the order and fruitfulness God gives. About a century after Micah, elders of the land quoted this exact prophecy in Jeremiah's defense (Jeremiah 26:18). They recalled that Hezekiah had feared the LORD and sought His favor, and that the LORD relented (Jeremiah 26:19). A warning heeded opened a door of mercy.

Ezekiel 34:25 and Micah 7:14 — Sleeping in the woods. Ezekiel 34 condemns Israel's selfish shepherds and promises that God will shepherd His flock Himself through "My servant David" (Ezekiel 34:23). Then comes the promise: "I will make a covenant of peace with them, and cause wild beasts to cease from the land; and they will dwell safely in the wilderness and sleep in the woods." The woods were where predators lived, so sleeping there is a vivid picture of safety. God does not promise to keep His flock out of the woods. He promises to make the woods safe by causing the wild beasts to cease. Micah prays in the same spirit for the flock "who dwell solitarily in a woodland" (Micah 7:14). He asks the Lord to shepherd them there with His staff and lead them into rich pasture.

Isaiah 32:15 and 29:17 — The Spirit and the forest of abundance. Isaiah 32 describes a land stripped of its joy, its palaces deserted and its fields bare. The desolation has a limit: "Until the Spirit is poured upon us from on high, and the wilderness becomes a fruitful field, and the fruitful field is counted as a forest." The growth comes in two stages. Barren wilderness becomes farmland, and farmland grows so lush that it looks like a forest. The forest now pictures overflowing life produced by God's Spirit, where elsewhere it pictured human pride. Isaiah 29:17 gives the same reversal: proud Lebanon becomes a field, "and the fruitful field be esteemed as a forest."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah's forest passages lead directly to the Messiah. Immediately after the Lord fells Assyria's forest (Isaiah 10:34), Isaiah writes that "a Rod from the stem of Jesse" will spring up (Isaiah 11:1). Upon this King "the Spirit of the LORD shall rest" (Isaiah 11:2). The proud forest is cut down, and from what looked like a dead stump of David's family God raises His King. Jesus is that Branch. He is anointed with the Spirit, and at Pentecost the exalted Jesus poured out the Spirit (Acts 2:33). Peter explained that outpouring by citing the prophet Joel (Acts 2:16–17). Isaiah 32:15 is a parallel promise of the Spirit poured out from on high, and its fields that grow into forests make a fitting picture of the life of Christ's kingdom.

Ezekiel's promised shepherd is fulfilled in Jesus, who calls Himself the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11). He prayed not that the Father would take His people out of the world, but that He would "keep them from the evil one" (John 17:15). His protection goes with them while they remain in a world still full of wolves.

Psalm 132 adds a quieter thread. Remembering David's search for the ark, the psalm says, "We found it in the fields of the woods" (Psalm 132:6). This is most likely a poetic allusion to Kirjath Jearim, a town whose name means city of forests. The ark "remained in Kirjath Jearim a long time" (1 Samuel 7:2). Twenty years into that stay, "all the house of Israel lamented after the LORD," and Samuel called the people to return to Him with all their hearts (1 Samuel 7:2–3). Much later, after Saul's reign, the anointed king David brought the ark out of the house of Abinadab (2 Samuel 6:2–3) and, after a costly delay, carried God's presence up to Zion, the City of David (2 Samuel 5:7; 6:12). The same psalm goes on to God's oath that a son of David would sit on David's throne (Psalm 132:11, 17). The forest-imagery story ends with the trees rejoicing because the King "is coming to judge the earth" (Psalm 96:13).

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 22:8 is a warning every believer needs. When Jerusalem was threatened, "You looked in that day to the armor of the House of the Forest." Solomon's cedar hall had once displayed God's blessing. Generations later Judah treated it as an armory to trust instead of the Lord, and Isaiah adds that they "did not look to its Maker" (Isaiah 22:11). Believers build their own houses of the forest, such as savings, reputations, and careful plans, and then look to them in the day of trouble. The lesson of the passage is to look to the God who commands the woods.

Ezekiel 34:25 and 28 promise God's flock, under His shepherd, a future covenant of peace: God will "cause wild beasts to cease from the land," and His sheep will "sleep in the woods" with "no one" to "make them afraid." Sleeping in exposed country is a vivid picture of safety and trust. By application, the passage can encourage anyone whose life has wandered into dark woods, such as illness, grief, or opposition. It does not say that every physical danger has already been removed from a believer's life. It does show the kind of Shepherd God is, and Jesus prays that His people be kept from the evil one while they remain in the world (John 17:15). David's own story offers an example. When the prophet Gad sent him out of the stronghold, he went into the forest of Hereth (1 Samuel 22:5). The rejected but anointed king found that God's guidance could lead into the woods and still keep him there.

Isaiah 32:15 gives hope for barren seasons. The drought had an "until." The Spirit's work does not merely patch up a dry life. It produces growth so full that a field looks like a forest. If the honeycomb is linked with woodland hives, it fits the same pattern: God can store sweetness in places that look wild and forbidding to us.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 19:5 — A man cutting timber in the woods kills his neighbor when the axe head flies off. The forest was ordinary workplace as well as wilderness, and this accident is an example of the unintentional killing for which God provided cities of refuge, so that the manslayer could escape the avenger of blood (Deuteronomy 19:4–6).

  • Joshua 17:15, 18 — Joshua tells the tribe of Joseph to clear the forested hill country for themselves. The land God gave still had to be cleared and worked in faith.

  • Psalm 50:10 — God claims every beast of the forest as His own. He needs nothing from His worshipers, because the wild already belongs to Him.

  • Psalm 104:20 — When God brings darkness, the beasts of the forest creep out. Even night in the woods follows His design.

  • Jeremiah 10:3 — An idol begins as a tree cut from the forest. Its worshipers bow to timber that God Himself grew.

  • Ezekiel 15:2, 6 — Compared with the trees of the forest, vine wood is useless except as fuel. Jerusalem, God's vine, had become fit only for burning.
