BE BEAUTIFUL, FAIR, DECORATE - (yâphâh, Strong's H3302)

Summary

The Hebrew verb yâphâh means to be beautiful or to be fair, and in some settings to make beautiful or to decorate. Scripture uses it only eight times, and each use carries weight. It describes the King of Psalm 45, who is "fairer than the sons of men." It voices a bridegroom's delight in his bride in the Song of Solomon. It also appears in the prophets, where Ezekiel shows the Lord turning an abandoned infant into a woman to whom He says, "You were exceedingly beautiful," and Jeremiah exposes people who decorate idols and paint their faces to win foreign allies.

Together these passages teach that true beauty comes from God. He possesses it fully in His anointed King. He gives it freely to people who have nothing to offer Him, and He takes real delight in what He has made lovely. Beauty that people manufacture to cover spiritual emptiness fails, and even God-given splendor becomes a trap when its owner forgets where it came from.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâphâh is a Hebrew verb. Its close relative, the adjective yâpheh (H3303), is the more common word for a beautiful person or object, used of people such as Sarah, David, and Esther. The verb appears only in poetry and prophecy: once in Psalms, three times in the Song of Solomon, twice in Jeremiah, and twice in Ezekiel. It never appears in narrative accounts. Because it is a verb, it tends to describe beauty as something that happens: a person is lovely, grows lovely, is made lovely, or tries to make something lovely.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To be beautiful or fair, describing a person's or thing's loveliness, often in an exclamation of admiration: "How fair is your love" (Song of Solomon 4:10).

2. To become beautiful, describing beauty that grows or is brought about: "You were exceedingly beautiful" (Ezekiel 16:13).

3. To make beautiful, adorn, or decorate, describing an effort to beautify something or oneself: craftsmen "decorate it with silver and gold" (Jeremiah 10:4), and Judah tries to "make yourself fair" (Jeremiah 4:30).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb determines much of its meaning. When the word describes the King or the bride, it expresses admiration. When it describes Jerusalem in Ezekiel 16 or the cedar in Ezekiel 31, the context makes clear that God produced the beauty. When human beings perform the action, as in Jeremiah, the beautifying is decoration laid over something dead or false.

Jeremiah 4:30 shows how one construction changes the picture. "Make yourself fair" means that Judah is both the one who adorns and the one being adorned. She dresses herself up. The prophet's verdict, "In vain," falls on that self-made beauty. Ezekiel 16 is the opposite case. Jerusalem's beauty there is the result of everything the Lord has done for her.

4. Theological Significance

The uses of yâphâh reveal that beauty belongs to God, and He gives it. The King's loveliness in Psalm 45 is joined to grace and righteousness. Jerusalem's loveliness in Ezekiel 16 is borrowed splendor. Even Assyria's grandeur in Ezekiel 31 came from His hand.

This theme leaves no room for human pride. Beauty that people assemble for themselves, whether by covering an idol with gold or by painting the face to attract a protector, cannot give life or security. Received beauty also carries a responsibility. The person who receives it must keep looking to the Giver, because the gift that once displayed God's goodness can become the ground of judgment if it is turned into self-worship.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 45:2 — The fairest King. Psalm 45 is "A Song of Love" for a royal wedding (v. 1). It opens its praise with "You are fairer than the sons of men; Grace is poured upon Your lips; Therefore God has blessed You forever." The King's beauty is tied to His speech, to His cause of "truth, humility, and righteousness" (v. 4), and to His character: "You love righteousness and hate wickedness" (v. 7). The psalm describes moral splendor that shows in the way He speaks and rules.

Ezekiel 16:13 — Beauty given to the unworthy. Ezekiel describes Jerusalem as a newborn girl, thrown into an open field, unwashed, and left to die (16:4–5). Nothing about her attracts anyone. The Lord passes by, tells her to live, washes her, clothes her, and adorns her: "Thus you were adorned with gold and silver... You were exceedingly beautiful, and succeeded to royalty." The next verse gives the source of her fame among the nations. Her beauty was perfect because of the splendor the Lord Himself had placed on her (16:14). The Lord did not choose her because she was lovely. She became lovely because He chose her. Her later unfaithfulness, which takes up the rest of the chapter, is so grievous because she treated His gift as her own (16:15).

Song of Solomon 4:10 — Beauty that is enjoyed. "How fair is your love, My sister, my spouse! How much better than wine is your love." The bridegroom has just said, "You have ravished my heart... With one look of your eyes" (4:9). Here yâphâh belongs to delight. The bride is seen, praised, and enjoyed. Beauty in Scripture has a relational purpose: it is meant to be received with joy by the one who loves.

Jeremiah 4:30 — The painted face. As the enemy approaches, Judah dresses in crimson, puts on gold ornaments, and enlarges her eyes with paint to win back her foreign "lovers." The Lord answers, "In vain you will make yourself fair; Your lovers will despise you; They will seek your life." The nations she courts are the nations that will destroy her. Jeremiah 10:4 uses the same verb for craftsmen who decorate a carved idol with silver and gold, then nail it down "So that it will not topple." In both passages, decoration covers a deadness it cannot cure.

Ezekiel 31:7 — When the gift becomes a snare. Ezekiel pictures Assyria as a great cedar, beautiful in its height and in the length of its branches, because its roots reached abundant waters. The Lord says that He made it beautiful (31:9). Its heart was then lifted up in its height (31:10), so the Lord handed it over to ruin. Assyria's beauty was real and God-given, and its fall came when the tree admired itself instead of the One who watered it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hebrews 1:8–9 applies Psalm 45:6–7 directly to the Son: "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever." The King who is "fairer than the sons of men" is Jesus. Isaiah 53:2, which uses different Hebrew words, says of the Servant, "There is no beauty that we should desire Him." Both statements are true of Christ. In His suffering, He was a root out of dry ground whom people despised. In His exaltation, He is the anointed King whose grace and righteousness surpass everyone else. The fairest One chose to be counted unlovely.

Through that humiliation the story of Ezekiel 16 reaches its fulfillment. Paul writes that Christ "loved the church and gave Himself for her, that He might sanctify and cleanse her with the washing of water by the word, that He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle" (Ephesians 5:25–27). Ezekiel's foundling, washed and robed by the Lord, foreshadows the church, washed and made glorious by Christ. Ezekiel does not announce this connection as a prophecy, but the pattern matches closely: the Lord finds someone with nothing, washes her, makes her beautiful, and brings her into a royal position. Paul also links marriage to "Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32). That link gives the Song of Solomon's delight a larger setting. The Bridegroom who cleanses His bride also rejoices over her.

7. Spirit & Life

Ezekiel 16 strips away every claim to self-made attractiveness before God. No one is chosen for their loveliness. Believers come to Him as the infant in the field came, unwashed and helpless. Whatever beauty they now have is His splendor bestowed on them. This truth frees believers from the exhausting work of Jeremiah 4:30, the effort to dress up and win approval, whether from people or from God. That painted face never works. Believers do not need to adorn themselves. They need to be washed.

The Song of Solomon adds something many believers struggle to accept. Having cleansed His people, the Lord does not merely tolerate them. "How fair is your love" is the language of real delight. A Christian can stop viewing God as someone who puts up with them reluctantly and can begin to receive His joy over the people He has made lovely.

Ezekiel 31 gives the needed warning. The fruit of grace, such as gifts, growth, influence, and spiritual maturity, can tempt the heart to rise like the cedar's. When a believer begins to admire the tree instead of the waters that feed its roots, that believer is repeating Assyria's sin. The safeguard is to keep every beauty connected to its source. Our beauty is borrowed, and the One who lends it is the King "fairer than the sons of men."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Song of Solomon 7:1 — The bridegroom marvels at the beauty of his bride's feet in sandals. His praise moves through her whole person, attentive and unhurried.

  • Song of Solomon 7:6 — He exclaims how fair and pleasant she is in her delights. Yâphâh is paired here with pleasure, which shows that beauty is meant to be enjoyed.

  • Jeremiah 10:4 — "They decorate it with silver and gold; They fasten it with nails and hammers So that it will not topple." The idol needs gold to look worthy and nails to stay standing.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3303 (yâpheh) — lexical relative: the adjective beautiful, fair, the more common word for describing lovely people and things, such as Sarah, David, and Esther.
