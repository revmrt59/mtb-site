GO OUT, COME OUT, BRING OUT, GO FORTH - (yâtsâʼ, Strong's H3318)

Summary

Yâtsâʼ is one of the Old Testament's everyday verbs of movement. It means to go out or come out of a place, and in its causative use it means to bring someone or something out. People go out of tents, cities, and countries. Armies go out to battle. Slaves go out free. Children come from their parents' bodies, plants come up from the ground, water flows from a source, and words come out of a mouth. The word points to the place of origin or separation more than to the destination.

Its most important use is God's own action. He brought Israel out of Egypt, and that act became the basis of Israel's identity, worship, and hope. The prophets carried the word forward. God's word goes forth and accomplishes what He sends it to do. A Ruler will come forth from little Bethlehem and from the stump of Jesse. A river will flow out from God's house and bring life wherever it flows, though Ezekiel notes that its swamps and marshes are left to salt (Ezekiel 47:11). Followed through Scripture, yâtsâʼ shows a God who delivers His people, calls them out of defilement, and sends life outward from His presence. These lines meet in Jesus Christ.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâtsâʼ is a common Hebrew verb found in nearly every book of the Old Testament. It is especially frequent in Exodus, Deuteronomy, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. Most uses are ordinary. Rebekah comes out to draw water (Genesis 24:15), Dinah goes out to see the women of the land (Genesis 34:1), and kings go out to war (2 Samuel 11:1). Because the word belongs so firmly to daily life, its theological uses are concrete. When the Bible says God "brought out" His people, it uses the same verb as for leading someone out a door.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is movement outward from a place, source, or condition. Context decides the nuance.

1. To go out, come out, or depart: leaving a place or setting out, as when Abram departed from Haran (Genesis 12:4) or soldiers went out to fight.

2. To bring out or lead out: the causative sense, where someone else causes the movement. God tells Moses, "bring My people, the children of Israel, out of Egypt" (Exodus 3:10).

3. To come forth or issue from a source: offspring (Genesis 15:4, "one who will come from your own body"), plants from the earth (Genesis 1:12), water from a rock, words from a mouth.

4. To go out free or be released: a Hebrew servant goes out free in the seventh year (Exodus 21:2), and property is released in the Jubilee (Leviticus 25:28).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject changes the weight of the word. When people are the subject, yâtsâʼ usually describes ordinary movement or military action. When God is the subject of the causative sense, it means that He brings people out. In the exodus passages that is deliverance, but God can also bring people out for judgment (Ezekiel 11:7–10). The saving sense comes from the passage, not from the verb form itself. The word appears again and again in the refrain about the God who brought Israel out of the land of Egypt (Exodus 20:2; Leviticus 25:38; Daniel 9:15). Exodus 20:2 also calls Egypt "the house of bondage."

The verb is often paired with "come in" (bôʼ) to describe a person's whole life and work. Moses asked God for a successor who would go out and come in before the people, so that they would not be like sheep without a shepherd (Numbers 27:17). Israel loved David because he went out and came in before them (1 Samuel 18:16). He led from the front and was seen in battle. Young Solomon confessed he did not know how to go out or come in (1 Kings 3:7) and asked for wisdom for exactly that task (2 Chronicles 1:10). The same pairing turns into a promise of protection in Psalm 121:8, where the LORD preserves His people's going out and coming in. Used this way, the phrase covers all of life, much as we might say "everything you do."

4. Theological Significance

The causative uses of yâtsâʼ show that salvation begins with God. Israel did not walk out of Egypt by its own strength. Again and again God says, "I brought you out." The same pattern appears earlier, when the LORD brought Abram out of Ur (Genesis 15:7) and then brought him outside to count the stars (Genesis 15:5). God leads people out of where they are into what He has promised.

What goes out from God also carries His power. "So shall My word be that goes forth from My mouth; It shall not return to Me void" (Isaiah 55:11). Isaiah compares this word to rain that makes the earth "bring forth and bud" (55:10). That recalls creation, when "the earth brought forth grass" at God's command (Genesis 1:12). God's word is not a report on events. It goes out on an errand and comes back with the errand done. The law goes forth from Zion (Isaiah 2:3), and judgment goes forth like a storm (Jeremiah 23:19). Whatever proceeds from God accomplishes His purpose.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 3:10–12 and Deuteronomy 4:20: brought out to belong. At the burning bush, God sends Moses to "bring My people... out of Egypt" (Exodus 3:10). The sign that follows shows the purpose of the deliverance: "When you have brought the people out of Egypt, you shall serve God on this mountain" (3:12). The exodus was a movement toward God and not only an escape. Deuteronomy calls Egypt "the iron furnace" and says the LORD "brought you out of the iron furnace, out of Egypt, to be His people" (4:20). Israel was helpless in a fire it could not leave. God's hand alone brought it out, and He brought it out in order to make it His own. Centuries later, Daniel confessed national sin by appealing to this same act: the God "who brought Your people out of the land of Egypt with a mighty hand, and made Yourself a name" (Daniel 9:15). Because the exodus rested on God's name and not on Israel's merit, Daniel could plead it again.

Isaiah 11:1, Micah 5:2, Jeremiah 30:21: the Ruler who comes forth. The prophets use yâtsâʼ for the arrival of the promised King, and each time the source is unlikely. "There shall come forth a Rod from the stem of Jesse" (Isaiah 11:1). Isaiah names a stump. Davidic kings still reigned in Isaiah's day, but the prophecy looks to a time when the dynasty would be cut down and humbled like a felled tree. Micah names a village: "Though you are little among the thousands of Judah, Yet out of you shall come forth to Me The One to be Ruler in Israel" (Micah 5:2). The same verse, using a related noun (H4163), says this Ruler's goings forth are "from of old, From everlasting." He comes out of a small town, yet His origins reach back before time. Jeremiah adds that "their governor shall come from their midst" (30:21). He will be one of the people, and God will bring Him near to approach Him. Together these texts show how God works. He brings the King out of a cut-down stump, an overlooked village, and the ranks of the ordinary.

Isaiah 52:11–12: going out clean. Isaiah tells the exiles to leave Babylon: "Go out from there, Touch no unclean thing; Go out from the midst of her, Be clean, You who bear the vessels of the LORD" (52:11). The verb appears twice, and the command joins leaving with purity. The next verse echoes the first exodus and contrasts with it. This time they will not go out in haste or flight, because the LORD will go before them and be their rear guard (52:12). The second departure is calmer than the first, but it is just as holy.

Ezekiel 47:1–12: the river from the sanctuary. Ezekiel sees water flowing out from under the threshold of the temple (47:1). It deepens from ankle depth to a river too deep to cross. It flows toward the east, reaches the Dead Sea, and heals it, and wherever it goes, things live (47:8–9). Only its swamps and marshes "will not be healed; they will be given over to salt" (47:11). Trees on its banks bear fruit every month because their water flows from the sanctuary (47:12). The pattern began earlier. A river went out of Eden to water the garden (Genesis 2:10). In the wilderness, water came out of the rock abundantly (Numbers 20:11; Psalm 78:16). Joel saw a fountain flowing from the house of the LORD (Joel 3:18), and Zechariah saw living waters flowing from Jerusalem (Zechariah 14:8). In each case the source is God's presence, and the result is life where there was barrenness.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Matthew states the fulfillment of Micah plainly. Jesus is born in Bethlehem because "out of you shall come a Ruler Who will shepherd My people Israel" (Matthew 2:6). Isaiah's Servant will "bring forth justice" (Isaiah 42:1, 3) and "bring out prisoners from the prison" (42:7). Matthew applies the opening of that Servant song to Jesus (Matthew 12:18–21). The God who brought Israel out of Egypt brings His people out of a deeper bondage through His Son.

The shepherd language points the same way. Moses prayed for a leader who would go out and come in before the flock. Jesus calls Himself the door through whom His sheep go in and out and find pasture (John 10:9). The river imagery also reaches its goal in Him. Jesus promised that rivers of living water would flow from those who believe in Him (John 7:38), and Revelation ends with the river of the water of life proceeding from the throne of God and of the Lamb (Revelation 22:1).

One further line deserves attention. Under the law, the bodies of the sin offerings were carried outside the camp and burned (Leviticus 4:12; 16:27), and yâtsâʼ describes that carrying out. Hebrews sees Jesus suffering outside the gate and calls believers to go forth to Him outside the camp, bearing His reproach (Hebrews 13:11–13). The call to come out (2 Corinthians 6:17; Revelation 18:4) is a call to follow Christ, who went out first.

7. Spirit & Life

Job could not find God. He looked forward and backward, to the left and to the right (Job 23:8–9). Yet he said, "He knows the way that I take; When He has tested me, I shall come forth as gold" (23:10). Job's word is the same verb used for Israel's exodus, and Deuteronomy calls Egypt an iron furnace. The text does not show Job consciously drawing on either, but the shared imagery runs across the canon. Job saw his testing as a fire he would come out of, and he expected it to show his integrity, not to prove that he had been impure. He did not yet see God in it, but he trusted that God saw him and that the testing had an end and a purpose. Peter writes in the same way about faith that is tested by fire and found to praise at Christ's appearing (1 Peter 1:7). A believer in the fire can say what Job said, even before feeling God's presence.

The word also teaches that what comes out of us shows what is in us. A fool vents all his feelings (Proverbs 29:11), and the preacher warns against letting the heart hurry to utter a word before God (Ecclesiastes 5:2). God's words go out and accomplish good. When our words go out, they should be sent with the same care.

God's presence never stays sealed in. Water from a rock, a river from the temple, a King from a village: what God holds, He sends out. A life near Him is meant to bear fruit. That is not a promise that believers never pass through dry or delayed seasons, but like the trees on Ezekiel's riverbank, their fruit comes because their water flows from the sanctuary.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 15:4. God promises Abram an heir "who will come from your own body," and the specific promise of an heir is spoken with this word.

  • Exodus 21:2. A Hebrew servant goes out free in the seventh year. The verb Israel used for its own release became the law that limited servitude among its people.

  • Numbers 16:27, 35. Dathan and Abiram came out to stand defiantly at their tent doors, and then fire came out from the LORD. The same word describes both the rebellion and the judgment.

  • Psalm 19:4–5. The heavens' message goes out through all the earth, and the sun comes out like a bridegroom from his chamber.

  • Malachi 4:2. Those who fear God's name will go out and grow fat like stall-fed calves when the Sun of Righteousness rises with healing.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H935 (bôʼ), "to come in, go in." This is a canonical pairing, not a lexical relative. "Going out and coming in" describes the whole of a person's life and leadership (Numbers 27:17; 1 Samuel 18:16; Psalm 121:8).
