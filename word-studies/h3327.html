ISAAC - (Yitschâq, Strong's H3327)

Summary

Isaac was the son God promised to Abraham and Sarah long after they were too old to have children. He married Rebekah, became the father of Esau and Jacob, and is the middle name in the title God gave Himself: "the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob." His name means "he laughs" or "laughter," from the Hebrew verb for laughing. Genesis builds his story around that word. Abraham fell on his face and laughed at the promise, Sarah laughed in disbelief, and when the child came Sarah confessed that God had made her laugh. Isaac's very name kept alive the memory that God had done what everyone agreed could not be done.

Isaac's life has three great scenes. As a son he was bound on the altar on Mount Moriah and spared when God provided a ram in his place (Genesis 22). As a grown man he reopened his father's wells, received God's promise again at Beersheba, and built an altar there (Genesis 26). As an old and blind father he tried to give the blessing to his favorite son, Esau, and then trembled when he realized God's purpose had gone to Jacob (Genesis 27). He was neither a wanderer like Abraham nor a wrestler like Jacob, yet the whole line of promise narrowed to him. The New Testament treats him as the "child of promise" whose birth pictures the way God's children are born today (Galatians 4:28). Hebrews says that Abraham received him back from the altar "in a figurative sense" as though from the dead (Hebrews 11:19), and that scene points ahead to the Father who gave His only begotten Son.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Isaac is a single individual: the son of Abraham and Sarah, the half-brother of Ishmael, the husband of Rebekah, and the father of the twins Esau and Jacob. Abraham was a hundred years old when Isaac was born (Genesis 21:5), and Sarah was ninety (Genesis 17:17). Isaac married at forty (Genesis 25:20), became a father at sixty (Genesis 25:26), and died at one hundred eighty (Genesis 35:28). Every occurrence of this Hebrew name refers to this one man, either directly in the Genesis narrative or through the title "the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob." The related spelling H3446, indexed separately, appears in Psalm 105:9, Jeremiah 33:26, and Amos 7:9, 16, including Amos's poetic use of the name for the northern kingdom (see below).

The name Yitschâq comes from the Hebrew verb tsachaq, "to laugh." It is usually understood as "he laughs," with "laughter" as a natural English rendering. The meaning is not guesswork. Genesis plays on it again and again.

  • When God promised a son by Sarah, Abraham fell on his face and laughed, wondering whether a man of a hundred and a woman of ninety could have a child (Genesis 17:17). God answered by naming the boy: "Sarah your wife shall bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac; I will establish My covenant with him for an everlasting covenant, and with his descendants after him" (Genesis 17:19).

  • Sarah laughed inwardly when she overheard the promise. The Lord asked why she laughed and whether anything was too hard for Him (Genesis 18:12–15).

  • At Isaac's birth Sarah said that God had made her laugh and that everyone who heard would laugh with her (Genesis 21:6).

  • At the feast for Isaac's weaning, Sarah saw Ishmael "scoffing," a form of the same verb (Genesis 21:9).

  • Years later in Gerar, Abimelech saw Isaac "showing endearment" to Rebekah, again a form of the same root, and realized she was his wife (Genesis 26:8).

The name therefore begins with the laughter of people who found the promise hard to believe and ends with the laughter of people who saw it fulfilled. The name itself does not explain Isaac's character. It is a memorial of what God did in giving him.

Isaac spent his whole life in the land of Canaan, mostly in the southern region called the Negev. Abraham came from Mesopotamia and went down into Egypt, and Jacob spent twenty years in Haran and died in Egypt. Isaac lived at Beer Lahai Roi (Genesis 24:62; 25:11), at Gerar (Genesis 26:6), at Beersheba (Genesis 26:23–33), and finally at Mamre near Hebron (Genesis 35:27). When famine came, God told him not to go down to Egypt but to stay in the land He would show him (Genesis 26:2–3).

A related spelling of the name, indexed separately as H3446, appears in Psalm 105:9, Jeremiah 33:26, and Amos 7:9 and 7:16. In Amos, "Isaac" stands poetically for the northern kingdom of Israel; that collective use belongs to H3446, not to a separate sense of H3327. Several verses in Genesis and Chronicles name Isaac twice, so the H3327 verses contain more mentions of his name than verses. The New Testament uses the Greek form Isaak (G2464). Both forms belong to the larger biblical picture but are not counted among the occurrences of H3327.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Promised Birth (Genesis 17–21)

Isaac's story begins before he is born. Abraham had already fathered Ishmael through Hagar, and he asked God to let Ishmael live before Him (Genesis 17:18). God did not reject Ishmael and promised to bless him. He was firm, though, that His lasting promise would rest on a son not yet conceived, born to Sarah within a year (Genesis 17:19–21). The Lord's question to Sarah, whether anything was too hard for Him, hangs over the whole account (Genesis 18:14).

God kept His word at the set time. Abraham named the child Isaac and circumcised him on the eighth day, as God had commanded (Genesis 21:3–4). Joy soon gave way to tension. At the weaning feast, Ishmael's scoffing led Sarah to demand that Hagar and her son be sent away. Abraham was deeply grieved, but God told him to listen to Sarah, because his seed would be called through Isaac (Genesis 21:8–12). That sentence would echo through Scripture for two thousand years.

The Binding on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22)

Some years later, God tested Abraham by telling him to take his son, his only son Isaac whom he loved, to the land of Moriah and offer him there as a burnt offering (Genesis 22:1–2). On the third day Abraham saw the place in the distance (Genesis 22:4). He laid the wood on Isaac, and he carried the fire and the knife himself (Genesis 22:6). On the way up Isaac spoke the only words he says in the chapter. He saw the fire and the wood and asked where the lamb was. Abraham answered that God would provide for Himself the lamb (Genesis 22:7–8). At the top Abraham bound Isaac and laid him on the wood. The Angel of the Lord stopped his hand, and Abraham offered a ram caught in a thicket in his son's place. He named the place The-LORD-Will-Provide (Genesis 22:9–14).

The chapter is mainly about Abraham's faith, and Hebrews and James both read it that way (Hebrews 11:17; James 2:21). Isaac's part is quieter but real. He was old enough to carry a load of wood up a mountain, and his father was well over a hundred. The text does not describe any struggle. Scripture does not say whether Isaac consented or resisted, nor how old he was. Given his strength and his father's age, it is a plausible inference that he cooperated, but the text keeps its focus on Abraham's faith. Genesis names the land of Moriah and "one of the mountains" there; 2 Chronicles 3:1 later names Mount Moriah as the site of Solomon's temple, without saying it was the very spot of Isaac's altar.

A Bride from Abraham's Family (Genesis 24)

The longest chapter in Genesis tells how Abraham's servant traveled to Abraham's relatives in Mesopotamia to find a wife for Isaac, so that the son of promise would not marry among the Canaanites (Genesis 24:3–4). The servant prayed for a sign, and God answered through Rebekah's kindness at the well (Genesis 24:14–27). Isaac first appears in the chapter near Beer Lahai Roi, the well where God had once seen and rescued Hagar (Genesis 16:13–14). He had gone out to the field in the evening to meditate, and when he looked up he saw the camels coming (Genesis 24:62–64). He took Rebekah into his mother Sarah's tent, loved her, and was comforted after his mother's death (Genesis 24:67). It is one of the tenderest lines in Genesis.

Heir, Husband, and Father (Genesis 25)

Abraham gave all he had to Isaac and sent his other sons away with gifts (Genesis 25:5–6). Isaac and Ishmael buried their father together in the cave of Machpelah (Genesis 25:9). After Abraham's death, God blessed Isaac (Genesis 25:11). Rebekah, like Sarah before her, was barren, and Isaac pleaded with the Lord for his wife. The Lord granted his plea (Genesis 25:21). The couple waited twenty years for children, since Isaac married at forty and the twins were born when he was sixty. Before the birth God told Rebekah that two nations were in her womb and that the older would serve the younger (Genesis 25:23). Esau became a hunter and Jacob a quiet man of the tents. Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game, and Rebekah loved Jacob (Genesis 25:27–28). That divided affection set up the crisis of chapter 27.

Gerar, the Wells, and Beersheba (Genesis 26)

Genesis 26 is the one chapter that belongs to Isaac alone. A famine drove him to Gerar in Philistine territory. There the Lord appeared to him, told him not to go down to Egypt, and confirmed to him personally the oath He had sworn to Abraham: the land, descendants like the stars, and blessing for all nations through his seed (Genesis 26:1–5). The promise was no longer only his father's. It was now spoken to him.

Isaac then repeated his father's failure. Afraid the men of Gerar would kill him for his beautiful wife, he said Rebekah was his sister, just as Abraham had done in Egypt and in Gerar (Genesis 12:10–20; 20:1–18; 26:7). Abimelech discovered the truth when he saw Isaac showing endearment to Rebekah, rebuked him, and ordered his people not to touch them (Genesis 26:8–11). Even the heir of promise carried his father's fear.

God blessed Isaac anyway. He sowed in that land and reaped a hundredfold in the same year, "and the LORD blessed him" (Genesis 26:12). The Philistines envied him and filled with earth the wells Abraham's servants had dug. Isaac dug those wells again and gave them the same names his father had given them (Genesis 26:14–18). When his servants found fresh water, local herdsmen quarreled over it. Isaac named the first disputed well Esek ("contention") and the second Sitnah ("enmity"), and he moved on. At the third well no one quarreled, and he called it Rehoboth, saying that the Lord had made room for them and they would be fruitful in the land (Genesis 26:19–22).

From there he went up to Beersheba. That night the Lord appeared to him, identified Himself as the God of his father Abraham, told him not to fear, and promised to be with him and bless him for Abraham's sake. Isaac built an altar, called on the name of the Lord, pitched his tent, and his servants dug a well (Genesis 26:23–25). Soon Abimelech came to make a treaty, admitting that they had seen plainly that the Lord was with Isaac (Genesis 26:26–31). The same day, Isaac's servants reported water in the new well (Genesis 26:32–33). The chapter closes on a sad note: Esau's marriage to two Hittite women was a grief of mind to Isaac and Rebekah (Genesis 26:34–35).

The Blessing and the Trembling (Genesis 27–28)

When Isaac was old and his eyes were too dim to see, he called Esau and asked for a meal of game so that he could bless him before he died (Genesis 27:1–4). Rebekah overheard and dressed Jacob in Esau's clothes, with goatskins on his hands and neck. Isaac was suspicious. The voice was Jacob's, but the hands felt like Esau's. He asked how the game was found so quickly, and Jacob answered that the Lord had brought it to him (Genesis 27:20–22). Isaac ate, kissed him, smelled his clothes, and blessed him with the dew of heaven, the richness of the earth, and lordship over his brothers (Genesis 27:26–29).

When Esau came in, Isaac trembled very violently and asked who had already come. Then he said that the one he had blessed would indeed be blessed (Genesis 27:30–33). He could not and would not take the blessing back. To Esau he gave a lesser word, one of hard living by the sword and of eventually breaking free from his brother's yoke (Genesis 27:39–40).

Scripture does not tell us whether Rebekah ever told Isaac what God had said before the twins were born, so we cannot say Isaac knowingly set himself against that word. What the text does show is a father led by his appetite and his preference, deceived by his wife and son, and then brought up short by the realization that the blessing had gone where God intended.

The sequel matters. When Isaac sent Jacob away to Paddan Aram to find a wife among Rebekah's family, he blessed him again, this time knowingly. He called on God Almighty to make Jacob fruitful and to give him "the blessing of Abraham," including the land (Genesis 28:1–5). The trembling old man of chapter 27 had come to agree with God.

Later Years and Death (Genesis 35)

Isaac lived far longer than he expected in chapter 27. Jacob returned after more than twenty years and came to his father at Mamre, near Hebron (Genesis 35:27). Isaac died at one hundred eighty, "old and full of days," and Esau and Jacob, now reconciled (Genesis 33), buried him together (Genesis 35:28–29). Jacob later records that Isaac and Rebekah were buried in the cave of Machpelah with Abraham and Sarah (Genesis 49:31).

Isaac's Name in the Rest of the Old Testament

After Genesis 35 Isaac's name lives on mostly in the way God is identified and remembered. Jacob swore by "the Fear of Isaac" (Genesis 31:42, 53). At Beersheba, on his way down to Egypt, Jacob offered sacrifices to "the God of his father Isaac" (Genesis 46:1). Blessing Joseph's sons, he spoke of the God before whom Abraham and Isaac walked, and he asked that the names of Abraham and Isaac be named upon the boys (Genesis 48:15–16). Joseph's dying words recalled the land God swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Genesis 50:24).

In Egypt God heard Israel's groaning and remembered His promise to the three fathers (Exodus 2:24). At the burning bush He told Moses, "I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob," and said this was His name forever (Exodus 3:6, 15). When Israel worshiped the golden calf, Moses pleaded with God to remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel (Exodus 32:13). Leviticus promises that even in exile, if Israel humbles itself, God will remember His promise to Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham (Leviticus 26:42). Deuteronomy repeatedly roots the land and Israel's undeserved inheritance in God's oath to the fathers (Deuteronomy 1:8; 6:10; 9:5, 27; 29:13; 30:20; 34:4). Joshua retells the story with God as the giver: He "gave him Isaac," and to Isaac God gave Jacob and Esau (Joshua 24:3–4).

The title appears at turning points of national prayer. On Mount Carmel, at the evening sacrifice, Elijah prayed, "LORD God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, let it be known this day that You are God in Israel" (1 Kings 18:36). In a time of Syrian oppression the Lord was gracious to Israel "because of His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" (2 Kings 13:23). David prayed to the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel as the temple offerings were gathered (1 Chronicles 29:18). Hezekiah's runners called the scattered northern tribes: "Children of Israel, return to the LORD God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel; then He will return to the remnant of you" (2 Chronicles 30:6).

The Psalms and Prophets carry the same memory. Psalm 105 celebrates "The covenant which He made with Abraham, And His oath to Isaac" (Psalm 105:9; compare 1 Chronicles 16:16). Jeremiah ties God's faithfulness to the descendants of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob to the fixed order of day and night (Jeremiah 33:26). Both of these verses use the related spelling H3446. Amos uses the name differently. Speaking against the northern kingdom, he warns, "The high places of Isaac shall be desolate, And the sanctuaries of Israel shall be laid waste" (Amos 7:9; compare 7:16). The name of the patriarch who built a true altar at Beersheba had become a label for a people whose shrines God would tear down.

Isaac in the New Testament

Isaac appears in both genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1:2; Luke 3:34). Jesus said many would come from east and west to sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 8:11; compare Luke 13:28). Answering the Sadducees, He pointed to God's words at the bush, "I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob," and drew from them that God is the God of the living, not the dead (Matthew 22:32; Mark 12:26–27; Luke 20:37–38). Peter preached that the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob had glorified His servant Jesus (Acts 3:13).

Paul uses Isaac to explain who God's children truly are (Romans 9:6–13; Galatians 4:21–31). Hebrews places him among the faithful, both as the son offered (Hebrews 11:17–19) and as a father who by faith blessed Jacob and Esau concerning things to come (Hebrews 11:20). Hebrews also notes that Abraham lived in tents with Isaac and Jacob, heirs with him of the same promise, waiting for the city whose builder and maker is God (Hebrews 11:9–10). James points to Abraham offering Isaac as faith shown in action (James 2:21–22).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture plainly states that God's promise to Abraham would continue through Isaac and not through Ishmael (Genesis 17:19–21; 21:12). The promise of land, descendants, and blessing for the nations narrowed to one child born when birth was no longer possible. The text makes the point twice over. Isaac's own children also came only after twenty years of barrenness and in answer to prayer (Genesis 25:21). The family line of promise did not run on human strength. It ran on God's word.

Scripture also states that God chose the younger over the older before either twin was born (Genesis 25:23). Paul draws from this that God's purpose stands "not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11–12). Isaac's preference for Esau could not change it. The blessing in Genesis 27 shows this, and Isaac's second, willing blessing of Jacob in Genesis 28 confirms it.

It is a fair implication, though not a direct statement, that Isaac's quieter role is part of the lesson. Much of what Scripture records about him is received rather than achieved. He was given as a son, provided with a ram, brought a bride, confirmed in his father's promise, and blessed in his harvest "for My servant Abraham's sake" (Genesis 26:24). That does not make him a lesser patriarch. It makes him a clear example that God's blessing is a gift passed on by God's faithfulness.

Most of all, God made Isaac part of His own name. For the rest of the Old Testament, when Israel sinned, suffered, or prayed, God's people appealed to the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. God bound His reputation to the oath He had sworn to these men, and He kept it through slavery in Egypt, rebellion at Sinai, the Syrian wars, and Assyrian exile.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The offered son. Hebrews gives the clearest New Testament reading of Genesis 22: "By faith Abraham, when he was tested, offered up Isaac, and he who had received the promises offered up his only begotten son" (Hebrews 11:17). It adds that Abraham was "concluding that God was able to raise him up, even from the dead, from which he also received him in a figurative sense" (Hebrews 11:19). So Scripture itself treats the return of Isaac from the altar as a picture of resurrection.

Beyond that explicit statement, the connections to the cross are strong and drawn from the biblical text itself. Hebrews calls Isaac Abraham's "only begotten son," the same phrase John uses when he writes that God "gave His only begotten Son" (John 3:16). Genesis stresses the father's love for the son he offered (Genesis 22:2). Paul's words that God "did not spare His own Son" (Romans 8:32) are widely heard as an echo of God's praise of Abraham, who had not withheld his son (Genesis 22:12, 16). Isaac carried the wood up the mountain, and Jesus went out "bearing His cross" (John 19:17). Abraham said God would provide the lamb, and John the Baptist would one day point to Jesus as the Lamb of God (John 1:29). Solomon's temple, where sacrifice was offered for centuries, stood on Mount Moriah (2 Chronicles 3:1), traditionally associated with the land of Moriah in Genesis 22. The shared name adds a meaningful canonical connection, although Genesis does not identify Isaac's precise mountain.

There is also a contrast. Isaac was spared and a ram died in his place. When the true Son came, no substitute was found for Him, because He was the substitute.

The "third day" detail in Genesis 22:4 belongs to Abraham's journey, and Hebrews does not draw on it. It may be noted as a resonance with the resurrection, but it should not be pressed as an explicit teaching.

The child of promise. Paul reads the births of Ishmael and Isaac as a pattern for two ways of relating to God. One son was "born according to the flesh," the other "through promise" (Galatians 4:23). Paul says plainly, "Now we, brethren, as Isaac was, are children of promise" (Galatians 4:28). He even sees the old hostility repeated: "as he who was born according to the flesh then persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit, even so it is now" (Galatians 4:29). In Romans 9:7–8 Paul quotes God's word that Abraham's seed would be called in Isaac, and concludes that the children of the promise are counted as the seed. Belonging to God's family has never been a matter of natural descent. It comes by God's promise and the Spirit's work, and that is exactly how believers in Christ enter it.

The miraculous birth. Isaac's birth to a barren mother begins a pattern that runs through Rebekah, Rachel, Samson's mother, Hannah, and Elizabeth. Paul connects it to Isaiah's song for the barren woman: "Rejoice, O barren, You who do not bear!" (Galatians 4:27). The pattern climaxes in a birth more impossible still, as the Son of God is born of a virgin. These births are not identical, and Scripture does not call Sarah a type of Mary. But the same lesson carries through them: God brings His saving line into the world where human ability has run out. Paul also identifies the singular "Seed" of Abraham as Christ (Galatians 3:16), and Isaac stands in that line as the first child of the promise.

The God of the living. Jesus used the name of Isaac to teach the resurrection. Because God called Himself the God of Isaac long after Isaac's death, Isaac must be alive to God (Luke 20:37–38). The man received back from the altar "in a figurative sense" will be raised in fact, and Jesus pictures him at the kingdom feast (Matthew 8:11).

5. Spirit & Life

Isaac's story begins with laughter that held astonishment and doubt. Abraham laughed and Sarah laughed, and God did not cancel His promise. He named the child after their laughter and then turned it into joy. The Lord's question to Sarah, "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" (Genesis 18:14), is the question behind every promise in Scripture, including the promise of new life in Christ. God did correct Sarah when she denied her laughter (Genesis 18:13–15), so unbelief is not simply excused. Yet He kept His promise despite their weakness, and He often fulfills His word in a way that leaves no room for anyone to boast but Him.

Genesis 26 speaks to anyone who feels they live in the shadow of a stronger faith. Isaac did not leave home on a great journey or wrestle with an angel. He dug again the wells his father had dug, called them by his father's names, and found that the water was still there. The Philistines had stopped up the wells his father dug, and Isaac's work was to open them again. Taken as a picture, the scene says that not every generation is called to discover something new. Many are called to recover what an earlier generation knew of God and what neglect or opposition has stopped up. Isaac also refused to fight over Esek and Sitnah. He kept moving until God made room at Rehoboth. Then, at Beersheba, God appeared to him again, as He already had at Gerar (Genesis 26:2–5), and Isaac answered with a renewed personal response. He built his own altar and called on the name of the Lord himself. Even his enemies could see that the Lord was with him (Genesis 26:28).

The same chapter is honest about inherited weakness. Isaac told his father's lie, out of his father's fear, in the same town (Genesis 26:7). A family can hand down its sins as well as its wells. God's response is striking. He rebuked Isaac through a pagan king and then went on blessing him, because His faithfulness rested on His own oath, not on Isaac's record.

Isaac's old age is a sober warning and a real comfort. He let his taste for Esau's game guide his choices, and his family nearly tore apart under the weight of favoritism and deceit. Yet when he saw what God had done, he trembled and submitted: the one he had blessed would be blessed. Then he blessed Jacob again with open eyes of faith (Genesis 28:3–4). Hebrews remembers him for the faith with which he blessed Jacob and Esau concerning things to come, not for his failure (Hebrews 11:20). God is able to overrule our misdirected wills, and there is grace for the believer who trembles, yields, and agrees with Him.

Jacob called God "the Fear of Isaac" (Genesis 31:42). Scripture does not explain the title. It is fitting for a man who once lay bound on an altar and rose from it alive, and who trembled again when God's purpose overturned his own. That kind of fear does not drive a person away from God. It is the awe of someone who has seen God provide the lamb.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:15–21; 18:9–15; 21:1–12 — The promised birth, the naming laughter, and God's word that Abraham's seed would be called in Isaac.

  • Genesis 22:1–19 — The binding of Isaac, the ram provided in his place, and The-LORD-Will-Provide.

  • Genesis 24:62–67 — Isaac meditating in the field and receiving Rebekah.

  • Genesis 25:19–28 — Prayer for a barren wife, God's word about the twins, and the parents' divided love.

  • Genesis 26 — The oath confirmed to Isaac, his repeated lie, the hundredfold harvest, the reopened wells, and the altar at Beersheba.

  • Genesis 27:1–40; 28:1–5 — The misdirected blessing, Isaac's trembling, and his later willing blessing of Jacob.

  • Genesis 31:42, 53 — "The Fear of Isaac" as a name for God.

  • Genesis 35:27–29 — Isaac's death and burial by Esau and Jacob.

  • Exodus 3:6, 15; 1 Kings 18:36; 2 Chronicles 30:6; Psalm 105:9 — Isaac's name built into God's self-identification and Israel's prayers.

  • Amos 7:9, 16 — "Isaac" (separately indexed spelling, H3446) as a name for the northern kingdom under judgment.

  • Luke 20:37–38; Matthew 8:11 — Jesus uses the God of Isaac to teach resurrection and pictures Isaac at the kingdom feast.

  • Romans 9:6–13 — The children of promise and God's choice of Jacob before birth.

  • Galatians 4:21–31 — Isaac as the child of promise, born according to the Spirit, and the pattern for believers.

  • Hebrews 11:9, 17–20 — Isaac as heir of the promise, as the son received back "in a figurative sense," and as a father who blessed by faith.

  • James 2:21–22 — Abraham's offering of Isaac as faith shown in action.
