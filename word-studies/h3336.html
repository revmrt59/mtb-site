INTENT, INCLINATION, FRAME - (yêtser, Strong's H3336)

Summary

The Hebrew word yêtser means "something formed." Sometimes it refers to a physical thing that has been shaped, such as a clay pot, a carved idol, or the human body made from dust. More often it refers to what the heart forms inwardly: the settled bent of a person's thoughts, purposes and desires. The NKJV translates it as "intent," "imagination," "inclination," "mind," "frame," "formed," and "mold." It appears only nine times, yet those verses follow humanity from Noah's day to the prophets.

Scripture uses yêtser to tell the truth about us on two levels. Our bodies are frail, and God "knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust" (Psalm 103:14). Our inner inclinations are worse than frail. The heart's "imagination" is "evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21). The word also carries hope. God searches this inner bent, He can "fix" it toward Himself (1 Chronicles 29:18), and He keeps in perfect peace the one whose mind is "stayed" on Him (Isaiah 26:3). The Potter who formed us also has mercy on what He has formed.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yêtser is a noun related to the verb yatsar, "to form" or "fashion." That verb describes a potter shaping clay. It also describes God forming man "of the dust of the ground" (Genesis 2:7). The noun appears in Genesis, Deuteronomy, 1 Chronicles, Psalms, Isaiah and Habakkuk. Its settings are serious ones: God's judgment at the flood, Moses' final days, David's charge to Solomon, a psalm of mercy, and prophetic warnings against pride and idols.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A formed thing or frame. This is a physical object that has been shaped, such as the human body made from dust (Psalm 103:14), a clay vessel (Isaiah 29:16), or a molded idol (Habakkuk 2:18).

2. An inward inclination or intent. This is the settled tendency of the heart and mind, the purposes and imaginings a person forms inwardly (Genesis 6:5; 1 Chronicles 28:9; Isaiah 26:3).

In the second sense, yêtser is more than a passing thought. It is the underlying bent from which particular thoughts arise.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the word refers to inner life, it is usually tied to the heart and its "thoughts." Genesis 6:5 speaks of "every intent of the thoughts of his heart." David uses nearly the same phrase in 1 Chronicles 28:9 and 29:18. The pairing shows that yêtser is the source and thoughts are what it produces.

The setting decides whether the word speaks of an inner bent or a physical object. In Deuteronomy 31:21 it describes the "inclination" of Israel's behavior, the direction their conduct would take. In Isaiah 29:16 and Habakkuk 2:18 it describes physical objects. Isaiah sets it beside the related verb to make a point: "the thing formed" stands opposite "him who formed it."

4. Theological Significance

Every use of yêtser places us in the position of something formed and God in the position of the One who forms. Isaiah 29:16 presses this point against people who plan in secret as though God could not see: "Surely you have things turned around! … Shall the thing formed say of him who formed it, 'He has no understanding'?" A pot that lectures its potter is absurd. Human pride that questions God's wisdom is just as absurd. Paul later makes the same appeal with the same image (Romans 9:20–21).

The word also shows that God knows people from the inside. He "searches all hearts and understands all the intent of the thoughts" (1 Chronicles 28:9). Nothing in our inner workings is hidden from the One who made us. That should sober a careless heart, and it should comfort a weak one.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 6:5 and 8:21: the same diagnosis before and after the flood. Before the flood, God saw that "every intent of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually." After the flood, as Noah's offering rose before Him, God repeated the diagnosis: "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth." This time, however, the diagnosis came with a promise never again to curse the ground or destroy every living thing. The flood swept away a violent generation, but it did not change the human yêtser. The same bent came off the ark inside Noah's family. Judgment can remove sinners, but it cannot remake a heart. God's patience after the flood rests on His own mercy, because humanity had not improved.

Deuteronomy 31:21: God knows the bent before the behavior. As Moses neared death, God gave him a song to stand as a witness against Israel's coming idolatry: "I know the inclination of their behavior today, even before I have brought them to the land." Israel had not yet crossed the Jordan, yet God already saw where their hearts were heading. Their rebellion would not catch Him off guard or overturn His purposes. The law could expose that inclination and testify against it. It could not change it. This verse echoes Genesis 8:21. The same bent that survived the flood would come with Israel into the land.

1 Chronicles 28:9 and 29:18: a prayer for a heart God keeps. David first warned Solomon that the Lord understands "all the intent of the thoughts." Later, the people gave freely and joyfully for the temple. David did not assume their generosity would last. He prayed, "keep this forever in the intent of the thoughts of the heart of Your people, and fix their heart toward You." He knew that a moment of wholehearted devotion does not sustain itself, and that the heart drifts. A willing heart has to be kept, and only God can keep it there.

Psalm 103:14: mercy that remembers our dust. "For He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust." The verse explains the one before it, where the Lord pities those who fear Him as a father pities his children. Yêtser here means the formed body. God remembers that He made us from dust, so He does not expect clay to bear what iron could bear. The psalm then sets our brief life against His lasting love. Our days are "like grass," and when the wind passes over us "it is gone" (vv. 15–16). "But the mercy of the LORD is from everlasting to everlasting" (v. 17). Our frailty does not wear out God's patience. In this psalm it is the very thing that moves His compassion.

Isaiah 26:3: a mind held steady. "You will keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on You, because he trusts in You." Isaiah 26 is a song for a day when lofty cities are brought down. In that setting, peace comes from where the yêtser rests, whatever is happening around it. "Stayed" describes something propped up or leaning its weight on a support. The Hebrew repeats the word for peace ("peace, peace"), which the NKJV renders "perfect peace." Isaiah then grounds this trust in God's character: "in YAH, the LORD, is everlasting strength" (v. 4). This is the reverse of Genesis 6:5. The restless inclination that produced "only evil continually" now leans on God, and He keeps it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

These nine verses trace a problem that runs through the whole Old Testament. The human inclination is bent from youth, it outlasted the flood, and it went with Israel under the law. The answer had to come from outside the clay. God promised exactly that through Ezekiel: "I will give you a new heart … I will put My Spirit within you and cause you to walk in My statutes" (Ezekiel 36:26–27). David's prayer that God would "fix their heart" anticipated this promise. In Christ the promise is kept. Through the gospel, God is the One who "works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13).

The physical yêtser is not thrown away. Paul calls believers "earthen vessels" carrying the treasure of the knowledge of God's glory in the face of Jesus Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6–7). This connection comes through the shared image of pottery, not through the Hebrew word itself. The fragile frame God pitied in Psalm 103 becomes the place where His power is displayed. Paul's description of the mind set on the Spirit as "life and peace" (Romans 8:6) completes the picture Isaiah 26:3 began.

7. Spirit & Life

These verses free a believer from two opposite mistakes. One is excusing the heart. Genesis 8:21 will not let us think that our inner bent is basically good and only needs better circumstances. The other is despairing over frailty. Psalm 103:14 says that the God who sees our dust responds with a father's pity, not contempt.

David's prayer is one every believer can pray daily. After a season of real devotion, it is tempting to trust the feeling. David asked God to keep what He had produced. The peace of Isaiah 26:3 comes in the same way. We do not hold our minds steady by effort. We lean our full weight on the Lord and trust Him, and He keeps us. Peace follows from where the mind is resting.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The most significant uses have been discussed above. One more deserves notice:

  • Habakkuk 2:18 mocks the idol maker who trusts "the maker of its mold." The idol is only a formed thing shaped by human hands, so it cannot teach or save. When people worship what they have formed instead of the One who formed them, they turn the true order upside down.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3335 (yatsar), "to form, fashion." This is the verb related to yêtser. It describes God forming man from dust (Genesis 2:7), and it stands beside yêtser in Isaiah 29:16 ("him who formed it").

10. Other Relevant Insights

Later Jewish teaching developed the idea of two inclinations in every person, an evil one and a good one. The Old Testament does name the default inclination as evil from youth (Genesis 8:21), but it never describes a matching good inclination built into human nature. Instead, it looks to God to fix, keep and support the heart (1 Chronicles 29:18; Isaiah 26:3).

Isaiah 29:16 uses the formed pot as a picture of human beings in relation to God. A pot talking back to its potter is a picture of arrogant rebellion against God's wisdom.

Scripture uses the same family of words for the potter's work and for the heart's purposes. This invites us to picture the heart as a workshop that actively forms desires, rather than an empty container. Genesis 6:5 shows that workshop turning out evil continually. Psalm 103:14 adds the related image of humanity as fragile earthenware made from dust.

In 1 Chronicles 28–29, David hands Solomon the plans for the temple and also prays for the inner "intent" of the people's hearts. Placing these side by side suggests a fruitful illustration: a house for God needed a God-given plan, and hearts that worship Him need a framework God sustains. This is an analogy drawn from the passage's setting, not something the text states.
