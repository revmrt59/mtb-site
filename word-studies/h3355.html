JOKTAN - (Yoqṭân, Strong's H3355)

Summary

Joktan was a son of Eber and a descendant of Shem who lived in the generations after the flood. Genesis introduces him beside his brother Peleg, "for in his days the earth was divided" (Genesis 10:25). His name is generally connected with a Hebrew root meaning "small," "little," or "younger." That derivation is probable rather than certain, and Scripture itself never explains the name. Joktan appears only in the parallel genealogies of Genesis 10 and 1 Chronicles 1. There he is the father of thirteen named sons, including Sheba, Ophir, and Havilah, whose families settled in the region usually understood to be southern Arabia (Genesis 10:26–30).

Joktan matters because of where he stands in the story. From Eber the family line splits in two. Peleg's branch leads through Genesis 11 to Abram, while Joktan's branch spreads into the far south. God's plan of redemption runs through Peleg's line, yet Joktan's family is not forgotten. His thirteen sons are named twice in Scripture. Several of the names connected with his family, especially Ophir and possibly Sheba, later appear in Scripture's pictures of distant peoples whose gold and incense are brought to the King and to the LORD. Joktan is a reminder that people outside the chosen line still have a place in God's world and a future in His purposes.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Joktan (Hebrew יָקְטָן) is a single individual: the son of Eber, the son of Shelah, in the line of Arphaxad and Shem (Genesis 10:22–25; 1 Chronicles 1:17–19). His only brother named in Scripture is Peleg. He should not be confused with Jokshan, a son of Abraham and Keturah (Genesis 25:2), who is a separately indexed name. Jokshan's family is also linked with Arabia, and he too has a son named Sheba (Genesis 25:3), so the similar spelling and shared family names make the two easy to blur.

The name is commonly derived from the Hebrew root q-ṭ-n, which carries the idea of being small, little, or young. On that view Joktan could mean something like "small" or "he is made small." This meaning is probable, not secure, and the text offers no explanation of it. Peleg's name is different. The narrator explicitly ties it to the earth being "divided" in his days (Genesis 10:25), which is genuine biblical wordplay. No such comment accompanies Joktan.

His descendants settled "from Mesha as you go toward Sephar, the mountain of the east" (Genesis 10:30). The exact locations of Mesha and Sephar are debated. The region is widely understood as the Arabian Peninsula, especially its southern and southwestern parts. Some of his sons' names have been linked by scholars to later Arabian places, such as Hazarmaveth with Hadramaut. Those links are proposals, not biblical statements. Later Arab tradition traces southern Arab tribes to a forefather called Qahtan, but that tradition lies outside Scripture and should not be treated as confirmed history.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Scripture records no deeds or words of Joktan. His entire biblical presence is his place in two family records. Genesis 10, the Table of Nations, maps how Noah's descendants spread across the earth after the flood. Within Shem's line the text slows at Eber, names his two sons, and then sets out Joktan's family in full: "Almodad, Sheleph, Hazarmaveth, Jerah, Hadoram, Uzal, Diklah, Obal, Abimael, Sheba, Ophir, Havilah, and Jobab. All these were the sons of Joktan" (Genesis 10:26–29). Genesis 10 does not continue Peleg's line at all. That line is picked up only in Genesis 11:16–26, where it narrows generation by generation to Terah and Abram.

The remark about Peleg, "in his days the earth was divided" (Genesis 10:25), is often connected with the scattering at Babel (Genesis 11:1–9). Scripture does not spell out the relationship, so it is not clear whether Joktan's family moved south before, during, or after that judgment.

Centuries later, after the exile, the Chronicler repeated the same record almost word for word (1 Chronicles 1:19–23). His only notable variation is "Ebal" for Obal (1 Chronicles 1:22). He then moves straight from Joktan's sons to the line "Shem, Arphaxad, Shelah, Eber, Peleg, Reu, Serug, Nahor, Terah, and Abram" (1 Chronicles 1:24–27, paraphrased in order). A people rebuilding after judgment were reminded that their history began with the whole human family.

Several names in Joktan's family recur later. Readers should be careful with them, because Scripture uses some of the same names for different lines. A Sheba and a Havilah also appear among the descendants of Cush (Genesis 10:7), and another Sheba descends from Abraham through Keturah (Genesis 25:3). Ophir, however, appears in Genesis only as Joktan's son. The "gold of Ophir" became proverbial for the finest gold. Solomon's fleet brought it to Jerusalem (1 Kings 9:28; 10:11), David gave it toward the temple (1 Chronicles 29:4), and Job and the Psalms use it as a standard of value (Job 28:16; Psalm 45:9). Linking the land of Ophir to Joktan's son is a reasonable reading, though its location remains debated.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Joktan and Peleg were brothers and that Abram came through Peleg (Genesis 11:16–26). It also gives Joktan's family more space in Genesis 10 than Peleg's. This follows a pattern Genesis repeats. The line that does not carry the promise is recorded first and briefly, and then the narrative turns to the chosen line. Ishmael's sons are listed before Isaac's story continues (Genesis 25:12–19), and Esau's descendants before Jacob's (Genesis 36–37). Joktan is not cast as a villain. He simply stands where Genesis honors a family and then moves on with the line through which God will act.

One observation is worth making, provided no one builds too much on it. The brother whose name probably means "small" heads a wide family of thirteen sons. The genealogy that follows Peleg, by contrast, is traced selectively down to one man and a barren wife (Genesis 11:30). God did not choose by the larger listed family. He worked through a narrow thread toward a promise meant for "all the families of the earth" (Genesis 12:3). That promise reaches back to include peoples like Joktan's.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Luke's genealogy of Jesus passes through "the son of Peleg, the son of Eber" (Luke 3:35). Joktan stands just one step off the Messiah's line. He is a close relative of the family of Christ, though he is not an ancestor.

The prophets picture the far southern lands, the region Genesis associates with Joktan, coming to Zion's King. Psalm 72 says, "The kings of Sheba and Seba will offer gifts," and "the gold of Sheba will be given to Him; prayer also will be made for Him continually" (Psalm 72:10, 15). The King who receives these gifts "will deliver the needy when he cries" (Psalm 72:12). Isaiah adds, "All those from Sheba shall come; they shall bring gold and incense, and they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). Because more than one Sheba appears in Genesis, these texts cannot be said to name Joktan's descendants specifically. What they clearly show is that the distant peoples of the south, among whom Joktan's family lived, are drawn into worship.

The New Testament carries this hope forward. The wise men from the East bring gold and frankincense to the child Jesus (Matthew 2:11). Matthew does not cite Isaiah 60 there, so the link is a resonance rather than a stated fulfillment, but the echo is hard to miss. In the end, "the nations of those who are saved shall walk in its light, and the kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into it" (Revelation 21:24).

5. Spirit & Life

God keeps careful records of people the main story passes by. Joktan's thirteen sons are named twice, and the second listing comes generations after the first, when Israel might have cared only about its own descent. The God who chose Abram did not forget Abram's distant relatives.

Joktan's story also shows that God's choices are not measured by size or apparent success. Genesis lists Joktan's thirteen sons at length, yet the narrative follows Peleg's chosen line toward Abram and barren Sarai, and God's purpose moved patiently through that line toward Christ, and through Christ it reaches outward again to every nation.

The wealth tied to these southern lands carries a warning as well as a hope. Ophir's gold adorned the temple, and the prophets saw Sheba's gold and incense laid before the LORD. Jeremiah, however, records God refusing frankincense from Sheba because His people would not listen to Him (Jeremiah 6:19–20). Rich gifts honor God only when they come from hearts that bow to Him.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:25 – Joktan's birth to Eber and his relation to Peleg.

  • Genesis 10:26–29 – The names of his thirteen sons.

  • Genesis 10:30 – Where his descendants settled.

  • Genesis 11:16–26 – Peleg's line narrowing to Abram, the contrasting branch.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:19–23 – The post-exilic restatement of Joktan's family.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:24–27 – The Chronicler's turn from Joktan's line to Abram.

  • 1 Kings 9:28; 1 Chronicles 29:4 – Gold of Ophir brought to Jerusalem and given for the temple.

  • Psalm 72:10–15; Isaiah 60:6 – Southern peoples bringing gold and incense to the King and to the LORD.

  • Luke 3:35 – Peleg and Eber in Jesus' genealogy.

  • Revelation 21:24 – The nations bringing their glory into the New Jerusalem.
