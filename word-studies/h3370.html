JOKSHAN - (Yoqshân, Strong's H3370)

Summary

Jokshan was a son of Abraham by Keturah, the woman Abraham took after the account of Sarah's death and Isaac's marriage. He was the second listed of Keturah's six sons, and Scripture remembers him chiefly as the father of Sheba and Dedan, names later tied to the trading peoples of Arabia (Genesis 25:2–3; 1 Chronicles 1:32). The meaning of his name is uncertain. It is often connected with a Hebrew verb meaning "to lay a snare," which would suggest something like "snarer" or "fowler." Scripture itself never comments on the name, and nothing in his story depends on it.

Jokshan never speaks in the Bible, and no word from God to him is recorded. His place in Scripture still teaches something important. He was truly Abraham's son, and he showed that God was keeping His promise to make Abraham fruitful and the father of nations. Yet "Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac," while Jokshan and his brothers received gifts and were sent "to the country of the east" (Genesis 25:5–6). Paul later draws out the principle at work here: being descended from Abraham does not by itself make someone an heir of the promise. The prophets also foresee people from Sheba coming to Zion with gold and incense to praise the LORD (Isaiah 60:6). That hope may include Jokshan's line, although which Sheba the prophet means cannot be settled from the text.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Jokshan is one person, a son of Abraham and Keturah. His full brothers were Zimran, Medan, Midian, Ishbak, and Shuah. Isaac and Ishmael were his half-brothers. His sons were Sheba and Dedan, and through Dedan came the Asshurim, Letushim, and Leummim (Genesis 25:2–3). Genesis 25:1 calls Keturah Abraham's "wife." First Chronicles 1:32 calls her "Abraham's concubine," and Genesis 25:6 groups her sons among "the sons of the concubines." She was a legitimate partner, but her standing was below Sarah's.

Jokshan should not be confused with Joktan (H3355), a descendant of Shem through Arphaxad who appears in the Table of Nations. Both men are linked to Arabian peoples, and their names look alike in English, but they are separate figures with separate Strong's entries.

The derivation of Jokshan's name is uncertain. Many connect it with the Hebrew root yaqash, "to lay a snare," which would give a meaning such as "snarer" or "fowler." Even that connection is a traditional explanation rather than a settled meaning. The Bible offers no wordplay on the name, so it should not be treated as a key to his character.

His name appears four times, all in Genesis 25:2–3 and 1 Chronicles 1:32. The Chronicles verse names him twice.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Jokshan's whole story is told in a few lines. Genesis reports Abraham's marriage to Keturah after Sarah's burial (Genesis 23) and the finding of a bride for Isaac (Genesis 24). It then lists her six sons and their descendants (Genesis 25:1–4). The next two verses decide Jokshan's future: "And Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac. But Abraham gave gifts to the sons of the concubines which Abraham had; and while he was still living he sent them eastward, away from Isaac his son, to the country of the east" (Genesis 25:5–6).

Nothing more is said of Jokshan himself. Chronicles repeats his name and his sons in Israel's family record (1 Chronicles 1:32), so he keeps a place in the story of the nations that came from Abraham.

The names of his sons have a longer history. Sheba and Dedan are both connected with Arabian trade. Ezekiel says, "The men of Dedan were your traders" in the markets of Tyre (Ezekiel 27:15). In Ezekiel's vision of Gog, "Sheba, Dedan, the merchants of Tarshish" ask Gog, "Have you come to take plunder?" (Ezekiel 38:13). The text records their question about his motive; whether they hoped to profit themselves is a matter of interpretation. There is one complication. Genesis 10:7 also names a Sheba and a Dedan as sons of Raamah, a descendant of Cush. Scripture does not explain how these lists relate. They may describe different groups with the same names, or Abrahamic and Cushite families may later have merged in Arabia. Later references to "Sheba" or "Dedan" therefore cannot always be traced with certainty to Jokshan.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Jokshan's birth shows that God's word to Abraham was coming true. God had promised to make him exceedingly fruitful and to make nations from him (Genesis 17:4–6). Through Keturah's six sons, peoples spread across the eastern desert, and Abraham became the father of more than one family.

Jokshan's dismissal makes a second point just as clear. God had already said that Abraham's promised line would be counted through Isaac (Genesis 21:12). Abraham honored that word, and the text states what he did: everything to Isaac, gifts to the others, and the others sent away while he was still alive. Scripture does not give his reasons. A reasonable inference is that he was preventing a later dispute over the land, as had happened with Ishmael (Genesis 21:10). Abraham treated these sons generously, but he did not divide what God had given to Isaac.

The direction of their journey may also carry meaning. In Genesis, movement eastward often goes with movement away from God's presence or His chosen place. The cherubim guarded Eden from the east (Genesis 3:24). Cain settled east of Eden (Genesis 4:16). Lot chose the plain toward the east and ended up near Sodom (Genesis 13:11). This is an observed pattern, not something the text says about Jokshan. Still, it fits his story: his family's history would continue away from the land where God's plan was unfolding through Isaac.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament explains the principle that separated Jokshan from Isaac. Paul writes that not all Abraham's descendants are his children in the sense of the promise, and he quotes the same word that guided Abraham: in Isaac his seed would be called (Romans 9:7–8). Jokshan was Abraham's son by birth, but birth did not make him an heir. The inheritance came by God's promise.

The gospel then extends that promise in a way Genesis only begins to suggest. Paul calls Abraham "the father of us all" for everyone who shares his faith (Romans 4:16–17), and he says that those who belong to Christ are Abraham's seed and heirs according to the promise (Galatians 3:29). The way into the inheritance was never descent, whether through Isaac or Jokshan. It was always faith in the promised Seed, and that way is open to the peoples who once went east.

The prophets expected this. Psalm 72:10 says, "The kings of Sheba and Seba Will offer gifts" to the righteous King, and Isaiah 60:6 says, "All those from Sheba shall come; They shall bring gold and incense, And they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD." These passages do not say which Sheba they mean, and the Cushite line of Genesis 10:7 is possible. Even so, a thematic possibility is striking. If these are Jokshan's descendants, a family that left Abraham's house with gifts appears in the prophets' vision returning with gifts; if not, the passage still belongs to the wider biblical picture of the nations bringing gifts to God's King. It is a fitting resonance, though not a stated fulfillment, and Matthew says nothing of their ancestry, that Matthew later describes wise men from the East bringing gold and frankincense to the child Jesus (Matthew 2:1, 11).

5. Spirit & Life

Jokshan's brief record shows God's generosity alongside His careful choices. God did not forget the sons who left Abraham's tent. Abraham provided for them, their families grew into peoples, and the prophets later included Sheba, whichever line the name refers to, among those drawn to the LORD. At the same time, God did not blur the line of promise to make everyone comfortable. Abraham's obedience was costly. He separated his household instead of keeping it together in a way that would have confused what God had promised to Isaac.

For readers today, the main lesson is not about family arrangements. It concerns how anyone becomes an heir of God. Jokshan had Abraham's blood and Abraham's gifts, yet he did not have Isaac's inheritance. No one inherits God's promises by family background, religious heritage, or nearness to the people of God. They are received by faith in Christ, the promised Seed. God's welcome reaches far. Scripture pictures people from the distant east and south, possibly including those once sent "to the country of the east," coming to "proclaim the praises of the LORD."

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 25:1–4 — Keturah's sons, including Jokshan and his descendants through Sheba and Dedan.

  • Genesis 25:5–6 — Isaac receives the inheritance, while Jokshan and his brothers receive gifts and are sent east.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:32 — Repeats Jokshan's place in Israel's record and calls Keturah Abraham's concubine.

  • Genesis 10:7 — A separate Cushite Sheba and Dedan, which complicates tracing later references.

  • Genesis 17:4–6; 21:12 — God's promise of many nations and His choice of Isaac's line (cited, not quoted).

  • Romans 9:7–8; Galatians 3:29 — Paul's teaching that heirs are counted by promise and faith, not descent (cited).

  • Psalm 72:10; Isaiah 60:6 — Sheba's kings and people bringing gifts and praise to the LORD's King and Zion.

  • Ezekiel 27:15; 38:13 — Dedan and Sheba as traders and onlookers among the nations.
