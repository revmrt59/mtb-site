FEAR, BE AFRAID, REVERE, AWESOME - (yârêʼ, Strong's H3372)

Summary

Yârêʼ is the ordinary Hebrew verb for fear, and it covers the whole range of that experience. It describes a soldier shaking before an enemy, a guilty man hiding from God, a worshiper bowing in reverent awe, and a child honoring his parents. Some forms describe what causes fear, so the same word can call God, His deeds, or His coming day "awesome," "terrible," or "dreadful." The word never shrinks to mere politeness. Even when it is directed toward God in worship, it carries real weight and trembling.

Scripture uses this word to tell a story. Adam was the first to be afraid, and he hid from God. At Sinai, Moses told a terrified people not to fear, so that the fear of God might keep them from sin. Deuteronomy joins fearing the Lord with loving and serving Him. A psalmist found that God's forgiveness, more than His severity, is what makes Him feared. Again and again God answers fear with the assurance of His presence: "I am with you." The person who rightly fears God is set free from fearing everything else.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yârêʼ is one of the most common verbs in the Old Testament, appearing from Genesis 3 through Malachi 4. It gathers most heavily in the Psalms, Deuteronomy, and Isaiah, the books most concerned with worship, faithful obedience, and trust under threat. It appears on battlefields, in royal courts, in household law, in wisdom sayings, and in prophetic visions of judgment. A related noun from the same root is also translated "fear." It appears in phrases such as "His fear" in Exodus 20:20, and it often stands beside the verb.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, yârêʼ describes a response to something greater than oneself: a threat, a power, or an authority that must be reckoned with. Context determines whether that response is terror or reverence.

1. To be afraid or frightened, a natural reaction to danger, enemies, or guilt. Israel was "afraid" of Goliath (1 Samuel 17:11), and Adam was "afraid" in the garden (Genesis 3:10).

2. To fear in the sense of revering or standing in awe, a response of honor and allegiance toward one who holds rightful authority. It is used chiefly of God (Deuteronomy 6:2), and also of parents: "Every one of you shall revere his mother and his father" (Leviticus 19:3).

3. To be feared, or to be awesome or terrible. In these forms the word describes what produces fear. Jacob called Bethel an "awesome" place (Genesis 28:17). Moses called the wilderness "terrible" (Deuteronomy 1:19). Above all, the word is used of God and His works (Psalm 66:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The object of the fear changes the whole meaning. Fearing people often leads to compromise. Saul confessed that he disobeyed because he "feared the people" (1 Samuel 15:24). The Hebrew midwives, by contrast, "feared God" and refused Pharaoh's order to kill the boys (Exodus 1:17). The verb is the same, but the direction is opposite, and so are the results.

The negative command "Do not fear" or "Do not be afraid" is one of the word's most frequent patterns. What matters is the reason God attaches to it. In passages such as Isaiah 41:10, He points His people to Himself, giving a fact about who He is. The passive use matters too. In Psalm 130:4 God is the One who is "feared," and the verse explains what brings that about.

4. Theological Significance

God does not simply demand fear; He is fearsome in Himself. Moses reassured Israel before the nations of Canaan with these words: "the LORD your God, the great and awesome God, is among you" (Deuteronomy 7:21). His awesomeness is the reason His people need not panic, because the most fearsome Being in the universe stands on their side. The same title becomes the opening of prayers of confession. Nehemiah prayed, "O great and awesome God, You who keep Your covenant and mercy with those who love You" (Nehemiah 1:5), and Daniel used almost the same words (Daniel 9:4). Both men were broken over the sins of their people, and both turned to the awesome God because His greatness is what guarantees His faithfulness. A small god could not be trusted to keep his word. The awesome God can.

Deuteronomy shows what fearing God looks like in daily life. "What does the LORD your God require of you, but to fear the LORD your God, to walk in all His ways and to love Him, to serve the LORD your God with all your heart" (Deuteronomy 10:12). In this list fear and love do not compete. They describe one loyal heart. To fear God means taking His authority seriously enough "to keep all His statutes and His commandments… all the days of your life" (Deuteronomy 6:2), so that walking in His ways becomes the natural shape of that fear (Deuteronomy 8:6).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 3:10 — The first fear. The word's first appearance comes right after the first sin: "I heard Your voice in the garden, and I was afraid because I was naked; and I hid myself." Nothing in the garden had changed except Adam. The same voice that once meant fellowship now sent him into the trees. Fear entered human experience as the dread of a guilty conscience before a holy God, and it drove the man away from the only One who could help him. Much of the rest of Scripture shows God turning that hiding fear into a worshiping fear.

Exodus 20:20 — Two fears in one sentence. At Sinai the people saw thunder, lightning, and smoke and stood far off in terror. Moses answered, "Do not fear; for God has come to test you, and that His fear may be before you, so that you may not sin." The first "fear" is the verb yârêʼ, and the second is its related noun. Moses tells the people to stop being afraid so that they may fear. The panic that makes people run from God is not what He wants. He wants a settled reverence that keeps them near Him and away from sin. Right fear of God is the cure for terror of God.

Psalm 130:4 — Forgiveness makes God feared. The psalmist cries "out of the depths" and admits that if God kept a record of sins, no one could stand. Then he writes, "But there is forgiveness with You, that You may be feared." Most people would expect forgiveness to remove fear and strict punishment to produce it. The psalm says the reverse. A God who only punished might be dreaded, but He would be avoided. A God who pardons rebels draws them close and humbles them in amazement. Grace does not cheapen the fear of the Lord; it creates it. Paul makes a related point when he says that "the goodness of God leads you to repentance" (Romans 2:4).

Isaiah 41:10 and its companions — God's presence answers fear. "Fear not, for I am with you; Be not dismayed, for I am your God." Israel faced real enemies, and God did not deny it. He pointed to Himself and promised to strengthen, help, and uphold them "with My righteous right hand." The same pattern runs from the beginning. Abram heard, "Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield" (Genesis 15:1). David walked through "the valley of the shadow of death" and said, "I will fear no evil; For You are with me" (Psalm 23:4). The valley stayed dark, but David's fear was answered by God's nearness. Joshua 1:9 gives the same reassurance with a different Hebrew verb: "the LORD your God is with you wherever you go."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word follows the shape of redemption. It begins in Eden with guilty people hiding. At Sinai God begins retraining them. In the Psalms forgiven sinners worship Him with trembling joy. The prophets close the Old Testament by pointing to a coming day that will be "great and very terrible; Who can endure it?" (Joel 2:11). Joel calls it "the great and awesome day of the LORD" (Joel 2:31), and Malachi promises that Elijah will come "before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD" (Malachi 4:5). The same awesomeness that worshipers adore becomes dread for those who refuse Him.

The New Testament picks up both threads. Jesus identified John the Baptist as the promised Elijah (Matthew 11:14), and Peter quoted Joel's "awesome day" at Pentecost (Acts 2:20). That day has begun to dawn in Christ. In Jesus the forgiveness of Psalm 130 is accomplished, and the presence promised in Isaiah 41 takes on flesh, so His repeated "Do not be afraid" carries the authority of God's own voice. Jesus also redirected fear. He told His disciples not to fear those who can kill only the body, but to fear the One with authority over body and soul (Matthew 10:28, paraphrased). Through Christ, sinners can come out from hiding, because the One they rightly fear is the One who has forgiven them.

7. Spirit & Life

Every person fears something, whether illness, loss, rejection, or powerful people. Scripture does not ask us to stop fearing altogether. It asks us to move our fear to its proper object. God told Isaiah not to be afraid of the threats that frightened everyone around him (Isaiah 8:12), and in the next verse He called Isaiah to treat the Lord of hosts as the One to fear. Jeremiah mocked idols that "must be carried" and told the people, "Do not be afraid of them, For they cannot do evil, Nor can they do any good" (Jeremiah 10:5). He then asked who would not fear the King of the nations (Jeremiah 10:7, paraphrased). When God becomes great in the heart, other threats shrink to their real size.

The Psalms describe what this looks like in a life. The man who "fears the LORD" (Psalm 112:1) is said a few verses later not to be afraid of bad news, because his heart is steady and trusting (Psalm 112:7–8, paraphrased). David admitted that he was sometimes afraid, and he answered his fear with trust in God (Psalm 56:3–4, paraphrased). God's answer to anxiety is not a guarantee that nothing hard will happen. He gives Himself: "I am with you." The believer who grasps Psalm 130:4 can approach this awesome God without hiding, because the reverence He wants grows from His mercy.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 28:17 — After his dream at Bethel, Jacob was "afraid" and called the place "awesome." The word appears twice in one verse, joining his trembling to the holiness of the place where God had met him.

  • Leviticus 19:3 — Israel is told to "revere" mother and father. Honor in the home belongs to the same vocabulary as honor toward God.

  • Jonah 1:5, 10, 16 — The pagan sailors were first "afraid" of the storm, then "exceedingly afraid" when they learned whom Jonah was fleeing, and finally "feared the LORD exceedingly" and offered sacrifice. In three uses their fear moves from panic to worship.

  • Psalm 139:14 — The psalmist praises God because he is "fearfully" made. The awe that belongs to God's great works also belongs to the way He forms each human life.

  • Ecclesiastes 12:13 — After all his searching, the Preacher concludes by calling his readers to fear God and keep His commandments, which he says is the whole duty of man.
