GO DOWN, DESCEND, COME DOWN - (yârad, Strong's H3381)

Summary

The Hebrew verb yârad means to go down or come down. It can describe a traveler heading downhill, rain falling from the sky, tears running down a face, or a body being lowered into the grave. Because Canaan is hill country, Israelites "went down" to Egypt, to the sea, and to the Jordan valley. As a result, the word appears constantly in the travels of Abraham, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Samson, and David.

The word becomes most important when God is the one who comes down. The Lord came down to see Babel's tower, came down to deliver Israel from Egypt, and descended on Sinai in fire. He promised Jacob that He would go down with him into Egypt. Scripture also uses the word for the opposite direction of human life. Proud kings are brought down to the Pit, and Jonah ran from God by going down, step by step, until he reached "the moorings of the mountains." Against that downward pull of sin and death, the Bible sets the God who stoops to rescue. That pattern reaches its fullest expression in Jesus, who said, "I have come down from heaven."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yârad is one of the most common verbs in the Old Testament, used across history, poetry, and prophecy. The word occurs 380 times in 345 verses of the Hebrew Bible. Its frequency comes partly from geography. The heart of Israel sits on a central ridge of hills. From there you go down west to the coastal plain and the sea, east to the Jordan valley and the Salt Sea, and south to Egypt and the Nile. Travelers also go "up" to Jerusalem. Many uses of yârad are simple travel language, such as going down to a spring, to a city gate, or to a riverbank (Exodus 2:5). The word gains moral or spiritual weight only when the surrounding story supplies it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Yârad means to move from a higher place to a lower one. Context decides what is moving and why.

1. To go or come down physically: down a mountain, down to another region, down from a donkey. When Abigail meets David, she dismounts quickly (1 Samuel 25:23).

2. To flow or fall downward, used of liquids and falling things: rain, dew, oil, water, and tears (Psalm 133:2; Jeremiah 13:17). It can also describe collapsing walls or fortifications (Deuteronomy 28:52).

3. To go down to death, the grave, Sheol (the realm of the dead), or "the Pit" (Psalm 30:3; Genesis 37:35).

4. To bring down, take down, or subdue. In this form someone causes another person or thing to go down. Joseph is "taken down" to Egypt (Genesis 39:1), and God "subdues the peoples" under David (2 Samuel 22:48).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes everything. When people go down, the word may simply report a journey, or it may mark a slide away from God's presence. When God comes down, the word marks heaven entering human history, either to judge or to save. When rain, dew, or fire comes down, the movement shows that the gift or the judgment originates above.

The fourth sense is the most significant grammatical pattern. In Hebrew, the verb can take a form that means to make something go down. That form puts the power in someone else's hands. In Ezekiel 31, God says of Assyria, the felled cedar, "I cast it down to hell" (Ezekiel 31:16), and warns Pharaoh that he too "shall be brought down" (Ezekiel 31:18). Hannah sings that the Lord both brings down to the grave and brings up (1 Samuel 2:6). The person who goes down is often not in control of the descent.

A single use of "down" does not make a moral point. Repetition does. In the stories of Jonah and Samson, the writer repeats the word until the reader feels the direction of the man's life.

4. Theological Significance

The Bible insists that God dwells on high and also comes near. At Babel, people tried to build a tower "whose top is in the heavens." Yet the Lord still had to come down to see it (Genesis 11:5, 7). The wording suggests that their greatest achievement was small from where He stood. Before judging Sodom, He said He would go down and see whether its outcry was true (Genesis 18:21). The story presents God's judgment as just: He looks into the outcry before He acts.

His descent to save is just as personal. In his song of rescue, David says, "He bowed the heavens also, and came down With darkness under His feet" (Psalm 18:9; 2 Samuel 22:10). David pictures the sky lowered so that the Lord could reach one hunted man. Isaiah later longs for God to tear open the heavens and come down again (Isaiah 64:1). God's people learned to pray in the language of this word.

The same God brings down the proud. The king of Babylon planned to ascend above the stars and was told he would be brought down to Sheol, to the lowest depths of the Pit (Isaiah 14:13–15). Edom built its nest high in the rocks, and God replied that He would bring it down even from among the stars (Obadiah 3–4). Ezekiel asks Pharaoh whom he is like in his greatness and points him to Assyria, once a towering cedar in Lebanon (Ezekiel 31:2–3). Of that cedar God says, "I made the nations shake at the sound of its fall, when I cast it down to hell together with those who descend into the Pit" (Ezekiel 31:16). Then He turns to Pharaoh: "Yet you shall be brought down with the trees of Eden to the depths of the earth" (Ezekiel 31:18). Chapter 32 then lists nation after nation going down to lie with the dead. In these oracles, height achieved without God ends in a fall. Against this fate David sings, "O LORD, You brought my soul up from the grave; You have kept me alive, that I should not go down to the pit" (Psalm 30:3).

Descending fire shows God's verdict. When Solomon finished praying, "fire came down from heaven and consumed the burnt offering and the sacrifices; and the glory of the LORD filled the temple" (2 Chronicles 7:1). The fire fell on the sacrifice, and when the people saw it, they bowed down and worshiped the LORD at the temple (2 Chronicles 7:3). King Ahaziah's first captain relayed the order "Come down!" and the second demanded, "Come down quickly!" Each time fire came down and consumed the captain and his fifty men (2 Kings 1:9–12). The third captain fell on his knees and pleaded for mercy, and he was spared (2 Kings 1:13–14). In one scene fire from heaven consumes an offering and the people worship; in the other it consumes the men sent with the king's command against God's prophet.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 3:8 and Sinai: coming down to bring up. At the burning bush God says, "So I have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians, and to bring them up from that land." God's coming down has a purpose: He descends so that His people can rise. At Sinai He descended on the mountain in fire (Exodus 19:18), and Moses went back and forth between God and the people. Then the story turns. While Moses was on the mountain, Israel made the golden calf, and God told him to go down at once because the people had corrupted themselves (Exodus 32:7; Deuteronomy 9:12). Moses carried the tablets down into a camp that had already broken the Sinai covenant (Exodus 32:15). The grace of the story is that God descended again. He came down in the cloud and proclaimed His name to Moses (Exodus 34:5). Moses then came down carrying the replacement tablets, with his face shining (Exodus 34:4, 29). Israel's sin did not stop the Lord from coming down.

Genesis 46:3–4: God goes down with His people. Going down to Egypt runs through Genesis. Abram went down because of famine (Genesis 12:10). Isaac was told not to go down (Genesis 26:2). Jacob, grieving Joseph, said he would go down to the grave mourning (Genesis 37:35). Joseph, sold by his brothers to traders (Genesis 37:28), had been taken down to Egypt (Genesis 39:1). His brothers later went down for grain (Genesis 43:15). Going down to Egypt meant leaving the promised land. Famine drove Abram there, and later Jacob's sons, because Egypt had grain when Canaan did not (Genesis 42:2). Yet Egypt would later become the place of Israel's slavery, and going down there can be read as a test of whether God's people would trust Him outside the land He had promised. When the aged Jacob finally had to go, God spoke to him at night. He told Jacob not to fear going down to Egypt and promised that He Himself would go down with him and would surely bring him up again. The Lord's presence was not confined to the hills of Canaan. Like Exodus 3:8, the promise pairs God's going down with His bringing up.

Jonah 1–2: the downward path of running from God. Jonah "arose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD. He went down to Joppa... and went down into it" (Jonah 1:3). When the storm struck, he "had gone down into the lowest parts of the ship, had lain down, and was fast asleep" (Jonah 1:5). Then the sea took him: "I went down to the moorings of the mountains" (Jonah 2:6). Each descent took Jonah deeper into his flight and closer to death. Yet the same verse turns: "Yet You have brought up my life from the pit, O LORD, my God." Psalm 139:7–8 had already said there is nowhere to flee from His presence, not even in Sheol.

Samson's story follows the same pattern. "Now Samson went down to Timnah, and saw a woman in Timnah of the daughters of the Philistines" (Judges 14:1). Again and again he went down to Philistine territory (Judges 14:5, 7, 19). Judges 14:4 says the LORD was at work in this, seeking an occasion to move against the Philistines. Even so, the Nazirite set apart to God kept drifting toward the people he was raised up to resist. At the end, the Philistines brought him down to Gaza blind and in chains (Judges 16:21). His family came down to carry his body home (Judges 16:31). Neither story treats ordinary travel as sin. In each one, however, the repeated word traces a life sliding away from God.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament keeps asking for God to come down. Agur asks who has ascended to heaven or descended (Proverbs 30:4). Isaiah begs God to tear the heavens open and come down (Isaiah 64:1). Jacob saw part of the answer at Bethel: "a ladder was set up on the earth, and its top reached to heaven; and there the angels of God were ascending and descending on it" (Genesis 28:12). Jesus applied that vision to Himself: "you shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man" (John 1:51). The traffic between heaven and earth now runs through a Person.

Jesus describes His mission in the language of descent: "For I have come down from heaven, not to do My own will, but the will of Him who sent Me" (John 6:38). Paul traces how low He went. Christ took "the form of a bondservant," and "He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross" (Philippians 2:7–8). He "first descended into the lower parts of the earth," and "He who descended is also the One who ascended far above all the heavens, that He might fill all things" (Ephesians 4:9–10). These New Testament passages are written in Greek and use different words. They complete the pattern of the Hebrew Scriptures: the Lord who came down to bring Israel up out of Egypt came down in His Son to bring His people up from the grave.

The story contains a sharp reversal. The proud powers of Babylon, Assyria and Egypt lifted themselves high, and God announced that each would be brought down to the Pit (Isaiah 14:15; Ezekiel 31:16, 18). The Son of God came from heaven, went willingly to death, and was raised far above all the heavens. By analogy, the fire that fell on Solomon's sacrifice can also point to the cross, where judgment for our sin fell on the offering God Himself provided.

The Jordan crossing gives a further picture, as an analogy. The river's waters normally run downhill into the Salt Sea. When the priests carrying the ark stepped in, "the waters which came down from upstream stood still," and "the waters that went down into the Sea of the Arabah, the Salt Sea, failed, and were cut off" (Joshua 3:16). As an analogy, the stopping of that downward flow before the Lord can picture His people passing over from death to life.

7. Spirit & Life

Scripture shows two kinds of descent. One is the pull of sin and death, which takes a person down step by step, the way it took Jonah and Samson. Many people do not fall away from God all at once. Usually they keep going down, and the next step feels small. The other descent is God's. He comes down to see injustice, to deliver slaves, to go with a frightened old man into Egypt, and finally to enter death in His Son. The believer's hope does not depend on climbing high enough to reach God. It rests on the God who came down far enough to reach us. Jonah could not sink lower than the reach of the Lord who brought his life up from the pit.

Blessing comes down as well. David compares the unity of God's people to precious oil "Running down on the beard, The beard of Aaron, Running down on the edge of his garments," and to "the dew of Hermon, Descending upon the mountains of Zion; For there the LORD commanded the blessing— Life forevermore" (Psalm 133:2–3). The oil flows from the high priest's head down to the edge of his garments. The psalm pictures the dew of lofty Hermon coming down on the mountains of Zion. God promises showers of blessing (Ezekiel 34:26) and faithful rain for His people (Joel 2:23). James puts the same truth simply: "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and comes down from the Father of lights" (James 1:17). Unity and spiritual life cannot be manufactured from below. They are received from above.

The word also carries tears. Jeremiah asks that his eyes run down with tears night and day over his people's ruin (Jeremiah 14:17; 13:17). Lamentations speaks of eyes overflowing with rivers of water (Lamentations 1:16; 3:48). The prophets who announced judgment did not stand above it. They wept as it fell on people they loved. Their grief reflects the heart of the God who sent them. That heart was shown fully when the Son came down and wept over Jerusalem.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

1 Samuel 2:6 – Hannah confesses that the Lord brings down to the grave and brings up. Life and death are in His hands, which is the foundation of every rescue from the Pit.

2 Kings 5:14 – Naaman went down and dipped seven times in the Jordan. A proud commander had to go down, literally and in humility, to be made clean.

Psalm 107:23 – Sailors who go down to the sea in ships see the Lord's works in the deep. The psalm treats the place of danger as a place where God's power is seen.

Isaiah 55:10 – Rain and snow come down from heaven and water the earth. In the same way, God's word does not return to Him empty.

Ezekiel 47:1, 8 – Water flows down from the temple and goes down into the Arabah, bringing healing and life wherever it goes. This is a picture of restoration flowing from God's dwelling place.
