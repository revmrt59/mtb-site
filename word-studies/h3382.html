JARED - (Yered, Strong's H3382)

Summary

Jared is a name the Hebrew Bible gives to two different men. The better known is the patriarch before the Flood: the son of Mahalalel, the father of Enoch, and the sixth generation from Adam through Seth (Genesis 5:15–20; 1 Chronicles 1:2). The second is a much later man of Judah, called "the father of Gedor" in a difficult genealogy of that tribe (1 Chronicles 4:18). The name comes from the Hebrew verb yarad, "to go down" or "descend," and is generally understood to mean "descent." Scripture never comments on the name or builds a lesson from it, so the meaning stays a matter of language rather than a key to either man's life.

The patriarch's whole biography fits in three verses, yet it carries real weight. He lived 962 years, longer than anyone else in Scripture except his grandson Methuselah, and his record still ends with the same words as nearly everyone else in Genesis 5: "and he died." His son Enoch lived only 365 years on earth, and then "he was not, for God took him" (Genesis 5:24). Set side by side, father and son show that a long life is not the measure of God's favor. Walking with God is. Luke names Jared in the ancestry of Jesus (Luke 3:37), so this ancient father stands in the line through which God carried His promise from Adam to the One who would defeat death itself.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew lemma Yered (Strong's H3382) appears seven times and refers to two separate individuals who should not be confused.

Jared the patriarch belongs to the line of Seth in Genesis 5. He is the son of Mahalalel and the father of Enoch, and Methuselah is his grandson. Jude counts Enoch as "the seventh from Adam" (Jude 14), which places Jared sixth. His name returns in the opening genealogy of 1 Chronicles (1:2). In the New Testament, Luke's genealogy of Jesus uses the Greek form Iared (Strong's G2391, a separately indexed entry) in Luke 3:37.

Jared of Judah appears only in 1 Chronicles 4:18, where he is the son of a woman of Judah (the NKJV names her Jehudijah) and the "father of Gedor." Many English versions, including the NKJV, spell his name "Jered," but the underlying Hebrew name is the same. He lived long after the Flood, and nothing in Scripture links him to the patriarch beyond the shared name.

The name Yered is drawn from the common Hebrew verb yarad (H3381), "to go down, descend," and is usually rendered "descent." That derivation is well grounded lexically. Genesis, however, offers no wordplay or explanation of the name, as it does for Noah (Genesis 5:29). Later Jewish writings outside the Bible, such as Jubilees and 1 Enoch, connected Jared's name with a supposed "descent" of rebellious angels in his days. That is tradition, not Scripture, and it should not be read back into Genesis.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Patriarch Before the Flood

Genesis 5 moves with a steady rhythm: a man lives, fathers a son, lives on and has other children, and dies. Jared's entry follows the pattern exactly. Mahalalel fathered him at sixty-five (Genesis 5:15). "Jared lived one hundred and sixty-two years, and begot Enoch. After he begot Enoch, Jared lived eight hundred years, and had sons and daughters. So all the days of Jared were nine hundred and sixty-two years; and he died" (Genesis 5:18–20).

These few lines carry a remarkable span of history. Using the Hebrew text's numbers, Jared was born while Adam was still living and died when Noah was a grown man, a little over two centuries before the Flood. He was a bridge between the memory of Eden and the eve of judgment. He belonged to the family of Seth, the line in whose days people first began to call on the name of the LORD (Genesis 4:26), while Cain's line built cities and boasted of violence (Genesis 4:17–24).

The most striking event of Jared's long life happened to his son. Enoch "walked with God; and he was not, for God took him" (Genesis 5:24). By the chronology of Genesis 5, Jared was still alive when this happened and lived another 435 years afterward. Scripture does not tell us what Jared saw or thought, but his son's departure from the earth without dying took place within his own lifetime.

Jared in Chronicles and Luke

After the exile, the Chronicler opened Israel's history by tracing it back to Adam, and Jared's name stands in that unbroken list (1 Chronicles 1:2). Luke reverses the direction, beginning with Jesus and climbing back to "Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38). Jared appears near the top: "the son of Methuselah, the son of Enoch, the son of Jared, the son of Mahalalel, the son of Cainan" (Luke 3:37).

Jared of Judah

The later Jared is named once, in the tangled family records of Judah (1 Chronicles 4:18). He is called the father of Gedor, a phrase the Chronicler often uses for the founder or leading ancestor of a town's clan. A Gedor appears among the hill-country towns of Judah (Joshua 15:58) and may be the same place. The verse's relationships among Mered, Bithiah the daughter of Pharaoh, and Jehudijah are hard to sort out, so it is best not to say more about this man than the text says.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states three things about the patriarch plainly. God preserved his line, he lived an extraordinarily long life, and he died. Each matters.

His place in the line matters because Genesis 5 is not a random list. It traces the family from Adam through Seth to Noah, the line God preserved through judgment, and Luke shows it running all the way to Christ. Jared was a necessary link in that chain. God's promise that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent (Genesis 3:15) passed through ordinary fathers whose names we know and whose stories we mostly do not.

His long life and his death belong together. Genesis records nearly a thousand years for him, an extraordinary span. Yet the refrain "and he died" closes Jared's entry, as it closes every completed ordinary lifespan in the chapter; Enoch is the one exception, and Genesis explains it (5:24), while Noah's death is told later (9:29). God had warned Adam that disobedience would bring death (Genesis 2:17), and Paul later looks back on this very era: "Nevertheless death reigned from Adam to Moses" (Romans 5:14). Even the most durable man could not outlast the sentence. That is stated by Scripture, not read into it.

A careful inference follows from where Genesis places Jared. His entry sits directly beside Enoch's, and the contrast is hard to miss. The father who lived 962 years died. The son who lived 365 years and walked with God did not see death. That contrast does not imply that Jared lacked faith or God's favor; Genesis simply does not record his inner life. Genesis does not spell out the lesson, but the arrangement points to it: length of life is not the greatest gift God gives. Fellowship with Him is, and death has no final claim on those He takes to Himself.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Luke 3:37 is the explicit link. Jared is named among the ancestors of Jesus, and Luke's whole genealogy reaches back past Abraham to Adam to show that Jesus belongs to the entire human race, not to Israel alone. Jared's descendants include all the generations descended through Noah since the Flood, and the Son of God took that same flesh.

The wider canon develops this. Paul calls Adam "a type of Him who was to come" (Romans 5:14) and contrasts the death that came through Adam with the life that comes through Christ (1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45). Genesis 5 is the record of death's reign, with Jared's long years making it unmistakable. The gospel is the announcement that the reign has been broken by Jesus' resurrection.

Enoch's translation, which happened in Jared's lifetime, offers an early glimpse of that victory. Hebrews explains that Enoch was taken by faith so that he did not see death, because he pleased God (Hebrews 11:5–6). This is not a claim that Jared or Enoch is a type of Christ. The point is thematic but well grounded: from the first pages of Scripture, God showed that death was not His last word for those who walk with Him.

5. Spirit & Life

Jared's life confronts a quiet assumption many of us carry, that more years would solve the deepest human problem. Jared had more years than we can imagine, and Scripture sums them up in a single verse that ends at the grave. Time alone does not rescue anyone. Moses prayed, in a psalm that echoes Genesis, that God would teach His people to number their days and gain a heart of wisdom (Psalm 90:10–12), and Jared's record pushes us toward that prayer.

Living beside Enoch's story, Jared's story also points to what does last. Enoch's life was shorter, yet he is remembered for walking with God and pleasing Him. Hebrews draws the conclusion that without faith it is impossible to please God, and that He rewards those who diligently seek Him (Hebrews 11:6). The question Genesis 5 leaves with us is not how long we will live, but whether we are walking with the God who holds life and death.

Finally, Jared reminds us that God's purposes often run through people Scripture barely describes. We know nothing of his prayers, his work, or his faith, yet God kept His promise moving through him toward Christ. Faithfulness in an unrecorded life is not wasted when it rests in God's hands.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 5:15–20 — Jared's birth to Mahalalel, the birth of Enoch, his 962 years, and his death; the full record of the patriarch.

  • Genesis 5:21–24 — The life of Enoch, taken by God during Jared's lifetime; the contrast that gives Jared's entry its sharpest meaning.

  • Genesis 4:26 — The line of Seth as the family in which people began calling on the LORD's name.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:2 — Jared preserved in the post-exilic record of humanity from Adam.

  • 1 Chronicles 4:18 — The separate, later Jared (Jered) of Judah, father of Gedor.

  • Luke 3:37 — Jared (Greek Iared, G2391) in the ancestry of Jesus.

  • Romans 5:14 — Paul's statement that death reigned from Adam to Moses, the reality Genesis 5 records.

  • Hebrews 11:5–6; Jude 14 — The New Testament's interpretation of Enoch's faith and his place as seventh from Adam, which fixes Jared's position as sixth.
