JERUSALEM - (Yᵉrûwshâlaim, Strong's H3389)

Summary

Jerusalem is the city God chose for His name. It first appears in Scripture as a Canaanite city in the hill country (Joshua 10:1), and it was the Jebusite stronghold that David later captured (2 Samuel 5:6–7). David made it his capital, and Solomon built the temple there. For centuries it was the place where Israel's kings reigned and the tribes went up to worship. The origin and meaning of the name are not certain. It is traditionally understood as foundation of peace or city of peace, because the end of the name sounds like the Hebrew word shalom, peace. Some scholars instead propose a historical origin, foundation of Shalim, from the name of a Canaanite god. The call to "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem" (Psalm 122:6) resonates with the traditional association, though the psalm does not explain the name.

Few places in the Bible carry so much glory and so much grief. Jerusalem saw the glory of the LORD fill the temple, and it saw Babylon burn that temple to the ground. The prophets called the city God's beloved daughter and bride, and they also condemned it as an unfaithful wife. Jesus wept over Jerusalem, died outside its gate, rose again there, and sent His gospel out from it to the nations. The Bible closes with "the holy city, New Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God" (Revelation 21:2). In that city God dwells with His people, and "the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple" (Revelation 21:22). The city traditionally called the city of peace finds its peace only in the One who reconciles sinners to God.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Jerusalem is a single, real city. It stands in the central hill country between the territories of Judah and Benjamin (Joshua 15:8; 18:28). The Kidron Valley lies on its east side and the Valley of the Son of Hinnom on its south and west, and steep slopes made the original ridge-top fortress hard to attack. Unlike many shared biblical names, the Strong's entry H3389 does not cover several different places. It names one city across its whole history, from the Canaanite kingdom of Adoni-Zedek (Joshua 10:1) to the capital of David's line and the city rebuilt after the exile.

Several related names belong to the same city's story, but they are indexed separately and are not counted among the occurrences of H3389. Salem (H8004), the city of Melchizedek in Genesis 14:18, is linked with Zion in Psalm 76:2. Jebus (H2982) was the Jebusite name of the city, and Judges 19:10 explains that Jebus is Jerusalem. Zion (H6726) first named the stronghold David captured, which 2 Samuel 5:7 also calls "the City of David." In poetry and prophecy Zion later came to stand for the whole city and its people. Ariel (H739) is a poetic name for the city in Isaiah 29:1. Abraham was sent to "the land of Moriah" to offer Isaac on one of its mountains (Genesis 22:2), and 2 Chronicles 3:1 names Mount Moriah as the place where Solomon built the temple. These are the only two places Scripture uses the name Moriah. Long tradition takes them as the same mountain, though Genesis does not identify the exact spot. In the New Testament, Greek uses two forms of the name, Hierosolyma (G2414) and Ierousalem (G2419).

The origin of the name is debated. The traditional explanation joins two parts. The first comes from a Hebrew root meaning to lay a foundation or to establish. The second is related to shalem, a word connected with peace, wholeness and completeness. Read this way, the name means foundation of peace or city of peace. Many scholars instead offer a historical proposal, foundation of Shalim, which would preserve the name of a god worshiped in Canaan before Israel came. Neither explanation can be proved, and the origin of the name remains uncertain. The meaning peace is best treated as a traditional interpretation, not as the established origin of the name.

Scripture's own use of the name is clearer than its history. Psalm 122:6–7 says, "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem," and adds, "Peace be within your walls." The psalm does not explain the name, but its prayer for peace resonates with the traditional association. Hebrews 7:2 is different. It gives an explicit interpretation of Salem, a separate name: Melchizedek, king of Salem, is "king of peace." Hebrews does not use this to explain where the name Jerusalem came from. Neither passage is a technical history of the name Jerusalem, and neither turns the name into a summary of the city's spiritual life. In fact, the gap between the city's traditional association with peace and its actual history becomes a sharp irony in the Bible's story of the city.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Before Israel: Salem, Moriah and the Jebusite Stronghold

Jerusalem first appears under the name H3389 when "Adoni-Zedek king of Jerusalem" heard that Joshua had destroyed Ai and that Gibeon had made peace with Israel (Joshua 10:1). He gathered a coalition of Amorite kings against Gibeon. Joshua defeated them, and the five kings were executed (Joshua 10:3–27), but Joshua did not take the city itself. Judges 1:8 records that the men of Judah fought against Jerusalem, took it and set the city on fire. Even so, the Jebusites retained or regained their hold on it. Judah "could not drive them out" (Joshua 15:63), Benjamin did not drive them out either (Judges 1:21), and the Jebusites still held the stronghold when David came. In Judges 19:10–12, a Levite travels past Jebus at nightfall and refuses to stay in "a city of foreigners." He presses on to the Israelite town of Gibeah, where a terrible crime follows. The man avoided the pagan city and found grievous wickedness among God's own people instead.

Behind this period lie two earlier scenes that later Scripture connects with the site. Melchizedek, "king of Salem" and "priest of God Most High," brought bread and wine to Abraham and blessed him (Genesis 14:18–20). Psalm 76:2 sets Salem alongside Zion as God's dwelling place. Years later, God sent Abraham to "the land of Moriah" to offer Isaac on one of its mountains, and Abraham named the place "The-LORD-Will-Provide" (Genesis 22:2, 14). Second Chronicles 3:1 calls the temple site Mount Moriah. These scenes use separate names, but Scripture itself ties Salem to Zion and the name Moriah to the temple mount.

David's City

As a young man, David carried Goliath's head to Jerusalem (1 Samuel 17:54), while the Jebusites still held its stronghold. Years later, after reigning seven and a half years in Hebron, David led his men against it. The Jebusites mocked him: "You shall not come in here; but the blind and the lame will repel you" (2 Samuel 5:6). "Nevertheless David took the stronghold of Zion (that is, the City of David)" (2 Samuel 5:7). David promised that whoever climbed up "by way of the water shaft" and defeated the Jebusites would be chief and captain (2 Samuel 5:8), and Joab went up first (1 Chronicles 11:6). David then lived in the stronghold and built all around from the Millo inward (2 Samuel 5:9). Joshua had assigned Jerusalem to Benjamin's inheritance (Joshua 18:28), and Judah had once taken and burned the city (Judges 1:8), but no Israelite tribe had kept lasting control of the Jebusite stronghold, and it stood near the border between Judah and the northern tribes. Choosing it as the capital allowed David to unite the nation around a center that did not favor one tribe over the others. Scripture does not state this motive outright, but the setting strongly suggests it.

David then brought the ark of God into the city with dancing and sacrifices (2 Samuel 6). With the ark in Jerusalem, the city became the seat of two thrones at once: the throne of the LORD, enthroned above the cherubim, and the throne of His anointed king. In 2 Samuel 7 God made His covenant with David, promising that David's house and kingdom would be established forever. From then on, Jerusalem's hopes were bound to the promise of a son of David.

David's own years in the city also reveal his failures. He stayed in Jerusalem while his army went to war, and there he took Bathsheba (2 Samuel 11:1–4). His son Absalom rebelled, and David fled the city barefoot and weeping. He sent the ark back, trusting that if he found favor with the LORD, God would bring him back to see it again (2 Samuel 15:14, 25–30). Near the end of his reign, David's census brought a plague. When the angel stretched out his hand over Jerusalem to destroy it, the LORD relented and told the angel to restrain his hand, and the angel was then by the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite (2 Samuel 24:16). Afterward, at God's word through the prophet Gad, David bought that threshing floor, built an altar and offered burnt offerings and peace offerings, and the plague was withdrawn from Israel (2 Samuel 24:18–25). That threshing floor became the site of the temple. Second Chronicles 3:1 calls it the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite, using the form of the owner's name found in Chronicles, while 2 Samuel calls him Araunah. Jerusalem's house of worship stands on ground where God first held back judgment in mercy and then answered sacrifice.

Solomon's Temple and Glory

Solomon built the temple on Mount Moriah and brought up the ark (1 Kings 8:1). When the priests came out of the holy place, "the cloud filled the house of the LORD... for the glory of the LORD filled the house of the LORD" (1 Kings 8:10–11). Solomon declared, "I have surely built You an exalted house, And a place for You to dwell in forever" (1 Kings 8:13). At the dedication, God's choice was stated plainly: "Yet I have chosen Jerusalem, that My name may be there, and I have chosen David to be over My people Israel" (2 Chronicles 6:6). The chosen city and the chosen king belong together.

The city prospered under Solomon. The queen of Sheba came to Jerusalem to test his wisdom (1 Kings 10:1–2), and silver became as common there as stones (1 Kings 10:27). Yet in that same reign Solomon built a high place for Chemosh and Molech on the hill east of Jerusalem (1 Kings 11:7). Ecclesiastes, written in the voice of "the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (Ecclesiastes 1:1), describes greatness beyond "all who were before me in Jerusalem" (Ecclesiastes 1:16; 2:9). It also confesses that this splendor could not satisfy the heart. The Song of Solomon addresses the "daughters of Jerusalem" (Song of Solomon 3:5), and those women of the royal city appear as companions throughout the song.

The Kings of Judah

When the kingdom divided, God kept Jerusalem for David's line, "that My servant David may always have a lamp before Me in Jerusalem, the city which I have chosen for Myself, to put My name there" (1 Kings 11:36). Jeroboam, king of the northern tribes, feared that the people's hearts would return to Rehoboam if they kept going up to sacrifice in Jerusalem. So he set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:27–28). His fear shows how strongly the true worship of God was tied to the city God had chosen.

The book of Kings measures each king of Judah by what he did in Jerusalem. Shishak of Egypt plundered the temple treasures (1 Kings 14:25–26). Jehoash of Israel broke down part of the wall (2 Kings 14:13). Rezin and Pekah besieged the city in Ahaz's day (2 Kings 16:5). The greatest crisis came when Sennacherib of Assyria sent his officials to mock Hezekiah's trust in the LORD (2 Kings 18:17–35). God answered through Isaiah that "the virgin, the daughter of Zion" despised the Assyrian king (2 Kings 19:21) and that a remnant would go out from Jerusalem (2 Kings 19:31). That night the angel of the LORD struck the Assyrian camp (2 Kings 19:35).

Reform and ruin alternated. Jehoshaphat stood in the temple court and prayed when a great army came against Judah, and the people were told to believe the LORD and be established (2 Chronicles 20:5–20). Hezekiah celebrated a Passover so joyful that nothing like it had happened in Jerusalem since Solomon's day (2 Chronicles 30:26). He even sent invitations to the northern tribes. Manasseh, however, set up idols in the temple and "shed very much innocent blood, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another" (2 Kings 21:16). God warned that He would wipe Jerusalem as a person wipes a dish (2 Kings 21:13). Yet when Manasseh humbled himself in captivity, God heard him and brought him back to Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 33:12–13). Josiah later read the Book of the Law to the people of Jerusalem, removed the idols, defiled the child-sacrifice site of Tophet, and held a great Passover (2 Kings 23:1–23). Even so, the LORD said He would cast off "this city Jerusalem which I have chosen" (2 Kings 23:27) because of Manasseh's sins.

Destruction and Exile

Babylon came in stages. In 597 BC Nebuchadnezzar took King Jehoiachin, the temple treasures and the leading people into captivity (2 Kings 24:10–16). Mordecai's family, the book of Esther notes, had been carried away from Jerusalem in that deportation (Esther 2:6). After Zedekiah rebelled, Babylon besieged the city again. In 586 BC Babylon burned the house of the LORD and the king's house and broke down the walls of Jerusalem all around (2 Kings 25:1–10). Chronicles explains that God had sent His messengers again and again "because He had compassion on His people," but they mocked them until there was no remedy (2 Chronicles 36:15–16).

The prophets interpret this catastrophe. Jeremiah stood at the temple gate and warned the people not to trust in the words, "The temple of the LORD," while they stole, murdered and followed other gods (Jeremiah 7:1–15). They had built the high places of Tophet in the Valley of Hinnom "to burn their sons and their daughters in the fire, which I did not command, nor did it come into My heart" (Jeremiah 7:31). In a vision, Ezekiel watched the glory of the LORD leave the temple and the city (Ezekiel 10; 11:22–23). Amos had already announced, "But I will send a fire upon Judah, And it shall devour the palaces of Jerusalem" (Amos 2:5). After the fall, Lamentations mourns: "How lonely sits the city That was full of people! How like a widow is she, Who was great among the nations!" (Lamentations 1:1). Psalm 79 cries out that the nations have defiled the temple and laid Jerusalem in heaps. Psalm 137 gives voice to the exiles who could not forget the city: "If I forget you, O Jerusalem, Let my right hand forget its skill!" (Psalm 137:5).

Return and Rebuilding

The LORD stirred Cyrus of Persia to send the exiles home to build the house of the LORD in Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 36:23; Ezra 1:1–4). The returning people rebuilt the altar and laid the temple's foundation. Some shouted for joy while the old men who remembered the first house wept (Ezra 3). Ezra later came to teach God's law (Ezra 7). When Nehemiah heard that the wall of Jerusalem lay broken and its gates burned, he wept and prayed (Nehemiah 1:3–4). He called the people to rise and build so that they would no longer be a reproach (Nehemiah 2:17, paraphrased), and they built with tools in one hand and weapons in the other (Nehemiah 4). The wall was dedicated with thanksgiving so loud that the joy of Jerusalem was heard far away (Nehemiah 12:27–43). Nehemiah's final chapter shows the struggle beginning again as Sabbath trading crept back into the city (Nehemiah 13:15–22).

Jerusalem in the Life of Jesus and the Church

Jerusalem's story continues in the New Testament under its Greek names. As an infant Jesus was presented in the temple, where Anna spoke of Him to all who looked for redemption in Jerusalem (Luke 2:38). He "steadfastly set His face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51), because "it cannot be that a prophet should perish outside of Jerusalem" (Luke 13:33). He entered the city as Zechariah's lowly king (Matthew 21:5; Zechariah 9:9). As He came near, He wept over the city because it did not know the things that made for its peace (Luke 19:41–44). He grieved, "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the one who kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to her! How often I wanted to gather your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, but you were not willing!" (Matthew 23:37). He foretold the destruction of the temple and the city (Luke 21:5–6, 20–24), which Rome carried out in AD 70.

Jesus was crucified outside the city and rose on the third day. He commanded that repentance and forgiveness be preached in His name to all nations, "beginning at Jerusalem" (Luke 24:47). The Spirit was poured out in Jerusalem at Pentecost (Acts 2), and the witness went out from Jerusalem to Judea, Samaria and the end of the earth (Acts 1:8). The church in Jerusalem gathered the apostles and elders to decide how Gentiles would be received (Acts 15), and Paul brought an offering from the Gentile churches to its poor saints (Romans 15:25–27).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Jerusalem's importance rests on God's choice. Rehoboam reigned "in Jerusalem, the city which the LORD had chosen out of all the tribes of Israel, to put His name there" (1 Kings 14:21). The psalmist adds that God "chose the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion which He loved" (Psalm 78:68). Jerusalem did not earn this place. It had been a Jebusite fortress, and Ezekiel later reminds the city that her father was an Amorite and her mother a Hittite (Ezekiel 16:3). God's choice of Jerusalem follows the same pattern as His choice of Israel. It is an act of love, not a reward.

Because God put His name there, Jerusalem gathered Israel's worship into one place. The tribes went up "To give thanks to the name of the LORD" (Psalm 122:4), and the thrones of David's house were set there for judgment (Psalm 122:5). The city held together the LORD's presence in the temple and the reign of His anointed king. Scripture presents this as God's design. Over time, Jerusalem's name came to stand for the whole people. When the prophets announced fire on the palaces of Jerusalem (Amos 2:5) or comfort for Jerusalem (Isaiah 40:1–2), they were speaking to the nation through its capital.

The same Scripture insists that the city's holiness depended on God's presence and the people's faithfulness, not on its stones. Jeremiah's temple sermon attacked the false confidence that God would never let His house fall (Jeremiah 7). Ezekiel saw the glory depart before the city was destroyed. The LORD's sentence in 2 Kings 23:27 shows that God was willing to cast off the very city He had chosen. From these texts we may fairly conclude that God will not allow His own institutions to shelter idolatry and injustice. He guarded His holiness even when it meant the loss of His chosen sanctuary.

Judgment was not God's last word. Isaiah foresaw that "the mountain of the LORD's house Shall be established on the top of the mountains," that "all nations shall flow to it," and that "out of Zion shall go forth the law, And the word of the LORD from Jerusalem" (Isaiah 2:2–3; see Micah 4:1). Isaiah also pictured watchmen set on Jerusalem's walls who give the LORD no rest until He makes the city a praise in the earth (Isaiah 62:6–7). Zechariah promised that the LORD would be a wall of fire around Jerusalem and the glory in her midst (Zechariah 2:5). Ezekiel's restoration vision ends by naming the city it describes: "THE LORD IS THERE" (Ezekiel 48:35). Ezekiel does not call that city Jerusalem, but his vision shares the same hope. Each of these promises places God's presence at the heart of the restoration of His people.

The prophets often describe Jerusalem as a woman, and that picture reveals how personally God regards the city. Ezekiel 16 tells the story of an abandoned newborn whom God found, washed, clothed and crowned. She trusted in her own beauty and gave herself to other nations and their gods. Isaiah lamented how "the faithful city" had become a harlot (Isaiah 1:21). After the fall, Lamentations portrays her as a grieving widow. Elsewhere she is the "daughter of Zion," whom God defends (2 Kings 19:21). The language is that of marriage and family, betrayal and grief. To God, Jerusalem was never merely a capital city.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

What the New Testament states directly. Jesus Himself tied His death to Jerusalem (Luke 13:33). He spoke of His body as the temple that would be destroyed and raised up in three days (John 2:19–21). John's comment moves the center of worship from the temple building to the person of Christ. Hebrews draws out the meaning of where He died: "Therefore Jesus also, that He might sanctify the people with His own blood, suffered outside the gate" (Hebrews 13:12). The city where Israel's sacrifices were offered became the place where the final sacrifice was made, yet that sacrifice was offered outside the walls, in the place of rejection and shame. Hebrews then calls believers to go out to Him there, because here we have no continuing city (Hebrews 13:13–14, paraphrased).

Paul gives the plainest New Testament interpretation of the two Jerusalems. Hagar "is Mount Sinai in Arabia, and corresponds to Jerusalem which now is, and is in bondage with her children— but the Jerusalem above is free, which is the mother of us all" (Galatians 4:25–26). Here Paul deliberately reverses the usual expectation. The earthly city, which saw itself as the center of God's people, is placed with Hagar, Sinai and slavery. The heavenly city is the true mother of all who belong to Christ, whether Jew or Gentile. Hebrews says the same thing in another way: believers have come to Mount Zion, the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem (Hebrews 12:22). Abraham himself waited for a city with foundations, whose builder and maker is God (Hebrews 11:10). Paul's allegory applies to a specific argument about the two covenants. It does not mean that every Old Testament mention of Jerusalem carries a hidden meaning. It does show where the city's story was heading.

Revelation brings the story to its end. John saw "the holy city, New Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Revelation 21:2). The prophets' image of Jerusalem as an unfaithful wife is answered at last by a holy city that comes down from God, prepared as a bride. The city also needs no temple: "But I saw no temple in it, for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple" (Revelation 21:22). In the old city, God's presence filled one house. In the new city, His presence fills everything. Revelation also uses the earthly city as a warning. The place "where also our Lord was crucified" is the great city "which spiritually is called Sodom and Egypt" (Revelation 11:8). Interpreters debate exactly what that great city stands for in Revelation, but the verse plainly reminds readers that the city of God's name can come to share the character of the places God judged.

Strong canonical connections. Two earlier scenes connected with this ground point toward Christ. In the land of Moriah, Abraham was told to offer his only son on one of its mountains, and God provided a ram in Isaac's place (Genesis 22). Second Chronicles 3:1 builds the temple on Mount Moriah, at the threshing floor of Ornan, the man 2 Samuel calls Araunah. There God first restrained the angel of judgment; afterward David built an altar and offered sacrifices there, and the plague was withdrawn (2 Samuel 24:16–25). Scripture links the two scenes by the name Moriah; that they took place on exactly the same ground is a long-standing traditional reading. Centuries later, God gave His own Son at Jerusalem. Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, ruled before Levi's priesthood existed (Genesis 14:18). Psalm 110:4 promises that David's Lord would be "a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek." Hebrews 7 applies that promise to Christ, the king of righteousness and king of peace. Salem is a separately indexed name. Hebrews never calls it Jerusalem, though Psalm 76:2 links Salem with Zion. Even with that caution, the canonical line is strong: the city tied to a priest-king of peace becomes the city where the true Priest-King offers Himself.

Thematic resonance. The name's traditional association with peace gives the story a sad irony. Jerusalem was fought over, besieged, plundered and burned again and again, and Jesus wept because the city did not recognize the things that made for its peace (Luke 19:42). Scripture never claims the city produced its own peace. That peace comes through Christ, who "Himself is our peace" (Ephesians 2:14) and made peace through the blood of His cross (Colossians 1:20). The Bible's other famous city offers a contrast as well. At Babel, people said, "Let us build ourselves a city" to make a name for themselves (Genesis 11:4). Jerusalem was the city God chose for His own name, and the New Jerusalem is a city God builds and sends down. This contrast is a thematic reading rather than an explicit biblical claim, but it helps explain why the Bible ends with a city rather than a garden alone.

5. Spirit & Life

Jerusalem teaches us that God's presence is a gift to be cherished, not a possession to be presumed upon. The people of Jeremiah's day repeated "The temple of the LORD" as if a building could guarantee their safety. Churches and believers can fall into the same trap when they trust in a heritage, a tradition or a religious setting while their hearts drift from God. Jerusalem had the ark, the priests, the psalms and the promises, and it still fell. God had sent His messengers again and again out of compassion (2 Chronicles 36:15), and when the city still would not turn, His judgment showed a holy God who refuses to be used as a cover for sin.

The city's history also shows how near the holy place and the hellish place can lie. The Valley of Hinnom ran along Jerusalem's edge. There, at the edge of the city where the temple stood, people burned their children in the fire, something God said never entered His heart (Jeremiah 7:31). Jesus later used the name of that valley, Gehenna, as a picture of final judgment. Religious nearness is no protection against terrible sin. The only safe place is a heart that truly belongs to God.

Mercy runs through the whole story, though. Manasseh, who filled Jerusalem with innocent blood, humbled himself in Babylon, and God brought him home (2 Chronicles 33:13). Psalm 137 weeps by the rivers of Babylon, remembering Zion. Psalm 147 answers with praise: "The LORD builds up Jerusalem; He gathers together the outcasts of Israel" (Psalm 147:2). Nehemiah wept over broken walls and then picked up his tools. Jesus wept over the city that would reject Him and still went in to die for it. His lament in Matthew 23:37 shows us the heart of God: "How often I wanted to gather your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings." God's grief over Jerusalem is the grief of a rejected love that does not stop loving.

The pilgrim songs give us a way to respond. Psalm 122 begins, "I was glad when they said to me, 'Let us go into the house of the LORD,'" and it calls God's people to "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem." Psalm 125:2 says that as the mountains surround Jerusalem, so the LORD surrounds His people. Christians share that gladness and that security when we draw near to God through Christ, our true temple. We pray for the peace that only He gives. We also live as citizens of "the Jerusalem above," which "is free" (Galatians 4:26). That means we are not slaves straining to earn a place with God. We are children of promise who wait for the city God is preparing, where He will dwell with His people, wipe away every tear, and put an end to death and sorrow (Revelation 21:3–4).

  • 2 Samuel 6–7 — The ark comes to Jerusalem, and God makes His covenant with David's house.

  • 1 Kings 8:10–13; 2 Chronicles 6:6 — The glory fills the temple, and God declares that He has chosen Jerusalem for His name.

  • 1 Kings 11:36; 14:21; Psalm 78:68 — The city God chose and loved is preserved as a lamp for David's line.

  • 2 Kings 19:21, 31–35 — God defends Jerusalem against Assyria.

  • 2 Kings 21:16; 23:27; 25:8–10 — Manasseh's bloodshed, God's sentence against the chosen city, and its destruction by Babylon.

  • Lamentations 1:1; Psalm 137 — Grief over the fallen city, pictured as a widow.

  • Psalm 122; 125:2; 147:2 — Pilgrimage, prayer for Jerusalem's peace, God's protection of His people, and His work of restoration.

  • Isaiah 2:2–3; Micah 4:1; Isaiah 62:6–7; Ezekiel 48:35 — The nations will stream to God's mountain, Jerusalem will be restored, and the city of Ezekiel's restoration vision is named "THE LORD IS THERE."
