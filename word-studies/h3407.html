CURTAIN, CURTAINS - (yᵉrîyʻâh, Strong's H3407)

Summary

The Hebrew word yᵉrîyʻâh means a curtain: a long panel of woven fabric or goats' hair that could be joined with other panels to form the walls and roof of a tent. Most of its uses describe the curtains of the wilderness tabernacle, the movable tent where God chose to live among Israel. The inner curtains were fine linen woven with cherubim. Over them lay a coarse tent of goats' hair. Fifty gold clasps joined the linen curtains "so that it may be one tabernacle" (Exodus 26:6), and fifty bronze clasps joined the goats'-hair tent "that it may be one" (Exodus 26:11). These curtains are the tent itself. They are not the inner veil that hung before the Most Holy Place.

Beyond the tabernacle, Scripture uses the word in ways that reveal God's heart. David was troubled that "the ark of God dwells inside tent curtains" while he lived in cedar. A psalmist pictures God stretching out the heavens "like a curtain." Isaiah commands barren Zion to "stretch out the curtains of your dwellings" for the children God was about to give her. Jeremiah grieves over curtains torn down in a moment. Taken together, these passages show a God who comes near to His people in humble ways, who widens His household by grace, and who remains a secure shelter when human shelters fail. These patterns come to their fullest meaning in Jesus Christ, the Word who "became flesh and dwelt among us."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yᵉrîyʻâh is a Hebrew noun used for the large cloth panels of a tent. In Israel's world, a tent was made by sewing or fastening strips of fabric together and stretching them over poles with cords and stakes. Some lexicons connect the word to a root meaning to tremble or quiver. That is a fitting picture of cloth moving in the wind, but the word's meaning comes from how Scripture uses it.

The word appears mostly in Exodus 26 and 36, which give God's instructions for the tabernacle curtains and then record how the craftsmen made them. It also appears in the setting of David's palace, in a psalm about creation, in a love song, and in prophetic oracles of judgment and hope.

Readers should not confuse these curtains with the veil. Exodus uses a different Hebrew word (pārōkeṯ) for the veil that separated the Most Holy Place. It uses yet another word (māsāk) for the screen at the entrance. The yᵉrîyʻâh panels formed the enclosure of God's dwelling. The veil that tore when Jesus died is a different object.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means a curtain or panel of a tent. Scripture uses it in three main ways:

1. A fabric panel of the tabernacle, either the inner linen curtains or the outer goats'-hair curtains that together made God's dwelling (Exodus 26:1, 7).

2. The curtain of an ordinary tent, which by extension stands for a home and household (Jeremiah 4:20; Habakkuk 3:7).

3. A poetic image of the sky as something God spreads out like tent-cloth (Psalm 104:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The tabernacle chapters are practical and exact. Each linen curtain was twenty-eight cubits long and four cubits wide, and "every one of the curtains shall have the same measurements" (Exodus 26:2). Exodus 36:8–17 repeats the details almost word for word as the work is carried out. That repetition shows Israel building exactly what God had commanded.

The word is often plural, because a tent is many curtains joined into one structure. It frequently appears beside "tent," as in Isaiah's "the place of your tent... the curtains of your dwellings" (Isaiah 54:2). The setting determines how the image is used. In Isaiah the curtains are stretched outward to make room. In Jeremiah they are plundered and pulled down. In the Song of Solomon they stand for beauty.

4. Theological Significance

The curtains show that the holy God chose to live close to His people. He did not ask Israel to build a fortress in the wilderness. He asked for a tent that could be taken down, carried by the Gershonites (Numbers 4:25), and set up again wherever He led them. God dwelt among a people on the move: "At the command of the LORD the children of Israel would journey, and at the command of the LORD they would camp" (Numbers 9:18).

Psalm 104:2 enlarges the same picture to the whole creation. The God who covers Himself "with light as with a garment" is the God "Who stretch out the heavens like a curtain." The tabernacle curtains required the work of skilled weavers. God spreads the sky as easily as a man unrolls tent-cloth. Isaiah 40:22 uses a related image with a different Hebrew word, describing God spreading the heavens "like a tent to dwell in."

A tent is also temporary. Hebrews 1:10–12 says of the heavens, "They will perish, but You remain... Like a cloak You will fold them up." The sky that seems permanent to us is, before God, a curtain that He will one day fold away. Only He remains unchanged.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 26:1–13: two layers joined as one. The inner set was "ten curtains of fine woven linen and blue, purple, and scarlet thread; with artistic designs of cherubim" (26:1). These inner curtains are themselves called "the tabernacle." Over them went "curtains of goats' hair, to be a tent over the tabernacle" (26:7). The glory was inside: cherubim like those who guarded Eden, woven in royal colors. Directly over the linen lay plain, weather-resistant goats' hair, much like the dark tents of desert nomads. Above that came two more protective coverings, one of ram skins dyed red and another of badger skins (26:14). Exodus 26 gives the order of the layers from the inside out but does not describe every surface a person outside could see. It does make clear that the cherubim and royal colors of the linen lay beneath the coverings above them. The sixth goats'-hair curtain was doubled over at the front (26:9), and the extra length hung down the back and sides "to cover it" (26:12–13). Some readers connect the goats' hair with the goat of the sin offering. That connection makes for good meditation, but Scripture does not draw it.

The joining of the curtains also matters. Blue loops and fifty gold clasps coupled the linen curtains "so that it may be one tabernacle" (26:6). Fifty bronze clasps joined the goats'-hair tent "that it may be one" (26:11). Joined together, the panels formed the single dwelling God had instructed Israel to build.

2 Samuel 7:2: David's cedar and God's curtains. Once David had rest from his enemies, he felt the contrast between his palace and God's tent: "See now, I dwell in a house of cedar, but the ark of God dwells inside tent curtains" (also 1 Chronicles 17:1). God's reply through Nathan reminds David that He had never asked for a house of cedar. He had moved about with Israel in a tent (7:6–7). God then turned the conversation around and promised to build David a house, a royal line that would last forever. David wanted to give God something permanent. God answered by promising David something permanent.

Isaiah 54:2: stretching the curtains. Isaiah 53 describes the Servant who "shall bear their iniquities" and who will "justify many." Immediately afterward, God calls barren Zion to sing and then commands: "Enlarge the place of your tent, And let them stretch out the curtains of your dwellings; Do not spare; Lengthen your cords, And strengthen your stakes." The reason follows in Isaiah 54:3: Zion's descendants will spread to the right and the left and inherit the nations. The order matters. Coming right after the Servant who bears the sins of many, the command suggests that the family born from His suffering will be too large for the old tent. Longer cords allow a wider tent, and deeper stakes keep it from collapsing as it grows. God tells Zion, "Do not spare." She is not to plan small.

Jeremiah 4:20 and 10:20: curtains torn down. As invasion approached, Jeremiah cried, "Suddenly my tents are plundered, And my curtains in a moment" (4:20). In 10:20 the picture returns: "My tent is plundered, And all my cords are broken; My children have gone from me... There is no one to pitch my tent anymore, Or set up my curtains." The fallen tent, the broken cords, the missing children and, in the next verse, the scattered flock work together as connected pictures of the nation's loss. That next verse also blames shepherds who "have not sought the LORD" (10:21). Without God, a nation's security is no stronger than cloth.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

John 1:14 says, "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory." The Greek verb translated "dwelt" is related to the word for a tent. It describes the Son of God pitching His tent among us. The tabernacle pattern comes to its fullness in Jesus, and John states plainly what the witnesses saw: "we beheld His glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father." By way of analogy, the tabernacle's plain outer coverings over its fine inner curtains can also recall Isaiah 53:2, which says the Servant had "no beauty that we should desire Him." The Son "made Himself of no reputation" (Philippians 2:7) and lived among His people as God had lived among Israel in the wilderness.

David's longing in 2 Samuel 7 also points to Christ. The house God promised David leads to David's Son, whose kingdom has no end. In Galatians 4:27–28 Paul quotes the promise of many children in Isaiah 54:1, "Rejoice, O barren," and then tells believers, "Now we, brethren, as Isaac was, are children of promise." Paul does not quote the curtain command, but it stands in the very next verse of Isaiah (Isaiah 54:2) and supplies the matching picture: a tent stretched wider to make room for that growing household.

The joined curtains offer a picture of the church. In Christ "the whole building, being fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord," and believers "are being built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:21–22). The story ends where the tabernacle pointed. The present heavens will be folded up like a cloak, and Revelation 21:3 announces that the tabernacle of God is with men and He will dwell with them.

7. Spirit & Life

The Shulamite says, "I am dark, but lovely, O daughters of Jerusalem, Like the tents of Kedar, Like the curtains of Solomon" (Song of Solomon 1:5). Kedar's tents were the dark, weathered tents of desert tribes. Solomon's curtains were the hangings of a king's pavilion. The bride holds both images together without denying either. She knows the sun has darkened her and that she is loved. Read alongside the tabernacle, her words fit the believer. We bear the marks of a hard world on the outside. Within, Christ is preparing "a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle" (Ephesians 5:27). John says, "now we are children of God; and it has not yet been revealed what we shall be" (1 John 3:2). Like the tabernacle, with its cherubim-woven linen hidden beneath goats' hair and skin coverings, we may look plain and worn on the outside, but the King lives within.

The clasps of Exodus 26 also carry a message for the church. By way of comparison, God's dwelling was not a pile of loose panels, however beautiful each one was; the clasps joined them into the one tabernacle He commanded. A believer who stays detached from God's people misses part of what God is building.

Jeremiah's fallen tent warns anyone who trusts in a home, a nation, or a carefully arranged life. Those curtains can come down "in a moment." Isaiah 54 gives the other side. The God who allows human tents to fall is the same God who tells His people to stretch their curtains wider. Faith hears that command as a call to make room for the people God is bringing in, because the Servant's work has already been done.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Exodus 36:8–17. The craftsmen made the curtains exactly as God had commanded. Faithful obedience in small details built the place where His glory came to rest.

Numbers 4:25. The Gershonites were assigned to carry the tabernacle curtains on the march. Holy things were placed in ordinary hands for the journey.

1 Chronicles 17:1. The Chronicler repeats David's words about the ark under curtains. Israel remembered that God had been content with a tent.

Jeremiah 49:29. In the oracle against Kedar, enemies take the tribe's tents, flocks, and curtains. The same nomadic tents admired in Song of Solomon 1:5 fall under judgment.

Habakkuk 3:7. Habakkuk sees the curtains of Midian trembling as God comes in power. Human dwellings shake when the Lord appears.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

H6532 (pārōkeṯ), veil. This is a distinct word, not a relative of yᵉrîyʻâh. It names the inner veil before the Most Holy Place, which separated people from God's presence. The curtains formed the enclosure around God's dwelling, while the veil guarded the way into the Most Holy Place (Exodus 26:31–33).

H4539 (māsāk), screen. This is also a distinct word. It names the hanging at the entrance of the tent (Exodus 26:36), which marked the doorway rather than forming the walls.
