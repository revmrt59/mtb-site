THIGH, HIP, SIDE - (yârêk, Strong's H3409)

Summary

The Hebrew word yârêk means thigh or hip, the strong upper leg and the joint that bears a person's weight. Scripture uses it for the body itself: where a soldier straps his sword, where a priest's linen covering must reach, and where a grieving person strikes himself in shame. Because the thigh is also a modest way of speaking of the body's power to father children, the word can stand for a man's descendants. By extension, it also names the side or central stem of objects, such as the side of the tabernacle and the main shaft of the golden lampstand.

The word appears at some memorable turning points in Scripture. In his old age Abraham has his servant swear with a hand "under my thigh" that he will seek Isaac's wife among Abraham's own kindred and not take one from the Canaanites, and Jacob, near death, has Joseph swear the same way to bury him in Canaan. The Lord touches Jacob's hip at Peniel, and the man who leaves limping has been renamed Israel. Ephraim strikes his thigh in repentance, and God answers him with a father's mercy. Psalm 45 pictures the King girding His sword on His thigh. In Revelation 19:16, Christ returns with His name written "on His robe and on His thigh." Taken together, these passages picture God taking hold of the place of human strength and the line of human descent and turning both toward His own purposes.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yârêk is an ordinary Hebrew word for a part of the body. Like many Hebrew body words, it could also describe parts of objects, in the same way English speaks of the "foot" of a mountain or the "arm" of a chair. The word occurs 34 times in 32 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and 23 of those occurrences fall in the first five books of the Bible. There it appears in the stories of the patriarchs and in the instructions for building the tabernacle. It appears again in battle stories in Judges, and in the poetry and prophets.

Two features of the ancient setting help a modern reader. First, a sword usually hung at the thigh, so a sword "on the thigh" meant a man was armed and ready. Second, the thigh was a modest way of referring to the reproductive body. That second use explains why the same word can be translated "descendants" in one verse and "thigh" in the next.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The thigh or hip of the body. The word names the upper leg and the hip joint, as in Jacob's hip being put out of joint (Genesis 32:25) or Ehud's dagger strapped "on his right thigh" (Judges 3:16).

2. The body as the source of descendants. Used as a modest figure of speech, the thigh stands for a man's power to father children. In the Hebrew idiom, people who came out of a man's thigh are his offspring. The NKJV translates this sense as "body," "descendants," or "offspring" (Genesis 46:26; Exodus 1:5; Judges 8:30).

3. The side or base of an object. The word can name the side of a structure or the central stem of an object: the north "side" of the tabernacle (Exodus 40:22) or the "shaft" of the lampstand (Exodus 25:31).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The sense of yârêk depends on its setting. In military scenes it marks readiness to fight. At Moses' command the Levites strap on their swords: "Let every man put his sword on his side" (Exodus 32:27). Solomon's guards carry swords on their thighs against the terrors of the night (Song of Solomon 3:8). In love poetry, the same word praises the graceful curves of the bride's thighs (Song of Solomon 7:1). In Ezekiel's parable of the boiling pot, the "thigh and the shoulder" are the choice cuts of meat (Ezekiel 24:4).

In the tabernacle passages, the word becomes a word of location. The tabernacle has a north "side" (Exodus 40:22, 24). Burnt offerings are slain on the north side of the altar (Leviticus 1:11). The Levite clans camp on the sides of the tabernacle (Numbers 3:29, 35). One later use is telling. King Ahaz moved the Lord's bronze altar off to the north side so that a pagan-style altar copied from Damascus could take its place (2 Kings 16:14). Pushing God's appointed altar to the side makes a fitting picture of what Ahaz was doing spiritually.

Judges 15:8 preserves a vivid idiom: Samson "attacked them hip and thigh with a great slaughter." The Hebrew pictures leg piled upon thigh, an image of a crushing and total defeat.

4. Theological Significance

Across its uses, yârêk keeps bringing God's purposes down into the human body. The God of Israel does not deal with people as disembodied souls. He makes promises that run through real families. He wounds a real hip and heals a real heart.

The word also shows that the body is accountable before a holy God. Aaron's sons had to wear linen trousers "to cover their nakedness; they shall reach from the waist to the thighs" (Exodus 28:42). The next verse warns that a priest who failed to wear them would "incur iniquity and die" (Exodus 28:43). Human flesh, especially in its most private and procreative part, could not be paraded before the altar. Only a covering God prescribed made it safe to draw near.

The law of jealousy in Numbers 5 makes the same point from the side of judgment. A wife suspected of secret adultery drank the bitter water before the Lord. If she was guilty, the Lord would make her thigh rot and her belly swell (Numbers 5:21–22, 27, paraphrased). The curse names the thigh and the belly as the signs of her guilt for unfaithfulness to her husband, though it does not explain exactly what physical condition is meant. No one else may have known, but the Lord knew, and He guards the faithfulness of marriage.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The oath under the thigh (Genesis 24:2, 9; 47:29). When Abraham "was old, well advanced in age" (Genesis 24:1), he told his oldest servant, "Please, put your hand under my thigh." He then made him swear by the LORD not to take a wife for Isaac from the Canaanites, but to go to Abraham's own country and family to seek one (Genesis 24:3–4). The oath did not bind the servant to succeed: if the woman would not come, he would be released from it, though he must never take Isaac back there (Genesis 24:8). Years later, "When the time drew near that Israel must die" (Genesis 47:29), Jacob asked Joseph to swear with the same gesture and made him promise to bury him with his fathers in Canaan, not in Egypt. The two oaths concern different things. Abraham's oath is about Isaac's marriage and the family God promised him; Jacob's oath is about his burial in the land God promised. The shared gesture may also say something. The hand was placed near the source of a man's descendants, where God had set the sign of circumcision (Genesis 17:10–11), and on that reading the oath called on the promised generations as witnesses. That is an interpretation of the gesture, since Scripture does not explain it. Either way, neither request was merely a private wish. Each oath expressed loyalty to what God had sworn to Abraham and his offspring, and Paul identifies that Seed ultimately as Christ (Galatians 3:16).

Jacob's hip at Peniel (Genesis 32:25, 31–32). Jacob had spent his life gripping, grasping, and outmaneuvering others. Now he wrestled through the night with a Man, and the struggle reached a standstill: the Man "saw that He did not prevail against him" (Genesis 32:25). The Man did not defeat him with a show of force. "He touched the socket of his hip," and with that single touch the hip was put out of joint. Yet only after that wound do we see Jacob doing what really mattered: clinging and refusing to let go until he was blessed. He received a new name, Israel, and said that he had seen God face to face (Genesis 32:30, paraphrased). Hosea calls his opponent an Angel (Hosea 12:4). Many Christians have seen the Son of God in this mysterious Man, though the text does not say so directly. Jacob left Peniel limping on his hip as the sun rose, and Genesis 32:32 explains that "to this day the children of Israel do not eat the muscle that shrank, which is on the hip socket," because of that touch. The nation carried the mark of its father's wound for generations.

Ephraim strikes his thigh (Jeremiah 31:19). Jeremiah lets us overhear the prayer of a broken people: "after I was instructed, I struck myself on the thigh; I was ashamed, yes, even humiliated, Because I bore the reproach of my youth." Striking the thigh was a gesture of sudden and painful realization, the kind of grief that comes when the truth finally lands. Ezekiel was told to strike his thigh as he announced the sword coming on Jerusalem (Ezekiel 21:12). What follows in Jeremiah is remarkable. In the next verse God answers, calling Ephraim His dear son and saying that His heart yearns for him and He will surely have mercy (Jeremiah 31:20, paraphrased). The thigh-striking does not earn that mercy. It shows the honest grief that God draws near to.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The thigh traces a line through Scripture from the patriarchs' bodies to the throne of Christ. Jacob's descendants were counted as those "who came from his body" (Genesis 46:26; compare Exodus 1:5). The Hebrew literally says they came out of his thigh. Hebrews uses the same way of thinking when it says Levi "was still in the loins of his father" when Abraham met Melchizedek (Hebrews 7:10). God's promise traveled through generations of real flesh until "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). Abraham's oath under the thigh guarded Isaac's marriage, and so the family line through which that Child would come.

Psalm 45, a royal wedding song, calls to the King: "Gird Your sword upon Your thigh, O Mighty One, With Your glory and Your majesty" (Psalm 45:3). He rides out for truth, humility, and righteousness. Hebrews applies this psalm directly to the Son of God (Hebrews 1:8–9). Revelation completes the picture in Greek. Christ comes as the rider on the white horse, the sword goes out of His mouth (Revelation 19:15), and "He has on His robe and on His thigh a name written: KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS" (Revelation 19:16). The place where a warrior's weapon hangs now bears His name. He needs no blade at His side, because His word does the striking.

The tabernacle uses also fit the larger story in a quieter way. God's dwelling was described with body words like side and shaft, and John 1:14 says that "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us." This is a resonance rather than a stated prophecy, but John invites the connection by saying the Word "dwelt," or pitched His tent, among us.

7. Spirit & Life

Peniel shows how God often blesses. God touched Jacob at the point of his strength. Paul learned the same lesson when the Lord told him, "My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness," and Paul concluded, "when I am weak, then I am strong" (2 Corinthians 12:9–10). Many believers can name the place where God touched them: a ruined plan, a failing body, a confidence that collapsed. The limp is not proof that God has abandoned us. It may be the mark of the night He would not let us go, and of the morning we learned to cling to Him.

Ephraim's prayer shows that God is not looking for polished repentance. Paul describes "godly sorrow" that "produces repentance leading to salvation" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Ephraim's grief took hold of his whole person. In the same prayer he bemoans his condition, asks God to restore him, confesses his shame, and strikes himself on the thigh (Jeremiah 31:18–19). The tax collector who "beat his breast" (Luke 18:13) is his New Testament brother. In both cases, God was already listening, and His mercy was ready.

The priestly linen points the same way. No one comes into God's presence on the strength of his own flesh. God Himself provides the covering, and the King who wears His name on His thigh is the One who brings us near.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 3:16, 21 — Ehud, a left-handed man, fastens his dagger under his clothes on his right thigh and later draws it from that same right thigh with his left hand, and the Lord uses it to deliver Israel from Moab.

  • Judges 8:30 — Gideon's seventy sons are described as his "offspring," literally those who came from his thigh, a large household that later suffered when Abimelech killed his brothers (Judges 9:5).

  • Exodus 25:31; Numbers 8:4 — The lampstand's "shaft" is its central stem, from which the branches grow, all hammered "of one piece" according to the pattern the LORD showed Moses.

  • Exodus 32:27 — After the golden calf, Moses commands, "Let every man put his sword on his side," as the Levites carry out judgment.

  • Ezekiel 24:4 — The "thigh and the shoulder," the best pieces, are thrown into the boiling pot, a picture of Jerusalem's finest people facing judgment.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The "right thigh" in Israel's offerings uses a different Hebrew word for the leg, not yârêk, and those verses do not appear among the occurrences of this word. Two different practices are involved. In the ordination rite, the ram's right thigh was among the portions put into the hands of Aaron and his sons, then taken back and burned on the altar (Exodus 29:22–25). In the peace offerings, the thigh of the heave offering was assigned to the priests as their ongoing portion (Exodus 29:27–28; Leviticus 7:32–34; compare Numbers 6:20): "the thigh of the heave offering I have taken… and I have given them to Aaron the priest" (Leviticus 7:34). These passages support a related biblical theme rather than adding to the meaning of yârêk itself.
