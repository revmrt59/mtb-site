ISHBAK - (Yishbâq, Strong's H3435)

Summary

Ishbak was one of the six sons Abraham fathered through Keturah, whose family Genesis records after the account of Sarah's burial and Isaac's marriage. He is named only twice in Scripture, both times in a list of his brothers (Genesis 25:2; 1 Chronicles 1:32). Nothing he said or did is recorded. The meaning of his name is uncertain. Some connect it to a root meaning "to leave" or "to forsake," but that link is speculative, and it should not be read as a comment on his life.

Ishbak's importance lies in where Genesis places him. Abraham "gave all that he had to Isaac," yet he also gave gifts to the sons of his concubines and sent them "eastward, away from Isaac his son" (Genesis 25:5–6). Ishbak is a real son of Abraham and part of God's promise that Abraham would be "a father of many nations" (Genesis 17:5). Still, he is not Isaac's heir or the bearer of the patriarchal promise. Scripture says nothing about his personal faith or final destiny. His brief appearance quietly illustrates what Paul later states plainly: "nor are they all children because they are the seed of Abraham" (Romans 9:7).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Ishbak is an individual: a son of Abraham and Keturah. Genesis 25:2 lists him fifth among six brothers: "Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak, and Shuah." The text does not say whether this order is the order of birth. His half-brothers were Isaac, born to Sarah, and Ishmael, born to Hagar. Genesis 25:1 calls Keturah a "wife," while 1 Chronicles 1:32 calls her "Abraham's concubine," and Genesis 25:6 groups her sons among "the sons of the concubines." No other person in Scripture bears this name. Unlike his brothers Jokshan and Midian, Ishbak has no descendants listed.

The name's meaning is not settled. A proposed connection to a Semitic root meaning "to leave" or "to forsake" has been suggested. The derivation is speculative, however, and Scripture makes no wordplay on the name. Its meaning is best described as uncertain.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Genesis records Abraham's marriage to Keturah after the account of Sarah's burial and Isaac's marriage (Genesis 23–25). The six sons of Keturah follow. Genesis 25:5–6 then settles the question of inheritance. Isaac receives "all that he had," and the other sons receive gifts. Abraham sends them east "while he was still living," so the matter is settled before his death. It is a reasonable inference that this was meant to prevent later rivalry, though Genesis does not state Abraham's motive or record the outcome. The chronicler later preserves Ishbak's name in the same sibling list (1 Chronicles 1:32), as part of a survey of the nations that came from Abraham before his focus narrows to Israel. After that list, Ishbak disappears from the biblical record.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states two things clearly. Ishbak is truly Abraham's son, and he is not Abraham's heir in the particular inheritance given to Isaac. Both facts matter. God had said, "I will make nations of you" (Genesis 17:6), and the sons of Keturah are part of that abundance. The same God had also said that the line of promise would run through Isaac (Genesis 17:19–21; 21:12). Abraham's gifts show a father's care for every son. His decision to send them away shows that he would not let anything blur the line God had chosen.

It is an inference, though a well-grounded one, that this passage shows God's purposes advancing by His choice rather than by bloodline alone. Ishbak had the same father as Isaac, yet that did not make him the bearer of the promised line. This concerns his place in the patriarchal inheritance, not a verdict on his personal faith, which Scripture does not record.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never names Ishbak. Paul's argument in Romans 9 nonetheless rests on the pattern Genesis 25 displays: "those who are the children of the flesh, these are not the children of God; but the children of the promise are counted as the seed" (Romans 9:8). Ishbak is a clear example of a physical descendant who was not the line of promise; Genesis does not tell us whether he personally trusted God. Paul goes on to say that the promised Seed is ultimately Christ, and that those who belong to Christ are Abraham's heirs (Galatians 3:16, 29). In the same chapter he teaches that God's original word to Abraham included blessing for the nations (Galatians 3:8; compare Genesis 12:3). The sons sent east were outside the line of promise, but they were not outside the reach of the blessing that would come through it.

There is also a thematic resonance in the direction Ishbak was sent. In Genesis, going "east" often goes along with leaving the place of blessing. The cherubim guard the way to Eden from the east (Genesis 3:24). Cain settles "on the east of Eden" (Genesis 4:16). Lot journeys east when he separates from Abraham (Genesis 13:11). Genesis never calls Ishbak's departure a judgment, so the pattern should be read as a resonance in the story rather than as a verdict on him.

5. Spirit & Life

Ishbak was a son of Abraham, the man of faith, and still was not the heir of the promised line. Scripture does not tell us what he himself believed, but his place in the story makes Paul's point clear. Nearness to a godly family is a great gift, but it does not make anyone a child of God. Paul says that only "the children of the promise" are counted as the seed (Romans 9:8). The good news is that the promise is not limited by the accidents of birth. It reaches the nations through Christ, and it is received by faith (Galatians 3:7–9).

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 25:1–4 — Ishbak's birth to Abraham and Keturah, named among six brothers.

  • Genesis 25:5–6 — The inheritance given to Isaac, the gifts given to the other sons, and their sending away eastward.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:32 — The chronicler's record of Ishbak among the sons of Keturah, "Abraham's concubine."

  • Genesis 17:4–6 — God's promise that Abraham would be "a father of many nations," which the sons of Keturah help illustrate.

  • Romans 9:7–8 — Paul's teaching that the children of promise, not merely physical descendants, are counted as Abraham's seed.

  • Galatians 3:8, 16, 29 — The promised Seed is Christ, and the blessing extends to the nations through faith (referenced, not quoted).
