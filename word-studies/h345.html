AIAH - (ʼAyâh, Strong's H0345)

Summary

Aiah is the name of two different men in the Old Testament, and neither one ever speaks or acts on the page. The first is a Horite, a son of Zibeon, listed among the original peoples of Mount Seir in the genealogies of Genesis 36:24 and 1 Chronicles 1:40. The second is an Israelite known only as the father of Rizpah, the concubine of King Saul (2 Samuel 3:7; 21:8, 10, 11). The name is Hebrew and means "bird of prey." It is the same word used for a kite or falcon among the unclean birds of Leviticus and Deuteronomy, and in Job's line, "Nor has the falcon's eye seen it" (Job 28:7).

The second Aiah carries the real weight of the entry, though only through his daughter. "Rizpah the daughter of Aiah" appears in two major narratives about the decline of Saul's house. Ishbosheth's quarrel with Abner over her drove Abner to David (2 Samuel 3:7–10). Later, during a famine in David's reign that the LORD traced to Saul's violence against the Gibeonites, her two sons were among the seven of Saul's descendants who were executed. She then kept watch over their bodies through the long dry months, driving off the birds and the wild animals (2 Samuel 21:1–14). The narrator gives her father's name three times in four verses, and her vigil moved David to bury Saul's family with honor. Only after that burial does the text say God answered prayer for the land. It is a hard passage about the seriousness of broken oaths, and it also shows that God sees a grieving mother whom the powerful had overlooked.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name ʼAyâh belongs to two separate men who lived centuries and nations apart. The Strong's number H0345 identifies the name, not one person, and their lives should not be merged.

Aiah the Horite was a son of Zibeon, a chief among the descendants of Seir the Horite (Genesis 36:20–24; 1 Chronicles 1:38–40). The NKJV spells his name Ajah in both genealogies. He is named first, ahead of his brother Anah. The Horites lived in the hill country of Seir, southeast of the Dead Sea, before Esau's descendants took the land and absorbed them (Genesis 36:20–30; compare Deuteronomy 2:12, 22).

Aiah the father of Rizpah was an Israelite of the time of Saul and David. Scripture names him only as Rizpah's father. It does not give his tribe, his hometown, or whether he was still living when the events of 2 Samuel took place.

The name comes straight from the common Hebrew noun ʼayyâh (H0344), which names a bird of prey, usually rendered "kite" or "falcon." That bird appears among the unclean birds in Leviticus 11:14 and Deuteronomy 14:13, and in Job 28:7, where the NKJV reads, "That path no bird knows, Nor has the falcon's eye seen it." The meaning of the name is well grounded. What is less certain is how much the narrator meant readers to hear in it, and that question is taken up in Section 5. Neither man's name is used to describe his character, and the meaning of the name should not be read as the meaning of either life.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Aiah the Horite

Aiah appears only in the genealogy of Seir's chiefs (Genesis 36:24; repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:40). Once he is named, the text moves on to his brother Anah, "the Anah who found the water in the wilderness" while pasturing his father's donkeys (Genesis 36:24, paraphrased). Genesis keeps careful record of the peoples around Israel, including those Esau's line displaced. Aiah's place in that record shows that God's Word does not treat the nations next to Israel as faceless. The Chronicler later repeats the line in his great survey of humanity, which begins with Adam and leads to Israel.

Aiah, Father of Rizpah

The quarrel that broke Saul's house (2 Samuel 3:7–11). After Saul died, his son Ishbosheth reigned over the northern tribes, and the real power belonged to his general Abner. Ishbosheth accused Abner of going in to "Rizpah, the daughter of Aiah," his father's concubine. In the ancient Near East, taking a dead king's concubine could signal a claim to his throne (compare 2 Samuel 16:21–22; 1 Kings 2:22). The text never says whether the accusation was true. Abner was furious and swore to hand the kingdom over to David (3:8–10). Rizpah has no voice in the scene, and men argue over her as a political token. Yet that argument began the end of Saul's dynasty.

The famine and the Gibeonites (2 Samuel 21:1–9). During David's reign a famine lasted three years. When David sought the LORD, he learned that the cause was Saul's bloodguilt for killing the Gibeonites (21:1–2). Israel had sworn to spare them in Joshua's day (Joshua 9:15–20), and Saul had broken that oath in his zeal. When David asked how he could make it right (21:3), the Gibeonites demanded seven of Saul's descendants. David spared Mephibosheth son of Jonathan because of his own oath to Jonathan (21:7). He handed over "Armoni and Mephibosheth, the two sons of Rizpah the daughter of Aiah, whom she bore to Saul," together with five grandsons of Saul (21:8). Rizpah's son Mephibosheth is a different man from Jonathan's son. The seven were put to death "on the hill before the LORD... in the beginning of barley harvest" (21:9).

The vigil (2 Samuel 21:10–14). Then comes one of the most moving scenes in Samuel: "Now Rizpah the daughter of Aiah took sackcloth and spread it for herself on the rock, from the beginning of harvest until the late rains poured on them from heaven. And she did not allow the birds of the air to rest on them by day nor the beasts of the field by night" (21:10). That stretch ran from spring barley harvest until the rains came, possibly months. "And David was told what Rizpah the daughter of Aiah, the concubine of Saul, had done" (21:11). David responded by bringing the bones of Saul and Jonathan from Jabesh Gilead. He buried them with the bones of the executed men in the tomb of Kish, Saul's father. Only after this, the text says, God heeded the prayer for the land (21:12–14).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that the famine came because Saul violated Israel's sworn oath to the Gibeonites (21:1). The oath was several hundred years old, made under deceptive circumstances, and given to a foreign people. Still, God held Israel to it. A change of king did not erase guilt that had never been dealt with.

Scripture also places Rizpah, and therefore her father's name, in two major narratives about Saul's house. The quarrel over her drove Abner toward David and the uniting of the tribes (2 Samuel 3). The famine account comes later, when David is already king, and the text does not say where it falls in his reign (2 Samuel 21:1). Her sons' deaths there belong to the settling of Saul's bloodguilt, not to David's rise to the throne. The text does not say God designed these events through her. Even so, a woman whom kings and generals treated as a token stands at the center of both accounts.

It is also worth noticing the order of events. The executions do not end the account. The narrative continues to Rizpah's vigil, David's honorable burial of Saul's house, and only then the words that God heeded the prayer for the land (21:14). The text does not explain exactly why the healing waited for the burial. The sequence suggests that the matter was not finished until the dead were treated with dignity. Deuteronomy 21:22–23 had required that a hanged body not remain overnight, and that requirement may stand in the background here.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Aiah is not a type of Christ, and the men executed in 2 Samuel 21 are not presented as innocent substitutes. The LORD Himself named the cause of the famine: "It is because of Saul and his bloodthirsty house, because he killed the Gibeonites" (21:1). The demand for seven of Saul's descendants came from the Gibeonites (21:5–6), and David granted it. The chapter explains the famine and later records that God heeded the prayer for the land (21:14), but it does not say whether these seven shared in Saul's guilt, and the Law forbade putting children to death for their fathers' sins (Deuteronomy 24:16). This is a hard question, and it is better left standing than settled beyond what the text says. Still, the episode belongs to a larger biblical thread that the New Testament develops directly. Deuteronomy 21:23 declares that one who is hanged is accursed of God, and Paul applies that verse to Jesus: Christ "has redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13, cited). The seven bodies on the hill before the LORD show how heavy the burden of guilt is and how long it can linger over a land.

The contrast with the cross is stronger than the likeness. Saul's descendants did not choose their deaths. Christ laid down His life willingly and bore the curse that would otherwise fall on us. Rizpah watched over her sons' bodies for months. Jesus' body was taken down and buried the same day with care (John 19:31, 38–42). One more connection is thematic, not intended by the text: Rizpah's watch recalls the women who stayed near the cross and the tomb (Luke 23:49, 55). In both scenes, love keeps faithful watch over the dead when others have gone.

5. Spirit & Life

The Horite Aiah is remembered only by name, and Rizpah's father is remembered only because of his daughter. Scripture often records people this way. Their importance lies less in anything they did than in where they stand in God's larger story.

The meaning of the name invites a careful observation. Aiah means "bird of prey," and his daughter spent months on a rock beating back "the birds of the air" (21:10) from her sons. The Hebrew word for those birds is the ordinary word for birds, not ʼayyâh, so this is not a stated wordplay. Still, a reader who knows her father's name can hardly miss it. The daughter of "Falcon" fought off the birds so they would not consume her children.

What the text clearly does is slow down to honor her. Three times in four verses she is called "Rizpah the daughter of Aiah," a full name that belongs to her and not only to Saul. Kings, generals, and foreign avengers made decisions over her life. She had no power to change any of them, so she did the one faithful thing left to her and kept watching. Her endurance reached the king, and the king did right. God takes seriously the promises His people make, and He also does not overlook a grieving mother on a rock.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:20–24; 1 Chronicles 1:38–40 — Aiah the Horite, son of Zibeon, in the line of Seir's chiefs.

  • Leviticus 11:14; Deuteronomy 14:13; Job 28:7 — the common noun ʼayyâh, "kite" or "falcon," the source of the name's meaning.

  • 2 Samuel 3:7–11 — the quarrel over "Rizpah, the daughter of Aiah" that drove Abner to David.

  • Joshua 9:15–20; 2 Samuel 21:1–7 — the oath to the Gibeonites and the famine caused by Saul's breaking it.

  • 2 Samuel 21:8–11 — Rizpah's sons executed, her vigil, and the repeated naming of her father.

  • 2 Samuel 21:12–14 — the burial of Saul's house and God's answer to prayer for the land.

  • Deuteronomy 21:22–23; Galatians 3:13 — the curse of the one hanged, fulfilled in Christ.
