ISHMAEL - (Yishmâʻêʼl, Strong's H3458)

Summary

Ishmael is a Hebrew name meaning "God hears" or "God will hear." Six different men carry it in the Old Testament, and two of them stand out. The first and most important is Ishmael, son of Abraham and Hagar, the Egyptian servant of Sarah. He was born when Abram and Sarai tried to obtain God's promised heir by their own arrangement. God gave him his name before he was born, because "the LORD has heard your affliction" (Genesis 16:11). Ishmael was circumcised with Abraham and blessed with twelve princely sons and a great nation. Yet God kept His covenant line with Isaac. When Ishmael was sent away into the wilderness, God heard the boy's voice, saved his life, and stayed with him as he grew up. Paul later uses Ishmael and Isaac to show the difference between being born "according to the flesh" and being born "through promise" (Galatians 4:23; Romans 9:7–9).

The second major Ishmael, son of Nethaniah, lived about fourteen centuries later. He was a member of Judah's royal family after Babylon destroyed Jerusalem. He shared a meal with Gedaliah, the governor Babylon had appointed, and then murdered him. He went on to kill pilgrims who were on their way to worship, and he carried off the survivors as captives (2 Kings 25:25; Jeremiah 40–41). His crime frightened the remnant into fleeing to Egypt against the word of the LORD. The two men share nothing but a name. Together, though, their stories expose two ways of resisting God. One is trying to bring about His promise by human effort. The other is violent defiance of the order God had set under His discipline, whatever motive lay behind it. Over both stories stands the God whose mercy toward an outcast mother and her son is written into the very name Ishmael.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The name Ishmael (Hebrew יִשְׁמָעֵאל, Yishmâʻêʼl) means "God hears" or "God will hear." It joins a form of the common Hebrew verb for hearing with El, the general Hebrew word for God. The meaning is not a guess drawn from later tradition. Genesis explains the name itself. The Angel of the LORD tells Hagar, "You shall call his name Ishmael, Because the LORD has heard your affliction" (Genesis 16:11). The name uses El, while the angel's explanation uses the covenant name, the LORD. That detail makes clear that the God of Abraham is the One who did the hearing.

Genesis keeps returning to this idea of hearing. God tells Abraham that He has heard him concerning Ishmael (Genesis 17:20). Later, in the wilderness, Scripture says that God heard the voice of the lad (Genesis 21:17). In Genesis 21 the narrator never actually uses the name Ishmael. He calls him "the lad" and "the son of the bondwoman." The wordplay still runs through the scene. The son whose name means "God hears" is heard by God at the moment he is dying of thirst. This is a clear biblical wordplay, and it is the author's own emphasis rather than a later symbolic reading.

The name meaning does not describe the character of every man who bore it. Six separate individuals share this lemma, and they must not be merged:

1. Ishmael son of Abraham and Hagar, the patriarch of the Ishmaelites (Genesis 16–25; 1 Chronicles 1:28–31).

2. Ishmael son of Nethaniah, son of Elishama, of the royal family of Judah, who murdered Gedaliah after the fall of Jerusalem (2 Kings 25:23–25; Jeremiah 40–41).

3. Ishmael son of Azel, a Benjamite descendant of Saul through Jonathan (1 Chronicles 8:38; 9:44).

4. Ishmael, father of Zebadiah. His son was "the ruler of the house of Judah" for the king's affairs under Jehoshaphat (2 Chronicles 19:11).

5. Ishmael son of Jehohanan, a captain of hundreds who joined Jehoiada the priest to overthrow Athaliah and crown Joash (2 Chronicles 23:1).

6. Ishmael of the sons of Pashhur, a priest listed in Ezra's reforms among those who had married foreign wives (Ezra 10:22).

Ishmael son of Abraham lived in the patriarchal age. He was Abraham's firstborn and Isaac's older half-brother. His mother Hagar was Egyptian (Genesis 16:1), and he later took an Egyptian wife (Genesis 21:21). He settled in the wilderness of Paran and in the desert regions toward the east. Ishmael son of Nethaniah lived in the early sixth century BC, at the end of the kingdom of Judah. The gentilic term "Ishmaelites," Ishmael's descendants, is a related but separately indexed word. Its appearances, such as the traders who bought Joseph in Genesis 37, are not counted among the occurrences of this entry.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Ishmael Son of Abraham: Born of a Human Plan

God had promised Abram an heir from his own body (Genesis 15:4), but Sarai remained barren. Following a custom of their world, she gave her Egyptian maid Hagar to Abram as a wife. Hagar conceived, began to despise her mistress, was treated harshly in return, and fled toward Egypt (Genesis 16:1–6). The Angel of the LORD found her by a spring in the wilderness. He told her to return and promised that her descendants would be too many to count. He also named her unborn son: "You shall call his name Ishmael, Because the LORD has heard your affliction" (Genesis 16:11).

The angel then described the kind of man Ishmael would be: "He shall be a wild man; His hand shall be against every man, And every man's hand against him. And he shall dwell in the presence of all his brethren" (Genesis 16:12). The Hebrew picture behind "wild man" is a wild donkey of a man. Elsewhere Scripture describes the wild donkey as an animal that roams the open desert and scorns the noise of the city (Job 39:5–8). The prophecy describes a free, restless, unyoked life lived in tension with neighbors. It reads more as a forecast of his way of life than as a curse. Hagar answered by naming the LORD as the God who sees her, and the well was called Beer Lahai Roi (Genesis 16:13–14). Ishmael was born when Abram was eighty-six (Genesis 16:15–16).

Thirteen Years Later: Blessing and Boundary

When Ishmael was thirteen, God appeared to Abraham, changed his name, and established the covenant of circumcision. He also promised that Sarah herself would bear a son. Abraham's first response was a father's prayer: "Oh, that Ishmael might live before You!" (Genesis 17:18). God's answer held both a firm "no" and a generous "yes." Sarah would bear Isaac, and God's covenant would be established with him. As for Ishmael, God said He had heard Abraham. He promised to bless Ishmael, make him fruitful, multiply him greatly, and make him the father of twelve princes and a great nation (Genesis 17:19–21). That same day Abraham circumcised Ishmael along with every male in his household (Genesis 17:23–26). Ishmael received the sign and a real blessing. The promised line, however, ran through Isaac.

The Wilderness: God Hears the Lad

At the feast for Isaac's weaning, Sarah saw Hagar's son mocking. She demanded that Abraham cast out the bondwoman and her son, so that he would not be heir with Isaac (Genesis 21:8–10). Abraham was deeply distressed because of his son. God told him to listen to Sarah, because in Isaac his seed would be called. God also promised to make a nation of the bondwoman's son, because he was Abraham's seed (Genesis 21:11–13).

Hagar and the boy wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba until the water ran out. She placed him under a shrub and sat at a distance so she would not have to watch him die. Then God heard the voice of the lad. The angel of God called to Hagar, told her not to fear, and repeated the promise of a great nation. God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water (Genesis 21:14–19). Scripture then adds a sentence that deserves more attention than it usually gets: God was with the lad. He grew, lived in the wilderness, and became an archer. He settled in the wilderness of Paran, and his mother took a wife for him from Egypt (Genesis 21:20–21).

Later Years and Legacy

When Abraham died, "his sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him in the cave of Machpelah" (Genesis 25:9). The brothers who were separated at the weaning feast stand together at their father's grave. Genesis does not describe any reconciliation, but it shows enough to soften the picture of unending hostility between them. The list of Ishmael's twelve sons follows, "princes according to their nations": Nebajoth, Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam, Mishma, Dumah, Massa, Hadar (spelled Hadad in 1 Chronicles 1:30), Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah. This list fulfills God's word in Genesis 17:20 point for point (Genesis 25:12–16; 1 Chronicles 1:29–31). Ishmael lived 137 years and was gathered to his people (Genesis 25:17). His descendants settled from Havilah to Shur, east of Egypt (Genesis 25:18).

Ishmael's family appears twice more in Genesis. Esau noticed that his Canaanite wives displeased his father, so he married Mahalath, Ishmael's daughter, to please Isaac (Genesis 28:8–9). Genesis 36:3 lists a daughter of Ishmael named Basemath among Esau's wives. In this way Ishmael's line became bound up with Edom.

Ishmael Son of Nethaniah: The Murder at Mizpah

After Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Jerusalem, he appointed Gedaliah son of Ahikam to govern the poor of the land who remained, with his seat at Mizpah. Gedaliah came from a family that had protected Jeremiah (Jeremiah 26:24). Army captains who had escaped capture gathered to him, and Ishmael son of Nethaniah was among them (2 Kings 25:23; Jeremiah 40:7–8). Gedaliah gave them the same counsel the LORD had given through Jeremiah: serve the king of Babylon, and it will go well with you (Jeremiah 40:9; compare Jeremiah 27:12). For a short time, people came back, gathered summer fruit, and the land breathed again (Jeremiah 40:10–12).

Johanan son of Kareah warned Gedaliah that Baalis king of the Ammonites had sent Ishmael to kill him. He even offered to kill Ishmael secretly, so that the remnant would not perish. Gedaliah refused and accused Johanan of speaking falsely about Ishmael (Jeremiah 40:13–16). In the seventh month, Ishmael, "of the royal family," came with ten men and ate bread with Gedaliah at Mizpah. Then they rose up and struck him with the sword. They also killed the Jews who were with him and the Chaldean soldiers stationed there (2 Kings 25:25; Jeremiah 41:1–3).

On the second day after the murder, eighty men came from Shechem, Shiloh, and Samaria. Their beards were shaved and their clothes torn, and they carried offerings and incense to the house of the LORD. Ishmael went out to meet them weeping as he walked and invited them in to Gedaliah. Once they were inside the city, he slaughtered them and threw them into a pit. Ten were spared only because they promised him hidden stores of wheat, barley, oil, and honey (Jeremiah 41:4–8). The pit was the one King Asa had made long before, when he feared Baasha king of Israel. Ishmael filled it with the dead (Jeremiah 41:9). He then took the rest of the people captive, including the king's daughters, and set out for Ammon (Jeremiah 41:10).

Johanan and the captains pursued him and caught up with him by the great pool in Gibeon. The captives turned and went over to Johanan, but Ishmael escaped with eight men to the Ammonites (Jeremiah 41:11–15). The rescued remnant camped near Bethlehem, intending to go to Egypt, because they were afraid of the Chaldeans after Ishmael had killed Babylon's governor (Jeremiah 41:16–18). When they asked Jeremiah for a word from the LORD, he told them to stay in the land and promised God's protection. They went to Egypt anyway and took Jeremiah with them (Jeremiah 42–43). The murder shattered Gedaliah's administration and precipitated the flight of the remnant under Johanan.

Other Men Named Ishmael

The remaining four appear briefly. Ishmael son of Azel belongs to the line of Saul and Jonathan, a family Scripture keeps on record even after the kingdom passed to David (1 Chronicles 8:38; 9:44). Ishmael the father of Zebadiah is known through his son, whom Jehoshaphat appointed over "all the king's matters" as part of his reform of justice (2 Chronicles 19:11). Ishmael son of Jehohanan was one of the captains who made a sworn agreement with Jehoiada to restore the Davidic king Joash (2 Chronicles 23:1). Here is an Ishmael who helped protect the royal line rather than misuse it. Ishmael of the sons of Pashhur was a priest listed in Ezra 10:22 among those who had married foreign wives. Ezra 10:18–19 records that the first priests named, the sons of Jeshua, pledged to put away their wives and offered a ram for their guilt; the text does not record that pledge and offering separately for Ishmael's group.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states plainly that God chose to establish His covenant with Isaac and not with Ishmael (Genesis 17:19–21; 21:12). Ishmael was Abraham's son, he was circumcised, and he was dearly loved by his father. Even so, God's redemptive line did not run through him. The text is just as clear that God blessed Ishmael personally and generously. He gave him a name, heard his mother, heard his father's prayer, heard the boy's own cry, stayed with him, and gave him twelve princes. Both truths stand in the text side by side, and neither cancels the other. God's choice of Isaac was not indifference toward Ishmael. The God who draws the boundary of promise is also the God who goes looking for a runaway servant by a desert spring.

Ishmael's birth also shows what happens when people try to secure God's promise by their own means. Genesis does not describe Sarai's plan as wicked in the way it describes murder. It records the plan's results: contempt, harshness, flight, and a household divided. Abraham's answer to God's announcement of Isaac, "Oh, that Ishmael might live before You!" (Genesis 17:18), shows how deeply he had already settled for the son of his own plan. God's reply is that the promise will come His way, through a barren woman and in His time. That is a carefully drawn implication from the narrative, and Paul makes it explicit in the New Testament.

A thread in later prophecy is worth noting. Two of Ishmael's sons, Kedar and Nebajoth, give their names to Arabian peoples who reappear in Isaiah's visions of the nations coming to the LORD. Isaiah calls the villages that Kedar inhabits to lift up their voice and sing (Isaiah 42:11). He sees the flocks of Kedar and the rams of Nebaioth offered with acceptance on God's altar in the day of Zion's glory (Isaiah 60:7). These are separately indexed names, and Isaiah does not mention Ishmael himself. Still, it is fitting that the prophets picture the descendants of the son God heard in the wilderness among those who come to worship.

Ishmael son of Nethaniah matters in a different way. God had decreed Babylon's rule over Judah as His discipline, and Jeremiah had urged the people to submit and live (Jeremiah 27:12; 38:17). Gedaliah's government was a small mercy within that judgment: a remnant left in the land, harvests gathered, people returning. Scripture does not tell us Ishmael's motive. It records that he was of royal blood and acted with the backing of the king of Ammon (Jeremiah 40:14; 41:1). It is a reasonable inference that a son of David's house could not accept a governor who was not a Davidic king. Whatever drove him, the results are recorded plainly. He broke the trust of a shared meal, he killed worshipers, and he drove the remnant into disobedience and exile in Egypt. Gedaliah's community at Mizpah was shattered by one of its own, and the remnant under Johanan fled. Even so, Judeans remained in the land, for Jeremiah 52:30 records a later deportation.

Jewish tradition has long mourned Gedaliah's murder with a fast in the seventh month, the month of the murder (Jeremiah 41:1). Many interpreters connect this with the fast in the seventh month that Zechariah mentions (Zechariah 7:5), which Zechariah 8:19 calls "the fast of the seventh." That identification is traditional rather than stated in the text, but it fits. In Zechariah 8:19 the LORD promises that the fasts of mourning will become "joy and gladness" for Judah. The grief caused by this Ishmael was not God's final word to His people.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

The most important later use of Ishmael comes from Paul, and it is explicit. Writing to Gentile believers who were being pressed to come under the law, he asks, "Tell me, you who desire to be under the law, do you not hear the law?" (Galatians 4:21). He then recalls that "Abraham had two sons: the one by a bondwoman, the other by a freewoman. But he who was of the bondwoman was born according to the flesh, and he of the freewoman through promise" (Galatians 4:22–23). Paul calls these things "symbolic." Hagar corresponds to Mount Sinai and to "Jerusalem which now is," which "is in bondage with her children," while "the Jerusalem above is free, which is the mother of us all" (Galatians 4:24–26).

Several points in Paul's argument need to be read carefully. First, Ishmael was "born according to the flesh" because of how he was conceived. He came through a human arrangement, while Isaac came through God's promise to a barren woman. Paul is not passing judgment on Ishmael's personality or on his physical descendants. Second, Paul's application turns expectations upside down. Those who rely on the law for standing with God are the ones he places with Hagar, while believing Gentiles, "as Isaac was, are children of promise" (Galatians 4:28). Third, Paul draws a present-day application from the old conflict: "as he who was born according to the flesh then persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit, even so it is now" (Galatians 4:29). He then quotes Sarah's demand as the word of Scripture: "Cast out the bondwoman and her son, for the son of the bondwoman shall not be heir with the son of the freewoman" (Galatians 4:30). Life with God cannot be inherited by mixing grace with law-keeping. "So then, brethren, we are not children of the bondwoman but of the free" (Galatians 4:31).

Romans 9 uses the same history for a related purpose. "Nor are they all children because they are the seed of Abraham; but, 'In Isaac your seed shall be called.' That is, those who are the children of the flesh, these are not the children of God; but the children of the promise are counted as the seed" (Romans 9:7–8). Ishmael was truly Abraham's seed, yet the promise was not carried by natural descent. God's word decided it: "At this time I will come and Sarah shall have a son" (Romans 9:9). The gospel follows the same pattern. People become children of God through His promise fulfilled in Christ, not through ancestry or human effort (Galatians 3:29).

Some readers link the "wild man" prophecy of Genesis 16:12 to Paul's teaching that "the carnal mind is enmity against God; for it is not subject to the law of God, nor indeed can be" (Romans 8:7). Paul himself does not make that connection. His use of Ishmael rests on the manner of his birth, not on the prophecy about his temperament. The link is a thematic association at most and should not be pressed as Paul's meaning.

There is also a thematic resonance in the Nethaniah story, though Scripture does not draw it out. Gedaliah was struck down by a man who had just eaten bread with him. The Psalms mourn the friend "who ate my bread" and turned against the psalmist (Psalm 41:9), and Jesus applied that psalm to Judas at His last supper (John 13:18). It is also worth noticing the contrast between this son of David's house, who struck with the sword, perhaps, as many infer, to reclaim royal power, and the true Son of David, who refused the sword and received the kingdom from His Father by way of the cross (Matthew 26:52–53). These are contrasts rather than types, but they are drawn from real biblical patterns.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

The first Ishmael's story keeps reaching for one word: hearing. God heard Hagar's affliction when she ran from a hard home (Genesis 16:11). He heard Abraham when he prayed for a son God had not chosen as the heir (Genesis 17:20). He heard a dying boy in the desert after the boy's own mother had turned away because she could not bear to watch (Genesis 21:16–17). Two of them stood outside the covenant line: a foreign slave and an outcast child. Abraham remained central to God's promise, and when he pleaded lovingly for Ishmael, God would not substitute Ishmael for Isaac, yet He heard the father's prayer and blessed the son. God listened to each of them, answered, and acted. Whoever feels pushed to the edge of God's purposes should look carefully at Beer Lahai Roi and at the shrub in the wilderness of Beersheba. The God of Genesis does not wait for people to qualify before He listens. His hearing is an assurance of His compassion, not a promise to grant every desire, and these scenes also warn against trying to manufacture what God has promised.

That same story also shows that God's mercy does not erase His purposes. God did not give Abraham the answer he asked for in Genesis 17:18. He gave a better one, which included blessing for Ishmael and the promise through Isaac. Many believers have prayed something like "Oh, that Ishmael might live before You," asking God to bless the thing they built instead of the thing He promised. God may well bless what we built, and He is kind. He will still bring His promise His own way. Paul puts the matter plainly in Galatians: the life of God's children rests on God's promise received by faith, not on our efforts to secure it. Galatians 4 calls us to stop trying to produce by the flesh what only the Spirit can give, and to rejoice that "we, brethren, as Isaac was, are children of promise."

The second Ishmael gives a sober warning. He was not an outsider attacking God's people from the outside. He belonged to David's royal line, and he wept as he walked toward the pilgrims he would murder. If, as many infer, his zeal was for David's throne, then zeal for a cause, even one tied to God's promises, became deadly when it set itself against what God had actually said. Ishmael's violence shattered the small community Babylon had left under Gedaliah. The remnant, frightened by what he had done, then refused Jeremiah's word and fled to Egypt. Fear produced by one man's sin led to a whole community's disobedience. Scripture urges God's people to submit to His dealings, even His discipline, rather than force a "correction" of their own. It also warns them not to let the sins of others drive them away from His word.

Finally, Genesis 25:9 gives a quiet scene worth remembering: "his sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him." The rift in Abraham's household was real and painful, yet the brothers stood together at their father's grave. Scripture does not tell us more than that. It is enough to show that the God who drew the line between promise and flesh did not shut the door on human kindness across that line.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 16:11–12 – The Angel of the LORD names Ishmael, explains the name ("the LORD has heard your affliction"), and foretells his free, contentious life.

  • Genesis 16:13–16 – Hagar names the God who sees her. Ishmael is born when Abram is eighty-six.

  • Genesis 17:18–21 – Abraham prays for Ishmael. God hears, blesses him with twelve princes, and establishes His covenant with Isaac.

  • Genesis 17:23–26 – Ishmael, at thirteen, is circumcised with Abraham.

  • Genesis 21:9–21 – The expulsion. God hears the lad, provides water, and is with him as he grows (the chapter tells his story without using his name).

  • Genesis 25:9, 12–18 – Isaac and Ishmael bury Abraham together. Ishmael's twelve sons, his lifespan, and his territory.

  • Genesis 28:9; 36:3 – Ishmael's daughters marry Esau, linking his line with Edom.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:28–31 – The Chronicler's record of Ishmael's sons.

  • 1 Chronicles 8:38; 9:44; 2 Chronicles 19:11; 23:1; Ezra 10:22 – The other four men named Ishmael.

  • 2 Kings 25:23–26; Jeremiah 40:7–41:18 – Ishmael son of Nethaniah murders Gedaliah, slaughters pilgrims, takes captives, and escapes to Ammon.

  • Jeremiah 42–43 – The remnant's flight to Egypt, which follows from Ishmael's crime.

  • Galatians 4:21–31 – Paul's explicit symbolic use of the bondwoman's son and the freewoman's son.

  • Romans 9:7–9 – Ishmael's history shows that the children of promise, not of the flesh, are counted as Abraham's seed.

  • Isaiah 42:11; 60:7 – Kedar and Nebaioth, Ishmael's descendants (separately indexed names), pictured among the nations worshiping the LORD.

Jeremiah 41:1 Now it came to pass in the seventh month that Ishmael the son of Nethaniah, the son of Elishama, of the royal family and of the officers of the king, came with ten men to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, at Mizpah. And there they ate bread together in Mizpah. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:2 Then Ishmael the son of Nethaniah, and the ten men who were with him, arose and struck Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, the son of Shaphan, with the sword, and killed him whom the king of Babylon had made governor over the land. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:3 Ishmael also struck down all the Jews who were with him, that is, with Gedaliah at Mizpah, and the Chaldeans who were found there, the men of war. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:6 Now Ishmael the son of Nethaniah went out from Mizpah to meet them, weeping as he went along; and it happened as he met them that he said to them, “Come to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam!” (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:7 So it was, when they came into the midst of the city, that Ishmael the son of Nethaniah killed them and cast them into the midst of a pit, he and the men who were with him. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:8 But ten men were found among them who said to Ishmael, “Do not kill us, for we have treasures of wheat, barley, oil, and honey in the field.” So he desisted and did not kill them among their brethren. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:9 Now the pit into which Ishmael had cast all the dead bodies of the men whom he had slain, because of Gedaliah, was the same one Asa the king had made for fear of Baasha king of Israel. Ishmael the son of Nethaniah filled it with the slain. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:10 Then Ishmael carried away captive all the rest of the people who were in Mizpah, the king’s daughters and all the people who remained in Mizpah, whom Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard had committed to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam. And Ishmael the son of Nethaniah carried them away captive and departed to go over to the Ammonites. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:11 But when Johanan the son of Kareah and all the captains of the forces that were with him heard of all the evil that Ishmael the son of Nethaniah had done, (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:12 they took all the men and went to fight with Ishmael the son of Nethaniah; and they found him by the great pool that is in Gibeon. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:13 So it was, when all the people who were with Ishmael saw Johanan the son of Kareah, and all the captains of the forces who were with him, that they were glad. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:14 Then all the people whom Ishmael had carried away captive from Mizpah turned around and came back, and went to Johanan the son of Kareah. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:15 But Ishmael the son of Nethaniah escaped from Johanan with eight men and went to the Ammonites. (NKJV)

Jeremiah 41:16 Then Johanan the son of Kareah, and all the captains of the forces that were with him, took from Mizpah all the rest of the people whom he had recovered from Ishmael the son of Nethaniah after he had murdered Gedaliah the son of Ahikam—the mighty men of war and the women and the children and the eunuchs, whom he had brought back from Gibeon. (NKJV)
