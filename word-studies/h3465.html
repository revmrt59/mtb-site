OLD - (yâshân, Strong's H3465)

Summary

The Hebrew word yâshân means "old" in the sense of something that has lasted from an earlier time: grain kept from last year's harvest, fruit stored up for later, a gate or a pool that has stood in Jerusalem for generations. Scripture never uses it for elderly people or for the ancient ages of eternity. It describes things that have survived, been stored, or been inherited.

This small word appears in only a handful of verses, yet those verses reveal a great deal. In Leviticus, God promises Israel that last year's harvest will still feed them while they rest from farming, and that obedience will bring such abundance that they must "clear out the old because of the new." In the Song of Solomon, the bride saves fruits "new and old" for her beloved. In Isaiah, Jerusalem's leaders trust the water of "the old pool" and ignore the God who made it. In Nehemiah, the returning exiles rebuild the Old Gate and sing as they pass over it. Together these passages show that God's past provision can sustain His people, that love grows richer with time, that inherited resources become a snare when they replace trust in God, and that God restores what His people's sin has broken.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Yâshân is an ordinary Hebrew adjective with a concrete, material focus. It appears in three kinds of settings: farming and food (Leviticus, the Song of Solomon), city walls and gates (Nehemiah), and the water system of a city under threat (Isaiah). It usually describes things a person can store, repair, or rely on.

Other Hebrew words carry different kinds of "old." Zaqen describes aged people and elders. Other terms speak of ancient days or everlasting time. Many New Testament ideas, such as the "old man" of Romans 6:6 or the "Old Testament" of 2 Corinthians 3:14, use Greek vocabulary and should not be read back into this Hebrew word.

In Nehemiah, some translations take the gate's name as a place name, "the Gate of Jeshanah," possibly meaning the gate facing a town of that name. The NKJV reads it as "the Old Gate," and this study follows that reading.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Yâshân means old in the sense of belonging to an earlier time or season and still present now. Two senses can be distinguished:

1. Stored from a previous harvest: grain or fruit kept from an earlier year and still usable, as in "eat old produce until the ninth year" (Leviticus 25:22).

2. Long-standing or existing from earlier days: a structure that has stood for a long time and is known by its age, such as "the Old Gate" (Nehemiah 3:6) or "the old pool" (Isaiah 22:11).

In neither sense does the word mean worn out or worthless. Whatever goodness or danger belongs to "the old" comes from the passage around it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word works differently depending on what it describes. When yâshân describes food, the setting is God's blessing, and it stands beside "new" (Leviticus 26:10; Song of Solomon 7:13). Old and new exist together, and the old is still good. When it describes the city's structures, it names something inherited from earlier generations. Whether that inheritance is a blessing or a snare depends on how God's people treat it. Nehemiah's people repair the Old Gate under God's hand. Isaiah's generation trusts the old pool in place of God.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God whose provision reaches beyond the present moment. He does not feed His people only from day to day. He can fill their storehouses ahead of time so thoroughly that what He gave last season carries them through the next one.

The same passages also expose how easily people can turn what God has given into a substitute for God Himself. A storehouse, a gate, or a reservoir can become a testimony to God's faithfulness or an excuse for ignoring Him. Finally, Nehemiah shows that God does not abandon what has been ruined. He brings old foundations back into use and sets them apart for His worship again.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 25:22; 26:10 — Eating the old while resting. In the Sabbath year Israel was forbidden to sow or reap (Leviticus 25:4). The people's natural question was, "What shall we eat?" (25:20). God's answer was to command His blessing in the sixth year so that it would yield enough for three years (25:21). Then He told them, "you shall sow in the eighth year, and eat old produce until the ninth year; until its produce comes in, you shall eat of the old harvest" (25:22). The old grain proved that Israel lived by God's blessing and not by unbroken labor. Rest required faith, and the grain left in the storehouse showed that faith was not misplaced.

Leviticus 26:10 extends this promise to obedience in general: "You shall eat the old harvest, and clear out the old because of the new." The old grain is still good. The barns simply cannot hold everything, and the new harvest arrives before the old one runs out. Israel's capacity is the limit. God's giving is not.

Song of Solomon 7:13 — Love laid up. The bride tells her beloved that "at our gates are pleasant fruits, All manner, new and old, Which I have laid up for you, my beloved." She offers fresh fruit and fruit that has been kept, and both have been saved for him. Her love has a history. She has set things aside with him in mind, and time has made her gift richer.

Isaiah 22:11 — The old pool and its Maker. Facing a siege, Jerusalem's leaders counted houses, strengthened the wall, and gathered water (22:8–10). "You also made a reservoir between the two walls For the water of the old pool. But you did not look to its Maker, Nor did you have respect for Him who fashioned it long ago." The pool was old, but its Maker was older. God had planned it "long ago." The leaders took care of the inheritance and neglected the One who gave it. Isaiah calls this the people's deepest failure in the crisis, and the following verses describe them feasting while God called them to weep and repent (22:12–14).

Nehemiah 3:6; 12:39 — The Old Gate rebuilt and dedicated. After the exile, "Jehoiada the son of Paseah and Meshullam the son of Besodeiah repaired the Old Gate; they laid its beams and hung its doors, with its bolts and bars." Later, at the dedication of the wall, a thanksgiving choir passed "above the Old Gate" on its way to the temple (12:39). The walls had fallen in judgment for the kind of self-reliance Isaiah condemned. Now the same old gateway was restored and filled with songs of thanksgiving. The exiles were not building a new city. They were reclaiming the one God had given their fathers.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The old and new harvests of Leviticus continue a pattern that began with the manna. God gave a double portion before the Sabbath and kept it from spoiling (Exodus 16:22–24). Jesus speaks in the same spirit when He tells His disciples not to worry about food, because their Father knows what they need (Matthew 6:31–33). His own rest from His finished work invites His people to stop relying on endless striving.

Jesus also describes a scribe instructed concerning the kingdom as a householder who brings out of his treasure "things new and old" (Matthew 13:52, paraphrased). That saying uses Greek, not yâshân, but it fits the Song's picture well. The gospel does not discard God's earlier dealings with Israel. It brings them to their fullness in Christ.

Isaiah's rebuke points ahead as well. The Maker whom Jerusalem ignored is the Word through whom all things were made (John 1:3). When He came, many people treasured their inheritance of Scripture and tradition yet would not come to Him for life (John 5:39–40). The Old Gate's restoration anticipates the larger promise that God's people will rebuild old ruins and raise up the foundations of many generations (Isaiah 58:12). That promise reaches its height in the city Christ prepares for His people.

7. Spirit & Life

The old grain in Israel's barns challenges anyone who believes everything depends on constant effort. God told His people to stop working for a whole year and then fed them from what He had already given. A believer can look at past answers to prayer, past provision, and past faithfulness as grain still in the storehouse. These are not memories to admire from a distance. They are God's evidence that obedient rest is safe.

The bride's "new and old" shows what a long walk with Christ can offer Him. Fresh praise matters. So does devotion that has been kept through many years and many trials and is still being offered to Him.

Isaiah's old pool gives a sober warning. Jeremiah describes the same failure as forsaking "the fountain of living waters" for "broken cisterns that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). A church's history, a person's past spiritual experiences, or long-practiced habits of self-protection can become stored water that people trust while they stop looking to God. The question in any crisis is whether we are looking to the Maker.

Nehemiah's gate offers hope to anyone whose foundations have fallen. God does repair old gates. Jeremiah calls people to "ask for the old paths, where the good way is" (Jeremiah 6:16). Recovering what God gave long ago, and dedicating it to Him with thanksgiving, is often how restoration begins. And when God's new blessing outgrows our capacity, we can release the old with gratitude. It was good, and the One who gave it is still giving.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The significant occurrences of yâshân have been discussed above: Leviticus 25:22 and 26:10, each with two uses of the word; Nehemiah 3:6 and 12:39; Song of Solomon 7:13; and Isaiah 22:11.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2319 (chadash), "new" — a canonical pairing. This word stands beside yâshân in Leviticus 26:10 and Song of Solomon 7:13, where old and new together picture overflowing provision and love.

  • H2205 (zaqen), "old, elder" — a distinct word used for aged people. It is a contrast that clarifies meaning, not a lexical relative of yâshân.
