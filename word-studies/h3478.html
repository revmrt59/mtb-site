ISRAEL - (Yisrâʼêl, Strong's H3478)

Summary

Israel is the name God gave to Jacob, son of Isaac and grandson of Abraham. Through his twelve sons it became the name of an entire people. Across the Old Testament, "Israel" can mean the patriarch himself, the twelve-tribe nation descended from him, the land God gave them, and, after Solomon's kingdom split, the ten northern tribes as distinct from Judah. The prophets can also address Judah and Jerusalem as Israel, and after the exile the name belongs to the returned community. Genesis explains the name when God gives it at Peniel: "for you have struggled with God and with men, and have prevailed" (Genesis 32:28). The name is generally understood to mean "he strives with God." Because of how the name is built grammatically, it may also be read "God strives" or "God rules." Either way, the name ties Jacob and his descendants to a night when a schemer was overpowered, wounded, and blessed by God.

The story of Israel runs from that night by the Jabbok through Egypt, the Exodus, Sinai, the wilderness, the land, the kingdom, its division, exile, and return. God redeemed Israel, called the nation His firstborn son, gave it His law, and came to dwell among the people in the tabernacle. He also called Israel to be "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:6). Israel repeatedly failed that calling, yet God refused to let His people go. The prophets promised a restored and reunited Israel under a Davidic shepherd. The New Testament presents Jesus as the faithful Son who lives out Israel's story without failing. Through Him, people from every nation are brought into the blessing God promised Abraham. Paul still insists that God has not finished with the people who bear this name (Romans 11:26).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Uses of the name Israel. The Hebrew name Yisrâʼêl (Strong's H3478) appears more than two thousand times in the Old Testament. The name has several related uses, and context makes clear who is meant:

  • The patriarch Jacob. Jacob is the son of Isaac and Rebekah and the twin brother of Esau. God renamed him Israel at Peniel (Genesis 32:28) and confirmed the name at Bethel (Genesis 35:10). The name Jacob is separately indexed (H3290).

  • The people descended from him. They are usually called "the children of Israel," literally "sons of Israel." The phrase first appears at the Jabbok (Genesis 32:32). It describes the twelve tribes as one family, which grew into a nation in Egypt (Exodus 1:1, 7), and it continues through the whole Old Testament.

  • The land. "The land of Israel" refers to the territory God gave the tribes in Canaan.

  • The Northern Kingdom. After the division under Rehoboam (1 Kings 12), "Israel" often names the ten northern tribes, whose capital eventually became Samaria. Judah (H3063) is the separately indexed southern kingdom. The prophets sometimes call the northern kingdom "Ephraim" (H669), after its leading tribe. A northern Israel distinct from Judah already appears earlier, when Ishbosheth reigned over Israel while "only the house of Judah followed David" (2 Samuel 2:9–11).

  • Later canonical use. In the New Testament the Greek Israēl (G2474) is a separately indexed name. It continues to refer to the Jewish people, and in a few passages it is developed theologically (Romans 9:6; Galatians 6:16).

These uses of the name are closely connected, but they should be distinguished. When a prophet speaks of "Israel" after 930 BC, the reader must ask whether he means all the tribes, the northern kingdom, or Judah and Jerusalem addressed as Israel (as in Isaiah 1:1–4). After the exile, the name also belongs to the returned community.

Name meaning. Genesis supplies the meaning of the name. When the mysterious Man who wrestled with Jacob asked his name, Jacob had to say "Jacob." That name was tied to grasping a heel and to deception (Genesis 25:26; 27:36). Then came the new name: "Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel; for you have struggled with God and with men, and have prevailed" (Genesis 32:28).

The name joins a Hebrew verb meaning to strive, persist, or contend with El, "God." Genesis applies it to Jacob as the one who "struggled with God." That is why the name is commonly rendered "he strives with God." In Hebrew names built this way, El usually acts as the subject, as in names meaning "God hears" or "God gives." So the form can also be read "God strives" or "God rules."

The biblical explanation is certain, because Genesis 32:28 gives it directly. The precise grammar of the name is less settled. Both readings fit the scene, since in that one night Jacob strove with God and God strove with Jacob. The phrase "and with men" also looks back over Jacob's earlier conflicts with Esau and with Laban. Hosea later returns to the same night and its wordplay. He says that Jacob struggled with God, that he wrestled with the Angel and prevailed, and that he wept and sought His favor (Hosea 12:3–4).

One detail separates this renaming from Abram's. Once Abram became Abraham, the rest of the Genesis narrative never calls him Abram again (later retellings still recall the earlier name, 1 Chronicles 1:27; Nehemiah 9:7). Jacob, by contrast, keeps appearing under both names for the rest of Genesis. God Himself speaks "to Israel in the visions of the night" and calls him "Jacob, Jacob" (Genesis 46:2). The old man and the new name live side by side in the narrative, much as they did in the nation that inherited both.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Patriarch: From Jacob to Israel (Genesis 32–50)

Jacob was returning home after twenty years with Laban and was terrified of meeting Esau. He sent his family across the Jabbok, "Then Jacob was left alone; and a Man wrestled with him until the breaking of day" (Genesis 32:24). The fight was not a fair one. "When He saw that He did not prevail against him, He touched the socket of his hip; and the socket of Jacob's hip was out of joint" (32:25). With a single touch, the Man showed who really held the power. Genesis does not say how the struggle ended physically, but it shows Jacob holding on: "I will not let You go unless You bless me!" (32:26).

The blessing came through a confession and a new name. Jacob's request to learn the Man's name went unanswered, but "He blessed him there" (32:29). Jacob named the place Peniel, "For I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved" (32:30). He walked into the sunrise limping (32:31). Israel's descendants remembered that limp at their own meals: "to this day the children of Israel do not eat the muscle that shrank" (32:32). This is the first time Scripture uses the phrase "children of Israel." It appears in connection with a wound.

The rest of Genesis follows Israel the father. His family went through grief and sin: the violation of Dinah, called "a disgraceful thing in Israel" (34:7), and Reuben's offense (35:22). Israel's favoritism toward Joseph (37:3) set off the long story that brought the family to Egypt. When Israel heard that Joseph was alive, he said he would go and see him before he died (45:28). God spoke to him at Beersheba and promised to make him a great nation in Egypt (46:1–3). Genesis counts the household as seventy persons, including Joseph and his sons already in Egypt (46:8–27).

Before he died, Israel adopted Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh as his own and crossed his hands to put the younger first (48:13–20). He then blessed his twelve sons, "the twelve tribes of Israel" (49:28). One of those blessings names God as "the Mighty God of Jacob" and "the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel" (49:24). Joseph also made the sons of Israel swear to carry his bones up from Egypt (50:25). Moses kept that oath generations later (Exodus 13:19), and Hebrews counts it as an act of faith (Hebrews 11:22).

The People Redeemed: Egypt and the Exodus (Exodus 1–15)

Exodus opens by listing "the names of the children of Israel who came to Egypt" (Exodus 1:1). It then reports that they "were fruitful and increased abundantly" (1:7), until Pharaoh saw them as a threat and enslaved them (1:9–13). Under that oppression, Israel cried out, and "God looked upon the children of Israel, and God acknowledged them" (2:25).

At the burning bush, God revealed His name to Moses for the sake of these people (3:13–15). He also gave Moses a startling message for Pharaoh: Israel is God's son, His firstborn, and Pharaoh must let God's son go (4:22–23). That father-and-son language becomes central to the rest of the Bible. Through the plagues God distinguished Israel from Egypt (9:4, 26; 11:7). Through the Passover lamb He spared Israel's firstborn (12:3–13). At the sea He brought "the children of Israel" through on dry ground (14:22, 29–31). Moses and Israel then sang the first great song of redemption in Scripture (15:1).

The People at Sinai: Calling and Dwelling (Exodus 19–40, Leviticus)

At Sinai God explained why He had done all this. "You have seen what I did to the Egyptians, and how I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to Myself" (Exodus 19:4). The goal of the Exodus was to bring Israel to God Himself, and freedom from Egypt was the means to that end. God then gave Israel its calling: "If you will indeed obey My voice and keep My covenant, then you shall be a special treasure to Me above all people; for all the earth is Mine. And you shall be to Me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (19:5–6). Israel's elders even "saw the God of Israel" and ate in His presence (24:9–11).

The rest of Exodus centers on the tabernacle, the place where God would dwell "among the children of Israel" and be their God (29:45; compare 25:8). The high priest wore two onyx stones on his shoulders engraved with the names of the sons of Israel, and twelve stones on his breastplate. He carried those names "before the LORD" whenever he entered the holy place (28:9–12, 21, 29).

Israel's story is never a clean one. At the foot of the mountain the people bowed to a golden calf and declared, "This is your god, O Israel" (32:4, 8). Moses interceded by appealing to God's oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Israel (32:13). God renewed His word (34:27), and the tabernacle was finished. The cloud of His glory then guided Israel's journeys (40:36–38).

Leviticus addresses one main problem: how a holy God can live among a sinful people. God set this standard for Israel: "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (Leviticus 19:2; compare 11:44). On the Day of Atonement the high priest made atonement "for the children of Israel, for all their sins, once a year" (16:34).

The People in the Wilderness (Numbers)

Numbers counts Israel by tribes and arranges the camp with the tabernacle at its center (Numbers 1–2). God set the Levites apart in place of Israel's firstborn (3:12–13), and the priests placed His name on the people through the Aaronic blessing (6:23–27). The wilderness also exposed Israel's unbelief. The spies' report led the whole congregation to wish they had died in Egypt (14:1–4), and that generation was barred from the land (14:26–35). Korah's rebellion brought judgment and a plague on the whole community (16:1–50).

God answered that rebellion with a quiet sign. He told Moses to collect a rod from each tribe, and Aaron's rod budded, blossomed, and bore almonds overnight. "Then Moses brought out all the rods from before the LORD to all the children of Israel; and they looked, and each man took his rod" (17:9). Every tribe saw that life and leadership come from God's choice.

Before Israel entered the land, Moses told them why they had been chosen. The reason was not their size, but that the LORD loved them and kept His oath to their fathers (Deuteronomy 7:7–8). Nor was it their righteousness, for Moses called them "a stiff-necked people" (Deuteronomy 9:4–6).

The People in the Land (Joshua through 1 Kings 11)

Under Joshua, Israel crossed the Jordan and received its inheritance by tribes (Joshua 3–4; 13–21). Judges then records a repeated cycle. Israel did evil, was oppressed, cried out, and was rescued, and the book's closing refrain says there was no king in Israel (Judges 2:11–19; 21:25). Israel asked for a king like the nations (1 Samuel 8:5). Saul failed, and God chose David, whom Scripture calls the sweet psalmist of Israel (2 Samuel 23:1). God promised David a house and a throne that would last forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16). Solomon built the temple, and the glory filled it as it had once filled the tabernacle (1 Kings 8:10–11). In his old age, however, Solomon turned to other gods, and God announced that the kingdom would be torn (1 Kings 11:9–13).

The Divided Kingdom: "To Your Tents, O Israel!" (1 Kings 12 – 2 Kings 17)

When Rehoboam answered the people harshly, the northern tribes broke away: "What share have we in David? We have no inheritance in the son of Jesse. To your tents, O Israel! Now, see to your own house, O David!" (1 Kings 12:16). From that point the narrator draws a line: "But Rehoboam reigned over the children of Israel who dwelt in the cities of Judah" (12:17). The name that once united twelve tribes around God's dwelling now belonged, in the political sense, to a kingdom that had broken away from David's house.

That kingdom began with idolatry. Jeroboam set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan and told the people these were the gods who brought them up from Egypt (1 Kings 12:28–29), repeating the words of Sinai's great failure. Through Elijah and Elisha God contended with Israel over Baal (1 Kings 18). Through Amos and Hosea He warned the nation. In 722 BC Assyria took Samaria. Second Kings 17 explains why: Israel had sinned against the LORD who brought them out of Egypt, and had rejected His statutes and warnings (2 Kings 17:7–23).

Even after the division, "Israel" never became a purely northern word. The prophets still speak of all the tribes as Israel. When Hezekiah invited the remnant of the North to the Passover in Jerusalem, Chronicles describes a gathering of "all Israel" (2 Chronicles 30:1–6).

Prophetic Hope and the Return

The prophets speak of judgment and of a love that refuses to let go. Hosea recalls that God loved Israel as a child and called His son out of Egypt (Hosea 11:1). He then records God's anguish over His rebellious people: how can He give up Ephraim and hand over Israel? (11:8). Isaiah repeatedly pairs the names, often as poetic equivalents. The LORD created Jacob and formed Israel, and He tells His people not to fear because He has redeemed them (Isaiah 43:1). In the Servant Songs, God addresses His Servant as "Israel," the one in whom He will be glorified, and gives that Servant a mission to restore the tribes and be a light to the Gentiles (Isaiah 49:3–6). Jeremiah promises a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, with the law written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31–34). Ezekiel sees dry bones brought to life as "the whole house of Israel," and two sticks, Judah and Joseph, joined in one hand under one king, God's servant David (Ezekiel 37:11, 15–24).

After the exile, the returned community still thought of itself as all Israel. At the dedication of the rebuilt temple they offered twelve male goats, "according to the number of the tribes of Israel" (Ezra 6:17). That verse is in Aramaic, and its name is the separately indexed Aramaic form H3479.

Israel in the New Testament (G2474, separately indexed)

The New Testament opens with faithful Israelites waiting for "the Consolation of Israel" (Luke 2:25). Simeon greets the infant Jesus as the glory of God's people Israel and a light to the Gentiles (Luke 2:32). Jesus calls twelve apostles and promises they will sit on twelve thrones judging the twelve tribes (Matthew 19:28). Paul grieves over his kinsmen who have rejected the Messiah (Romans 9:1–5), and he argues that God's word has not failed (Romans 9:6; 11:1–2, 26). Revelation describes the New Jerusalem with the names of the twelve tribes of the children of Israel written on its gates (Revelation 21:12).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Israel is the people God chose to bring the blessing of Abraham to the world. Scripture states this plainly. God promised that all the families of the earth would be blessed in Abraham (Genesis 12:3), and He called Israel to be a kingdom of priests "for all the earth is Mine" (Exodus 19:5–6). Israel's election was a calling to serve the nations as well as a privilege. Priests stand between God and others. Israel was meant to show the world what life looks like when God lives among a people.

Israel's election rests on God's love, not Israel's merit. Deuteronomy 7:7–8 grounds it in God's love and oath, Deuteronomy 9:4–6 denies that it rests on Israel's righteousness, and the story shows it on nearly every page. The calf at Sinai, the refusal at Kadesh, the rebellion of Korah, the cycle of Judges, the calves of Jeroboam, and the fall of Samaria all show a people who did not deserve their calling. The continued existence of Israel shows that God keeps His word.

God's presence is the heart of Israel's identity. The tabernacle, the priesthood, the sacrifices, and the holiness laws all serve one purpose: that God might dwell "among the children of Israel" (Exodus 29:45). Moses later said that God's presence going with them was what made Israel different from every other people (Exodus 33:15–16). When the North turned to calves and Baal, it kept the name Israel while abandoning what the name was meant to serve.

The name itself fits the nation's history, though Scripture does not state this directly. Genesis explains the name only in relation to Jacob's night at the Jabbok. Even so, the history that follows reads like a long continuation of that struggle. Israel contended with God at Massah (Exodus 17:7), at Kadesh, and through centuries of resisting the prophets. God contended with Israel by sending prophets, discipline, and pleas to return. Hosea 12 itself returns to Jacob's wrestling while confronting the nation of his own day, which suggests the prophet saw the nation's story in its father's. To say that "Israel" defines the nation's destiny would go beyond the text. Reading Israel's history alongside Jacob's night is a canonical observation that Hosea himself invites.

The division of the kingdom changed what the name means in much of Kings. From 1 Kings 12 onward, readers must notice whether "Israel" refers to the twelve tribes, to the northern kingdom, or to Judah and Jerusalem addressed as Israel. The prophets' hope that Israel and Judah would be one again (Ezekiel 37) is a hope that the name would recover its full meaning.

"Israel" is not simply a matter of descent. Paul writes, "For they are not all Israel who are of Israel" (Romans 9:6). Within the physical nation, God had always kept a believing remnant (Romans 11:1–5; compare 1 Kings 19:18). This is Paul's own interpretation of Israel's history, not a later inference. Paul does not conclude from it that God has given up on ethnic Israel. He concludes the opposite: "And so all Israel will be saved, as it is written: 'The Deliverer will come out of Zion, And He will turn away ungodliness from Jacob'" (Romans 11:26). Faithful interpreters differ on exactly what "all Israel" means here. Paul's point is clear, though: God's gifts and calling are irrevocable (Romans 11:29).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus as God's faithful Son, the Israel who does not fail. This connection rests on explicit New Testament interpretation. God called Israel His firstborn son (Exodus 4:22), and Hosea remembered that God called His son out of Egypt (Hosea 11:1). Matthew applies Hosea's words to the child Jesus returning from Egypt (Matthew 2:15). Matthew then traces Israel's path in Jesus' life. Jesus passes through the waters at His baptism (Matthew 3:13–17) and goes into the wilderness to be tested for forty days, just as Israel was tested for forty years. In His temptations He answers Satan three times from Deuteronomy, from the chapters where Moses reviews Israel's wilderness failures (Matthew 4:1–11; Deuteronomy 8:3; 6:16; 6:13). Where Israel complained about bread, tested God, and bowed to an idol, Jesus trusted, obeyed, and worshiped God alone. Isaiah had already addressed the Servant as "Israel" while giving that Servant a mission to restore Israel itself (Isaiah 49:3–6). The Servant who embodies Israel also rescues Israel, and the Gospels show that Servant to be Christ.

A new exodus. At the Transfiguration, Moses and Elijah spoke with Jesus "of His decease which He was about to accomplish at Jerusalem" (Luke 9:31). The Greek word behind "decease" is the word from which "exodus" comes. The deliverance God worked for Israel in Egypt anticipates the greater deliverance Christ accomplished by His death, and Paul calls Christ "our Passover" (1 Corinthians 5:7).

An Israelite with no deceit. This connection is strongly suggested by the Gospel text. When Jesus met Nathanael, He called him a true Israelite in whom there is no deceit (John 1:47). A few verses later He promised that Nathanael would see angels ascending and descending on the Son of Man (John 1:51), a clear echo of Jacob's ladder at Bethel (Genesis 28:12). The man once named for deception became the father of Israel. Jesus now looks for Israelites without deceit and presents Himself as the true meeting place between heaven and earth.

Names carried before God. The connection here is thematic resonance rather than an explicit New Testament identification. Israel's high priest bore the names of the twelve tribes on his shoulders and over his heart whenever he entered the holy place (Exodus 28:12, 29). Hebrews presents Jesus as the High Priest who always lives to make intercession for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). The breastplate is not called a type in the New Testament, but the picture fits what Hebrews says Christ does.

The wider people of God. In the olive tree image, Gentile believers are grafted into Israel's root rather than planted as a separate tree (Romans 11:17–24). Paul closes Galatians with a blessing: "And as many as walk according to this rule, peace and mercy be upon them, and upon the Israel of God" (Galatians 6:16). Many readers see "the Israel of God" as including Gentile believers who live by the new creation. Others read it as believing Jews within the church. Both readings agree that in Christ the promises made to Israel reach all who belong to Him, and Revelation's city with Israel's tribal names on its gates (Revelation 21:12) shows that Israel's story is not set aside in the end.

5. Spirit & Life

God makes His people strong by breaking their self-reliance. Jacob spent his early life getting ahead by cleverness. At the Jabbok he met Someone he could not outmaneuver. The Man touched his hip, and from then on Genesis shows Jacob clinging and asking for a blessing. Read devotionally, that is the moment Scripture says he "prevailed." Hosea describes it plainly: Jacob wept and sought favor (Hosea 12:4). The first time the phrase "children of Israel" appears, it is connected to their father's limp (Genesis 32:32). Devotionally, the people of God began with a man who limped away wounded and would not let go of God. Many believers have learned the same thing in their own struggles. When God wounds our self-sufficiency, He is preparing us to receive His blessing.

God keeps holding on to people who keep pulling away. The history of Israel is honest about human failure. That makes God's persistence the most striking feature of the story. He redeemed a complaining people, renewed His word after the golden calf, sent prophet after prophet to a rebellious kingdom, and still cried out over Ephraim (Hosea 11:8). This is the same God who saves through Jesus Christ. His love for sinners does not depend on their improvement. It holds on because He has bound Himself by His own promise.

Belonging to God means being called to serve. Israel was chosen to be "a kingdom of priests." Peter applies the same language to the church (1 Peter 2:9). Those who have received mercy are made a priestly people for the sake of others. The danger Israel faced, keeping the name while forgetting the calling, is a danger every church faces.

God's presence is the treasure. All the detail of Exodus and Leviticus serves one longing on God's part: to dwell among His people. The fitting response is to value His nearness above everything else, as Moses did when he said he would not go on without God's presence (Exodus 33:15).

Bearing the name is not the same as trusting the God who gave it. "They are not all Israel who are of Israel" (Romans 9:6) is a sober word for anyone who relies on religious heritage. Paul's answer to that warning is the gospel. God shows mercy, grafts in, and has not finished saving. Israel's story points us to the One who struggled for us and won, and it calls us to cling to Him.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 32:24–32 — Jacob wrestles with God, receives the name Israel and its meaning, and limps away blessed. The source of the name.

  • Genesis 35:10; 46:2 — God confirms the name, yet continues to call him "Jacob"; the two names remain side by side.

  • Genesis 49:24, 28 — Israel's twelve sons become "the twelve tribes of Israel"; God is "the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel."

  • Exodus 4:22; Hosea 11:1; Matthew 2:15 — Israel as God's firstborn son, a theme Matthew applies to Jesus.

  • Exodus 19:3–6 — Israel's calling as God's treasured possession, kingdom of priests, and holy nation.

  • Exodus 28:9–29; 29:45 — Israel's names borne before the LORD; God's purpose to dwell among His people.

  • Leviticus 16:34; 19:2 — Atonement and holiness for the people among whom God dwells.

  • Numbers 14; 16–17 — The wilderness rebellions and God's sign of the budding rod.

  • Deuteronomy 7:7–8; 9:4–6 — Israel chosen by God's love and oath, not its size or righteousness.

  • 1 Kings 12:16–20, 28–29; 2 Kings 17:7–23 — The kingdom divides, the North takes the name Israel, turns to idolatry, and falls.

  • Hosea 11:1–9; 12:3–4 — God's grieving love for Israel; the prophet's return to Jacob's wrestling.

  • Isaiah 43:1; 49:3–6 — Jacob/Israel created and redeemed; the Servant called Israel who restores Israel and enlightens the nations.

  • Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 37:11–24 — Promises of a new covenant and of a reunited, revived Israel under David's Son.

  • Matthew 4:1–11; Luke 9:31; John 1:47–51 — Jesus as the faithful Son tested in the wilderness, accomplishing a new exodus, and fulfilling Jacob's Bethel vision.

  • Romans 9:6; 11:1–29 — Paul's explanation of Israel's unbelief, the remnant, the olive tree, and the promise that "all Israel will be saved."

  • Galatians 6:16; Revelation 21:12 — "The Israel of God" and the twelve tribes' names on the gates of the New Jerusalem.
