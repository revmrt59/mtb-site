HOW, WHERE, WHY - (ʼêyk, Strong's H0349)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼêyk means "how?" It is a small word, yet it often appears at the moments when people reach the end of themselves. Judah uses it when he cannot face his father without Benjamin. Moses uses it when the people are too heavy to carry. Israel uses it when stronger nations seem impossible to overcome. Exiles use it when their guilt seems to leave them no future: "how can we then live?" (Ezekiel 33:10). In these places the word is less a request for instructions than a confession that something looks impossible. Again and again, God answers that "how" with His own word, His past faithfulness, and His promise of life.

The same word also becomes a cry of astonishment. The prophets use it to stand over the ruins of proud empires such as Babylon, Moab and Nineveh and exclaim at how suddenly they have fallen. In the Song of Solomon, it carries both the longing of love ("Where you feed your flock") and the excuses of a heart too comfortable to rise. Wherever it appears, ʼêyk measures the distance between what people can see or do and what God is doing.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼêyk is a Hebrew question word. The NKJV usually translates it "how," and occasionally "where" or "why," depending on what the English sentence needs. It appears in narrative dialogue, love poetry, prophetic oracles and laments. The prophets make prominent use of it, especially Jeremiah. That fits the word's character, because prophets deal constantly with what seems impossible to people and with sudden reversals brought about by God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, ʼêyk asks about manner or possibility: "how?" Context shows which of three uses is meant:

1. A genuine question asking for direction or information, as when Rehoboam asks his elders how they advise him to answer the people (2 Chronicles 10:6).

2. A question that expects the answer "it cannot be done," voicing impossibility, protest or rebuke, as in "How can I put it on again?" (Song of Songs 5:3).

3. An exclamation of astonishment, grief or taunt over a sudden change, as when Jeremiah marvels at how Babylon, the hammer of the whole earth, has been broken (Jeremiah 50:23).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Many occurrences fall into the second use, the question that expects "it can't be done." Sometimes this kind of question exposes weakness. The officials of Samaria, terrified of Jehu, ask how they could possibly stand (2 Kings 10:4). The people of the coastland ask how they will escape (Isaiah 20:6). Sometimes the question rebukes. David asks the Amalekite how he was not afraid to destroy the LORD's anointed (2 Samuel 1:14). God asks Israel how she can claim she is not polluted (Jeremiah 2:23). He asks Moab how it can boast of being mighty (Jeremiah 48:14). An enemy can use the word too. The Assyrian Rabshakeh mocks Hezekiah by asking how he could turn back even one captain (Isaiah 36:9).

The exclamation, the third use, appears mostly in the prophets and in Lamentations, and also in David's lament over Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel 1:19, 25, 27). In those settings the word often stands at the opening of a funeral song or taunt and announces that what seemed permanent has collapsed.

God Himself speaks this word. In Jeremiah 9:7 the LORD says He will refine His people and asks how else He could deal with the daughter of His people. In Isaiah 48:11 He asks how His name could be profaned. On God's lips, ʼêyk is never helplessness. It reveals the reasons that move Him to act.

4. Theological Significance

ʼêyk shows that God takes human impossibility seriously without being bound by it. People ask "how?" when they reach the limit of their strength, their courage or their innocence. Scripture records these questions honestly. Moses' "How can I alone bear your problems" (Deuteronomy 1:12, paraphrased) leads to shared leadership. Israel's fear of stronger nations (Deuteronomy 7:17) is answered by the command to remember what the LORD did to Pharaoh (7:18). The answer to "how?" is rarely a technique. It is the character and record of God.

The word also reveals God's opposition to pride. The exclamations over Babylon, Moab and Nineveh show that the empires that looked unshakable were always held in His hand. Zephaniah pictures Nineveh, the city that said in her heart that no one else existed, becoming a desolation that passersby hiss at (Zephaniah 2:15). Judgment does not stop with the nations, though. Lamentations 2:1 turns the same cry toward Jerusalem, lamenting how the Lord covered the daughter of Zion with a cloud in His anger. God's people are not exempt from the humbling they once watched others receive.

Most remarkable is Jeremiah 3:19. There God asks how He can set His unfaithful people among His children and give them a pleasant land. He then answers His own question: they will call Him "My Father" and no longer turn away. Where the human "how?" ends in despair, the divine "how?" ends in a provision of grace.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 44:34 — Judah's impossible return. Judah's question carries the weight of a changed man: "For how shall I go up to my father if the lad is not with me, lest perhaps I see the evil that would come upon my father?" This is the brother who once helped sell Joseph. Now he cannot bear his father's grief, and in the previous verse he offers himself as a slave in Benjamin's place (Genesis 44:33). His "how" is the voice of love that would rather suffer than see the beloved son lost. That offer breaks Joseph's composure and opens the way to reconciliation.

Isaiah 14:4, 12 and Jeremiah 50:23 — "How you are fallen." Isaiah gives Israel a taunt against the king of Babylon. It begins by marveling at how the oppressor has ceased (14:4). It then rises to the famous cry over the one fallen from heaven, called Lucifer, son of the morning (14:12). The surrounding verses explain why. This ruler had resolved in his heart to ascend into heaven and exalt his throne above the stars of God (14:13–14), yet he is brought down to the lowest depths of the Pit (14:15). The word ʼêyk holds the whole gap between that boast and that ruin. Nobody in the taunt asks how the collapse happened. They stand amazed that it happened at all. Jeremiah uses the same cry for the same empire, picturing the hammer of the whole earth cut apart and broken (50:23), and again in 51:41. The taunt has to do with God's sovereign reversal. The world's most feared power becomes an object of astonishment.

Ezekiel 33:10–11 — "How can we then live?" The exiles finally stop blaming others and admit their guilt: "If our transgressions and our sins lie upon us, and we pine away in them, how can we then live?" Their honesty is real, but it leads them toward hopelessness. They see their sin as a weight that can only crush them. God does not let the question stand. In verse 11 He swears by His own life that He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked, and that He wants the wicked to turn from his way and live. He then pleads with them, asking why they would die. The despairing "how?" of conscience is met by God's oath, which is one of the clearest invitations to repentance in the Old Testament.

Song of Songs 5:3–6 and Judges 16:15 — testing what love claims. In the Song, the beloved knocks at night, and the bride answers with two lazy questions: "I have taken off my robe; How can I put it on again? I have washed my feet; How can I defile them?" The cost she is avoiding is trivial, and her heart soon yearns for him (5:4). Yet when she rises, "my beloved had turned away and was gone… I sought him, but I could not find him" (5:6). Delilah uses the same word as a weapon when she asks Samson how he can say he loves her when his heart is not with her (Judges 16:15). Delilah's question is manipulation, and the bride's question is an excuse, but both uses of "how" expose a gap between what love says and what love is willing to do.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Judah's offer in Genesis 44 anticipates something the New Testament makes central. From Judah comes the royal line, and from that line comes the Son who did not merely offer to take another's place but actually did so. Genesis does not label Judah a type of Christ. Still, the pattern points forward: a son of Judah who would rather be bound than return to the father without his brother.

Ezekiel 33:11 points toward the gospel. The exiles' question, "how can we then live?", is the question of every conscience that sees its sin clearly. The full answer to God's oath of life came in Christ, who bore the sins that "lie upon us" so that the wicked can turn and live.

The taunt of Isaiah 14 is first aimed at the king of Babylon. Many readers have also heard in it the fall of the proud spirit behind earthly rebellion. Jesus' words about seeing Satan fall like lightning (Luke 10:18) show that the defeat of that power belongs to His kingdom. Whatever the extent of Isaiah's intent, the canon moves the same way: every throne raised against God ends with the cry "how fallen," and the risen Christ reigns.

7. Spirit & Life

The Song's opening cry shows what a healthy "how" sounds like: "Tell me, O you whom I love, Where you feed your flock, Where you make it rest at noon. For why should I be as one who veils herself By the flocks of your companions?" (Song of Songs 1:7). She does not want to wander among other shepherds. She wants him. The answer comes back simply: "Follow in the footsteps of the flock" (1:8). The Song is first a love poem, but believers have long heard in it the longing for the Lord's presence. Jesus speaks the same shepherd language: "My sheep hear My voice, and I know them, and they follow Me" (John 10:27). Usually He is found by walking where His flock has walked, among His people and in His Word.

Set this cry beside Song 5:3, and two kinds of "how" stand side by side. One asks how to find Him. The other asks how to avoid the bother of rising for Him. The difference is not knowledge but desire. When He knocks, the honest response is to get up while His hand is still at the latch.

Jeremiah 12:5 adds a bracing word for the weary. God asks His prophet how he will contend with horses if running with footmen has worn him out. God is not mocking Jeremiah. He is preparing him. Our "how can I?" is the place where He calls us to lean on His strength for what lies ahead.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 7:17 — Israel wonders how it can dispossess stronger nations; God points them to His past deliverance from Egypt.

  • 2 Samuel 12:18 — David's servants are afraid to tell him his child has died, asking one another how they can break the news after his severe grief and fasting.

  • Esther 8:6 — Esther asks twice how she can endure to see disaster come on her people, and she risks herself again to intercede for them.

  • Jeremiah 3:19 — God asks how He can place His people among His children, then answers that they will call Him "My Father."

  • Jeremiah 47:7 — The prophet asks how the sword of the LORD can be quiet when He has given it a charge.

  • Lamentations 4:1 — "How the gold has become dim! How changed the fine gold!" The lament opens by mourning how Zion's glory has lost its shine.
