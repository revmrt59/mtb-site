QUENCH, PUT OUT, EXTINGUISH - (kâbâh, Strong's H3518)

Summary

The Hebrew verb kâbâh means to go out or to put out. It is used for a fire or a lamp, either one that dies for lack of fuel or one that someone deliberately extinguishes. Scripture uses it for the altar fire in the tabernacle, the lamps in God's house, and a household hearth. It also uses the word for a family line, a king's life, and the burning of God's judgment. Most of its uses are negative. Some fires "shall not be quenched," and that phrase carries much of the word's weight.

The word lets us see what lasts. God told the priests never to let His altar fire go out. The prophets warned that His judgment against hardened idolatry would burn with no one able to put it out. The Song of Solomon declares that "many waters cannot quench love." The Servant of the Lord is the One who can snuff out the proud with no effort at all, yet He refuses to put out a smoldering wick. Here the word shows us the gentleness of Jesus Christ toward people who are barely burning.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kâbâh is a common Hebrew verb from a world lit by oil lamps and warmed by wood fires. Every household knew how easily a wick could die and how much work it took to keep a flame alive through the night. The word appears in the Law, the historical books, the wisdom writings, and especially the Prophets, which account for more than half of its uses. In each setting the biblical writers start from that ordinary experience of a fire going out or being put out.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Kâbâh describes a flame ceasing to burn. Context determines two related senses:

1. To go out, of a fire or lamp that dies down, often because nothing feeds it. Proverbs 26:20 says a fire goes out when there is no wood.

2. To put out or quench, of someone actively extinguishing a flame. In 2 Chronicles 29:7, men "put out the lamps" of the temple.

When the writers speak of a family line, a king, or God's anger, they are applying this plain meaning of a flame going out as a picture. Those pictures do not add new dictionary senses.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's most noticeable pattern is negation. In all but a handful of its occurrences, the verb appears with "not," "never," "not yet," "lest," or "no one to quench it." The question these passages ask is whether something will survive. The altar fire must never go out (Leviticus 6:12–13). God's wrath will not be quenched (Jeremiah 7:20). Love cannot be quenched (Song 8:7). The Servant will not quench (Isaiah 42:3).

The few positive uses are telling. They describe a grieving widow afraid her last ember will be put out (2 Samuel 14:7), a king darkening God's house (2 Chronicles 29:7), and God extinguishing proud powers such as Pharaoh (Ezekiel 32:7; Isaiah 43:17). Taken together, the word measures what endures and what does not.

4. Theological Significance

The prophets turn kâbâh toward God's holiness. When Judah forsook the Lord for idols, Huldah told King Josiah that God's wrath was kindled against Jerusalem and would not be quenched (2 Kings 22:17; 2 Chronicles 34:25). Jeremiah and Ezekiel use the same language repeatedly (Jeremiah 7:20; 17:27; Ezekiel 20:47–48). God's judgment has this unstoppable quality because it comes from His own character, which no idol, no ritual, and no human effort can resist (Isaiah 1:31).

The warnings are not cold verdicts, however. Several are pleas. Jeremiah calls the people to turn to the Lord before His fury burns with no one to quench it (Jeremiah 4:4). Jeremiah 17:27 makes the fire conditional on whether the people keep the Sabbath. Amos puts the invitation first: "Seek the LORD and live, Lest He break out like fire in the house of Joseph, And devour it, With no one to quench it in Bethel" (Amos 5:6). Even Josiah, who heard that the wrath would not be quenched, was personally spared because his heart was tender before God (2 Kings 22:19). The fire nobody can put out is announced so that people will run to the only One who can save them from it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The fire God commanded and the priests kept burning (Leviticus 6:12–13; 1 Samuel 3:3; 2 Chronicles 29:7). God commanded that the fire on the altar of burnt offering be kept burning and never go out. The priests had their part from the beginning. The Law told them to put fire on the altar and lay the wood in order on it (Leviticus 1:7), and every morning they were to add wood (Leviticus 6:12). Yet the altar fire was never simply their own work. When Aaron and his sons began their service, fire came out from before the LORD and consumed the burnt offering on the altar (Leviticus 9:24). God Himself answered the offerings of that day and showed that He accepted them. The priests were then responsible for keeping that altar fire burning as God had commanded, so that sacrifice and worship continued without a break.

The lamps of the sanctuary show the same concern. Samuel heard God's voice "before the lamp of God went out in the tabernacle of the LORD" (1 Samuel 3:3). The verse refers to the night lamp before morning, but the detail fits the moment closely. Eli's eyes were dim, and the word of the Lord was rare. The light was still burning when God spoke to a boy. Centuries later, Hezekiah described what his father Ahaz's generation had done: "They have also shut up the doors of the vestibule, put out the lamps, and have not burned incense or offered burnt offerings" (2 Chronicles 29:7). What God had ordered to burn continually, faithless leaders deliberately put out. The condition of the lamps showed the condition of the nation.

The smoldering wick (Isaiah 42:3; 43:17). Isaiah introduces God's chosen Servant: "A bruised reed He will not break, And smoking flax He will not quench; He will bring forth justice for truth" (Isaiah 42:3). "Smoking flax" is a linen wick that is barely burning. It gives off more smoke than light, and a breath could finish it. The Servant will not put it out.

The next chapter shows how much that restraint means. Isaiah 43:16–17 recalls the Lord who made a path through the sea and brought out Pharaoh's chariot, horse, and army, which lay down and did not rise, quenched like a wick. The same Hebrew word for flax or wick appears in both verses. Egypt's army was snuffed out like a wick, and the Servant will not snuff out the wick that smolders. The Lord who extinguished the most powerful army of the age refuses to extinguish a faltering life. He is gentle by choice, not from any lack of power.

Love stronger than floods (Song 8:6–7). The Song reaches its climax with this declaration: "Its flames are flames of fire, A most vehement flame. Many waters cannot quench love, Nor can the floods drown it." Many readers render the final phrase of verse 6 as a flame of the LORD, though translations differ. In its setting, the passage celebrates committed, exclusive love between husband and wife. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, it also echoes God's own love for His people, which their unfaithfulness, suffering, and the passing of time cannot drown.

The lamp of Israel (2 Samuel 14:7; 21:17). The woman of Tekoa pleaded that her family wanted to put out her one remaining ember, meaning her last son and her husband's name. Later, after David nearly died fighting the Philistines, his men swore he would no longer go to battle, so that he would not put out the lamp of Israel. David's life was the nation's light because God had promised to establish David's house and throne forever (2 Samuel 7).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

God kept David's lamp burning. He later said He would give David a lamp in Jerusalem for the sake of His promise (1 Kings 11:36). Through exile and the near-extinction of the royal line, the light was not put out. It came to its fulfillment in Jesus, the Son of David.

Matthew applies Isaiah 42:1–4 directly to Jesus (Matthew 12:15–21). He quotes it as Jesus healed the crowds, withdrew from conflict, and told people not to make Him known. Matthew uses the Greek equivalent of kâbâh for "quench." The King of David's line is the Servant who does not snuff out the weak.

The same Lord speaks the other side of the word as well. Jesus took up the final verse of Isaiah, "their worm does not die, And their fire is not quenched" (Isaiah 66:24), and used it to describe hell: "where 'Their worm does not die And the fire is not quenched'" (Mark 9:44; compare 9:48). The fire of judgment that cannot be quenched and the love that cannot be quenched meet at the cross. There God's judgment against sin was fully carried out on His Son, and His love was not drowned by it.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers know what it is to be smoking flax. Faith feels like smoke without light, and prayer is little more than a breath. Isaiah 42:3 means that Jesus does not treat such people as disposable. He had all the power He needed to put them out, as Isaiah 43:17 shows, and He refuses to use it against them. Those who are barely burning can come to Him without fear.

The altar fire also gives a pattern for life with God. God commanded the fire, answered the offering with fire from His presence, and charged the priests to keep it burning. Paul writes, "Do not quench the Spirit" (1 Thessalonians 5:19), using the Greek word that corresponds to kâbâh. The comparison is an analogy rather than something Leviticus teaches directly, but the parallel is real. The Spirit is God's gift, and we respond to Him, or neglect Him, every day.

Proverbs adds a down-to-earth lesson: where there is no wood, the fire goes out, and where there is no talebearer, strife ceases (Proverbs 26:20). Some fires should be allowed to die. A believer can refuse to add fuel to gossip and quarrels.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 31:18 — The capable wife's lamp does not go out at night. The detail pictures her steady, careful work for her household.

  • Isaiah 1:31 — The strong man becomes like tinder and his work like a spark. Both burn together with no one to quench them, so the idolater's own deeds fuel his ruin.

  • Isaiah 34:10 — Edom's burning is not quenched night or day. The picture is of a final, lasting desolation.

  • Jeremiah 21:12 — The house of David is told to do justice every morning, or God's fury will burn with no one to quench it. The warning holds the royal line itself accountable.

  • Ezekiel 20:47–48 — God kindles a fire in the forest of the south that will not be quenched, so that all flesh will see that the Lord kindled it.

  • Ezekiel 32:7 — God declares that when He puts out Pharaoh, He will cover the heavens and darken the stars.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G4570 (sbennymi) — The Greek verb for quench. It is a distinct word in another language that serves as the New Testament counterpart to kâbâh. It appears in Matthew 12:20 (quoting Isaiah 42:3), Mark 9:48, and 1 Thessalonians 5:19.

  • H6594 (pishtah) — A distinct Hebrew word meaning flax or wick. It is paired with kâbâh in Isaiah 42:3 and 43:17, where the contrast between the two verses turns on the wick.

  • H784 ('esh) — A distinct Hebrew word meaning fire. It is the thing that kâbâh quenches, for example in Leviticus 6:12–13 and Jeremiah 4:4.
