DOE, DEER - (ʼayâlâh, Strong's H0355)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼayâlâh means a doe, or female deer, the graceful wild animal of the hills and forests of Israel. This word never appears in a sacrifice or a food law. Nearly every time she appears, she is a picture. Her sure footing on steep, rocky heights, her hidden birthing in the wild, and her shy, easily startled nature each become a way of seeing something about God and the people He cares for.

The best-known use is David's and Habakkuk's confession: "He makes my feet like the feet of deer." David sang it looking back on deliverance from his enemies, and Habakkuk sang it looking ahead to famine and invasion. God does not promise to level the mountains. He makes His people able to stand on them. Elsewhere the doe shows that God watches over the hidden and vulnerable moments of creation that no human eye sees (Job 39:1; Psalm 29:9), and that love, like a wild doe, cannot be forced or hurried (Song of Solomon 2:7).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼAyâlâh is the feminine form of the Hebrew word for deer. Its masculine partner, ʼayyâl (the stag or hart), is the word behind Psalm 42:1, "As the deer pants for the water brooks." The thirsting image belongs to that masculine word. The doe passages carry different pictures of footing, hiddenness, and gentleness, and they are best read on their own terms.

The word occurs 11 times in 11 verses of the Hebrew Bible. That count includes forms that traditional Strong's numbering lists separately as H0365 (the Psalm 22 title, Proverbs 5:19 and Jeremiah 14:5), which is why some lists give eight. Apart from the Psalm 22 title, every use of ʼayâlâh appears in poetry. Even the one in 2 Samuel sits inside David's song of praise. Israel's hill country was home to wild deer, which people saw but did not tame or raise, and that is part of why the doe works so well as an image. She belongs to the wild places, beyond human control.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one plain meaning: a female deer, a doe or hind. The NKJV translates it as "deer," "deer's," or "does." There are no separate senses to distinguish. The richness comes from what the biblical writers notice about the animal. They mention her agility on the heights, her giving birth in the wild, her freedom, and her timidity.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The uses gather into a few clear patterns:

  • Feet on the heights. In 2 Samuel 22:34, Psalm 18:33, and Habakkuk 3:19, the doe's feet picture God-given stability in dangerous places. In each passage God is the one who acts. He makes the feet like a deer's and sets His servant on the high places.

  • Birth in the wild. Job 39:1 and Psalm 29:9 speak of the doe giving birth, a moment of hidden vulnerability known to God and governed by His voice. Some interpreters read the Hebrew of Psalm 29:9 as makes the oaks whirl, but the NKJV follows the reading "makes the deer give birth," which fits the psalm's picture of the wilderness shaken by the LORD's voice.

  • Freedom and gentleness. Genesis 49:21 calls Naphtali "a deer let loose." In the Song of Solomon (2:7; 3:5), the does of the field are named in a solemn charge about love.

The doe is never the hero of these passages. She is the comparison that lets readers see what God does or what love is like.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God whose care reaches places people cannot go. He equips His servants for terrain that would destroy them. He watches the birth of animals no shepherd keeps, in thickets no hunter finds. He is also the God who made love with its own timing, so that it must not be stirred up "until it pleases."

The common thread is that God's strength is given to the weak. A doe has no horns, armor, or fangs, and she survives by being light, alert, and well placed. When Scripture uses her as a picture of the believer, the comparison never praises human toughness. It praises the God who makes fragile creatures secure.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

2 Samuel 22:34 / Psalm 18:33 and Habakkuk 3:19: the same confession from opposite sides. David's song appears twice in Scripture, and both times it says, "He makes my feet like the feet of deer, And sets me on my high places." According to its heading, David sang it on the day the LORD delivered him from Saul and from all his enemies. For David, the high places are the heights of rescue and victory, and he sings looking back over a life God had preserved.

Habakkuk takes up the same words in a very different situation. He has been told that the Chaldeans are coming (Habakkuk 1:6), and his body trembles at what he has heard (Habakkuk 3:16). Then he faces the worst outcome with open eyes: "Though the fig tree may not blossom, Nor fruit be on the vines; Though the labor of the olive may fail, And the fields yield no food; Though the flock may be cut off from the fold, And there be no herd in the stalls— Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation" (Habakkuk 3:17–18). Only after that does he say, "The LORD God is my strength; He will make my feet like deer's feet, And He will make me walk on my high hills" (Habakkuk 3:19).

David looks back on triumph. Habakkuk looks ahead to losing everything. The same God gives both men the same feet. For Habakkuk the high hills are not the reward of good circumstances. He expects to walk there even if circumstances collapse. God does not flatten the mountain for him. He changes the person who has to cross it.

Job 39:1 and Psalm 29:9: the hidden birth God governs. At the climax of Job, the LORD asks, "Do you know the time when the wild mountain goats bear young? Or can you mark when the deer gives birth?" Job cannot answer. No one watches over wild does in labor. The doe goes off alone, at the moment she is most exposed. God's question shows that He knows that hour precisely, even when no person does. Psalm 29 goes further. There "the voice of the LORD makes the deer give birth, And strips the forests bare," and the result is worship: "in His temple everyone says, 'Glory!'" The same voice that shakes the wilderness attends the birth of a fawn in it.

Song of Solomon 2:7 and 3:5: love that must not be forced. The woman in the Song charges the daughters of Jerusalem "By the gazelles or by the does of the field, Do not stir up nor awaken love Until it pleases." The choice of witness fits the charge. Gazelles and does startle easily and run from anyone who approaches roughly. They cannot be grabbed and held without losing the grace that makes them what they are. Love is the same. It has its own God-given timing and cannot be pushed into existence or awakened before its season. Repeating the charge (2:7; 3:5; compare 8:4) gives it the weight of a refrain the reader is meant to remember.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The song of the deer's feet did not stay in David's lifetime. Paul cites Psalm 18:49 in Romans 15:9 as Scripture fulfilled in Christ's work of bringing the Gentiles to praise God. This puts David's song of deliverance into the larger story of the Son of David, whom God vindicated and set on the heights.

Habakkuk's confession belongs to the same story. His book gave the church the line "the just shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4), which Paul and Hebrews quote to describe life with God through Christ (Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11; Hebrews 10:38). The deer's feet of chapter 3 show what that faith looks like in practice: rejoicing in "the God of my salvation" even if the fields yield no food.

Jesus also spoke of the Father's care for small, unwatched creatures. Not one sparrow falls to the ground apart from Him (Matthew 10:29). That is the same truth Job 39 teaches through the birthing doe, now spoken by the Son to frightened disciples. A related image may also deserve mention. Psalm 22, the psalm Jesus cried from the cross, bears the title "The Deer of the Dawn" (Aijeleth Hashahar). That title uses a related Hebrew form, and its exact meaning, possibly the name of a tune, is uncertain. Even so, the psalm that begins in forsakenness ends in praise and worldwide worship.

7. Spirit & Life

The doe teaches a kind of strength most people would not choose. She cannot fight her way up a mountain. She climbs because of how she was made. God makes His people's feet "like the feet of deer" (Psalm 18:33), giving them sure footing. In the same song David thanks God who "arms me with strength" and teaches his hands to make war (Psalm 18:32, 34). The deer's feet add something to that strength: God gives us the strength and sure footing we could not supply for ourselves, so that we are able to stand. This matches what the Lord told Paul, that His strength is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9). Believers who feel defenseless are not disqualified from the high places. Often they are exactly the ones God sets there.

Habakkuk also shows that this sure footing does not wait for good news. He resolved to rejoice even if the fig tree failed to blossom. Some believers can echo David, looking back on God's rescues. Others are standing where Habakkuk stood, seeing the loss coming. The promise covers both.

The birthing doe speaks to the hidden seasons of life: illness kept private, grief no one else sees, a long labor of faith without witnesses. God's question to Job means that He marks those hours too. Nothing that happens in the thicket happens outside His knowledge or apart from His voice.

The Song's refrain applies directly to human love, and its wisdom reaches further by analogy. Many good things God gives, such as love, readiness, and spiritual fruit, cannot be forced without being damaged. Patience with God's timing is not passivity. It is respect for the way He made things to grow.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 49:21 — "Naphtali is a deer let loose; He uses beautiful words." Jacob's blessing pictures a tribe marked by freedom and grace, quick and unhindered like a released doe.

  • Psalm 18:33 — The psalter's copy of David's song repeats 2 Samuel 22:34 word for word. Israel kept this confession in its worship, not only in its history.

  • Song of Solomon 3:5 — The charge by "the does of the field" returns unchanged. Love's timing is a lesson the woman insists on more than once.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H0354 (ʼayyâl), stag, hart — a lexical relative. This masculine form of the same word appears in Psalm 42:1, "As the deer pants for the water brooks." It carries the image of thirst, which belongs to that passage, not to the doe texts.

  • Canonical pairings: the doe is paired with "the wild mountain goats" in Job 39:1 and with "the gazelles" in Song of Solomon 2:7 and 3:5. In both pairings the animals share wildness and shyness.
