CUP, OWL - (kôwç, Strong's H3563)

Summary

Kôwç is the ordinary Hebrew word for a cup, the vessel a king, a servant, or a poor man in his hut would lift to his lips. Scripture lets this everyday object carry a great deal. Someone who hands you a cup has decided what you will drink, so the cup became a natural picture of a person's lot in life, the portion measured out and placed in a person's hands. David can say of the Lord, "You are the portion of my inheritance and my cup" (Psalm 16:5). In Psalm 23 the cup runs over, and in Psalm 116 the believer takes up "the cup of salvation."

The prophets turn the same image toward judgment. In the Lord's hand is a cup of red, fully mixed wine that the wicked must drain to the dregs (Psalm 75:8). It makes nations stagger, strips them of their glory, and leaves them exposed in shame. That background explains what Jesus faced when He prayed in Gethsemane, "Take this cup away from Me" (Mark 14:36). The same Hebrew spelling also names an unclean bird, probably an owl, which the psalmist uses as a picture of desolate loneliness (Psalm 102:6).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kôwç is a common Hebrew noun for a drinking vessel. It appears in a royal court, where Pharaoh's butler places the cup in the king's hand (Genesis 40). It appears in a poor man's home (2 Samuel 12:3) and in the temple furnishings, where the rim of Solomon's great bronze Sea was shaped like the brim of a cup (1 Kings 7:26; 2 Chronicles 4:5). It belongs to scenes of mourning (Jeremiah 16:7), to wisdom warnings about wine (Proverbs 23:31), and most of all to the poetry of the Psalms and the oracles of the prophets.

The same spelling names an unclean bird listed in the food laws (Leviticus 11:17; Deuteronomy 14:16), usually translated "owl." Some have suggested a link to the cup-like hollows around an owl's eyes, but that connection is uncertain. It is best to treat the bird as a separate word that happens to share the same form.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A cup or drinking vessel. This is the literal sense, as with Pharaoh's cup in the butler's dream (Genesis 40:11).

2. A portion or lot, pictured as a cup someone is given to drink. What fills it may be blessing or judgment: "the portion of my inheritance and my cup" (Psalm 16:5), or "fire and brimstone and a burning wind" as "the portion of their cup" (Psalm 11:6).

3. An unclean bird, probably an owl, in the dietary laws and once in poetry (Leviticus 11:17; Psalm 102:6).

The word itself means neither wrath nor blessing. The cup takes its character from what is poured into it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The literal uses already carry relational weight. In Nathan's parable the poor man's ewe lamb "ate of his own food and drank from his own cup" (2 Samuel 12:3, paraphrased). Nathan chose that detail because sharing a cup meant belonging. The lamb was treated almost as a daughter of the house. A cup is passed from hand to hand, and a cup given is always given by someone to someone.

Jeremiah 16:7 shows a cup offered in grief. Neighbors brought the "cup of consolation" to a family mourning a father or mother, much as Job's friends agreed "to come and mourn with him, and to comfort him" (Job 2:11). God told Jeremiah that in the coming judgment no one would offer that cup. Ordinary kindness would collapse along with the nation. Even the comfort people give one another at a graveside was something God had been supplying all along.

One pattern changes how the figurative cup reads. Scripture often names whose hand holds it: "in the hand of the LORD there is a cup" (Psalm 75:8), or "the cup of the LORD's right hand" (Habakkuk 2:16). The contents also give the cup its name: the cup of salvation, the cup of fury, "the cup of horror and desolation" (Ezekiel 23:33). These phrases keep both the giver and the gift in view.

4. Theological Significance

The cup passages present God as the host of all the earth who measures out each portion. Nothing in a person's cup arrives by accident. The psalmist rejoices that the Lord will "maintain my lot" (Psalm 16:5), and the prophets warn that the Lord pours the judgment Himself. His wrath is measured and deliberate, and it is never careless rage. It is personal as well as just, because a cup is handed directly from one person to another.

The same God who pours judgment can give Himself as His people's cup. That is the striking turn of Psalm 16. The believer's portion is the Lord Himself, which echoes the Levites, who received no share of the land because the Lord was their inheritance (Numbers 18:20). The cup also shows God's freedom to reverse what He has poured. He can take a cup out of one hand and place it in another, and He rules even over the vessels He uses.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 75:8 and Jeremiah 25 — the cup no one escapes. Psalm 75 gives the picture its full strength: "For in the hand of the LORD there is a cup, and the wine is red; it is fully mixed, and He pours it out; surely its dregs shall all the wicked of the earth drain and drink down." Jeremiah is then told to take the wine cup of God's fury from His hand and make the nations drink it, Jerusalem first (Jeremiah 25:15–18). If any nation refuses, the prophet is to tell them they will certainly drink (25:28). Refusal is not an option.

Other prophets describe what the cup does. Ezekiel calls it "the deep and wide one" that "contains much" (Ezekiel 23:32), "the cup of horror and desolation" (23:33). Habakkuk speaks to a proud oppressor: "You also—drink! And be exposed as uncircumcised!… And utter shame will be on your glory" (Habakkuk 2:16). Lamentations tells Edom the cup will pass to her and she will become drunk and strip herself naked (Lamentations 4:21, paraphrased). The image recalls Noah's drunkenness and exposure (Genesis 9:21). The wrath cup takes away self-control and dignity and lays bare what pride had covered. In contrast, the overflowing cup of Psalm 23 never produces that kind of shame.

Isaiah 51:17–22 — the cup taken away. Jerusalem lies in the street, having drunk "the cup of His fury" to the dregs and staggered (51:17, paraphrased). Then the Lord, who "pleads the cause of His people," announces that He has taken the cup of trembling out of her hand and she will never drink it again (51:22, paraphrased). He places it in the hands of those who afflicted her (51:23). God disciplines His people severely, but His loyalty to them goes deeper than His discipline. He removes the cup Himself.

Jeremiah 51:7 — Babylon, the golden cup. "Babylon was a golden cup in the LORD's hand, that made all the earth drunk." Babylon's splendor was real, and so was her role as God's instrument against the nations. Yet the very next verse announces her sudden fall. God may use an empire as a cup in His hand, and He still holds it accountable for its pride and cruelty.

Psalms 16:5, 23:5, 116:13 — the Lord as the cup. David does not only say God gives him a good cup. He says, "You are… my cup" (16:5). In Psalm 23 the Shepherd becomes the host: "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies… My cup runs over" (23:5). The enemies are still present, yet the cup still overflows. Psalm 116 adds the response: "I will take up the cup of salvation, and call upon the name of the LORD" (116:13). A gift like this is not earned. It is received with gratitude and answered with prayer.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Greek Gospels use a different word for cup, but they carry the Hebrew image forward. When Jesus prayed, "Take this cup away from Me; nevertheless, not what I will, but what You will" (Mark 14:36), He spoke as a Jew who knew Psalm 75 and Jeremiah 25. The cup He dreaded was more than suffering in general. It was the portion of God's fury that the prophets said the wicked must drain, and He drank it in their place. Isaiah 51 promised that God would take the cup of trembling out of His people's hands. At the cross that promise found its deepest answer, because the One who took the cup also drank it.

Only hours earlier, Jesus had handed His disciples another cup and called it the new covenant in His blood (Luke 22:20). He offered the cup of blessing at the table and accepted the cup of wrath in the garden. The two Old Testament cups meet in Him.

The warning still stands for those who refuse Him. Revelation speaks of "the cup of His indignation," poured "full strength" (Revelation 14:10). It also pictures a final Babylon holding "a golden cup full of abominations" (Revelation 17:4), an echo of Jeremiah's golden cup. The story ends where the prophets said it would. Every cup will be drunk, and the only question is which one.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 16:5 sets a higher aim than asking God for a full cup. It invites us to want Him as the cup. Gifts can be lost, but a person whose portion is the Lord cannot be left with nothing. That is why the cup of Psalm 23 can overflow while enemies are still watching. The table is spread in their presence, not after they have gone away.

The cup of salvation in Psalm 116 is taken up, and the hand that lifts it also calls on the Lord's name. Thanksgiving is how a believer drinks it. Every time a Christian takes the Lord's Supper, he is lifting a cup that is full only because Another emptied the cup of fury.

Scripture is honest about seasons when the cup feels empty. "I am like a pelican of the wilderness; I am like an owl of the desert" (Psalm 102:6). The psalmist pictures himself as a bird of the ruins, alone among broken walls at night. God put those words in His book so the afflicted would have a prayer to pray. The psalm does not end in the ruins. It turns to the Lord who endures forever and who will rebuild Zion (Psalm 102:12–16, paraphrased). The owl's cry is spoken toward the God who hears it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 40:11–21 — The butler dreams of pressing grapes into Pharaoh's cup, and Joseph's interpretation comes true when he again places the cup in Pharaoh's hand. Restoration to the king's presence is pictured by the cup returned to service.

  • 1 Kings 7:26; 2 Chronicles 4:5 — The rim of the bronze Sea was worked like the brim of a cup, like a lily blossom. Even the temple's great basin borrowed the shape of an everyday vessel.

  • Proverbs 23:31 — The wise father warns against gazing at wine when it sparkles in the cup. Its attraction is the first step toward its sting.

  • Jeremiah 49:12 — If those who did not deserve the cup have drunk it, Edom will surely drink. Privilege brings no exemption from God's justice.

  • Leviticus 11:17; Deuteronomy 14:16 — The owl stands among the birds Israel was not to eat. This is the literal setting behind the psalmist's later image.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The silver cup Joseph hid in Benjamin's sack (Genesis 44) is a different Hebrew word. It should not be read into the study of kôwç or linked with the butler's cup in Genesis 40.

Scripture itself names the cup by its contents and treats it as a person's appointed portion: "the portion of my inheritance and my cup" (Psalm 16:5), and "the portion of their cup" (Psalm 11:6). In these verses the cup stands for one's God-given lot.

The cup of judgment repeatedly brings drunkenness, staggering, and shameful exposure (Habakkuk 2:16; Lamentations 4:21; Ezekiel 23:31–33), as developed in Section 5. The Gospels carry the double cup of blessing and wrath into the Lord's Supper and Gethsemane.

Two passages offer suggestive pictures without being stated symbols. In Genesis 40 the butler's cup is linked with pardon and life before the king, while the baker's dream ends in death. This contrast foreshadows the cup of salvation, but it should not be pressed into a formal type of the Lord's Supper. In Jeremiah 35:5, cups of wine are set before the Rechabites in the Lord's house, and they refuse to drink out of loyalty to their father's command. God holds up their faithfulness against Judah's failure to keep His word. Their refusal gives a fitting picture of loyalty that declines what the surrounding culture pours.
