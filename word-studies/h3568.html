CUSH - (Kûwsh, Strong's H3568)

Summary

The Bible uses Cush for different people and lands, whose histories should be distinguished. Most often it names the land and people south of Egypt along the upper Nile, roughly ancient Nubia and today's northern Sudan, which the NKJV usually renders "Ethiopia." The same name belongs to a son of Ham and grandson of Noah, the ancestor listed first among Ham's sons and the father of Nimrod (Genesis 10:6–8). It also names a land bordered by Eden's river Gihon, whose location is uncertain (Genesis 2:13), and a Benjamite named in the title of Psalm 7. The meaning of the name Cush is uncertain. The Hebrew form appears to come from an Egyptian name for the Nubian region rather than from a Hebrew word with a known meaning.

Scripture's portrait of Cush moves in a striking direction. In Isaiah's day Cushite kings ruled Egypt, and Judah was tempted to lean on their armies against Assyria. God had Isaiah walk naked and barefoot for three years so Judah would see that Cush, "their expectation," would end up in shame (Isaiah 20:3–5). Prophets announced judgment on Cush along with Egypt. Yet the same Scriptures describe Cush stretching out her hands to God (Psalm 68:31), name Cush among those whom God records as born in Zion (Psalm 87:4), and picture worshipers bringing God's offering "from beyond the rivers of Ethiopia" (Zephaniah 3:10). Centuries later, "a man of Ethiopia" returning from worship in Jerusalem heard the good news about Jesus from Isaiah's own scroll and was baptized (Acts 8:27–38). The farthest southern edge of the biblical map became a place the gospel reached early, a sign that God's welcome extends to the ends of the earth.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew lemma is כּוּשׁ (Kûwsh), found 30 times in the Old Testament. It names at least four different people or places.

Cush the son of Ham. He is listed among Noah's grandsons in the Table of Nations: "The sons of Ham were Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan" (Genesis 10:6). His sons were Seba, Havilah, Sabtah, Raamah, and Sabtechah, and he "begot Nimrod; he began to be a mighty one on the earth" (Genesis 10:7–8; repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:8–10). Like the other names in Genesis 10, Cush stands both for an ancestor and for the people descended from him. Several of the names in his line, such as Havilah, Sheba, and Dedan, also appear in other genealogies (Genesis 10:28–29; 25:3). They should not be merged with Cush's descendants without clear evidence.

Cush the land and nation. This is the most common use. It refers to the region south of Egypt, beyond the first cataract of the Nile and extending into modern Sudan. The NKJV usually translates it "Ethiopia," following older English tradition. Readers should note that the modern nation of Ethiopia is not the same as biblical Cush. The book of Esther uses Cush as the southern limit of the Persian Empire, "from India to Ethiopia" (Esther 1:1; 8:9).

The Cush of Eden. Genesis 2:13 says the Gihon "goes around the whole land of Cush." Many readers have identified this with the African Cush. Because the passage also mentions the Tigris and Euphrates, some scholars propose a Mesopotamian people (sometimes linked to the Kassites) instead. The text does not settle the matter, and this study leaves it open.

Cush the Benjamite. Psalm 7's title says David sang the psalm to the LORD concerning the words of Cush, a Benjamite. Nothing else is known of him. Jewish tradition has sometimes read the name as a veiled reference to Saul, who was also from Benjamin, or to one of Saul's supporters. That is a guess, not a biblical statement. He should also be kept distinct from the unnamed Cushite runner who brought David news of Absalom's death (2 Samuel 18:21–32).

The name's meaning. The etymology of Cush is unsettled. The Hebrew name appears to be borrowed from the Egyptian Kash (Kꜣš), the Egyptians' name for the Nubian region on the upper Nile. If so, Cush is a foreign place-name that Hebrew adopted, and it has no known Hebrew meaning. Scripture does not make wordplay on the name, and its spiritual significance should be drawn from what the Bible says about the people and land, not from speculation about the word.

Related names. The gentilic Kuwshiy ("Cushite," Strong's H3569) is a separately indexed word. It carries important stories, including Ebed-Melech, the Cushite official who rescued Jeremiah (Jeremiah 38:7–13), the Cushite runner who brought David news (2 Samuel 18:21–32), and Amos 9:7. Moses' Cushite wife (Numbers 12:1) is described with the feminine form Kuwshiyth (H3571), which is formed from H3569 and also indexed separately. In the New Testament, the Greek Aithiops ("Ethiopian," G128) describes the official in Acts 8:27. These are discussed below as related evidence, not as occurrences of H3568.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Cush in the Beginnings

Cush appears near the very start of the Bible, first as a land bordered by a river of Eden (Genesis 2:13) and then as a son of Ham after the flood (Genesis 10:6). His son Nimrod became "a mighty one on the earth" (Genesis 10:8). The next verses describe Nimrod as a mighty hunter and say that his kingdom began in Babel and other cities of Shinar before reaching Assyria and Nineveh (Genesis 10:9–12). Through Nimrod, Cush's line is linked with the rise of the first great kingdoms after the flood, including the region where Babel would later rebel against God (Genesis 11:1–9). Genesis does not say that Nimrod built the tower, but it does place the beginning of his kingdom in Babel.

One misunderstanding needs correcting. Noah's curse after Ham's sin fell on Canaan, not on Ham as a whole and not on Cush (Genesis 9:25). Noah never cursed Cush or his descendants; the later prophetic judgments on Cush, discussed below, are a different matter.

The Land of the Far South

For Israel, Cush was the distant south, known for wealth and powerful warriors. Job uses its treasure to measure the worth of wisdom: "The topaz of Ethiopia cannot equal it, Nor can it be valued in pure gold" (Job 28:19). Esther names Cush as one boundary of Ahasuerus's realm (Esther 1:1; 8:9). Isaiah describes Cush as a land "beyond the rivers," sending envoys by sea in papyrus boats, a nation of tall, smooth-skinned people feared far and wide (Isaiah 18:1–2, paraphrased).

Cush and the Assyrian Crisis

In the late eighth and early seventh centuries BC, a Cushite dynasty ruled Egypt, so Egypt and Cush were closely bound together. When Sennacherib of Assyria threatened Jerusalem, he heard that Tirhakah king of Cush was marching out to fight him (2 Kings 19:9; Isaiah 37:9). That report led him to send a threatening letter to Hezekiah, which Hezekiah spread before the LORD in prayer (2 Kings 19:14–19). Jerusalem's deliverance came from God, not from the Cushite army (2 Kings 19:35–36).

Isaiah 20 shows what God thought of Judah's hope in Egypt and Cush. The LORD said, "Just as My servant Isaiah has walked naked and barefoot three years for a sign and a wonder against Egypt and Ethiopia, so shall the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptians as prisoners and the Ethiopians as captives, young and old, naked and barefoot" (Isaiah 20:3–4). The result would be shame for those who had leaned on them: "Then they shall be afraid and ashamed of Ethiopia their expectation and Egypt their glory" (Isaiah 20:5).

Judgment with Egypt

Because Cush stood with Egypt, it shared Egypt's judgment. Cush and Put marched in Pharaoh's army in the battle Jeremiah describes, the campaign that ended in defeat at Carchemish (Jeremiah 46:2, 9). Ezekiel announces that Egypt will be desolate from Migdol to Syene, "as far as the border of Ethiopia" (Ezekiel 29:10), that is, Cush. When the sword falls on Egypt, anguish will seize Cush, and messengers will go out in ships to terrify the careless Cushites (Ezekiel 30:4–5, 9). Nahum later reminds Nineveh that Thebes (No Amon) had Cush and Egypt as boundless strength, yet it was still carried into exile (Nahum 3:8–10). Cushite power had not saved Thebes, and Assyria's own strength would not save Nineveh.

Ezekiel also lists Cush with Persia and Put in the far-flung army of Gog, the coalition God gathers and judges on the mountains of Israel (Ezekiel 38:5). Here Cush stands among the distant nations that set themselves against God's people.

Cush Turning to God

Alongside these warnings runs a very different line. Isaiah promises that the Lord will recover the remnant of His own scattered people "from Cush" and other distant lands (Isaiah 11:11, paraphrased). In Isaiah 18, after describing the land beyond the rivers of Cush, the prophet says that a present will one day be brought to the LORD of hosts from that tall and smooth-skinned people, to Mount Zion, the place of the LORD's name (Isaiah 18:7, paraphrased). Isaiah 45:14 pictures the wealth of Egypt and the merchandise of Cush coming over to God's people, with the nations confessing that God is truly among them.

The psalms speak even more directly. "Envoys will come out of Egypt; Ethiopia will quickly stretch out her hands to God" (Psalm 68:31). Psalm 87 goes further, naming Cush among foreign peoples whom God counts as His own: "I will make mention of Rahab and Babylon to those who know Me; Behold, O Philistia and Tyre, with Ethiopia: 'This one was born there'" (Psalm 87:4). Zephaniah echoes Isaiah's phrase about the rivers of Cush: "From beyond the rivers of Ethiopia My worshipers, The daughter of My dispersed ones, Shall bring My offering" (Zephaniah 3:10). Interpreters differ on whether these worshipers are scattered Israelites living in the far south, people of the nations, or both. In any case, worship is coming to God from the farthest edge of the known world.

Cushites in Israel's Story (related names, H3569 and H3571)

The stories of individual Cushites, though indexed under the related gentilic (H3569) and, for Moses' wife, its feminine form (H3571), give these prophecies a human face. When Jerusalem's leaders threw Jeremiah into a muddy cistern to die, Ebed-Melech the Cushite went to King Zedekiah, pleaded for the prophet, and pulled him out with ropes and old rags (Jeremiah 38:7–13). God then promised to spare him when the city fell, because he had trusted in the LORD (Jeremiah 39:15–18). Moses' marriage to a Cushite woman drew criticism from Miriam and Aaron, and God rebuked them, not Moses (Numbers 12:1–10). Amos even used the Cushites to humble Israel's pride, as God asked whether Israel was any more to Him than the people of Cush (Amos 9:7).

The Ethiopian Official (Greek Aithiops)

The New Testament completes the picture. Philip was sent to a desert road, "and behold, a man of Ethiopia, a eunuch of great authority under Candace the queen of the Ethiopians, who had charge of all her treasury, and had come to Jerusalem to worship" (Acts 8:27). He was reading Isaiah's words about the suffering Servant led like a sheep to the slaughter (Acts 8:32–33). Beginning at that Scripture, Philip preached Jesus to him, and the man was baptized and went on his way rejoicing (Acts 8:35–39).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Cush was a real people and land, that its strength could not save those who trusted it, and that God judged it along with Egypt. Scripture states just as plainly that Cush would stretch out her hands to God, be counted among those born in Zion, and bring offerings to the LORD. Holding both together is the key to Cush's place in the Bible.

The first lesson concerns misplaced trust. Judah's leaders looked south because Cush had the army that seemed able to match Assyria. Isaiah's three years of public humiliation made the point that nothing from Cush could replace the Holy One of Israel. Nahum's reminder about Thebes makes the same point from another angle: Cushite might had already failed once.

The second concerns God's rule over every nation. Ezekiel's oracles reach to "the border of Ethiopia" (Cush), and Esther's empire stretches "from India to Ethiopia." No people lies outside God's reach, whether for judgment or for mercy. Even the appearance of Cush in Gog's army (Ezekiel 38:5) shows a God who governs the far edges of the map.

The third, and the most surprising, is Isaiah 43:3: "I gave Egypt for your ransom, Ethiopia and Seba in your place." God speaks of handing over great southern nations to secure Israel's freedom. The verse shows how much God valued the people He had chosen and redeemed. It is also a ransom spoken of in terms of nations given up, which makes the later promises about Cush all the more remarkable. The people once "given in place of" Israel are later described as worshipers bringing God's offering.

From these passages one can draw a careful inference. Cush stands near the beginning of human empire through Nimrod, and Cush stands among the nations God registers in Zion. The Bible does not spell out this arc in a single passage, but taken together the texts show God's purpose to gather worshipers even from the peoples most associated with human power and distance from Israel.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament does not quote Psalm 68 or Zephaniah 3 when it tells of the Ethiopian official, so his conversion is best described as a strongly supported canonical development rather than a stated fulfillment. The links are still hard to miss. Jesus had told His followers they would be witnesses "to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8). In Acts 8 the gospel reaches a man from the very region the prophets had used to mark the earth's far edge. He had already made the long trip to Jerusalem to worship, and on his way home he found the Servant of Isaiah 53 in Jesus. Isaiah had spoken both judgment and hope over Cush, and now Isaiah's own words brought a Cushite to Christ.

Isaiah 43:3 invites a careful comparison. There God gave Cush as a ransom for Israel. In the gospel the ransom is no longer a nation handed over but the Son of Man Himself, who came "to give His life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Isaiah 43 is not presented as a type of the cross. The contrast still matters: the gospel brings a man of Cush into God's people through the ransom Christ paid, not by giving him up in exchange for others.

Psalm 87 also resonates with the New Testament's teaching that believers belong to "the Jerusalem above" (Galatians 4:26) and that their citizenship is in heaven (Philippians 3:20). The psalm pictures God Himself writing outsiders into Zion's birth record. That is an Old Testament glimpse of what the gospel makes real, a people from every nation, tribe, and tongue standing before the throne (Revelation 7:9).

5. Spirit & Life

The story of Cush exposes the hope we place in visible strength. Judah could see Cush's chariots and could not see God's arm, so it looked south. God's answer was costly. He made His prophet a public spectacle so His people would stop relying on what would fail them. God still loves His people too much to let them rest their weight on something that will collapse, and He may strip away a false confidence so that our hope comes back to Him.

Cush also shows the reach of God's grace. The psalm writer could have chosen nearby, friendly peoples to illustrate God's welcome. Instead he named Rahab (Egypt), Babylon, Philistia, Tyre, and Cush, and he heard God say of each, "This one was born there." Belonging to God's people does not depend on where we started. It depends on God, who registers His children by grace.

That grace is personal. The Bible's Cushites are not only armies and envoys. One is Ebed-Melech, who risked his standing to plead for a prophet left to die in a dungeon and then, with the king's permission and thirty men, drew him out and whom God noticed and protected because he trusted in the LORD. Another is a royal treasurer on a desert road, holding a scroll he could not understand, to whom God sent a messenger. God, who rules the nations, also sees one person reading Scripture in a chariot. The worship Zephaniah foresaw "from beyond the rivers of Ethiopia" finds an early illustration in that man going home rejoicing, a canonical anticipation rather than a fulfillment Acts itself announces.
