THUS, SO, IN THIS MANNER - (kâkâh, Strong's H3602)

Summary

The Hebrew word kâkâh is a small pointing word meaning thus, so, in this manner or such. It points to something specific: a way of acting, a condition, a spoken plan, or a set of events. The word has no theological content of its own. Its weight comes from what it points to, and it points to some remarkable things. God tells Israel exactly how to eat the first Passover, dressed and ready to leave Egypt. A prophet shatters a clay jar and announces that Jerusalem will be broken "even so." A psalm describes a nation's flourishing and calls "the people who are in such a state" happy.

Because kâkâh ties a visible reality to its meaning, it often marks the place where God makes His ways concrete. His salvation calls for faith that is ready to move. His judgment fits the sin it answers. His blessing on a people traces back to belonging to Him. Even a bride's urgent search for her beloved, described with this word, leads others to ask what makes him worth seeking. A humble adverb becomes a finger pointing to how God acts and how His people respond.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kâkâh is a Hebrew adverb, a longer form related to the very common word koh (H3541), which also means thus. Its exact formation is uncertain; one proposal is that it doubles koh. Both words point. They answer the question "how?" or "in what way?" by directing attention to something just described or about to be described.

The word occurs 37 times in 35 verses of the Hebrew Bible. They are spread across the Law (including Exodus and Numbers), the historical books (including 2 Chronicles, Nehemiah and Esther), the poetic books (including Psalms and the Song of Solomon), and the prophets (including Jeremiah and Hosea). The NKJV renders it according to context with words such as "thus," "so," "manner," and "state."
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Kâkâh means thus or so, pointing to a particular way or condition. Context determines what kind of thing it points to:

1. The manner of an action: in this way. "And thus you shall eat it" (Exodus 12:11) introduces the exact way the Passover must be eaten.

2. A condition or state of affairs: such. "Happy are the people who are in such a state" (Psalm 144:15) looks back over a described scene of prosperity.

In Esther 9:26 the same pointing function works inside the expression the NKJV renders "concerning this matter." The days were named Purim after Pur, the lot Haman had cast (Esther 9:24, 26). The word points instead to what the Jews had seen and what had happened to them, which, with Mordecai's letter, are given as reasons they committed themselves to keep the days every year (Esther 9:26–27). This is a setting for the word's usual pointing sense, not a separate meaning.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses fall into a few settings. In instructions, kâkâh introduces how something must be done. Exodus 12:11 governs the Passover meal, and Numbers 15:12 requires that the grain and drink offerings set for each kind of animal, in quantities that differ from kind to kind, be repeated for every animal of that kind brought.

In description, the word sums up a scene. Esther 9:26 gathers up the events of Haman's defeat, and Psalm 144:15 sums up the flourishing of a blessed people. In 2 Chronicles 18:19 the word appears twice, in two parallel phrases: "one spoke in this manner, and another spoke in that manner." The paired phrases picture different voices offering different plans.

In prophecy, kâkâh links a picture of destruction with the judgment that will follow. In "Even so I will break this people" (Jeremiah 19:11), the word points to the jar Jeremiah has just smashed: what happened to the jar will happen to the city. In "Thus it shall be done to you, O Bethel, Because of your great wickedness" (Hosea 10:15), the word points back to the scene in the verse before, where Israel's fortresses will be plundered "As Shalman plundered Beth Arbel in the day of battle" (Hosea 10:14). Bethel will suffer that kind of devastation, and its wickedness is named as the reason.

4. Theological Significance

Because this word points to the concrete, its uses show that God does not deal in vague generalities. He tells His people how to eat, how to offer, and how judgment will come. The Lord who rescued Israel cared about sandals, belts, and staffs, and the way His people ate that night expressed their readiness for Him to bring them out.

The prophetic uses show that God's justice is measured. Judgment in Jeremiah and Hosea answers specific, named sins: idolatry in the Valley of Hinnom and corruption at Bethel. Sin is not punished at random, and it is not ignored. Paul states the same principle in Galatians 6:7: what a person sows, he reaps.

The psalm shows the other side of God's dealings. When the psalmist calls such a state of peace and plenty happy, he immediately names its source in the Lord Himself. Blessing, like judgment, comes from Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 12:11: eating ready to leave. God's Passover instructions cover the lamb, the blood on the doorposts, and the roasted meat with unleavened bread and bitter herbs. Then kâkâh introduces the manner of eating: "And thus you shall eat it: with a belt on your waist, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand. So you shall eat it in haste." No one eats a leisurely dinner in traveling clothes. Israel was to eat as people ready to leave at a moment's notice, even though no one was to go out the door of his house until morning (Exodus 12:22). They had not yet seen the deliverance, but their clothing expressed readiness for it. Hebrews 11:28 calls Moses' keeping of the Passover an act of faith: "By faith he kept the Passover." Faith that night had a belt and sandals on.

Jeremiah 19:11 and Hosea 10:15: judgment that fits. God sends Jeremiah to buy "a potter's earthen flask" (Jeremiah 19:1), take elders to the Valley of Hinnom, and smash the flask before them. Then comes the word: "Even so I will break this people and this city, as one breaks a potter's vessel, which cannot be made whole again." The detail "cannot be made whole again" matters. Just one chapter earlier, Jeremiah watched a potter remake soft clay into a new vessel (Jeremiah 18). Clay that has been fired and shattered cannot be remade that way. In Jeremiah 18 the Lord had called the nation to turn from its evil way, and the people answered that they would follow their own plans (Jeremiah 18:7–12). Chapter 19 then announces the coming judgment: the people and the city would be broken like the flask (Jeremiah 19:10–11). The jar's pieces on the ground made that judgment visible. Hosea's word to Bethel is just as direct, though it works differently. Hosea performs no sign like the broken jar. His "Thus" points back to the battle scene of the previous verse, where Beth Arbel was plundered and a mother was dashed in pieces upon her children (Hosea 10:14). Then he says, "Thus it shall be done to you, O Bethel, Because of your great wickedness." Bethel means house of God, yet Israel had turned it into a center of calf worship. Bethel's great wickedness is the stated reason for the devastation that would come on it.

Psalm 144:15: a happy people and their God. The closing verses of the psalm picture sons like well-grown plants, daughters like carved corner pillars, full storehouses, multiplying flocks, and streets with no outcry (Psalm 144:12–14). The psalmist then says, "Happy are the people who are in such a state; Happy are the people whose God is the LORD!" The two lines belong together. The first looks at the gifts, and the second names the Giver. The verse does not let the reader admire the harvest and forget the Lord who gave it.

2 Chronicles 18:19: the heavenly council. The prophet Micaiah describes seeing the Lord on His throne with the host of heaven around Him. The Lord asks who will persuade King Ahab to go up and fall at Ramoth Gilead, and "one spoke in this manner, and another spoke in that manner." The outcome had already been settled by God. The question was only how it would come about. A spirit offers to be a lying spirit in the mouths of Ahab's prophets, and the Lord sends it (2 Chronicles 18:20–22). The scene is unusual and should not be stretched beyond what it says. It does show the Lord fully in command, judging a king who hated the prophet who told him the truth (2 Chronicles 18:7), and doing so through agents who serve His decree. It also shows a measure of mercy: Micaiah told Ahab the whole vision. The king was warned and went anyway.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Passover posture looks forward to Christ, whom Paul calls "our Passover" sacrificed for us (1 Corinthians 5:7). Israel ate the lamb ready to leave Egypt. Believers live ready for the final deliverance. Peter echoes the Exodus image when he writes, "Therefore gird up the loins of your mind, be sober, and rest your hope fully upon the grace that is to be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 1:13). Jesus Himself told His disciples to keep their waists girded and their lamps burning as servants waiting for their master (Luke 12:35–36).

The broken jar of Jeremiah 19 pictures a judgment that no potter could undo. The gospel answers that hopelessness with something no potter can do. In Christ, God makes the broken new, because Jesus bore the judgment that sin deserved. The exact justice that Hosea and Jeremiah announced did not disappear. At the cross it fell on Him.

Psalm 144's "people whose God is the LORD" finds its fullest meaning in those who belong to God through His Son. The happiness the psalm describes rests finally on the relationship it names.

7. Spirit & Life

Song of Solomon 5:9 gives one of this word's most searching uses. The bride has searched the city at night for her beloved, been beaten by the watchmen, and still begs the daughters of Jerusalem to tell him she is lovesick. They answer, "What is your beloved more than another beloved, that you so charge us?" The intensity of her plea is what makes them ask. Her answer in the following verses is a long description of his beauty (Song of Solomon 5:10–16), and by the next chapter they want to seek him with her (Song of Solomon 6:1). Applied to Christ and His people, this is an analogy, not the poem's direct statement, but the pattern fits the rest of Scripture. Peter tells believers to be ready to give a reason for their hope (1 Peter 3:15), which assumes that others have seen something worth asking about. Earnest love for Christ often raises the question before any argument is made.

Exodus 12:11 asks a similar question of every believer. If someone looked at how you live, would they see a person expecting the Lord to act? Readiness does not mean anxiety. It means holding the things of this world loosely enough to leave when He calls.

Psalm 144:15 guards the heart in seasons of plenty. God's good gifts are meant to direct our thanks to Him, and the gifts themselves should not take His place.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 15:12: Each offering of a young bull, ram, lamb, or young goat was to be prepared "so": the grain and drink offerings prescribed for its kind, in quantities that differ from kind to kind, were repeated for each animal brought (Numbers 15:11–12). God's worship was ordered and fair for every worshiper.

  • Esther 9:26: The days were named Purim after Pur, the lot Haman cast. Because of Mordecai's letter and all the Jews had seen "concerning this matter," they committed themselves to keep the days every year. Here the word points to what they had seen and experienced. The rescue from Haman's plot was remembered as a concrete deliverance, kept alive through a yearly feast.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3541 (koh), thus, so, here. A lexical relative: kâkâh is a longer form connected to koh. Jeremiah 19:11 uses both, since the prophet's "Thus says the LORD" introduces the "Even so" of judgment.
