AWESOME, TERRIBLE - (ʼâyôm, Strong's H0366)

Summary

The Hebrew adjective ʼâyôm describes something so overwhelming that it stops a person in their tracks. English translations render it "terrible" or "awesome." The word occurs 3 times in 3 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and the settings could hardly be further apart. Habakkuk uses it for the Babylonian army sweeping toward Judah as God's instrument of judgment. The Song of Solomon uses it twice for the bride, who is "awesome as an army with banners." The same word that names the terror of an invading empire also names the arresting majesty of a beloved woman.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

That pairing is the heart of the study. Babylon is terrible because its power answers to no one: "their judgment and their dignity proceed from themselves." The bride is awesome because she is wholly devoted to her king, and her gaze overcomes him. Read with the rest of Scripture, these two uses set two kinds of overwhelming presence side by side. One is the self-made power of a proud empire. The other is the radiant beauty of the people Christ loves and makes holy.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼâyôm is a Hebrew adjective related to ʼêymâh, a common word for terror or dread. The family resemblance points to the word's basic sense: something that inspires fear or overwhelming awe. Because the word is so rare, its meaning comes into focus mainly through its two very different settings: a prophet's complaint about coming judgment and a love poem celebrating the bride.

Two place names in Song of Solomon 6:4 help the reader. Tirzah and Jerusalem were both royal cities. Tirzah later served as an early capital of the northern kingdom (1 Kings 15–16), and Jerusalem was the city of David. Comparing the bride to them honors her with the splendor of a capital. The poem then moves from cities to an army.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʼâyôm means "terrible," "dreadful," or "awe-inspiring." It describes a presence powerful enough to overwhelm the person who meets it. Context decides whether that overwhelming effect is fear or wonder.

1. Terrifying or dreadful, describing a threatening power that produces fear. In Habakkuk 1:7 the Chaldeans "are terrible and dreadful."

2. Awe-inspiring or majestic, describing a presence so splendid that it overpowers the beholder. In Song of Solomon 6:4, 10 the bride is "Awesome as an army with banners."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word does not mean merely "very bad" or "very pretty." In both settings it describes an effect on the observer: the onlooker is mastered by what he sees. What changes is the subject being described.

In Habakkuk, ʼâyôm stands beside "dreadful," and the two words together stress the fear the Babylonians spread. In the Song, it stands beside "beautiful," "lovely," and "fair," and the comparison "as an army with banners" supplies its force. An army marching under raised standards was the most imposing sight an ancient observer could see. The bride's beauty has that same commanding effect. The comparison does not make her violent. It makes her impossible to look at casually.

4. Theological Significance

The three uses together show that God's world contains two very different kinds of overwhelming power, and Scripture judges them by their source.

Babylon's terror comes from autonomy. The Chaldeans recognize no law above their own will, and the Lord, who raised them up (Habakkuk 1:6), also pronounces them guilty for treating their own strength as their god (1:11). God can use proud power to discipline His people without approving that pride. Its self-exaltation already marks it for judgment. Habakkuk's answer in the next chapter sets the person whose soul is "lifted up" against the just who "shall live by his faith" (2:4).

The bride's awe comes from the opposite direction. Nothing in Song of Solomon 6 suggests she has seized power for herself. Her majesty is the splendor of being loved and of loving in return. These passages do not treat beauty as trivial, and holy beauty carries an authority that brute force cannot imitate.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Habakkuk 1:7 — the terror of self-made power. Habakkuk has asked why God tolerates violence in Judah. God's answer shocks him: He is raising up the Chaldeans. The Lord's description of them is unsparing: "They are terrible and dreadful; Their judgment and their dignity proceed from themselves." The second line explains the first. Babylon is fearsome because it is its own court of appeal. It decides what is just and grants itself its own honor. Power with no one above it has no reason to restrain itself. That explains why the surrounding verses describe Babylon as swift, scornful, and sweeping like the wind. Human pride, armed and unaccountable, is one of the most frightening things on earth.

Song of Solomon 6:4–5 — majesty that overcomes the king. The bridegroom praises his beloved in rising order: "beautiful as Tirzah, Lovely as Jerusalem, Awesome as an army with banners!" The comparisons climb from the grace of royal cities to the commanding sight of an assembled host. The next words show who is conquered: "Turn your eyes away from me, For they have overcome me" (Song of Solomon 6:5). The army image does not describe her defeating enemies. It describes her undoing him. The same thought appears earlier: "You have ravished my heart With one look of your eyes" (Song of Solomon 4:9). Her awesome quality is bound up with the focused devotion of her gaze.

Song of Solomon 6:10 — radiance as well as strength. The second use comes in a question others ask as they watch her: "Who is she who looks forth as the morning, Fair as the moon, Clear as the sun, Awesome as an army with banners?" Dawn, moon, and sun prepare for the final line. Her majesty is luminous. Like the sun, she is bright and clear, and what overwhelms observers is light rather than threat. Placed beside Habakkuk 1:7, the contrast is sharp. Babylon's dignity proceeds from itself. The bride's splendor is seen and celebrated by others, and it is praised first by the one who loves her.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Christians have long read the Song of Solomon as a picture of the love between the Lord and His people, completed in Christ and the church. The Song itself does not say this. The New Testament, however, uses marriage as its central image for Christ and the church, and that makes the reading fruitful. Paul says Christ gave Himself for the church "that He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing, but that she should be holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27). The bride's beauty comes from her bridegroom's work.

Revelation brings the picture to its end: "the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready." Her clothing is granted to her, "fine linen, clean and bright, for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints" (Revelation 19:7–8). A few verses later the armies of heaven follow Christ, likewise clothed in fine white linen (Revelation 19:14). In Revelation the bride and the army wear the same garment. That echoes the Song's joining of bride and bannered host, and it is a strong resonance even though Revelation does not quote the Song.

The two subjects of ʼâyôm also meet in Revelation's larger structure. Revelation sets the great city Babylon, proud and self-glorifying, against the bride of the Lamb (Revelation 17–19). Habakkuk's terrible Babylon and the Song's awesome bride point toward that final contrast. The self-made empire falls, and the bride who received her beauty is presented in glory.

7. Spirit & Life

These three verses ask a searching question: where does our strength come from? Habakkuk says of the Chaldeans, "Their judgment and their dignity proceed from themselves" (Habakkuk 1:7), a line that describes a self-made life. Whether in an empire or a person, power that answers to no one eventually becomes dreadful to others and ruinous to itself. Habakkuk shows that God is not impressed by it and will judge it.

The church's strength runs the other way. The bride does not become awesome by making herself formidable. Her majesty grows out of the king's love and her answering devotion. The verse that calls her "awesome as an army" is followed immediately by the king saying her eyes have overcome him. Scripture dares to say that the Lord is moved by the undivided love of His people. Their single-hearted attention is precious to Him.

The order in Revelation matters here. The bride is clothed with fine linen first, and only afterward do the white-robed armies ride out. Faithful witness and holy courage grow out of being loved, cleansed, and clothed by Christ. A church that seeks to look powerful by worldly measures resembles Babylon. A church that turns its eyes fully toward its Lord carries a different kind of weight, the clear radiance of the bride who "looks forth as the morning."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

ʼâyôm occurs only in Habakkuk 1:7 and Song of Solomon 6:4, 10, and all three have been discussed above. The closest supporting verse is Song of Solomon 4:9, "You have ravished my heart... With one look of your eyes," which does not use ʼâyôm. It shows that the overwhelming effect of the bride's gaze runs through the Song.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H0367 (ʼêymâh), lexical relative:** a related noun meaning terror or dread, sharing the sense of overwhelming fear behind ʼâyôm.

  • **H3372 (yârêʼ), canonical pairing:** the verb "to fear." Its form nôrâʼ, rendered "dreadful," stands beside ʼâyôm in Habakkuk 1:7 ("terrible and dreadful"), and the pairing strengthens the picture of fear.
