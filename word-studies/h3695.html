CASLUHIM - (Kaçluchîym, Strong's H3695)

Summary

Casluhim is the name of an ancient people listed among the descendants of Mizraim, the Hebrew name for Egypt, in the family tree of nations that grew from Noah's sons after the flood. The name appears only twice in the Bible, in Genesis 10:14 and in the repeated genealogy of 1 Chronicles 1:12. The Casluhim never appear in a story, a prophecy, or a psalm. What makes them matter is the short note attached to their name: they are the people "from whom came the Philistines."

That single note carries real weight. Long before Goliath, Delilah, or the Philistine wars of Saul and David, Scripture has already recorded where the Philistines came from. It places them in the same branch of the human family as Egypt, which means Israel's first great oppressor and its most stubborn neighbor-enemy in the land grow from the same branch. For readers who are tempted to skip genealogies, the Casluhim show that God knows the origins of every nation, including the ones that will later trouble His people, and that none of them stands outside His rule.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kaçluchîym is a Hebrew proper noun, the name of a people group. It has the ordinary Hebrew plural ending -im, which also appears in the neighboring names Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, and Naphtuhim (Genesis 10:13). Names like these mark a people descended from or associated with an ancestor. They do not name a single individual.

Both occurrences fall in the "Table of Nations," the record that opens, "Now this is the genealogy of the sons of Noah: Shem, Ham, and Japheth. And sons were born to them after the flood" (Genesis 10:1). The Casluhim belong to Ham's line through Mizraim (Genesis 10:6, 13). Their exact homeland is not known with certainty. Their connection to Egypt and to the Caphtorim points toward the region of Egypt and the eastern Mediterranean coast, but Scripture does not locate them more precisely, and the derivation of the name itself is uncertain.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Casluhim has one meaning: the name of a people descended from Mizraim (Egypt), identified in Scripture as the people from whom the Philistines came. It is a name, so it has no dictionary sense beyond identifying this group. Its significance comes from where it stands in the genealogy and from the parenthetical note attached to it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The two uses are functionally identical. Genesis 10:14 reads, "Pathrusim, and Casluhim (from whom came the Philistines and Caphtorim)." First Chronicles 1:12 repeats the line almost word for word: "Pathrusim, Casluhim (from whom came the Philistines and the Caphtorim)."

The parenthesis is the important feature. Genealogies usually move from one name to the next without comment. Here the writer stops to tell the reader where a later and well-known people came from. The Casluhim themselves then pass out of the record, while the Philistines go on to fill whole chapters of Judges and Samuel. The name works as a bridge between Egypt and Philistia.

4. Theological Significance

The Table of Nations treats the whole human family as God's concern. Genesis 10 lists Japheth's coastland peoples "separated into their lands, everyone according to his language, according to their families, into their nations" (Genesis 10:5), and then Ham's and Shem's descendants with the same care. The Casluhim are a minor name in that record, yet the writer still accounts for them. God's attention is not limited to the line that will lead to Abraham.

That attention includes Israel's future enemies. By the time the Philistines hold the coastal plain and oppress Israel in the days of Samson and Saul, Scripture has already recorded their origin. Paul told the Athenians that God made every nation of mankind from one blood and determined their appointed times and the boundaries where they would live (Acts 17:26, paraphrased). The note about the Casluhim is a small, concrete case of that truth. The Philistines did not appear out of nowhere, and they were never beyond the knowledge or rule of the God of Israel.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:13–14: Egypt's branch and the Philistines. The Casluhim are listed as sons of Mizraim, alongside Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim, and Pathrusim. That places the Philistines, by way of the Casluhim, in the same branch of the family as Egypt. Israel's history bears out how close these two powers were. Egypt enslaved Israel in its infancy as a nation and required the Exodus to break its grip. When Israel left Egypt, God deliberately did not lead them by the short road through Philistine territory, because they were not ready to face war (Exodus 13:17). Egypt and Philistia were neighbors on the map before they were successive oppressors in Israel's history. Genesis 10 shows their family connection before either one has lifted a hand against Israel. The God who broke Egypt's power at the Red Sea is the same God who would later give victory over the Philistines through Samuel at Mizpah and through David in the Valley of Elah. Israel's oppressors are named in the same family tree, and so is the reach of God's deliverance.

1 Chronicles 1:12: the same name for a people returning from exile. Chronicles opens with nine chapters of genealogy, beginning with Adam and moving through the nations before narrowing to Israel. Its first readers were a small community resettling a ruined land after the Babylonian exile, surrounded by stronger peoples and with no king. For them, hearing again that the Philistines came from the Casluhim, and the Casluhim from Mizraim, placed the nations that had troubled them for centuries back where they belonged: as branches of the human family, listed and ordered under the God who made them all. The Chronicler begins Israel's story with the whole world because Israel's God is Lord of the whole world.

Amos 9:7 and Jeremiah 47:4: Casluhim and Caphtor. The prophets connect the Philistines with Caphtor. Through Amos the LORD asks, "Did I not bring up Israel from the land of Egypt, The Philistines from Caphtor, And the Syrians from Kir?" (Amos 9:7). Jeremiah calls the Philistines "The remnant of the country of Caphtor" (Jeremiah 47:4). Genesis traces the Philistines' descent through the Casluhim and names the Caphtorim alongside them. Amos and Jeremiah name Caphtor as the land the Philistines came from. One possible way to fit these together is that Genesis gives the family line and the prophets give the place of departure, since a group can descend from one family and still be remembered as coming from another region. The texts do not narrate a Casluhim migration through Caphtor, so the full historical relationship is not settled. Amos 9:7 also makes a pointed claim. The LORD who brought Israel out of Egypt also directed the Philistines' migration. Israel's enemies were moved across the map by the same hand that redeemed Israel.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Genesis 10 stands just before the call of Abraham, where God promises that "all the families of the earth" will be blessed in him (Genesis 12:3). The families just listed, Casluhim and Philistines among them, are the families that promise has in view. God records the nations first, then begins the work through Abraham that will reach them.

The prophets carry that purpose forward with Philistia specifically. Psalm 87:4 pictures the LORD writing Philistia, along with Tyre and Cush, into the register of those born in Zion. Zechariah 9:7 foresees a remnant of the Philistines belonging to God and becoming like a clan in Judah. The people descended from the Casluhim, Israel's longtime enemy, are named among those God intends to bring home.

The New Testament shows the gospel beginning to reach that very coastline. An angel sends Philip down the road to Gaza, an old Philistine city, where an Ethiopian official believes in Jesus and is baptized; Philip is then found at Azotus, the former Ashdod, preaching in all the cities as he went (Acts 8:26–40). Christ's gospel reaches the old Philistine territory and the descendants of Ham alike. The story that began with names on a list moves toward the scene of Revelation 7:9, a multitude from every nation, tribe, people, and tongue standing before the throne and before the Lamb.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers skim names like Casluhim, and it is easy to see why. Yet this name holds out something a struggling believer needs. The Philistines would one day field Goliath, capture the ark, and press the battle on Mount Gilboa that ended in Saul's death. Before any of that, God had them written down, with their family line traced back to its source. Whatever opposes God's people has an origin God already knows and a place in a history He governs.

The returning exiles needed exactly this, and so do we. When pressure comes from the same direction again and again, it can feel as though the opposition has no end. The genealogy tells a different story. Egypt and Philistia shared a family tree, and the God of Israel overcame both. Believers can face recurring opposition with confidence because the Lord who delivered them before is the Lord over what comes next, even when His deliverance does not come as quickly or in the form they expect.

The larger story adds a second, quieter lesson. God's purpose for the Philistines included judgment, but Psalm 87 and Zechariah 9 show it included mercy as well. The people we most readily see as enemies are also named on God's register of nations, and the gospel has already traveled the Gaza road. Knowing that should affect how we pray for those who oppose us.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Casluhim occurs only in Genesis 10:14 and 1 Chronicles 1:12, and both occurrences have been discussed above. The related passages in Amos 9:7 and Jeremiah 47:4 speak of Caphtor and the Philistines without using this word, and are treated in Section 5.
