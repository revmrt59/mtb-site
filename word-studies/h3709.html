PALM, HAND, SOLE - (kaph, Strong's H3709)

Summary

The Hebrew word kaph names the flat or hollowed part of something. Most often it means the palm of the hand or the sole of the foot. The same shape explains its other uses: the socket of a hip joint, a handful of flour, an animal's paw, and the small gold pans that held incense in the tabernacle and temple. The NKJV usually translates it "hand" or "hands," but the word describes the open, cupped surface of the hand: the part that receives, holds, and touches.

Scripture uses this ordinary word at important moments. Worshipers spread their palms toward heaven in prayer, and God refuses those same palms when they are full of blood. Israel had to plant the soles of their feet on the land God had already given them. God touched the socket of Jacob's hip and broke his strength. Most remarkably, God tells grieving Zion, "See, I have inscribed you on the palms of My hands" (Isaiah 49:16). That promise points to the risen Christ, who showed His disciples the marks in His hands. The word moves from our open hands reaching toward God to God's marked hands holding His people.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kaph is a common Hebrew noun found throughout the Old Testament, from Genesis to Malachi. It is used most heavily in two kinds of writing. Tabernacle and temple records use it for gold utensils, and Numbers 7 alone lists twelve tribal leaders, each bringing a gold "pan" full of incense. Poetry and prophecy use it for the hands of people praying, working, and sinning.

Hebrew has a more general word for "hand," yad, which often stands for strength or control. Kaph draws attention to the palm itself: open, cupped, or flat. That difference helps explain why kaph shows up so naturally when Scripture describes prayer, holding, and the steps of a person's feet.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word's basic idea is a hollow or flat surface. Its main senses are:

1. The palm of the hand, often simply translated "hand" or "hands": "He who has clean hands and a pure heart" (Psalm 24:4).

2. The sole of the foot, the part that touches the ground (Genesis 8:9; Joshua 1:3).

3. A hollow or cupped object or measure, including the socket of Jacob's hip (Genesis 32:25), a "handful" (Ecclesiastes 4:6), and the sanctuary's pans, spoons, and ladles (Numbers 7:14; 1 Kings 7:50). In one passage it describes the broad branches of palm trees (Leviticus 23:40).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of kaph in a passage often depends on what the palm is doing.

  • Spreading the palms describes prayer. Moses spreads his hands to the Lord so the hail will stop (Exodus 9:29, 33). Solomon spreads his hands toward heaven at the temple dedication (1 Kings 8:22, 54).

  • Striking the palms together can express several different things. In 2 Kings 11:12 the people "clapped their hands and said, 'Long live the king!'" Psalm 47:1 calls the nations to clap in joy. In Lamentations 2:15, those passing by clap in mockery over Jerusalem. In Ezekiel 21:14, God tells the prophet, "strike your hands together," as a sign of the sword coming in judgment. A few verses later, God says He will strike His own palms together and bring His fury to rest (Ezekiel 21:17).

  • Filling the palms belongs to the priests' ordination. Portions of the offering were placed in the palms of Aaron and his sons, and they waved them before the Lord (Exodus 29:24; Leviticus 8:27).

The same hand can be open in prayer, closed in a fist, or clapping. Each gesture shows a different heart, and the context makes that clear.

4. Theological Significance

The spread palm is the Bible's picture of a person coming to God with nothing in hand and everything to ask. David connects this posture directly to worship: "Let my prayer be set before You as incense, the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice" (Psalm 141:2). Lifted hands and rising incense belong together. The same word names both the praying palm and the gold pans that carried incense into God's presence. That link should not be pushed too far. Even so, the sanctuary's utensils and the worshiper's hands served the same purpose before God: holding something up to Him.

God also looks at what those hands have been doing. Psalm 24:4 asks who may climb the Lord's hill and answers, "He who has clean hands and a pure heart." David says, "I will wash my hands in innocence; so I will go about Your altar, O LORD" (Psalm 26:6). Job defends himself by saying, "no violence is in my hands, and my prayer is pure" (Job 16:17). The other side appears in Isaiah 1:15. When Judah spreads out its palms, God hides His eyes and will not listen, because their hands are full of blood. Isaiah 59:3, 6 repeats the charge of defiled and violent palms. The palm that reaches up to God on the Sabbath is the same palm that took a bribe on Tuesday, and God does not separate the two. Prayer that leaves behavior unchanged is gesture without substance.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 49:16: inscribed on God's palms. Zion has said, "The LORD has forsaken me" (Isaiah 49:14, paraphrased). God first answers that a nursing mother may forget her child, but He will not forget Zion. Then He goes further: "See, I have inscribed you on the palms of My hands; Your walls are continually before Me." A worker's palms are always in front of his eyes while he works. God says His people are engraved there, so He cannot put His hand to anything without seeing them. Zion felt forgotten. God answers that she is permanently marked on the very part of Him that acts.

The sole of the foot: rest, inheritance, and exile. After the flood, "the dove found no resting place for the sole of her foot" (Genesis 8:9), so she returned to the ark. God later promised Israel that every place the sole of their foot touched would be theirs (Deuteronomy 11:24; Joshua 1:3). God gave the land as a gift, but His people still had to walk onto it. At the Jordan, the waters were cut off only when the soles of the priests' feet, as they carried the ark, rested in the river (Joshua 3:13; 4:18). Deuteronomy 28:65 warns of the reverse. If Israel abandoned the Lord, then in exile "nor shall the sole of your foot have a resting place." The disobedient nation would become like the dove over the floodwaters, finding nowhere to land.

Genesis 32:25, 32: the socket of Jacob's hip. Jacob had spent his life gaining advantage through his own cleverness. At Peniel, the Man wrestling with him touched the socket of his hip and put it out of joint. Jacob's strength was gone. Yet he would not let go until he received a blessing (Genesis 32:26). God weakened the joint that gave Jacob his leverage, and the man who once fought to win was left holding on to God. Israel remembered that night by not eating the muscle of the hip socket (32:32).

Ezekiel 43:7: the soles of God's feet. Ezekiel had watched God's glory leave the temple. In his vision of restoration, the glory returns, and God says, "this is the place of My throne and the place of the soles of My feet, where I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel forever." Heaven is God's throne and the earth His footstool (Isaiah 66:1). Even so, after rebellion and exile, God chooses to set His feet down among His people for good.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

After the resurrection, Jesus "showed them His hands and His side. Then the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord" (John 20:20). He later invited Thomas to touch the marks (John 20:27). John uses a Greek word for "hand," so this is not a recurrence of kaph. The connection with Isaiah 49:16 is a theological echo, not a quoted fulfillment, but it is a fitting one. God had promised that His people were inscribed on His palms. In Christ, the hands of God carry real marks made by the nails, received while He accomplished salvation. John sees the same Lamb in heaven "as though it had been slain" (Revelation 5:6). His wounds remain His identification.

The other images in this study come to rest in Christ as well. The incense of lifted hands (Psalm 141:2) appears again in heaven's "golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints" (Revelation 5:8). The restless sole of the exile finds its answer in the One who invites the weary to come to Him for rest (Matthew 11:28). The God who promised to set the soles of His feet among His people forever (Ezekiel 43:7) came and lived among them in the Word made flesh (John 1:14). Scripture's final vision declares that God's dwelling is with people (Revelation 21:3).

7. Spirit & Life

The open palm teaches a person how to come to God. You cannot grasp and receive with the same hand. Spreading the palms means admitting that what you need is not in your grip. The closed fist of Ezekiel 21 shows the opposite: a hand set on striking or holding on. When we pray, our hands are meant to be emptied of our own schemes and filled with God's answer.

Clean hands are equally necessary. God does not wait for perfect people, since David's prayer to wash his hands in innocence assumes a need for washing. But He will not let lifted hands cover up dirty ones. Before asking God to fill our palms, it is right to ask what they have been holding during the week.

Jacob's limp carries its own lesson. Sometimes God touches the very place we depend on most, the strength or skill we lean on. That touch feels like loss, yet it can be the moment a person finally holds on to God and asks to be blessed.

Under all of this lies Isaiah 49:16. The believer's confidence does not rest on the strength of his own grip. It rests on being engraved on God's palms, marks the risen Christ still bears. When it feels as if God has forgotten, His hands answer otherwise.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ecclesiastes 4:6: "Better a handful with quietness than both hands full, together with toil and grasping for the wind." One palm held in peace is better than two clutched in endless striving.

  • 1 Kings 17:12: The widow of Zarephath has "only a handful of flour in a bin." Her single handful is the point where God's supply begins.

  • 1 Kings 18:44: After Elijah prays seven times, his servant sees "a cloud, as small as a man's hand." God's answer to a long drought begins with something no bigger than a palm.

  • Leviticus 14:15: The priest pours oil "into the palm of his own left hand" to anoint a cleansed leper. Here his own palm serves as a holy vessel for the work of restoration.

  • Psalm 91:12: God's angels will bear the faithful up in their hands so they do not strike a foot against a stone. Satan quoted this promise to Jesus in the wilderness (Matthew 4:6).

  • Proverbs 31:20: The worthy woman extends her hand to the poor. Her open palm gives to others as well as receives.
