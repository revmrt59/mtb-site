RANSOM, PITCH, BRIBE, HENNA - (kôpher, Strong's H3724)

Summary

The Hebrew word kôpher most often means a ransom: the price paid so that a life under sentence may go free. Scripture also uses it for the pitch that sealed Noah's ark, for country villages, for the fragrant henna plant, and, in its darkest use, for a bribe that blinds a judge. The ransom passages carry the weight of the word. In ordinary life a ransom could settle a real liability, as when an ox owner paid "to redeem his life" (Exodus 21:30). Before God, Israel learned that every person needed a ransom "that there may be no plague among them" (Exodus 30:12), and that the rich man and the poor man paid exactly the same.

The Old Testament then presses an urgent question. If every life needs a ransom, who can pay it? The Law refused any ransom for a murderer. The psalmist declared that no one can "give to God a ransom" for his brother (Psalm 49:7). Elihu warned that "a large ransom" cannot turn aside God's wrath (Job 36:18). Yet the same book of Job records God Himself saying, "I have found a ransom" (Job 33:24). That tension is resolved when Jesus says He came "to give His life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). God does not accept a bribe to overlook sin. He supplies the true ransom Himself, at the cost of His Son.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kôpher is a Hebrew noun found in the Law, the historical books, Job, Psalms, Proverbs, the Song of Solomon, Isaiah, and Amos. Strong's dictionary groups its uses under the idea of "a cover": pitch coats a surface, henna was used as a dye, and a ransom covers a debt or offense. The link between the concrete senses and the ransom sense is not certain, especially in the case of henna, and some dictionaries treat these uses as separate words that happen to sound alike. For that reason the ransom meaning should be learned from its own passages and not from the picture of something laid over a surface.

The ransom passages share a setting. Someone's life has been forfeited by law, by guilt, or by danger, and a payment stands between that person and death.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Ransom or life-price. This is a payment that releases a person whose life is legally forfeited or under threat. The NKJV usually translates it "ransom," and also "money" (Exodus 21:30) and "recompense" (Proverbs 6:35).

2. Bribe. This is a payment offered to a judge or leader so that he overlooks wrongdoing or denies justice (1 Samuel 12:3; Amos 5:12). It is the ransom idea corrupted. Money that should satisfy justice is used to defeat it.

3. A physical substance or place. The word names the pitch that waterproofed the ark (Genesis 6:14), the henna plant (Song of Solomon 1:14; 4:13), and unwalled "country villages" (1 Samuel 6:18).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word changes with the person who receives the payment. When an injured family receives it under God's law, it is a legitimate settlement. When God is the One whose justice must be satisfied, Scripture repeatedly says that no human payment is enough. When a judge receives it, the payment becomes a bribe.

Samuel's farewell speech shows this last use. He asks Israel, "from whose hand have I received any bribe with which to blind my eyes?" (1 Samuel 12:3). The bribe does not cover the offender's guilt. It covers the judge's eyes so that he cannot see the truth. Samuel's clean record stood against the leaders who later took bribes.

The concrete uses appear in their own settings. Genesis uses the word for construction, the Song of Solomon for gardens and fragrance, and 1 Samuel for a list of settlements. In 1 Samuel 6:18 the "country villages" stand beside "fortified cities," which suggests small communities without walls of their own.

4. Theological Significance

The ransom passages teach first that sin and bloodguilt are not small matters that can be smoothed over. A life can truly be forfeited, and God takes that forfeiture seriously enough to refuse a payoff. His justice cannot be bought. This is exactly what separates Him from the corrupt judges Amos condemned.

The same passages also show God as the One who provides what His people cannot. He gives nations "in your place" (Isaiah 43:3), and He finds the ransom for a dying man (Job 33:24). Scripture holds both truths together: God's justice is never set aside, and His mercy finds a way to satisfy that justice. The difference between a ransom and a bribe is moral. A ransom honors what is right. A bribe destroys it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 30:11–16 — the census ransom. When Israel was counted, "every man shall give a ransom for himself to the LORD... that there may be no plague among them." To be numbered was to stand before the Lord as someone who belonged to Him, and no one could do that without a ransom. The amount was fixed: "The rich shall not give more and the poor shall not give less than half a shekel." Wealth bought no advantage, and poverty brought no exclusion. Every life stood at the same value and in the same need. The silver supported the tabernacle and served as "a memorial for the children of Israel before the LORD, to make atonement for yourselves" (v. 16). Every time Israel saw the tabernacle's service, they were reminded that each of them had been covered by a price they did not set.

Numbers 35:31–32 — where ransom is refused. Exodus 21:30 allowed an ox owner to pay "to redeem his life." His guilt was negligence, and the payment satisfied the victim's family. Murder was different: "you shall take no ransom for the life of a murderer who is guilty of death." The next verse goes further. Even the person who killed accidentally and fled to a city of refuge could not pay to return home "before the death of the priest." Money could not shorten that exile. Only the high priest's death released him. The Law was teaching that some debts cannot be paid with money at all.

Psalm 49:7 and Job 36:18 — no one can pay. Psalm 49 speaks to people who "trust in their wealth" and states the matter for everyone: "None of them can by any means redeem his brother, Nor give to God a ransom for him." The rich man cannot buy his own life, much less his brother's. Elihu says the same to Job: "Because there is wrath, beware lest He take you away with one blow; For a large ransom would not help you avoid it." Size is not the problem. No amount of earthly currency answers to God's wrath. The psalm's hope rests in God Himself, who will redeem the psalmist's soul from the power of the grave (Psalm 49:15, paraphrased).

Job 33:23–24 — God finds the ransom. Elihu describes a man wasting away toward death. Then comes a turn: "If there is a messenger for him, A mediator, one among a thousand, To show man His uprightness, Then He is gracious to him, and says, 'Deliver him from going down to the Pit; I have found a ransom.'" The dying man does not negotiate or pay. God is gracious, and God finds the ransom. The ransom is connected to a mediator, someone who stands between God and the man. Elihu does not name this figure. The pattern of a rare mediator, divine grace, and a ransom found by God anticipates the gospel.

Isaiah 43:3 — God pays for His people. "I gave Egypt for your ransom, Ethiopia and Seba in your place." The Holy One of Israel speaks as Savior and treats whole nations as the price of His people's rescue. The next verse gives His reason: Israel was precious in His sight. God is the One who pays.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

These passages build toward one unanswered need. Everyone needs a ransom (Exodus 30). Money cannot cover the gravest guilt (Numbers 35). No person can pay for another (Psalm 49). Yet God has found a ransom (Job 33). Jesus answers this need directly: "the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). The Greek word for ransom there belongs to the New Testament, but it fills the place the Hebrew ransom passages left open. Peter draws the contrast that Psalm 49 prepares: believers "were not redeemed with corruptible things, like silver or gold... but with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot" (1 Peter 1:18–19).

Several Old Testament patterns point toward Christ. The census price was the same for everyone, and the cross also leaves no one too rich to need it or too poor to receive it. The refugee in Numbers 35 went free through the death of the high priest, not through any payment. Hebrews presents Christ as our great High Priest, and this pattern reads naturally in His light, although Scripture does not state that Numbers 35 is a prophecy of Him. The pitch that covered Noah's ark "inside and outside" is a further connection through imagery and not through the meaning of the word. Those inside the ark were sealed against the waters of judgment, and those in Christ are kept safe from condemnation.

The ransom imagery also has a limit. Scripture never says the price was paid to Satan. The emphasis falls on how costly the rescue was to God and on our inability to rescue ourselves. In Job 33 the mediator and the ransom stand before God's own justice.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 49 removes a quiet hope many people carry: that enough goodness, religious effort, or generosity might settle their account with God. A "large ransom would not help." That truth is hard, but it frees us. Once we stop trying to raise the price ourselves, we can hear God say, "I have found a ransom."

The equal half shekel speaks to a believer's sense of worth. No one in God's family stands higher because he paid more, and no one stands lower because he had less. Every one of us was covered by the same price, and the church's fellowship rests on that equal footing.

The contrast between a ransom and a bribe also speaks to how we deal with God. We cannot bribe Him with religious performance to overlook what we refuse to confess. He does not need to be bribed, because His mercy has already met the full claim of His justice. Paul describes Christ's self-giving as "an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma" (Ephesians 5:2). The bride in the Song calls her beloved "a cluster of henna blooms." The two passages are not connected by the word's meaning, but the comparison helps a believer see the Ransom as a Person who is loved and not only a legal payment.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 21:30 — "If there is imposed on him a sum of money, then he shall pay to redeem his life, whatever is imposed on him." Here the ransom is a lawful civil settlement for the owner of a dangerous ox, the background against which Numbers 35 sets its limits.

  • Proverbs 13:8 — "The ransom of a man's life is his riches." In earthly dangers such as extortion or threats, money can buy safety. The wise man notes this without pretending it reaches the judgment of God.

  • Proverbs 6:35 — The wronged husband "will accept no recompense, Nor will he be appeased though you give many gifts." Even human justice recognizes wrongs that gifts cannot settle.

  • Proverbs 21:18 — "The wicked shall be a ransom for the righteous." The trouble that threatens the upright falls on the wicked instead, a pattern of reversal under God's rule.

  • Amos 5:12 — "taking bribes; Diverting the poor from justice at the gate." Bribery is listed among Israel's "mighty sins" because it turns the courts against the vulnerable.

  • Song of Solomon 4:13 — Henna appears among the fragrant plants in the bridegroom's description of his bride as a garden, an image of delight and beauty.
