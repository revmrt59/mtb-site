MAN, HUSBAND, EACH - (ʼîysh, Strong's H0376)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼîysh is the Old Testament's ordinary word for "man." It usually means a particular male person, often a husband. It also works as a simple everyday word for "each" or "anyone," marking out the individual within a group. Because it is so common, it appears in almost every kind of biblical setting: family stories, laws, battle reports, proverbs, and prophecy.

That ordinary word carries some of the most remarkable moments in Scripture. Jacob wrestles all night with a Man and comes away having seen God. God promises that Israel will one day call Him "My Husband" rather than "My Master." He searches Jerusalem for a man to stand in the gap and finds none. Isaiah foresees a suffering Man of sorrows, and Zechariah announces, "Behold, the Man whose name is the BRANCH!" Followed through Scripture, ʼîysh traces God's patient movement toward the day when He would provide the Man His people needed: Jesus Christ, true God and true man.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼîysh is one of the most frequent nouns in the Hebrew Old Testament, and the word occurs 1,663 times in 1,430 verses of the Hebrew Bible. Hebrew has several words for human beings, and the differences help. ʼādām usually views people as a race, mankind or humanity. ʼîysh tends to point to a specific person, often male, and often with his standing, role, or character in view. Its natural partner is ʼishshāh, woman or wife. Genesis 2:23 plays on the sound of the two words when the first woman is named because she was taken out of man.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A man or male person. This is the basic sense, as when "a Man" wrestles with Jacob (Genesis 32:24) or the law speaks of what a man must do (Leviticus 20:10).

2. A husband. In marriage settings ʼîysh simply means "husband." Leah speaks of her husband this way (Genesis 29:32; 30:15), and Ruth's story turns on the loss and provision of a husband (Ruth 1:3–13).

3. Each, anyone, everyone. Very often the word works like a pronoun that singles out individuals within a group. In Deuteronomy 24:16 the NKJV translates it "a person": "a person shall be put to death for his own sin." These uses are mainly grammatical and should not be loaded with extra meaning.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When ʼîysh is joined to another noun, the phrase describes a person by what defines him. A man of the ground is a farmer (Genesis 9:20). A man of war is a warrior. A man of God is a prophet who carries the Lord's message (Deuteronomy 33:1; 1 Kings 17:18). The same pattern also describes moral character. David's followers include "wicked and worthless men" (1 Samuel 30:22), and Abigail calls Nabal a scoundrel using the same kind of phrase (1 Samuel 25:25). So the word that can describe a man of valor can also describe a man of worthlessness. In this idiom, a man is named by what he is given to.

The distributive use, "each man" or "everyone," shows up in camp orders (Numbers 1:52), in laws, and in the bleak refrain of Judges: everyone did what was right in his own eyes (Judges 21:25).

4. Theological Significance

Several truths about God's ways emerge from passages using this word.

God speaks through a man. The title man of God belongs to Moses, the unnamed man of God who rebuked Eli's house (1 Samuel 2:27), Elijah, and Elisha. The title marks a person who stands between God and the people with God's word. After Elijah raised her son, the widow of Zarephath confessed that she now knew he was a man of God and that the Lord's word in his mouth was truth (1 Kings 17:24). God chose to make Himself known through human messengers whose words carried His authority.

God fights for His people. After the Red Sea, Moses sang that the Lord is a man of war (Exodus 15:3). Isaiah uses the same picture for a coming day when the Lord goes out like a mighty man and stirs up His zeal like a man of war (Isaiah 42:13). God does not watch His people's oppression from a distance. He enters the battle as their champion.

God holds each person accountable. Because ʼîysh can mean "each," it naturally appears where Scripture insists on personal responsibility. Deuteronomy 24:16 forbids punishing children for their fathers' crimes. Jeremiah rejects the proverb about sour grapes and says each one will die for his own iniquity (Jeremiah 31:30). Ezekiel adds the gracious other side: God judges every one according to his ways, and so every one is called to repent and turn (Ezekiel 18:30). Belonging to a nation never hides a person from God, and it never excludes anyone from His mercy.

Human distinction cannot save. Psalm 62:9 sets ʼîysh beside ʼādām in order to humble both: "Surely men of low degree are a vapor, Men of high degree are a lie." The "men of high degree" are ʼîysh. Rank, wealth, and reputation weigh nothing on God's scales.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The Man who came to Jacob, Joshua, and Manoah (Genesis 32; Joshua 5; Judges 13). In these three passages Scripture uses this ordinary word for a visitor who turns out to be far more than a man. Jacob wrestles a Man until daybreak, receives the name Israel, and names the place Peniel because he has seen God face to face (Genesis 32:24–30). Joshua meets a Man with a drawn sword who calls himself Commander of the Lord's army and tells Joshua to remove his sandals on holy ground (Joshua 5:13–15). Manoah's wife describes a Man of God whose appearance was awesome. When he rises in the altar flame, Manoah fears they will die because they have seen God (Judges 13:6, 20–22). Interpreters differ on how fully these visitors should be identified with the Son before His incarnation. Even so, the pattern is plain. The God of Israel was willing to meet His people in human form, close enough to wrestle and to speak face to face. These encounters prepare the way for the day when God would come as a man permanently.

"My Husband," not "My Master" (Hosea 2:16). Hosea's marriage to an unfaithful wife pictures Israel's unfaithfulness to God. Yet God promises restoration: "you will call Me ‘My Husband,’ And no longer call Me ‘My Master’" (Hosea 2:16). "Husband" here is ʼîysh. "Master" is baʻal, a word that also meant "husband" or "owner," and it was the name of the Canaanite god Israel had been chasing. God wants more than a servant's fearful obedience to an owner. The promise recalls the closeness of the first marriage, when the man recognized his wife as bone of his bones (Genesis 2:23–24). Paul later shows where that first marriage was pointing: "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32).

The empty gap (Ezekiel 22:30). Ezekiel 22 indicts Jerusalem's prophets, priests, princes, and people. Then God says that He sought for a man among them to build up the wall and stand in the gap before Him for the land, so that He would not destroy it, but He found no one. The verse exposes God's heart. Even while pronouncing judgment, He was looking for an intercessor. Isaiah records something similar: God saw that there was no man and wondered that there was no intercessor, so His own arm brought salvation (Isaiah 59:16).

The Man of war becomes the Man of sorrows (Isaiah 53:3). The same book that pictures the Lord marching out like a man of war (Isaiah 42:13) also describes the Servant as despised and rejected by men, a Man of sorrows acquainted with grief (Isaiah 53:3). Rescue does not come through a conquering warrior who avoids pain. It comes through a Man who takes grief into Himself and on whom the Lord lays the iniquity of us all (Isaiah 53:6). The victory belongs to the Champion who wins by suffering.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Read together, these passages describe a need that only grows. Men of God spoke God's word and then died. No man was found to stand in the gap. Israel kept breaking faith with her Husband. "Each man" stood guilty for his own sin. Through all of it, God at times drew near in human form and kept promising that He Himself would act.

Zechariah gives that hope a name. Joshua the high priest is crowned as a sign, and the prophet declares: "Behold, the Man whose name is the BRANCH! From His place He shall branch out, And He shall build the temple of the LORD" (Zechariah 6:12). This Man would rule as king and also serve as priest. Centuries later Pilate led out Jesus, crowned with thorns, and said without understanding what he was saying, "Behold the Man!" (John 19:5). The connection is one of strong resonance rather than a quotation Pilate meant. Still, the crown of thorns sits on the head of the true Branch.

In Jesus every thread comes together. He is the man of God whose words are the Father's words. He is the Man who stands in the gap; Paul calls Him the one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus (1 Timothy 2:5). He is the Husband who loves His bride and gives Himself for her. He is the Man of sorrows who bears the guilt that individual accountability had laid on each person.

7. Spirit & Life

Ezekiel 22:30 shows something about God that many people would not expect: before judging Jerusalem, He looked for someone to stand in the gap. The gap that no one in Jerusalem filled, Christ has filled. In that light, those who belong to Him are invited to stand alongside Him in prayer for their families, churches, and cities. They do this not as saviors but as people who share the Savior's heart.

The phrase "each man" works in two directions. No one can hide inside a family or a congregation, and no one is shut out because of their parents' failures. God deals with you personally, and His invitation to turn and live is addressed to you personally (Ezekiel 18:30–32).

Psalm 41:9 brings the word into the most painful kind of relationship. The phrase translated "my own familiar friend" is literally the man of my peace, someone who shared David's table. "Even my own familiar friend in whom I trusted, Who ate my bread, Has lifted up his heel against me." Jesus applied the verse to Judas: "He who eats bread with Me has lifted up his heel against Me" (John 13:18). The Lord knows what it is to be betrayed by someone close, so believers wounded that way have a High Priest who has been there.

Finally, Hosea 2:16 invites a change in how believers speak to God. Many relate to Him as an exacting Master whom they serve from a nervous distance. He calls His people to know Him as their faithful Husband, who pursues them, restores them, and is never ashamed to be joined to them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ezekiel 9:2–3 — "One man among them was clothed with linen and had a writer’s inkhorn at his side." Before judgment falls, this heavenly figure marks those who grieve over Jerusalem's sins (9:4).

  • Daniel 9:21 — "the man Gabriel" reaches Daniel while he is still praying. Heaven's messenger appears in a recognizable human form, which shows that a visionary "man" is not always the Lord Himself.

  • Daniel 10:5; 12:6–7 — "a certain man clothed in linen" brings revelation about the end and swears by Him who lives forever.

  • Psalm 1:1 — The blessed man is pictured as an individual who does not walk in the counsel of the ungodly.

  • Micah 4:4 — In the latter days Micah foresees, everyone will sit under his own vine and fig tree, safe and unafraid.

  • Judges 21:25 — Everyone did what was right in his own eyes. Here "each man" describes a nation that has lost its moral center.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H0120 (ʼādām), canonical pairing: mankind, a human being. It is set beside ʼîysh in Psalm 49:2, which addresses "both low and high," and in Psalm 62:9, which humbles both.

  • H0802 (ʼishshāh), canonical pairing: woman, wife. It is paired with ʼîysh in the wordplay of Genesis 2:23 and throughout the marriage laws.

  • H1167 (baʻal), canonical pairing: owner, master, husband. It is contrasted with ʼîysh in Hosea 2:16, where God sets aside "My Master" in favor of "My Husband."
