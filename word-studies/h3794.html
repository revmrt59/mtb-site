KITTIM, CYPRUS - (Kittîy, Strong's H3794)

Summary

Kittîy is the Hebrew name for Kittim, a people descended from Javan, and for the island of Cyprus where they settled. Over time the name came to stand for the distant western coastlands of the Mediterranean, the sea peoples and naval powers beyond the horizon of Israel's world. The NKJV renders the same Hebrew word two ways. It keeps "Kittim" in the genealogies of Genesis and 1 Chronicles and uses "Cyprus" in the prophets. English readers can easily miss that a single name runs from the Table of Nations to the visions of Daniel.

The word appears only eight times, and each use is purposeful. Jeremiah uses Kittim as the far western edge of the world in order to expose Israel's unfaithfulness. Isaiah and Ezekiel tie it to the wealth of Tyre and show that no trading partner can shelter a proud city when God judges it. Balaam and Daniel both picture ships coming from Cyprus at God's appointed time to strike great powers. Together these passages present the farthest powers of the earth as still under God's hand.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kittîy is a proper name. Genesis 10:4 lists Kittim among the sons of Javan, next to Elishah, Tarshish, and Dodanim. This places them in the line of Japheth, among the coastland and island peoples of the Mediterranean. The name is generally linked with Kition, an ancient town on the southeastern coast of Cyprus that had both Phoenician and Greek settlers.

For Israel, Cyprus lay west across the sea, close enough for trade and far enough to seem like the edge of the world. That position explains how the word is used. It appears in genealogy, in prophetic argument, in oracles about Phoenician commerce, and in visions of future kingdoms.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Kittîy names a people and the land they lived in. Context determines how wide the reference is.

1. The people descended from Javan, recorded in the genealogies as "Kittim" (Genesis 10:4; 1 Chronicles 1:7).

2. The island of Cyprus and, more broadly, the western maritime coastlands and the peoples who sailed from them. The NKJV translates this sense as "Cyprus" (Isaiah 23:1; Daniel 11:30).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The genealogies use the name to trace descent. The prophets use it as a place on the horizon, and each prophet draws a different meaning from that position. In Jeremiah, Kittim is a distant point of comparison. In Isaiah and Ezekiel, it belongs to Tyre's network of ports and suppliers. In Numbers and Daniel, it is the starting point of a fleet.

The shared image of ships from Cyprus is especially significant. Numbers 24:24 says "ships shall come from the coasts of Cyprus," and Daniel 11:30 says "ships from Cyprus shall come against him." The wording is not identical, but in both places ships from the west come against a powerful king and change the course of events. Daniel may well echo Balaam's oracle, and readers who knew the older oracle could hear it in the newer one.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show God ruling the edges of the map as fully as its center. Israel's history was mostly played out between Egypt and Mesopotamia, and Kittim lay beyond both. Scripture still treats the western sea as part of God's domain. Its peoples are listed in His record of the nations in Genesis 10. Its harbors receive word of His judgment on Tyre. Its ships sail when He sends them.

The word also exposes the false confidence people place in what is far away. Tyre trusted its trade routes, and the refugees from Sidon hoped to find safety across the water. In every case, distance from Jerusalem did not mean distance from God. The same God who brings judgment through the ships of Kittim also brings judgment on the nations that send them out.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Jeremiah 2:10 — the whole world as a witness. God tells Israel to cross over to the coasts of Cyprus in the west and to send to Kedar in the eastern desert, and to look carefully. The question He puts to them is whether any nation has ever exchanged its gods, even though those gods are no gods at all. From one end of the known world to the other, the answer was no. Pagan peoples stayed loyal to idols that had never done anything for them. Israel alone had exchanged its Glory for something worthless (2:11). God then calls the heavens to be appalled (2:12) and names the two evils: His people had forsaken Him, the fountain of living waters, and had dug cisterns for themselves that were cracked and could hold no water (2:13).

Kittim serves as one end of the survey. The irony cuts deep. In Jeremiah's prophetic rhetoric, the nations held tightly to gods that were nothing, while the one nation that knew the living God let Him go. God measured Israel's unfaithfulness by setting it against the entire world, and Israel fared worse than the world.

Isaiah 23:1, 12 and Ezekiel 27:6 — no rest across the water. Tyre was the great sea power of its time, and Cyprus was part of its wealth. In Ezekiel's lament, Tyre is pictured as a magnificent ship built from the finest materials the world could supply, and the coasts of Cyprus are among its sources (27:6). In Isaiah's oracle, the ships of Tarshish are told to wail, and the news of Tyre's ruin reaches them from the land of Cyprus (23:1). Tyre's trading partners learn of its fall before they reach home.

Isaiah then makes clear who ordered it: the Lord of hosts purposed it, to bring down the pride of all glory (23:9). The people of Sidon are told to get up and cross over to Cyprus, yet even there they will find no rest (23:12). The island was a natural refuge for Tyre's people, a nearby partner and a familiar harbor. When God judges pride, the old alliances still exist, but they no longer offer shelter. Rest cannot be found by putting more sea between yourself and the God you have defied.

Numbers 24:24 and Daniel 11:30 — ships at the appointed time. Balaam's last oracle, spoken in the wilderness before Israel had even entered Canaan, sees ships coming from the coasts of Kittim. They afflict Asshur and Eber, and the oracle ends, "And so shall Amalek, until he perishes." The NKJV supplies "Amalek" here and notes that the Hebrew reads literally "he" or "that one"; some read the pronoun as the attacker from the west, so that the power God uses is itself not permanent. That is one interpretive reading, not the only one. The wider point stands on firmer ground: God governs and limits every imperial power, as Daniel's visions make plain.

Centuries later Daniel picks up the same image: "For ships from Cyprus shall come against him; therefore he shall be grieved, and return in rage against the holy covenant, and do damage" (Daniel 11:30). The king of the North is generally identified as Antiochus IV Epiphanes. In 168 BC, as he advanced on Egypt, a Roman envoy who had come by sea ordered him to withdraw, and he gave way. Rome came from the western sea, as Kittim had always done.

A careful reading matters here, because the ships do not end the suffering of God's people. Daniel says that Antiochus, humiliated, turned his anger against the holy covenant. Desecration and persecution followed (11:31). God stopped the king's ambitions in Egypt, but He did not prevent all harm to His people. What He did was set limits. The people who know their God would be strong and act (11:32), and the faithful would be refined and purified until the appointed time (11:35). The ships of Kittim arrived on God's schedule, and the suffering that followed was also measured out on that schedule.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of Kittim moves from a name in the family of nations to a fleet in a prophetic vision. It follows the same course as the larger biblical story of the nations, which come from one family, scatter across the world, and remain answerable to their Maker. Daniel's visions look forward to a kingdom that no fleet can overturn, given to one like the Son of Man (Daniel 7:13–14). Jesus later claims that authority directly: all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Him (Matthew 28:18). He is called the ruler over the kings of the earth (Revelation 1:5). The powers that once seemed to decide history from beyond the horizon belong to His dominion.

Jeremiah's image also finds its answer in Christ. The fountain of living waters that Israel abandoned stands in the Temple courts and calls the thirsty to come to Him and drink (John 7:37–38). There is a further thematic link worth noting. The New Testament uses a different word, but Cyprus appears there as well. Barnabas came from Cyprus (Acts 4:36), and Paul and Barnabas began their first missionary journey on the island (Acts 13:4). The coastland that once marked how far Israel had fallen became one of the first places the gospel reached beyond Israel.

7. Spirit & Life

Jeremiah 2:10 confronts believers in a way that is easy to avoid. The world is often remarkably loyal to its substitutes. People give their lives to careers, ideologies, and pleasures that never repay them. God's people, who have tasted living water, can drift from Him with very little resistance. Jeremiah does not tell believers to look down on the nations. He sends them to observe the nations and come back ashamed. Faithfulness to Christ deserves at least the persistence that idolaters give to nothing.

Isaiah 23 removes a quieter kind of trust. Most people have their own version of Cyprus: a backup plan, a network of connections, savings, or some other place to retreat when things fall apart. Isaiah does not say these things are evil. He says they cannot give rest when God is dealing with pride. Rest is found by turning toward God, not by escaping across the sea.

Daniel 11 gives a sturdier comfort than simple optimism. God does sometimes send ships at the right moment, and the threatening power gives way. Sometimes, though, the check on one front releases anger on another, and the faithful suffer. Even then the suffering has limits, a purpose of refining, and an appointed end. The believer under pressure can know that the distant powers move only when God sends them, and that the One who sends them knows the hour.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 10:4 — Kittim appears among the sons of Javan. The people who will later appear as a fleet in prophecy begin as one family in God's record of the nations.

1 Chronicles 1:7 — The Chronicler repeats the line of Javan, including Kittim, as he opens Israel's story with the whole human family. The genealogy places Israel within a world that God made and keeps account of.

The remaining occurrences (Numbers 24:24; Isaiah 23:1, 12; Jeremiah 2:10; Ezekiel 27:6; Daniel 11:30) are discussed in Section 5.
