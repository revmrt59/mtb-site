WALL - (kôthel, Strong's H3796)

Summary

The Hebrew word kôthel means "wall," specifically the wall of a house. It appears only once in the Old Testament, in Song of Solomon 2:9, where the bride, waiting inside her home, says of the one she loves: "Behold, he stands behind our wall; He is looking through the windows, Gazing through the lattice." This is a household wall, the ordinary boundary between the inside of a home and the world outside. It is not a fortress or a city wall built to keep enemies out.

That single scene carries real spiritual weight. The beloved has come "leaping upon the mountains" (Song 2:8) and now stands as close to her as the wall allows. He looks in through the openings and calls her to "rise up... and come away" (2:10). Read alongside the rest of Scripture, the picture helps believers understand their present life with Christ. We do not yet see Him face to face, and something still stands between us. Yet He has come near, He makes Himself known through the openings He has provided, and the barrier that remains is a threshold He intends to cross. It is not a wall of anger.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Kôthel is a Hebrew noun for the wall of a dwelling. Its closest relative is the Aramaic word kethal, which appears in the Aramaic portions of Ezra and Daniel for the walls of buildings. Ezra 5:8 reports that "timber is being laid in the walls" of the rebuilt temple. Daniel 5:5 describes the hand that wrote "on the plaster of the wall of the king's palace." Both uses point to the walls of a structure people live or worship in, which fits the domestic setting of Song of Solomon 2:9.

Hebrew has other words for "wall." Chomah is the usual term for a city wall or fortification. Gader refers to a stone fence around a vineyard or sheepfold. Song of Solomon uses chomah later, for "the keepers of the walls" (5:7) and in the image of a sister who is "a wall" (8:9). In 2:9, by contrast, the poet chooses the house wall.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Kôthel has one plain meaning: the wall of a house, the physical structure separating the interior of a home from the outside. Because the word occurs only once, its meaning comes from that verse and from its Aramaic relative. The word itself does not mean "separation" or "alienation." Whatever the wall comes to signify spiritually grows out of how it functions in the scene.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Song of Solomon 2:9 is the word's only use, and two details in that verse influence how the wall is understood.

The first detail is the little word "our." In Hebrew, "our" is attached directly to the word for wall, so the bride speaks of our wall. It belongs to the household where she waits. The beloved is the one who comes to it. She does not have to search for his house.

The second detail is that this wall has openings. He is "looking through the windows, gazing through the lattice." A lattice was a screen of crossed wooden strips over a window that let in air and light while partly obscuring the view. The wall limits contact, but sight and voice still pass through it.

4. Theological Significance

The scene shows that nearness and limitation can exist together. The beloved has crossed mountains and hills to reach her, and only the wall remains between them. He does not wait for her to come to him. He arrives, looks in, and speaks first. That order, in which the lover comes, seeks, and calls, matches the way Scripture consistently presents God's dealings with His people. He takes the initiative in drawing near.

Scripture also tells of a very different kind of wall. Isaiah 59:2 says, "your iniquities have separated you from your God; And your sins have hidden His face from you." Ephesians 2:14 speaks of Christ, who "has broken down the middle wall of separation." Those passages use other words, and their walls stand for hostility, guilt, and division. The wall of Song 2:9 is not one of them. Nothing in the scene suggests anger or rejection. Love is present on both sides, and what remains between the lovers is simply the structure of the house before the moment of meeting.

This distinction matters for believers. Christ has torn down the wall of hostility that sin built. What a Christian still experiences is the limitation of present life in a mortal body. Paul names it plainly: "while we are at home in the body we are absent from the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:6). That absence does not mean God is displeased. It means the wedding day has not yet come.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:8–10: the beloved arrives at the wall. The passage moves in three steps. It opens with sound: "The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes Leaping upon the mountains, Skipping upon the hills" (2:8). Next comes his position: he "stands behind our wall," looking through the windows and lattice (2:9). Then he speaks: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away" (2:10). The gazelle and young stag are animals known for speed, and they make the point that distance has not slowed him. Mountains could not keep him away, and the wall does not end the scene. He presses as close as he can and invites her out. The wall marks the point where his pursuit has brought him, and he waits there with a call to come away with him.

The house wall and the city wall in the Song. The book has two kinds of walls, and they belong to different experiences. In 5:7 the bride goes out into the city at night, and "the keepers of the walls" strike her, wound her, and take her veil. That wall belongs to public defense, to watchmen and patrols, and there it becomes a place of harm. The wall in 2:9 belongs to the home. Behind it she is safe, and the one on the other side is her beloved. The choice of kôthel places this meeting at the door of her own dwelling, in the ordinary setting of daily life, far from the guarded perimeter of the city.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Christians have long read the Song's bridegroom and bride as a picture of Christ and His people. The New Testament speaks of Christ as Bridegroom and the church as His bride, so this reading is a canonical application of the poem rather than its stated meaning. Read this way, Song 2:9 shows the whole movement of redemption.

The beloved comes to our wall. The eternal Son did not ask humanity to climb up to Him. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and "He Himself likewise shared in the same" flesh and blood as His brothers and sisters (Hebrews 2:14). He came down to the boundary of human weakness and took that weakness on Himself, "made like His brethren, that He might be a merciful and faithful High Priest" (Hebrews 2:17).

Many interpreters in church history also saw the wall and lattice as the era before Christ came. On this reading, He stood behind the wall of the old order and showed Himself through the lattice of sacrifices, types, and prophecies, which Hebrews calls "a shadow of the good things to come" (Hebrews 10:1). Paul says of such things that "the substance is of Christ" (Colossians 2:17). This reading is an analogy, not an interpretation the Song itself states, but it fits well. The saints of the Old Testament truly knew the Lord, even though they saw Him only through the lattice.

The story moves toward a wall that comes down. Through His flesh Christ opened "a new and living way... through the veil" (Hebrews 10:20). Through His blood He broke down the wall of hostility. The final step is still ahead: "now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12).

7. Spirit & Life

A believer can take real comfort in how this wall is described. When God seems hidden, it is easy to assume He is angry or far away. Song 2:9 offers a different picture. The beloved is right outside, close enough to see in, and he speaks first. The present limitation is real, but it is the wall of a home that is waiting for him, and he means to cross it.

The windows and lattice also teach believers where to look. Christ makes Himself known now through the openings He has given, above all His Word and His Spirit. Through them He sees His people and they hear His voice. That is what it means that "we walk by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:7). Faith does not see nothing. It sees through the lattice.

The scene also calls for a response. The beloved does not only look in. He says, "Rise up... and come away." Waiting for Christ is not passive. It means answering the voice He sends through the wall now, while we long for the day He comes all the way in.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Kôthel occurs only in Song of Solomon 2:9, which is discussed fully above. No other Hebrew occurrences exist. Related walls in the Song and elsewhere are treated in Sections 1, 4, 5, and 9.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H3797 (kethal)**: the Aramaic relative of kôthel, used for the walls of buildings (Ezra 5:8; Daniel 5:5).

  • **H2346 (chomah)**: a distinct Hebrew word for a city wall or fortification. The Song uses it for the walls patrolled by watchmen (5:7) and for the sister who is "a wall" (8:9). It contrasts with the house wall of 2:9.

  • **H1447 (gader)**: a distinct word for a stone fence or enclosure around vineyards and sheepfolds.

  • Thematic connection: the "middle wall of separation" in Ephesians 2:14 uses a Greek word and stands for hostility that Christ removed. It is a contrasting image, not a lexical relative.

10. Other Relevant Insights

In later Jewish usage, kotel became the name for the Western Wall in Jerusalem. Rabbinic commentary on the Song applied "he stands behind our wall" to God's presence remaining near His people even after the temple was destroyed. This is a later tradition, not biblical interpretation, but it shows how readers across the centuries have heard in this verse a promise that the Beloved stays close while His people wait.
