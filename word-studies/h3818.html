LO-AMMI - (Lôʼ ʻAmmîy, Strong's H3818)

Summary

Lo-Ammi was the third child born to Gomer, the wife of the prophet Hosea, in the northern kingdom of Israel during the eighth century BC. His name is Hebrew for "Not my people," and God Himself chose it. When the boy was born, the Lord told Hosea, "Call his name Lo-Ammi, For you are not My people, And I will not be your God" (Hosea 1:9). Each time the family called the child, the nation heard God's verdict on years of Baal worship. The people who had broken faith with Him had lost the relationship that made them His people at all.

The name was never God's last word, though. In the very next verse the Lord promises that "in the place where it was said to them, 'You are not My people,' There it shall be said to them, 'You are sons of the living God'" (Hosea 1:10). Later He commands the name to be spoken without its "not," simply as "My people" (Hosea 2:1, 23). Paul and Peter both take up Hosea's reversal to describe what God has done in Christ. People who had no claim on God, including Gentiles who were never part of Israel, are now called His people by grace (Romans 9:24–26; 1 Peter 2:10). A name that announced the end of a relationship became one of the Bible's clearest pictures of how God makes outsiders His own.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

Lo-Ammi is the second son and third child of Gomer, the woman the Lord told Hosea to marry (Hosea 1:2–3). His older brother was Jezreel, and his older sister was Lo-Ruhamah. All three children received names that God dictated, and each name carried a prophetic message to the northern kingdom, which Hosea often calls Israel or Ephraim. The siblings' names are indexed separately (Jezreel, H3157; Lo-Ruhamah, H3819). They belong to Lo-Ammi's story but are not occurrences of his name.

The meaning of Lo-Ammi is clear and not disputed. The name joins three Hebrew elements: lo', the ordinary word for "not"; 'am, the common word for "people"; and an ending that means "my." Put together, the name means "Not my people." No guesswork about its origin is needed, because God explains the name in the same breath that He gives it: "For you are not My people, And I will not be your God" (Hosea 1:9). The child's name is therefore not a description of the boy's character. It is a message about the nation, carried by the child as a living sign.

Hosea then plays on the name directly. In Hosea 2:1 the Lord tells the faithful to say to their brethren, "My people." In Hebrew that phrase is 'ammi, which is the same name with the word "not" removed. Hosea 2:23 repeats the reversal: "I will say to those who were not My people, 'You are My people!'" The undoing of the name is built into the wording itself.

The concordance data attached to this entry lists a second occurrence in Song of Solomon 6:12, which the NKJV renders, "My soul had made me As the chariots of my noble people." That verse does not contain the name Lo-Ammi and has nothing to do with Hosea's son. Its phrase "my noble people" uses the word for "my people" followed by a word for "noble" or "willing," which older translations read as a personal name, Amminadib. The listing appears to be a data error. The only true biblical appearance of the name Lo-Ammi is Hosea 1:9.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Hosea preached in the northern kingdom during its last decades, in the days of Jeroboam II and the turbulent reigns that followed (Hosea 1:1). It was a time of outward prosperity and inward decay. Israel credited Baal with the grain, wine, and oil that the Lord had actually given, and turned God's gifts into offerings for an idol (Hosea 2:8). Within a generation the Assyrians would destroy the nation and carry its people into exile (2 Kings 17:6).

God told Hosea to make his own household a picture of that broken relationship. He was to marry Gomer and have "children of harlotry," because the land had played the harlot by departing from the Lord (Hosea 1:2). The three children came one after another, and each name drove the message deeper. Jezreel, the first son, recalled the bloodshed at the city of Jezreel and announced that God would break Israel's military strength there (Hosea 1:4–5). Lo-Ruhamah, the daughter, meant "No mercy," and declared that God would no longer show pity to the house of Israel, though He would still have mercy on Judah (Hosea 1:6–7). Then came the last child: "Now when she had weaned Lo-Ruhamah, she conceived and bore a son" (Hosea 1:8). God named him Lo-Ammi.

The order of the names reads like a steady stripping away. First God breaks the nation's strength, then He withholds His compassion, and finally He declares that the relationship itself is over. Lo-Ammi is the climax of that sequence, the point beyond which there seems to be nothing left to lose.

One detail in the birth reports has drawn careful attention. When Jezreel was born, Gomer "bore him a son," which names Hosea as the father (Hosea 1:3). For the second and third children the text says only that she conceived and bore a child (Hosea 1:6, 8). Hosea 2:4–5 later speaks of children whose mother "has played the harlot." Many readers conclude that Lo-Ammi may not have been Hosea's son. The text never says so outright, so certainty is not possible. The silence fits the message, however. A child whose father goes unnamed carries a name that says "not mine," just as a nation that had given itself to other gods could no longer claim the Lord as its Father.

The judgment does not end the passage. Immediately after Lo-Ammi's naming, Hosea 1:10–11 promises that Israel will become as countless as the sand of the sea, that the people once called "not My people" will be called "sons of the living God," and that Judah and Israel will be gathered under one head. Hosea 2 then traces how God will bring this about. He charges Israel with unfaithfulness in the language of a broken marriage: "she is not My wife, nor am I her Husband!" (Hosea 2:2). He takes back the grain and wine she credited to her lovers (Hosea 2:9), hedges her way with thorns until she longs to return (Hosea 2:6–7), and then speaks tenderly to her in the wilderness and betroths her to Himself forever (Hosea 2:14–20). The chapter ends with the full reversal of all three children's names: "Then I will sow her for Myself in the earth, And I will have mercy on her who had not obtained mercy; Then I will say to those who were not My people, 'You are My people!' And they shall say, 'You are my God!'" (Hosea 2:23).

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

To feel the weight of Lo-Ammi's name, a reader has to hear what it cancels. When God brought Israel out of Egypt, He gave them His promise: "I will take you as My people, and I will be your God" (Exodus 6:7). That two-part promise sat at the heart of the covenant God made with Israel at Sinai. Hosea 1:9 turns both halves upside down: "you are not My people, And I will not be your God." The words are not an outburst of temper. They are a legal verdict, much like the formal declarations a great king might issue against a subject nation that had broken its treaty. Israel had pursued other gods for generations, and God now declared the relationship forfeited.

The Hebrew wording of Hosea 1:9 may sharpen the point further. The phrase translated "I will not be your God" does not actually contain the word "God." It reads more literally, "I will not be to you," and the verb "I will be" is 'ehyeh. That is the same Hebrew word God used at the burning bush when He told Moses, "I AM WHO I AM," and "I AM has sent me to you" (Exodus 3:14). The NKJV's "your God" is a sound rendering that matches the wording of Exodus 6:7. Many readers also hear in the original an echo of the name by which God first revealed Himself to Israel. If that echo is intended, God is saying something like, "I will not be 'I AM' for you." The One who had promised Moses His presence would withdraw from the nation that had rejected Him. Israel's story as a people began when God made Himself known by name, and Lo-Ammi marks the undoing of that story.

What Scripture states plainly, though, is that the verdict was not final. The promise of Hosea 1:10 echoes God's word to Abraham that his descendants would be as the sand of the sea (Genesis 22:17). God's purpose to bless Abraham's family and the world through them did not depend on Israel's faithfulness at Sinai, and Israel's failure could not cancel it. Hosea even says the renaming will happen "in the place where it was said to them, 'You are not My people'" (Hosea 1:10). God does not simply cover over the scene of rejection. He brings His blessing to the very place where judgment was pronounced.

This also guards against a misreading. Lo-Ammi does not mean that God threw away Israel forever. Hosea promises restoration in the same chapter, and Paul later insists that God has not cast away His people (Romans 11:1–2, 25–29). The judgment was real and the exile was devastating. Still, the sentence "not My people" served God's larger purpose of bringing His people back to Himself.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

The New Testament does far more with Lo-Ammi's name than anyone in Hosea's day might have expected. In Romans 9 Paul explains how God has formed a people "even us whom He called, not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles" (Romans 9:24). To support this he quotes Hosea directly: "I will call them My people, who were not My people, And her beloved, who was not beloved" (Romans 9:25), and "it shall come to pass in the place where it was said to them, 'You are not My people,' There they shall be called sons of the living God" (Romans 9:26).

Paul's use of the passage is striking. Hosea spoke to Israelites who had lost their standing with God, yet Paul applies the promise to Gentiles who never had that standing in the first place. His reasoning follows from what the name declared. Once God said to Israel, "You are not My people," Israel stood where the nations stood, with no claim on God's favor. The promise to call "not My people" His people therefore reaches anyone in that condition. Israel's judgment did not shut the door on the world. God used it to show that His people would be formed by His call and mercy alone, and that calling reaches the nations too.

Peter makes the same connection when he writes to believers scattered across Asia Minor. They are those "who once were not a people but are now the people of God, who had not obtained mercy but now have obtained mercy" (1 Peter 2:10). Peter echoes both of Hosea's sign-names in a single sentence: Lo-Ammi in "not a people" and Lo-Ruhamah in "had not obtained mercy." For Peter, the reversal Hosea promised has happened in the church, where people from many backgrounds have been brought to God through Jesus Christ.

Two further connections follow from the larger story of Scripture. Neither is drawn explicitly from Hosea in the New Testament, but both grow out of it. The first concerns the title "sons of the living God" (Hosea 1:10). The New Testament teaches that people become God's children through faith in His Son (John 1:12; Galatians 3:26). The Son brings others into the family that Lo-Ammi's name had shut out. The second concerns the cost of the reversal. On the cross Jesus cried out, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Matthew 27:46; quoting Psalm 22:1). The One who was always God's beloved Son entered the darkness of being forsaken, so that those who deserved to hear "not My people" could hear "You are My people." Hosea does not predict the cross in these words. The gospel does show how God could pronounce the reversal without pretending that sin did not matter.

Hosea 1:11 adds one more thread: Judah and Israel will be gathered together and "appoint for themselves one head." Hosea later says that Israel will return and seek "David their king" in the latter days (Hosea 3:5). Read in light of the whole Bible, that single head is fulfilled in Jesus, the Son of David, under whom God gathers His scattered people into one.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Lo-Ammi teaches us to take sin seriously. Israel did not drift into the name "Not my people" overnight. The nation spent years enjoying God's gifts while giving the credit to Baal, until the Lord said of His people, "Me she forgot" (Hosea 2:13). The deepest loss in that story was not the grain, the wine, or even the land. It was God Himself. Persistent idolatry leads in the end to the loss of the relationship that defines who we are. When someone has learned to lean on God's gifts while ignoring the Giver, Hosea's warning is meant to wake them up.

Lo-Ammi also shows how near God's mercy stands to His judgment. Only one verse separates "you are not My people" from "You are sons of the living God" (Hosea 1:9–10). God did not soften the verdict, and the exile came. But His intention in judging Israel was to bring the nation back to Himself. Hosea 2 shows Him hedging up Israel's path with thorns so that she would say, "I will go and return to my first husband, For then it was better for me than now" (Hosea 2:7). Sometimes God's severity is the means by which He brings a wandering heart home.

For believers today, especially those who were never part of Israel, Lo-Ammi's name tells our own story. Peter's words describe every Christian: we "once were not a people but are now the people of God" (1 Peter 2:10). Nothing in us earned the change. God spoke a new name over people who had no claim on Him, in the very place where they stood condemned. That is reason for humility, because we were once outsiders, and also for confidence, because God's word of adoption does not depend on our worthiness. The proper response is the one Hosea foresaw. When God says, "You are My people," we answer, "You are my God!" (Hosea 2:23), and we give Him the loyalty that Israel gave to Baal.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Hosea 1:8–9 — Records Lo-Ammi's birth and God's command to name him, with the explanation that defines the name: "you are not My people, And I will not be your God."

  • Exodus 3:14; 6:7 — Establish the name "I AM" and the promise "I will take you as My people, and I will be your God," which Hosea 1:9 reverses.

  • Hosea 1:4–7 — The names of Jezreel and Lo-Ruhamah, which build toward Lo-Ammi as the final sign in the series.

  • Hosea 1:10–11 — The immediate promise that those called "not My people" will be called "sons of the living God," and that Israel and Judah will be gathered under one head.

  • Hosea 2:1 — Commands the name to be spoken without its negative: "My people."

  • Hosea 2:2–13 — Explains the unfaithfulness behind the judgment, using the language of a broken marriage and of gifts credited to Baal.

  • Hosea 2:14–23 — God's tender restoration of Israel, ending with the full reversal of the children's names.

  • Romans 9:24–26 — Paul applies Hosea's reversal to God's calling of Gentiles as well as Jews.

  • Romans 11:1–2, 25–29 — Shows that the judgment on Israel was not a permanent rejection of God's people.

  • 1 Peter 2:10 — Peter describes the church as those who were once "not a people" and had not obtained mercy but now have.
