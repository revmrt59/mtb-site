HEART, MIND, UNDERSTANDING - (lêb, Strong's H3820)

Summary

Lêb is the Old Testament's main word for the heart, and it covers far more than the English word usually does. A modern reader thinks of the heart as feelings, set against the "head." A Hebrew reader made no such split. The heart was where a person thought, understood, decided, desired, grieved, and rejoiced. That is why the NKJV sometimes renders lêb as "mind," "understanding," or "will," and why Proverbs describes a foolish man as one who "lacks understanding," literally one who lacks a heart. The same word can also name the center of a physical thing, such as the heart of the sea.

Scripture's verdict on the human heart is sober. Before the flood, "every intent of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually" (Genesis 6:5), and Jeremiah calls the heart "deceitful above all things." Pharaoh shows what a hardened heart does with God's word. Scripture also reveals that God has a heart of His own. It grieves over sin and churns with compassion for rebellious people. God draws near to the brokenhearted, and He promises to remove the heart of stone and give His people a new heart with His law written on it.

That promise comes to its fulfillment in Jesus Christ. He announced that He was sent to heal the brokenhearted, and the new covenant He inaugurated writes God's law on the heart. Because everything in a person's life flows from the heart, Scripture calls believers to guard it and to fill it with God's word.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lêb has a longer twin, lebab (H3824), and the two are used almost interchangeably. Several of the best-known "heart" verses actually use lebab. Examples include "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart" (Deuteronomy 6:5) and "a man after His own heart" (1 Samuel 13:14). This study follows lêb itself, which runs from Genesis to Malachi. It gathers most heavily in Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. In Exodus it clusters in two places: the hardening of Pharaoh's heart and the building of the tabernacle.

Hebrew had no separate everyday word that set "mind" against "heart." One word held the whole inner person, so translators have to choose an English word that fits each context.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The inner person as the seat of thought and understanding. To lack a heart is to lack sense. The adulterer "lacks understanding" (Proverbs 6:32), and God "takes away the understanding of the chiefs of the people of the earth" (Job 12:24).

2. The seat of will, resolve, and purpose. The heart is where a person decides. Pharaoh "hardened his heart" (Exodus 8:15), and Daniel resolved in his heart not to defile himself (Daniel 1:8).

3. The seat of emotion and inner disposition. Grief, joy, fear, and courage all belong to the heart. God Himself "was grieved in His heart" (Genesis 6:6).

4. The middle or center of something. The word can describe the core of a physical object. The depths congealed in the heart of the sea (Exodus 15:8), and Tyre's borders lay in the midst of the seas (Ezekiel 27:4). Second Samuel 18:14 uses the word for Absalom's physical heart and for the "midst" of the tree.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The wisdom books treat the heart as the place where wisdom lives or is missing. Proverbs keeps describing the fool as "devoid of understanding" (Proverbs 7:7; 10:13). A Hebrew reader heard in that phrase a person with no heart at the center to weigh choices. Wisdom also reaches the hands. The NKJV's "gifted artisans" at the tabernacle are literally the "wise of heart." God says He has "filled [them] with the spirit of wisdom" to make Aaron's garments (Exodus 28:3). At the same work, "all the women who were gifted artisans spun yarn with their hands" (Exodus 35:25). Skill, discernment, and moral sense all come from the same inner place.

One idiom is worth knowing. To "speak to the heart" means to comfort or reassure. The NKJV renders it as Joseph speaking "kindly" to his frightened brothers (Genesis 50:21), as God speaking "comfort" to Jerusalem (Isaiah 40:2), and as the Lord alluring Israel into the wilderness to speak comfort to her (Hosea 2:14). Each time, words are aimed at the inner person to change fear into trust.

The subject of a sentence also matters. When people are the subject, the heart is where they plan, boast, or turn aside. When God is the subject, He searches the heart, tests it, hardens it, or renews it (Jeremiah 17:10; Ezekiel 36:26). Scripture never pictures the heart as a sealed room. God sees into it and acts upon it.

4. Theological Significance

The heart is where a person is most truly himself. Before the flood, the LORD looked past the visible violence to "every intent of the thoughts of his heart" (Genesis 6:5). Jesus later made the same diagnosis: "from within, out of the heart of men, proceed evil thoughts" (Mark 7:21). Sin is a matter of what a person is at the center as well as what he does. That is why Jeremiah 17:9 asks of the heart, "Who can know it?" The next verse answers: the LORD searches the heart.

Scripture also speaks of God's own heart, and this changes how we read every judgment. The verse after Genesis 6:5 says the LORD "was grieved in His heart" (Genesis 6:6). God felt pain over the hearts He was about to judge. In Hosea 11:8, God sets His justice against His love for a stubborn Israel and declares that His heart churns within Him. He will not give His people up. God's heart also has purposes He intends to carry out. He promises shepherds "according to My heart" who will feed His people with knowledge and understanding (Jeremiah 3:15). The leaders God provides are meant to share what He desires.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 6:5–8:21 — The heart judgment could not cure. The flood narrative opens and closes with nearly the same verdict. Before the waters, man's heart was "only evil continually" (6:5). After the waters, as Noah's sacrifice rises, "the LORD said in His heart" that He would never again curse the ground, "although the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (8:21). The flood cleared the earth but did not change the human heart. The same diagnosis that once called for judgment now stands beside a promise of mercy. God's forbearance does not wait for people to improve. By the end of the flood account, a lasting cure has to come from somewhere other than judgment.

Exodus 4–14 — The hardened heart. Two strands run side by side in Pharaoh's story. God announces, "I will harden his heart" (Exodus 4:21; 7:3). Pharaoh also hardens his own heart. "When Pharaoh saw that there was relief, he hardened his heart" (8:15). After the hail stopped, "he sinned yet more; and he hardened his heart" (9:34). Relief from judgment became the occasion for more defiance. God's hardening confirmed and judged a resistance Pharaoh freely chose, and God used it to "multiply My signs and My wonders" (7:3). Centuries later, the Philistines warned each other not to harden their hearts as Pharaoh had done (1 Samuel 6:6). The prophets described Israel itself as stubborn and hard-hearted (Ezekiel 2:4; 3:7).

Ezekiel 36:26 and Jeremiah 31:33 — A new heart. The prophets do not ask a corrupt and stubborn heart to try harder. God promises to act on it directly. In Ezekiel, He will give a new heart and a new spirit. He will take the heart of stone out of His people's flesh and give them a heart of flesh (36:26). He also promises "one heart," an undivided one (11:19). The images are deliberately physical. Stone is cold, dead, and impervious, and nothing written on it can soak in. Flesh is alive, warm, and responsive to touch. In Jeremiah, God promises a heart to know Him (24:7) and announces a new covenant in which He writes His law on their hearts (31:33). The same people who could not obey from the inside will be given a new inside.

Psalm 51 and Isaiah 57:15 — The heart God draws near to. After his sin with Bathsheba, David asks God to create a clean heart in him (Psalm 51:10). The verb is the one used of God's creating in Genesis 1, though here it applies to the heart. David then says that God's acceptable sacrifices are a broken spirit and a broken and contrite heart, which God will not despise (51:17). Isaiah carries this to its most surprising point. The High and Lofty One who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy, dwells in the high and holy place. He also dwells with the contrite and humble, to revive the heart of the contrite (Isaiah 57:15). The proud heart keeps God at a distance, while the crushed heart is where He chooses to live. Psalm 34:18 says the same thing more simply: the LORD is near the brokenhearted.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament story of the heart builds steadily toward one need. Judgment did not change the heart (Genesis 8:21), and the law written on stone did not change it either. The prophets promised that God would do it Himself. Hebrews 8:10 quotes Jeremiah 31:33 directly and applies it to the new covenant of which Jesus is Mediator. The new heart is a central promise of the covenant Christ established by His blood, a promise the Spirit applies to believers.

In the synagogue at Nazareth, Jesus read Isaiah 61:1, which speaks of the Anointed One sent to heal the brokenhearted. He then declared that this Scripture was fulfilled that day (Luke 4:18–21). The God who dwells with the contrite in Isaiah 57 came in person to the broken.

Jesus also confirmed the diagnosis of Genesis 6 and Jeremiah 17. Evil comes "from within, out of the heart" (Mark 7:21). In the context of Mark 7, this means that washing hands and keeping food laws cannot reach the problem. Jeremiah's promise of shepherds "according to My heart" (Jeremiah 3:15) finds its fullest answer in the Good Shepherd, though the New Testament does not cite that verse directly. Malachi closes the prophets with a promise to turn the hearts of fathers to their children and the hearts of children to their fathers (Malachi 4:6). Luke applies that promise to John the Baptist as he prepares the way for the Lord (Luke 1:17). The renewal of hearts was the door through which the Messiah came.

7. Spirit & Life

"Keep your heart with all diligence," Proverbs urges, because the issues of life spring from it (Proverbs 4:23). The verb pictures a watchman guarding a gate. Proverbs does not tell us to guard the heart by emptying it. We guard it by filling it. Write mercy and truth on the tablet of your heart (3:3). Trust the LORD with all your heart instead of leaning on your own understanding (3:5). The psalmist hid God's word in his heart so that he would not sin against Him (Psalm 119:11). A heart stocked with God's word has something to draw on when temptation comes.

The deeper discovery is about which hearts God welcomes. Pharaoh's story warns that relief can harden us. When the pressure lifts, we can drift back to what we wanted before. David's story shows the other way. A heart broken over its own sin is exactly the kind God revives. No one has to present Him with a repaired heart first. He asks for an honest one and promises to do the creating Himself. When a believer finds the old stubbornness still present, the right response is to ask for what Ezekiel promised: a heart of flesh, soft enough for God's word to be written on it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 12:2 — Flatterers speak with a double heart, literally "a heart and a heart." They show one center in public and keep another in private.

  • 1 Kings 3:9 — Solomon asks for an understanding heart to judge God's people and discern between good and evil. The request links the heart directly with judgment and discernment.

  • Daniel 1:8 — Daniel purposed in his heart not to defile himself. Faithfulness in a foreign court began with a settled inner decision.

  • Psalm 27:8 — The psalmist's heart answers God's invitation to seek His face. The heart becomes the place where prayer starts.

  • Ecclesiastes 3:11 — God has put eternity in human hearts, so no earthly thing fully satisfies them.

  • Jeremiah 20:9 — When Jeremiah tried to stop speaking, God's word became like a burning fire shut up in his heart and bones.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H3824 (lebab), the longer form of the same word. It has the same range of meaning and appears in Deuteronomy 6:5 and 1 Samuel 13:14.

  • Canonical pairing: H7307 (ruach, "spirit"). Scripture pairs it with lêb in the promise of a new heart and a new spirit (Ezekiel 36:26) and in the contrite heart and broken spirit of Psalm 51:10, 17.

  • New Testament counterpart: G2588 (kardia, "heart"). This is a Greek word, not a Hebrew relative. It carries the same inner-person meaning in Mark 7:21 and Hebrews 8:10.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The heart as a tablet. Scripture directly pictures the heart as a writing surface, like the stone tablets of Sinai. Judah's sin is engraved with a pen of iron on the tablet of their heart (Jeremiah 17:1). Wisdom tells the reader to write mercy and truth on that same tablet (Proverbs 3:3). In the new covenant, God Himself does the writing (Jeremiah 31:33). Whatever is engraved there, whether sin or God's law, governs the life.

Stone and flesh. In Ezekiel 11:19 and 36:26, stone stands for a heart that is dead and closed to God, and flesh stands for one that is living and responsive. Elsewhere in Scripture "flesh" can describe fallen human weakness, but in this image it is wholly positive.

The uncircumcised heart. God warns that being circumcised in the body means nothing if Israel is uncircumcised in heart (Jeremiah 9:26). Ezekiel condemns bringing foreigners who were uncircumcised in heart into God's sanctuary (Ezekiel 44:7, 9). The outward sign of the covenant with Abraham is applied to the inner person. A heart still covered and closed to God makes the outward mark empty.

The heart as a guarded city and storehouse. Proverbs 4:23 and Psalm 119:11 together picture the heart as a stronghold to defend and a treasury to fill, as developed in Section 7.

Two further connections are offered as reflections rather than stated symbolism. First, the "wise-hearted" craftsmen of the tabernacle received wisdom from God in their hearts to build His dwelling (Exodus 31:6; 36:1–2). This suggests the heart as the place where God's design takes shape through human skill. Second, Psalm 51:16–17 sets the contrite heart beside burnt offerings. It invites the reader to see the broken heart as the altar where acceptable sacrifice is now offered, though the psalm does not use that image itself.
