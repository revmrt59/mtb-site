RAVISHED, MADE WISE, MADE CAKES - (lâbab, Strong's H3823)

Summary

The Hebrew verb lâbab is built from the ordinary Old Testament word for "heart." It appears only five times, and those few uses carry two very different meanings. In Job and the Song of Solomon, it describes something happening to a person's heart. In one case, a foolish man would have to "get a heart" and become wise. In the other, a bridegroom confesses that his bride has captured his heart completely: "You have ravished my heart" (Song of Songs 4:9). Here "ravished" means enchanted or carried away by love, with no sense of violence. In 2 Samuel 13, a word that looks and sounds the same means "to make cakes." That use comes from a separate Hebrew root connected to baking and describes Tamar preparing food for her brother Amnon.

This small word opens onto large realities. Job 11:12 shows that wisdom cannot be produced from human emptiness. A real heart for God has to be given. Song of Songs 4:9 shows a lover so moved by his beloved that a single glance from her undoes him. Read alongside the Bible's portrait of God rejoicing over His people and of Christ as the Bridegroom of the church, that verse lets believers see something they rarely dare to believe. The Lord is not merely patient with those He has redeemed. He delights in them. The dark story in 2 Samuel 13, where a sister's kindness is exploited, shows by contrast how badly counterfeit "love" can corrupt what God made good.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lâbab is a Hebrew verb formed from lēb or lēbāb, the common words for "heart." In Hebrew thought, the heart is the center of the whole inner person. It includes thinking and understanding as well as feeling and desire. A verb built on "heart" can therefore describe gaining understanding or having one's affections stirred.

A second Hebrew root with identical letters is connected to a word for a kind of cake, and from it comes the verb "to make cakes." Strong's places both under one number. The two words share spelling and sound but differ in origin and meaning. The word appears in narrative (2 Samuel 13), in wisdom poetry (Job 11), and in love poetry (Song of Solomon 4).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The uses of lâbab fall into three distinct senses:

1. To gain a heart, become wise. This means acquiring understanding that was missing. "For an empty-headed man will be wise" (Job 11:12).

2. To capture or enchant the heart. This means being moved and carried away by love. "You have ravished my heart" (Song of Songs 4:9).

3. To make cakes, bake. This sense comes from the separate root tied to a type of cake. Tamar "made cakes in his sight, and baked the cakes" (2 Samuel 13:8).

The first two senses both describe something happening to the inner person. The third concerns cooking and should not be blended with them.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Job, the heart sense concerns understanding. Zophar describes a man with nothing inside and asks whether he could ever become wise. In the Song, the same heart-centered verb concerns love. The bridegroom does not gain understanding. He loses his composure.

The Song repeats the verb within one verse: "You have ravished my heart... You have ravished my heart." Hebrew poetry often uses repetition for emphasis, and here it slows the reader down. The bridegroom says it twice because once is not enough to express what has happened to him.

In 2 Samuel 13, Amnon asks that Tamar "make a couple of cakes for me in my sight" (13:6), and verse 8 records her doing it. The phrase "in my sight," repeated in both verses, is a narrative detail that matters. Amnon wants to watch her.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these passages say something important about the human heart and the heart of God. Job 11:12 assumes that a person can be hollow at the center, lacking the understanding needed to know God's ways. Jeremiah 17:9 states the diagnosis plainly: "The heart is deceitful above all things, And desperately wicked; Who can know it?" Wisdom begins with "the fear of the LORD" (Proverbs 9:10). It does not begin with human cleverness.

The Song then shows love strong enough to overwhelm the one who loves. Scripture elsewhere applies this kind of rejoicing love to God Himself: "He will rejoice over you with gladness, He will quiet you with His love, He will rejoice over you with singing" (Zephaniah 3:17). The God of the Bible is not cold toward His people. His love has warmth, gladness, and song in it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Job 11:12 — A heart that must be given. Zophar says, "For an empty-headed man will be wise, When a wild donkey's colt is born a man." To "be wise" here is to "get a heart." The comparison is meant to sound impossible. A wild donkey will never give birth to a human being, and a hollow man will never manufacture wisdom out of himself. Zophar misapplied this to Job, and God later rebuked Job's friends for not speaking rightly of Him (Job 42:7, paraphrased). Even so, Zophar's picture of human helplessness is true in itself. Gaining a real heart toward God requires a change as radical as a change of nature. That is exactly what God promises: "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you; I will take the heart of stone out of your flesh and give you a heart of flesh" (Ezekiel 36:26). In Job the change is impossible for man, and in Ezekiel God undertakes it Himself.

Song of Songs 4:9 — The captured heart. "You have ravished my heart, My sister, my spouse; You have ravished my heart With one look of your eyes, With one link of your necklace." Notice how little it takes. He does not need a long display. One glance and one link of her necklace are enough. "My sister, my spouse" joins family closeness to married love, so she is both kin and bride. The bridegroom is not describing a woman who has made herself impressive. He is describing what her attention does to him. Her look is turned toward him, and that look undoes him. The repeated "You have ravished my heart" shows a lover who has willingly let his beloved move him.

2 Samuel 13:6–10 — Kindness turned into a trap. Amnon, acting on the advice of the "very crafty" Jonadab (13:3), pretends to be sick and asks that Tamar make cakes for him. David sends her, and she kneads flour, makes cakes "in his sight," and bakes them (13:8). The act was an ordinary family kindness, food prepared for a sick brother. Amnon uses it to clear the room and draw her into his bedroom (13:9–10), where he assaults her. Afterward his so-called love turns into hatred (13:15, paraphrased). The cakes have nothing to do with the heart sense of the word, but the story shows how sin takes the good things of home and care and turns them into tools of deceit and domination.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song is love poetry between a bridegroom and his bride. The wider Bible gives believers good reason to hear in it the love of Christ for His people. Paul writes that "Christ also loved the church and gave Himself for her," so that "He might present her to Himself a glorious church" (Ephesians 5:25, 27). The Song does not state this fulfillment, but the Bridegroom whose heart is captured by His bride fits the New Testament's portrait of Christ closely. Jesus speaks of His love in the same deep terms: "As the Father loved Me, I also have loved you; abide in My love" (John 15:9).

The two heart senses also trace the gospel's movement. Humanity begins as Job's empty-headed man, unable to produce a heart for God. In the new covenant promised through Ezekiel and given through Christ and the Spirit, God supplies the new heart. Those who receive it become the bride whose look toward her Bridegroom delights Him. Amnon stands at the opposite pole. His desire took what it wanted and then despised it. Christ's love gives itself so that it can cleanse and present the beloved "holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27).

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers live as though they were still the empty-headed man of Job 11, straining to generate spiritual life they do not have. Job's wild donkey settles the matter: you cannot become what you are not by trying harder. Ezekiel's promise is the answer. God gives the heart, and the Spirit places it within you. The striving ends at the gift.

Song of Songs 4:9 then confronts a quieter unbelief. Many Christians assume the Lord tolerates them, putting up with their slow growth. The bridegroom's words point another way. "One look of your eyes" was enough. Your devotion may feel small and halting, but the Bridegroom is moved when your eyes turn toward Him. Paul feared that the Corinthians would be led away from their simple devotion to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2–3, paraphrased). The Song shows why that devotion matters so much. Undivided attention is what captures the Bridegroom's heart, and polished performance is not.

The contrast with Amnon also protects us. Desire that uses people, exploits kindness, and discards what it wanted is not love, however strongly it feels. Christ's love sets the standard. It delights in the beloved and gives itself for her good.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word occurs only five times in four verses, and all of them are discussed above. Job 11:12 and Song of Songs 4:9 carry the heart senses. 2 Samuel 13:6 ("make a couple of cakes for me in my sight") and 13:8 ("made cakes in his sight") record the baking sense within the account of Amnon and Tamar.

10. Other Relevant Insights

In modern English, "ravished" can suggest violence. In Song of Songs 4:9 it means enchanted, carried away with love. The surrounding uses sharpen the contrast. The Song's "my sister, my spouse" describes an honored, rightful love. In 2 Samuel 13, Amnon's desire for his sister Tamar ends in actual violation. One captivated heart gives and treasures, while the other seizes and destroys.
