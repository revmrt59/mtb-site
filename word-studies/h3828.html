FRANKINCENSE - (lᵉbôwnâh, Strong's H3828)

Summary

The Hebrew word lᵉbônâh names frankincense. Frankincense is a costly, sweet-smelling resin that Israel imported from distant lands such as Sheba. In the tabernacle it was one of the four ingredients of the holy incense. It was also laid on the grain offering and on the Bread of the Presence. When the priest burned it, it rose as "a sweet aroma to the LORD" and served as a "memorial" before Him (Leviticus 2:2; 24:7). Throughout Scripture, frankincense marks what God gladly receives from people who come to Him in devotion.

That is why its absence matters as much as its presence. God forbade frankincense on the sin offering of the poor and on the offering brought when a husband suspected his wife of unfaithfulness. Those two offerings called sin to mind, and nothing about sin is fragrant to God. The prophets later heard God refuse expensive frankincense that came from rebellious hearts. Yet Isaiah also foresaw the nations bringing frankincense to praise the LORD, and wise men from the East laid frankincense before the child Jesus. Taken together, these passages show that God delights in the fragrance of a life given to Him, and that He opens that worship to the whole world through Christ.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Frankincense is a hardened resin taken from Boswellia trees. Harvesters cut the bark, and the tree slowly releases a milky sap that dries into pale "tears." The Hebrew name is very likely related to the word for white (lāḇān). It probably describes that pale color, so the name tells us how the resin looked rather than what it meant. These trees did not grow in Israel. Frankincense came by caravan from southern Arabia, which is why Jeremiah calls it "frankincense from Sheba" (Jeremiah 6:20). It was a luxury.

The word appears in four kinds of settings. The Law gives instructions for worship (Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers). Two historical books describe how temple supplies were stored (1 Chronicles, Nehemiah). The Song of Solomon uses it in love poetry. The prophets Isaiah and Jeremiah use it in their warnings and promises. The NKJV usually translates the word as "frankincense." In six places it uses "incense," so English readers may not realize that frankincense is meant in verses such as Isaiah 60:6 and Jeremiah 17:26.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lᵉbônâh means frankincense, the aromatic resin itself. It is a different word from the general Hebrew term for incense or incense smoke (qᵉṭōreṯ), which often refers to the blended holy incense. Frankincense was one ingredient in that blend: "pure frankincense with these sweet spices; there shall be equal amounts of each" (Exodus 30:34). It could also be offered by itself, as on the grain offering. Frankincense has one meaning throughout the Old Testament. Whatever it comes to signify grows out of how Scripture uses it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In worship, frankincense is something added to an offering. It was laid on top of fine flour and oil, and it was set on the rows of holy bread. Priests and Levites guarded it among the temple supplies (1 Chronicles 9:29; Nehemiah 13:5). In the prophets, frankincense stands for worship at its most expensive. That makes it the obvious example when God exposes the emptiness of offerings brought without obedience. In the Song of Solomon, the same fragrance leaves the sanctuary and enters the language of love between a king and his bride. Myrrh is its regular partner there (Song 3:6; 4:6, 14).

4. Theological Significance

Frankincense teaches that God does more than accept offerings. He takes pleasure in them. Scripture says the burning portion was "a sweet aroma to the LORD." God chose this language of delight to describe what happens when His people come to Him on His terms. The offering is called a "memorial," which pictures God favorably remembering the worshiper.

God's holiness governs where that fragrance belongs. He withheld it from the two flour offerings that called sin to mind, and He rejects it when people try to substitute it for obedience. The God who delights in worship cannot be bribed by it. His purposes also reach beyond Israel. A resin carried in from foreign lands becomes, in Isaiah's vision, the gift the nations bring when they come to praise Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The memorial that rises (Leviticus 2:1–2; 24:7). The grain offering was a bloodless gift of fine flour. The worshiper poured oil on it and put frankincense on it. The priest then took "his handful of fine flour and oil with all the frankincense" and burned it "as a memorial on the altar." The word all matters. The priests ate most of the flour, but none of the frankincense was held back. The whole of it went up to God.

The Bread of the Presence shows the same pattern even more clearly. Twelve loaves, one for each tribe of Israel, lay before the LORD. The priests later ate the bread, and it was never burned. Instead, "pure frankincense" was placed on each row "that it may be on the bread for a memorial, an offering made by fire to the LORD" (Leviticus 24:7). The frankincense went into the fire on behalf of the bread. It rose before God as the fragrant representative of the people whom the loaves stood for. The phrase "pure frankincense" fits the setting: only what is pure is offered in God's presence.

The offerings with no fragrance (Leviticus 5:11; Numbers 5:15). A person too poor to bring an animal could bring flour as a sin offering. God commanded that no oil and no frankincense be put on it, "for it is a sin offering." The offering in a case of suspected adultery likewise had no oil or frankincense. Numbers explains that it was an offering that brought iniquity to remembrance. Both offerings involve remembrance, but of different things. The grain offering rose as a pleasing memorial before God. These two offerings called guilt to mind, and God would not let the sweetness of glad fellowship be mixed into them. That was not a rule for every sacrifice connected with sin. The burnt offering was accepted "to make atonement" for the worshiper, and it still rose as "a sweet aroma to the LORD" (Leviticus 1:4, 9).

Costly worship refused (Jeremiah 6:20; Isaiah 66:3; 43:23). In Jeremiah's day Judah ignored God's word but kept the temple supplied. God asked, "For what purpose to Me comes frankincense from Sheba, and sweet cane from a far country? Your burnt offerings are not acceptable, nor your sacrifices sweet to Me." The worshipers had paid a high price and traveled a long way, and God still said no. Isaiah goes further: "He who burns incense, as if he blesses an idol" (Isaiah 66:3). The verse explains why: "they have chosen their own ways." Worship offered on the worshiper's own terms becomes another kind of idolatry. Isaiah 43:23 adds a tender detail: God says, "I have not... wearied you with incense." He never asked for frankincense as a heavy burden laid on His people. His heart was always the point.

The storeroom reclaimed (Nehemiah 13:4–9). After the exile, the priest Eliashib cleared out the chamber where the grain offerings and frankincense were kept and gave it to Tobiah, a known enemy of the rebuilding. Nehemiah returned, threw Tobiah's household goods out, ordered the rooms cleansed, and brought back "the articles of the house of God, with the grain offering and the frankincense" (13:9). The frankincense could not go back until the enemy's goods were removed and the room was made clean again.

The fragrance of love (Song of Solomon 3:6; 4:6, 14). The king's procession comes out of the wilderness "like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense." Later the bride is pictured as an enclosed garden "with all trees of frankincense." The bridegroom resolves to go "to the mountain of myrrh and to the hill of frankincense" until the day breaks. The fragrance that rose to God at the altar now describes the delight a bridegroom and bride take in each other.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Paul supplies the key to all of this: Christ "has loved us and given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma" (Ephesians 5:2). Jesus is the true grain offering. He held nothing back from the Father, so every part of His life went up like "all the frankincense." He is also the fragrant memorial who stands before God on behalf of His people, as the frankincense did for the twelve loaves.

The offerings without fragrance point toward the cross. God "made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Jesus then cried out in the darkness, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Matthew 27:46). In that hour, He bore the judgment that the fragrance-less sin offering pictured. His willing obedience in bearing it was still a sweet aroma to the Father. Because Christ carried what had no sweetness, sinners can now be welcomed as fragrance before God.

The material itself offers an analogy, though Scripture never draws it directly. Frankincense flows only when a tree is cut. Isaiah says of Christ that "He was wounded for our transgressions" (Isaiah 53:5). The fragrance of our redemption came out of His wounds.

The story also widens to the nations. Jeremiah heard God refuse frankincense from Sheba. Isaiah saw a different day: "All those from Sheba shall come; they shall bring gold and incense, and they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). Malachi expected that "in every place incense shall be offered to My name" (Malachi 1:11). Matthew does not quote Isaiah, but the echo is hard to miss. Gentile wise men "fell down and worshiped Him," and "presented gifts to Him: gold, frankincense, and myrrh" (Matthew 2:11). The tribute Isaiah expected for Zion is laid at the feet of a child.

7. Spirit & Life

Frankincense shows that God wants your worship to be wholehearted. The priest burned all of it and kept none back. Hebrews names the offering God now asks for: "Therefore by Him let us continually offer the sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of our lips" (Hebrews 13:15). David prayed, "Let my prayer be set before You as incense" (Psalm 141:2). In heaven, the golden bowls of incense "are the prayers of the saints" (Revelation 5:8). Your prayers do not vanish into silence. God receives them as fragrance, and that is possible because they rise "by Him."

The Song of Solomon offers a fitting picture here, though the poem itself is about a bridegroom and his bride. The bridegroom says, "Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, I will go my way to the mountain of myrrh And to the hill of frankincense" (Song of Solomon 4:6). As an analogy, that hill of fragrance until morning suits what Scripture says of Christ now. He "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25), and He carries on that work through the night of this present age until the day of His return. Our prayers, set before God "as incense" (Psalm 141:2), rise to a Savior who is already praying for us.

The prophets warn that nothing expensive can stand in for obedience. A believer can give generously, serve faithfully, and sing well while still choosing his own ways. When that happens, God's answer through Jeremiah is the same today. Even so, Isaiah 43:23 shows that He is not a God who wears His people down with demands. What He asks for is the heart.

Nehemiah's storeroom speaks directly to anyone who belongs to Christ: "you are the temple of the living God" (2 Corinthians 6:16). Compromise rarely kicks the door in. Like Tobiah, it usually moves in by permission, through a friendship with the wrong influence. The remedy is also the same: put the intruder's belongings out, let the room be cleansed, and bring the frankincense back. Finally, the Song of Solomon reminds us that Christ does not receive His people with mere tolerance. He finds His church as lovely as a garden full of frankincense, because He gave Himself to present her "holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Chronicles 9:29 – Certain Levites were appointed over the temple supplies, including the frankincense. Even the storage of worship materials was a holy trust.

  • Leviticus 6:15 – The priest's instructions repeat that all the frankincense goes up with the memorial portion of the grain offering.

  • Jeremiah 17:26 – God promised that if Judah kept the Sabbath, people would come from every region "bringing burnt offerings and sacrifices, grain offerings and incense, bringing sacrifices of praise to the house of the LORD." Obedience and glad worship go together.

  • Jeremiah 41:5 – After the temple fell, eighty grieving men came carrying grain offerings and frankincense to the house of the LORD, and most of them were murdered. Their devotion survived even the ruin of Jerusalem.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H3836 (lāḇān), white. The name frankincense is very likely drawn from this word, describing the resin's pale color.

  • Distinct related word: H7004 (qᵉṭōreṯ), incense. This is the blended holy incense, of which frankincense was one ingredient (Exodus 30:34–35).

  • Canonical pairings: H8081 (šemen), oil, which was poured on the grain offering alongside frankincense and withheld with it from the sin offering (Leviticus 2:1; 5:11). H4503 (minḥâ), grain offering, the offering frankincense most often accompanied (Leviticus 2:1). H0234 (ʾazkārâ), memorial portion, the burned portion that rose with the frankincense (Leviticus 2:2). H4753 (mōr), myrrh, frankincense's regular partner in the Song (Song 3:6; 4:6, 14).
