MOON - (lᵉbânâh, Strong's H3842)

Summary

The Hebrew word lᵉbânâh means moon. It is a rare, poetic name for the moon that pictures it as the white one, the pale light that rules the night sky. Ordinary Hebrew had another word for the moon, the one tied to months and calendars. Scripture uses lᵉbânâh only three times, and each time the moon appears beside the sun at its most beautiful and most glorious. In the Song of Solomon the bride is "fair as the moon." In Isaiah the moon shines as brightly as the sun on the day God heals His people. Isaiah also sees a day when the moon is "disgraced," because the Lord's own reign outshines it.

Together these three verses form a clear progression. The moon at its loveliest becomes a picture of beauty that leaves people in awe. The moon at its brightest becomes a sign of a healed creation. Even that brightened creation then gives way before the presence of God Himself. The Bible's final picture of the city of God completes the progression: it has no need of sun or moon, "for the glory of God illuminated it. The Lamb is its light" (Revelation 21:23). Creation's brightest night-light points beyond itself to the God who made it.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lᵉbânâh comes from a Hebrew root meaning to be white. The name describes the moon by what anyone sees when they look up at night: a pale, shining disc. The word means simply moon, but its form gives it a poetic, descriptive tone.

The common Hebrew word for the moon is yârêach (H3394). It appears in passages about months and festivals and in many psalms. The creation account uses neither moon word; Genesis 1:16 calls the moon simply "the lesser light to rule the night." Lᵉbânâh never appears in the Law, the historical books, or the Psalms. It occurs only in the Song of Solomon and Isaiah. In all three verses it is paired with chammâh (H2535), an equally poetic word for the sun meaning the hot one. The white one and the hot one appear side by side whenever Scripture wants to speak of the heavens in their full splendor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lᵉbânâh has one meaning: the moon, named for its white brightness. The word does not carry the timekeeping associations of the ordinary moon word. Each time it appears, the moon serves as a standard of radiant beauty, the brightest light the night sky can show. What that light means in each passage comes from the passage itself, not from the word.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The three uses fall into two settings.

In love poetry, the moon is a comparison for beauty. Song of Solomon 6:10 says the bride is "fair as the moon."

In Isaiah's prophecies about the coming day of the Lord, the moon serves as a measure of glory, in two directions. In Isaiah 30:26 its light rises to equal the sun's. In Isaiah 24:23 it is "disgraced" before the reigning Lord. In both settings the moon stands for the highest glory found in created things. In Isaiah, that glory is then measured against God.

4. Theological Significance

These three verses show that God made creation genuinely glorious, and that its glory remains created and borrowed. Elsewhere Scripture gives the moon a steady, faithful role. "He appointed the moon for seasons" (Psalm 104:19), and Israel's calendar of feasts followed its phases: "Blow the trumpet at the time of the New Moon" (Psalm 81:3). God even used the moon's regular return as a pledge of His promise to David: "It shall be established forever like the moon, Even like the faithful witness in the sky" (Psalm 89:37). In Jeremiah 31:35–36, the fixed "ordinances of the moon and the stars" vouch for Israel's lasting place before God.

The poetic lᵉbânâh passages carry the theme further. The same moon that marks time and bears witness to God's faithfulness will one day be brightened beyond nature by His healing, and then outshone by His presence. The moon's faithfulness reflects the faithfulness of the God who appointed it. Its glory is real, but it is never ultimate.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 6:10 — beauty that inspires awe. "Who is she who looks forth as the morning, Fair as the moon, Clear as the sun, Awesome as an army with banners?" The images climb as the verse goes on. First comes the soft light of dawn, then the white moon commanding the night, then the full blaze of the sun. Finally the comparison leaves the sky altogether for a host marching under its standards. The bride's beauty is pure and luminous like the moon, and it is also overwhelming. Like moonlight cutting through the dark, it holds the gaze and makes people stop.

Isaiah 30:26 — healing that brightens the heavens. "The light of the moon will be as the light of the sun, And the light of the sun will be sevenfold... In the day that the LORD binds up the bruise of His people And heals the stroke of their wound." Isaiah ties the brightening of the heavens directly to the bandaging of wounds. The God who handles bruises like a physician is the God who multiplies the light of the sun. This healing follows a promise of patient grace: "the LORD will wait, that He may be gracious to you" (Isaiah 30:18). When God restores His people, creation itself is lifted with them. The moon at night becomes as bright as the noonday sun.

Isaiah 24:23 — glory that outshines the moon. "Then the moon will be disgraced And the sun ashamed; For the LORD of hosts will reign On Mount Zion and in Jerusalem And before His elders, gloriously." Just before this, the Lord punishes "the host of exalted ones" on high together with "the kings of the earth" (24:21). The sun and moon are not condemned with those powers. They are shamed the way a lamp looks dim beside the noon sun. In their own sphere they are the brightest things in creation, and when the Lord reigns openly in Zion their light becomes embarrassing by comparison. Nations had long bowed to the sun and moon as gods. Here the two lights that seemed most divine are set aside by the God they could never rival.

Reading Isaiah 30 and 24 together. At first the two prophecies seem to pull in opposite directions: in one the moon is magnified, and in the other it is disgraced. Read together, they describe one future. Creation will be healed and filled with light beyond anything it has known. Yet even that sevenfold-bright creation will pale before the Creator Himself. The highest glory of the restored world is still only the setting for His glory.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets also saw the moon fail. "The sun shall be turned into darkness, And the moon into blood, Before the coming of the great and awesome day of the LORD" (Joel 2:31). Peter quoted this prophecy at Pentecost (Acts 2:20). John saw it again when "the moon became like blood" (Revelation 6:12). The steady witness in the sky falters when God shakes the old order in judgment.

Isaiah's lᵉbânâh prophecies reach past that darkening to what follows. Isaiah later promises, "Nor for brightness shall the moon give light to you; But the LORD will be to you an everlasting light" (Isaiah 60:19). John sees that promise fulfilled in the New Jerusalem: "The city had no need of the sun or of the moon to shine in it, for the glory of God illuminated it. The Lamb is its light" (Revelation 21:23). The Lord of hosts reigning gloriously in Zion has a face and a name. He is the Lamb who was slain.

Paul writes that creation "was subjected to futility... in hope" and "will be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God" (Romans 8:20–21). Isaiah 30 pictures that liberation as a moon blazing like the sun. Revelation shows where it ends. The moon that once marked Israel's feasts and pledged God's faithfulness to David's line gives way to the Son of David Himself, whose presence is the light of the city.

7. Spirit & Life

Scripture allows us to admire the moon. The Song of Solomon reaches for it to praise a bride, and that kind of wonder honors the God who made the night sky. Job, however, knew the danger hidden in that admiration. He refused to let his heart be "secretly enticed" when he saw "the moon moving in brightness," because that would mean he "denied God who is above" (Job 31:26–28). Beauty that should lead us to worship God can instead become the object of our worship. Job shows the safeguard: let the brightness send your heart upward to the One who made it.

Isaiah 30 offers deep comfort to wounded people. God's healing is personal, a binding of "the bruise of His people," and it is also immense enough to brighten the sun. Your wounds are not too small for His notice, and His remedy is not too small for the whole created order.

Christians have long seen the moon as a picture of the church. The moon has no light of its own, yet it brightens the dark. This is an analogy rather than the teaching of these verses. Still, it fits Paul's call for believers to shine as lights in a dark world (Philippians 2:15). For now we reflect a light that comes from Christ. One day we will stand in that light directly, in a city where no lesser light is needed. Our hope is more than a healed universe. Our hope is the presence of the God who outshines it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All three occurrences of lᵉbânâh (Song of Solomon 6:10; Isaiah 24:23; 30:26) are discussed above. The following verses use other words for the moon and add to the larger biblical picture:

  • Psalm 121:6 — "The sun shall not strike you by day, Nor the moon by night." God's care covers the dangers of the night hours as well as the heat of the day.

  • Isaiah 13:10 — "The moon will not cause its light to shine." In this judgment on Babylon, the moon fails in its appointed task.

  • Revelation 12:1 — "A woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet." The image echoes Joseph's dream of the sun, moon, and stars bowing to him (Genesis 37:9). It portrays God's people raised above the changing order of time.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3394 (yârêach) — a distinct Hebrew word, the ordinary term for the moon, used for months, seasons, and the calendar (Psalm 104:19). It refers to the same moon but is not a related word.

  • H2535 (chammâh) — a canonical pairing. This poetic word for the sun, the hot one, appears alongside lᵉbânâh in all three of its occurrences (Song of Solomon 6:10; Isaiah 24:23; 30:26).

  • G4582 (selēnē) — the Greek word for the moon, connected by theme. It appears in New Testament passages that continue Isaiah's imagery (Revelation 21:23).
