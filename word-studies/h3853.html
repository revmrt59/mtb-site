LEHABIM - (Lᵉhâbîym, Strong's H3853)

Summary

Lehabim is the name of an ancient people descended from Mizraim, the biblical name for Egypt, who was a son of Ham. The word appears only twice in the Bible, both times in the same line of the "Table of Nations": "Mizraim begot Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim" (Genesis 10:13; 1 Chronicles 1:11). It carries no theological meaning of its own. It names a family of peoples who settled in and around the Nile Valley after the Flood. Many students of the Bible identify them with the Lubim, the Libyans of North Africa, who appear later in Israel's history.

The name matters because of where God placed it. Genesis records it as part of His account of how humanity spread across the earth, and Chronicles repeats it centuries later for a people rebuilding after exile. If the Lehabim are the Libyans, the name on that ancient list later became an army marching against Judah, and that army was defeated when King Asa relied on the LORD. A single name in a genealogy shows that God knows where every nation came from long before any of them stand in His people's path.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lᵉhâbîym is a Hebrew proper noun. Its -im ending marks it as a plural name for a people, the same pattern found in its neighbors on the list, Ludim and Anamim. The word belongs to one kind of biblical setting only: the genealogy of the nations after the Flood. It never appears in a story, a psalm, or a prophecy.

Genesis 10 traces the descendants of Noah's three sons. Through Ham came Mizraim, and from Mizraim came a cluster of peoples who lived in and around Egypt. The Lehabim are third in that cluster. Their usual identification with the Lubim (Libyans), who lived west of the Nile, rests on geography and on the close resemblance of the names. Standard older reference works accept it, though the Bible never states the link outright. The connection is probable rather than certain, and the study treats it that way.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one sense. It names a North African people descended from Mizraim, listed among the peoples of Egypt's family line (Genesis 10:13). The name does not describe a quality or an action, and nothing should be read into it from its sound or possible roots. Its meaning is its identity: a real people with a real origin, remembered in the record of the nations.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Both occurrences use identical NKJV wording, and both sit in genealogical lists. In Genesis the list follows the Flood and comes just before Babel, explaining how the earth was repopulated. In 1 Chronicles the same names open the long genealogy that runs from Adam to the families of Israel who returned from Babylon.

The only grammatical point that helps is the plural form. "Lehabim" does not name one son of Mizraim the way "Canaan" names one son of Ham. It names a people. Genesis 10 is recording the beginnings of nations as well as the descent of individuals, and the Lehabim are counted among those nations.

4. Theological Significance

The Table of Nations shows God as the Lord of all peoples and not the God of Israel alone. Before Abraham is even introduced, Scripture pauses to name the families of the earth, including obscure North African tribes that Israel would rarely meet. The Lehabim have no recorded role in the story of redemption up to that point, yet God's Word records them. That is a theological statement in itself. God's attention reaches peoples who play no visible role in His dealings with Israel.

The list also shows that the nations have one shared origin. The Lehabim, the Canaanites, and the line of Shem that leads to Abraham all go back to Noah's family. Paul later states openly what Genesis 10 implies, that God "has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26). Wherever the Lehabim settled, whether west of the Nile as the probable Libyan identification suggests or elsewhere, their place was appointed within God's ordering of history, not an accident of migration.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:13 — A people with a place on God's map. In context the Lehabim stand between the Flood and Babel. God had told Noah's family to fill the earth, and Genesis 10 shows that command being carried out family by family. The sons of Mizraim spread through the lands that became Egypt and its neighbors. Nothing more is said about most of these names. Genesis records them as part of God's account of the human race He had preserved through the Flood. Even a people mentioned once has a documented beginning.

1 Chronicles 1:11 — The same name, remembered again. The Chronicler wrote for Israelites who had come back from Babylon to a ruined land and a diminished identity. He began his history with Adam and worked through the nations, repeating this line from Genesis word for word. For a small, recovering community the effect was steadying. Their story did not begin with defeat and exile. It belonged to an account God had been keeping since the first man, and that account still held the name of a distant African people. If God's record had not lost the Lehabim, it had not lost Israel. The Chronicler framed Israel's particular calling inside God's knowledge of the whole world, so that the returning exiles could see their place in a history that had never come apart.

2 Chronicles 16:8 — When the names become an army. The word Lehabim does not appear here, but the Lubim do, and on the likely identification they are the same people. The Lubim first enter Judah's history with Pharaoh Shishak, who invaded in Rehoboam's day with Lubim among his forces (2 Chronicles 12:3). Later the prophet Hanani confronted King Asa. Hanani reminded him that the Ethiopians and the Lubim had come against him as a huge army, and yet the LORD delivered them into his hand because Asa relied on Him (paraphrase of 2 Chronicles 16:8). Hanani's point was a rebuke. Asa had just paid the king of Syria for protection instead of trusting the God who had already given him victory over a far larger threat.

Seen alongside Genesis 10, the passage gains depth. If the identification holds, the people God listed quietly among Mizraim's sons grew into a military force that threatened a king of Judah. Their size did not change who ruled history. The God who recorded their beginning decided the outcome of their campaign, and the only question that mattered for Asa was whether he would rely on that God.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Genesis 10 is the backdrop for the promise of Genesis 12. Right after Scripture names the nations and tells of their scattering at Babel, God calls Abraham and promises that in him all the families of the earth will be blessed. The Lehabim are among those families. The promise to one man was always meant to reach the peoples on this list.

The New Testament carries that thread toward fulfillment. At Pentecost, Luke names among the hearers people from the parts of Libya near Cyrene (Acts 2:10). On the day the Spirit was poured out, North Africans heard the wonderful works of God in their own languages. On the road to the cross, Simon of Cyrene, a man from that same Libyan region, carried the cross of Jesus (Matthew 27:32). These are connections of geography and theme, not claims about the Lehabim's descendants. Even so, they fit the pattern Scripture sets up. The region whose peoples first appear as a name in Genesis and later as Judah's enemies is present at the cross and at the birth of the church. Paul's sermon in Athens ties the thread together, since the God who set the boundaries of the nations did so "so that they should seek the Lord" (Acts 17:27).

7. Spirit & Life

Genealogies tempt readers to skim, but this line shows how carefully God attends to what we pass over. God preserved this obscure people's name and set Israel's story within His account of the nations, and when the Chronicler copied the line again, he kept every name. A God who keeps track of a forgotten North African tribe keeps track of His people. That was the comfort offered to the returning exiles, and it holds for anyone who feels small or lost in the sweep of history.

The Lehabim's likely later history carries a further lesson. If the Lehabim are the Lubim, a name listed quietly in Genesis became, centuries later, part of an army that put Judah in danger. The pressures that seem to arrive suddenly in a believer's life did not take God by surprise. He knew their beginnings long before they became a threat. Asa's story adds a sobering note, because he trusted God against the Lubim and then, facing Baasha's later threat, reached for a political alliance instead. Remembering past deliverance is meant to steady present trust. Hanani's rebuke shows that a believer can know what God has done and still fail to rely on Him the next time.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word occurs only in Genesis 10:13 and 1 Chronicles 1:11, and both have been discussed above. Passages about the Lubim, such as Nahum 3:9 ("Put and Lubim were your helpers"), use a different Hebrew word and belong to the related-word connection below.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3864 (Lûwbîym, Lubim / Libyans): a distinct lemma that is widely, though not certainly, identified with the same people. It appears in Judah's history and in prophecy (2 Chronicles 12:3; 16:8; Nahum 3:9). This is a probable historical identification, not a verified lexical cognate.
