LUD - (Lûwd, Strong's H3865)

Summary

Lud is first a person and then a people. Genesis names him as a son of Shem, listed with his brothers Elam, Asshur, Arphaxad, and Aram (Genesis 10:22; 1 Chronicles 1:17). The prophets later use the same name for a distant nation known for its soldiers, and English translations often render it "Lydia." Since ancient times that nation has been identified with the Lydians of western Asia Minor, though Scripture itself does not draw the map. Some scholars think the prophetic Lud may instead be an African people related to the Ludim, so Shem's son and every prophetic use need not be the same people. The meaning of the name Lud is uncertain. No reliable etymology has been established, and the name's importance comes from how Scripture uses it.

Lud appears in two very different settings. In Ezekiel, the men of Lud are hired fighters who hang shield and helmet in Tyre and stand with Egypt's allies, and they fall with the powers they served (Ezekiel 27:10; 30:5). In Isaiah's final chapter, God names "Lud, who draw the bow" among the far-off nations that "have not heard My fame nor seen My glory," and He promises to send messengers to them (Isaiah 66:19). A people remembered for fighting other nations' wars appears at the end of Isaiah as a destination for the news of God's glory. The New Testament shows the gospel reaching the region long associated with Lydia, and that gives this small name a place in the larger story of God gathering the nations.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name Lûwd (Strong's H3865) names a man and a people who may have descended from him. The first is an individual, the fourth-listed son of Shem in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:22) and in the Chronicler's genealogy (1 Chronicles 1:17). The second is a people called Lud in Isaiah 66:19, Ezekiel 27:10, and Ezekiel 30:5. Traditionally they are identified with Lud's descendants, the Lydians, but the Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon treats these prophetic uses as possibly an African people related to the Ludim, so their link to Shem's son is not certain. In both Ezekiel passages the NKJV translates the name as "Lydia."

The meaning of the name is unknown. No verified derivation is available, and this study does not offer a guessed one. What has come down is an identification rather than a definition. From at least the time of the Jewish historian Josephus, Lud has been associated with the Lydians, a kingdom in western Asia Minor whose capital was Sardis. Lydia became known for great wealth and for its soldiers, and it remained a major power until Cyrus of Persia conquered it in the sixth century BC. The identification is traditional and widely held, but it is not stated in Scripture.

Readers also need to know about a related name. Genesis 10:13 lists "Ludim" (H3866) among the descendants of Mizraim, who is Egypt: "Mizraim begot Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim." The Ludim belong to Ham's line, while Lud belongs to Shem's. The two have separate Strong's entries and should not be merged, though separate indexing does not by itself prove the peoples were historically unrelated. Jeremiah 46:9 uses the related name Ludim for bowmen in Egypt's army. Some older works treat the two names as one, and how closely the prophets' usage of them overlaps is still discussed.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

A son of Shem. Lud enters Scripture as one name in a family list. Genesis 10 traces how the nations spread out after the flood. Within Shem's line, the story of God's promise continues through Arphaxad toward Abraham (Genesis 11:10–26). Nothing more is said about Lud the man. His line moves out of the narrative, and his name returns only later as the name of a nation.

Soldiers in Tyre's service. Ezekiel 27 is a lament over Tyre, which the chapter pictures as a magnificent trading ship. Among the people who made the city glorious were foreign troops: "Those from Persia, Lydia, and Libya were in your army as men of war; they hung shield and helmet in you; they gave splendor to you" (Ezekiel 27:10). The shields hung in Tyre were part of its display of power. The same chapter ends with the ship broken and sunk in the seas (27:26–36), and the men who adorned it went down with it.

Allies of a doomed Egypt. Ezekiel 30 announces the day of the LORD against Egypt. Its allies share its fate: "Ethiopia, Libya, Lydia, all the mingled people, Chub, and the men of the lands who are allied, shall fall with them by the sword" (Ezekiel 30:5). If Lud here is Lydia, it is surprising at first to see a people from the north listed with African nations. Herodotus records Pharaohs of this era hiring soldiers from Asia Minor, though the troops he names are Ionians and Carians, so Lydian service in Egypt's ranks is plausible under the traditional identification rather than demonstrated. If Lud here is instead an African people related to the Ludim, the list is simply a roster of Egypt's neighbors. Either way, Ezekiel's list reflects a real coalition, and every part of it falls together.

A destination for God's glory. Isaiah's last chapter looks past judgment to a gathering of "all nations and tongues" (Isaiah 66:18, paraphrased). Then God says: "I will set a sign among them; and those among them who escape I will send to the nations: to Tarshish and Pul and Lud, who draw the bow, and Tubal and Javan, to the coastlands afar off who have not heard My fame nor seen My glory. And they shall declare My glory among the Gentiles" (Isaiah 66:19). God still identifies Lud by its archery, yet these bowmen now appear as hearers of His glory rather than as hired troops. The chapter goes on to describe the nations bringing God's people as an offering to His holy mountain, and God taking some of them as priests and Levites (66:20–21).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states two things plainly. The strength of Lud could not save Tyre or Egypt when God judged them (Ezekiel 27; 30), and Lud is named among the faraway nations to whom God will send word of His glory (Isaiah 66:19).

From these statements we can draw a careful conclusion. Lud's place in the prophets shows both the limits of human power and how far God's mercy reaches. Ezekiel does not condemn the men of Lud for being skilled. He shows that empires which trusted in bought strength were trusting something that could not stand on the day of the LORD. Isaiah then names Lud, very possibly the same distant people, in God's plan of blessing. They had no part in Israel's worship and, in God's own words, they had not seen His glory. Lud's distance from Israel, its history of serving God's enemies, and its ignorance of Him do not put it outside His purpose. The name the reader last saw falling with Egypt is one God intends to reach with His glory.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament does not quote Isaiah 66:19 or mention Lud by name. Even so, there is a real canonical development worth seeing. Isaiah foresaw messengers going out to distant coastlands to declare God's glory among the Gentiles. Acts records that kind of mission spreading across Asia Minor, until people throughout the province of Asia heard the word of the Lord (Acts 19:10, paraphrased). That province included the old Lydian heartland.

Two details make the connection more concrete. Revelation addresses a church in Sardis, the former Lydian capital (Revelation 3:1–6). In Philippi, Paul met "a certain woman named Lydia," who came from Thyatira, a city in the Lydian region: "The Lord opened her heart to heed the things spoken by Paul" (Acts 16:14). Luke does not present her as a fulfillment of Isaiah, so the link is thematic rather than explicitly declared. It is still fitting that the first convert named in Europe bore a name tied to the Lydian region; if Isaiah's Lud is Lydia, the name also recalls that far-off region. Christ, the light to the Gentiles, sent His word to the kind of place the prophet had in view, and the Lord opened hearts there.

There is also a thematic connection to the bow. Psalm 46:9 says of God, "He breaks the bow and cuts the spear in two." In Isaiah 66:19, the people "who draw the bow" are named as recipients of the news of His glory, though the same chapter also speaks of judgment (66:24) and does not promise that every individual of Lud will turn. God's peace comes by conquering hearts, and not only by silencing weapons.

5. Spirit & Life

Ezekiel's picture of Lud's shields hanging in Tyre raises a sober question for anyone with gifts and strength: whose splendor is it adding to? The men of Lud were good at what they did, and their skill made a proud city look magnificent until the city sank. Ability given to a system that sets itself against God will share that system's end. This is not a call to try harder. It is a call to belong to a different King.

Isaiah supplies the hope. God names Lud specifically, a people far from Israel with no knowledge of His fame. He remembers distant peoples, perhaps branches of Shem's family, and the hired soldiers of other nations' wars, and He sends messengers to them. The same grace reaches people today who feel far from Him. The Lord who opened Lydia's heart in Philippi still opens hearts, and those who were once strangers to His glory are given a share in declaring it.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:22; 1 Chronicles 1:17 establish Lud as a son of Shem.

  • Genesis 10:13 names the Ludim (H3866) as Egypt's descendants, a related but separately indexed name.

  • Ezekiel 27:10 shows Lud's soldiers adding to Tyre's splendor before its fall.

  • Ezekiel 30:5 places Lud among Egypt's allies who fall by the sword.

  • Isaiah 66:19 names Lud among the far-off nations to whom God sends word of His glory.

  • Acts 16:14; Revelation 3:1–6 show the gospel reaching people and churches in the Lydian region, a thematic and canonical development rather than an explicit fulfillment.

  • Psalm 46:9 is a thematic background for God's triumph over "the bow."
