UNLESS, IF NOT, HAD IT NOT BEEN - (lûwlêʼ, Strong's H3884)

Summary

This Hebrew word opens a door onto something that never happened. It introduces a condition contrary to fact, usually in the past: "If this had not been so…" The sentence that follows describes the result that would have come, and that result is almost always disaster. The NKJV renders it "unless," "if… not," or "were it not that." Most often the speaker is looking back with relief or gratitude: Israel would have become like Sodom, the psalmist would have gone down to the silence of death, a household would have been wiped out. The word names the one thing that stood between people and ruin, and very often that one thing is the LORD Himself. Reading it well means hearing these sentences as testimony about rescue rather than as warnings about the future.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

This word sets up an unreal condition, a situation the speaker knows did not happen. The full sentence has two parts. The first part, introduced by this word, names what did not happen or what was in fact present: "Unless the LORD had been my help." The second part spells out what would have followed: "My soul would soon have settled in silence" (Psalm 94:17). Because the condition is contrary to fact, the second half is contrary to fact too. The psalmist did not go silent. The structure lets a speaker hold up an averted outcome so the reader can see how close it came and what prevented it.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Looking back at an averted disaster ("unless," "if… not")

This is the word's main use. In Isaiah 1:9 the prophet says, "Unless the LORD of hosts Had left to us a very small remnant, We would have become like Sodom, We would have been made like Gomorrah." The word points to the one reason Judah still exists: God preserved a remnant. Psalm 124 builds an entire song of ascents on the same structure: "If it had not been the LORD who was on our side, When men rose up against us" (v. 2). The verses that follow describe being swallowed alive and swept away by floods.

The same pattern appears in ordinary human speech. Jacob tells Laban that if the God of his father had not been with him, Laban would have sent him away empty-handed (Genesis 31:42, paraphrased). Judah tells his father that if they had not delayed, they could have gone to Egypt and back twice by now (Genesis 43:10, paraphrased).

Within an oath or solemn declaration ("unless," "were it not that")

Sometimes the word appears inside a sworn statement. The oath gives weight to the averted outcome. After Abigail intercepts him, David swears by the LORD that unless she had hurried to meet him, not one male in Nabal's household would have been left by morning (1 Samuel 25:34, paraphrased). Elisha swears that if he did not respect the presence of Jehoshaphat king of Judah, he would not even look at Israel's king (2 Kings 3:14, paraphrased; NKJV "were it not that"). In both cases the word isolates the single factor that holds back a severe action.

A condition left without its conclusion

In Psalm 27:13 the Hebrew sentence begins with this word and then never states the consequence: "unless I had believed That I would see the goodness of the LORD In the land of the living." The NKJV supplies "I would have lost heart" to complete the thought. The outcome is left unspoken, and readers feel the weight of what David does not say.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Wherever this word appears, ask what it names as the deciding factor. The answers are striking. In Psalms 94 and 124 and in Isaiah 1, it is the LORD. In Psalm 106:23 it is Moses, God's chosen one, standing in the breach before Him so that He would not destroy His people (paraphrased). In Psalm 119:92 it is delight in God's law, without which the psalmist says he would have perished in his affliction (paraphrased). In Psalm 27:13 it is David's confidence that he would see the LORD's goodness while still alive.

These sentences teach God's people to remember their deliverance accurately. Psalm 124 says, "Let Israel now say." It invites the whole nation to speak the "if it had not been" aloud together. The word does not ask us to speculate about alternate histories. It asks us to look at the drop we were spared and to name who held us back from it. Gratitude grows sharper when we see exactly what God prevented.

4. Reading Guidance

English "unless" usually looks forward, as in "unless you repent." With this word, Scripture almost always looks backward at something that did not happen. Do not read these verses as threats. Also, NKJV "unless" can translate other Hebrew constructions. Jacob's "I will not let You go unless You bless me" (Genesis 32:26) uses a different expression and states a real future condition.
