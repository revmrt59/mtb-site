LODGE, ABIDE, COMPLAIN - (lûwn, Strong's H3885)

Summary

This Hebrew word most often describes stopping for the night: a traveler looking for shelter, an army making camp, a guest received into a home. Abraham's servant asks for room to lodge, and Ruth tells Naomi, "wherever you lodge, I will lodge." From that ordinary picture the poets and prophets draw vivid language. Weeping may "endure for a night" but must leave at morning (Psalm 30:5). The one who dwells in God's secret place "shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty" (Psalm 91:1). Jerusalem once had righteousness lodging in it (Isaiah 1:21), and later it let evil thoughts lodge within (Jeremiah 4:14).

The same Strong's number also covers a very different use: Israel's complaining in the wilderness. When the people grumbled about water, bread, and the dangers of Canaan, Moses told them plainly that their complaints were against the LORD. Taken together, these uses raise two searching questions. What are we letting stay with us? And where are we resting our trust? Scripture calls God's people to lodge under His shadow and warns them against the grumbling unbelief that treats His care as if it could not be trusted.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Strong's dictionary places two groups of meaning under H3885. The first is "to lodge" or "to pass the night." The second is "to murmur" or "to complain." Strong traced the second meaning from the first, but most modern Hebrew dictionaries treat them as two separate words that happen to be spelled alike, much as English "bear" can mean an animal or carrying a load. This study follows Strong's single entry for convenience. It makes no attempt to build a lesson that ties lodging to complaining.

The lodging sense runs through the narratives of Genesis, Joshua, Judges, and Samuel. It shows up in the poetry of Job, Psalms, and Proverbs, and in the prophets. The complaining sense is concentrated in the wilderness accounts of Exodus and Numbers, with one later echo in Joshua 9:18. In the ancient world, nightfall made shelter a matter of safety. A traveler without a host could be exposed to wild animals, thieves, or worse. That background gives the poetic uses their force.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To spend the night, lodge, or stay: to stop somewhere overnight, as Jacob did at Bethel (Genesis 28:11), or to remain somewhere for a time. By extension, the word can describe something that stays or rests in a place, such as weeping (Psalm 30:5) or a person resting under God's shadow (Psalm 91:1).

2. To complain or murmur: to voice resentful dissatisfaction against leaders and, through them, against God, as when "the people complained against Moses, saying, 'What shall we drink?'" (Exodus 15:24).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the narratives, lodging is usually literal. Laban's household offers Abraham's servant room to lodge (Genesis 24:23, 25). Israel camps at the Jordan before crossing (Joshua 3:1). Elijah spends the night in a cave at Horeb before the LORD speaks to him (1 Kings 19:9). Judges 19 uses the word more than any other chapter. A Levite and his concubine look in vain for someone to take them in, and when an old man finally does, the night ends in horror. The repeated word underlines how badly hospitality and safety had collapsed in Israel.

The Law uses the word for things that must not "remain" overnight. A hired worker's wages may not stay with the employer until morning (Leviticus 19:13). Sacrificial fat and Passover meat may not be kept over the night (Exodus 23:18; 34:25; Deuteronomy 16:4). A hanged body may not stay on the tree overnight (Deuteronomy 21:23).

In poetry and prophecy, the subject changes, and so does the picture. Weeping, righteousness, evil thoughts, and the believer himself become the ones who "lodge." When the meaning is complaining, the word is always aimed at someone: against Moses, against Aaron, against the leaders. In every case Scripture shows that the complaint finally reaches the LORD.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God who takes seriously both where His people rest and what they say about Him. He offers Himself as a lasting place of shelter. Moses prays, "Lord, You have been our dwelling place in all generations" (Psalm 90:1), and Psalm 91 pictures the believer lodging under His shadow. Jeremiah's lament that God seemed "like a traveler who turns aside to tarry for a night" (Jeremiah 14:8, paraphrased) shows how much Israel depended on His staying presence. The worst thing they could imagine was a God who only passed through.

The God who shelters also hears. "The LORD hears your complaints which you make against Him" (Exodus 16:8). Grumbling is never private, and it is never only about circumstances. It is a verdict on God's goodness. At the same time, His holiness extends to the overnight hours. He does not allow a poor man's wages to stay with the employer overnight, and He does not allow a curse to linger on the land. Even the smallest details of the Law show that He cares about what is permitted to stay.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The wilderness complaints (Exodus 15–17; Numbers 14; 16–17). At Marah the issue is water. In Exodus 16 it is food. The congregation "complained against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness" (Exodus 16:2). Moses corrects them: "Your complaints are not against us but against the LORD" (16:8). The people thought they were criticizing human leaders, but God counted it as speech about Himself. Numbers 14 makes the stakes clear. After the spies' report, the people wish they had died in Egypt (14:2), and the LORD asks, "How long will these people reject Me? And how long will they not believe Me, with all the signs which I have performed among them?" (14:11). The complaining was unbelief spoken aloud, and it cost that generation the land (14:29). In Numbers 16:41 the people blame Moses and Aaron for the deaths of Korah's rebels, and judgment breaks out. Aaron runs with incense and stands between the dead and the living. God then makes Aaron's rod bud so that the complaints against him would cease (17:5). God answered Israel's grumbling by confirming His chosen priest and by providing someone to stand between the people and judgment.

Ruth's vow (Ruth 1:16). "Wherever you lodge, I will lodge." Naomi has nothing to offer: no husband, no sons, no security. To share her nights is to share her poverty and exposure in a foreign land. Ruth binds her lodging to her loyalty: "Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God." Boaz later names what she has really done. She has come to the LORD God of Israel, "under whose wings you have come for refuge" (Ruth 2:12). Her willingness to sleep wherever Naomi slept led her to a far greater shelter.

Lodging under the Almighty (Psalm 91:1). "He who dwells in the secret place of the Most High Shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty." The night was the most dangerous time for an ancient traveler, and the psalm goes on to name terrors by night (91:5). The person who dwells with God does not merely stop by. He spends the night under God's shadow, at the hour of greatest exposure. Proverbs 19:23 extends the same promise: the one who fears the LORD "will abide in satisfaction; He will not be visited with evil." Psalm 25:13 adds that such a person "shall dwell in prosperity."

Weeping as an overnight guest (Psalm 30:5). "Weeping may endure for a night, But joy comes in the morning." Here sorrow is pictured as a lodger who has been given one night only. The verse sets God's anger, which lasts "but for a moment," beside His favor, which is "for life." Grief is real enough to keep someone awake, but it does not own the house.

What lodges in a city and a heart (Isaiah 1:21; Jeremiah 4:14). Isaiah grieves over Jerusalem: "Righteousness lodged in it, But now murderers." Righteousness had been the welcome resident, and murderers have taken its place. Jeremiah turns the same image inward: "How long shall your evil thoughts lodge within you?" Jerusalem has become an innkeeper giving a bed to wicked schemes, and the call is to "wash your heart from wickedness" and turn them out. Isaiah also gives the hope: God will restore the city until it is again called "the city of righteousness, the faithful city" (Isaiah 1:26).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Deuteronomy 21:23 says that a hanged man's body must not remain overnight on the tree, because the one hanged is accursed of God. Paul applies this law directly to Jesus in Galatians 3:13: Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us. John records that the body of Jesus was taken down before the Sabbath began (John 19:31). The One who bore the curse was not allowed to stay overnight on the tree, so the curse did not stay on the land.

The wilderness complaints point forward as well. Paul warns the Corinthians not to complain as some in the wilderness did and were destroyed (1 Corinthians 10:10). Aaron standing between the dead and the living foreshadows the greater High Priest who lives to intercede for His people. Ruth, who chose to lodge with Naomi, appears by name in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5).

The hope of lodging with God reaches its fullness in Christ. Jesus says, "Abide in Me, and I in you" (John 15:4). He uses a different Greek word, but He offers what Psalm 91 pictured: a lasting place to rest in the presence of God.

7. Spirit & Life

Every heart gives lodging to something. Jeremiah's question, "How long shall your evil thoughts lodge within you?", assumes that thoughts can be welcomed or refused. A resentment kept overnight, again and again, becomes a resident. Paul's word "nor give place to the devil" (Ephesians 4:27) comes just after his warning not to let the sun go down on your wrath. The two texts point in the same direction: deal with sin before it settles in.

The wilderness shows where an unexamined complaint leads. Israel's grumbling started over thirst and hunger, real needs, and ended in wishing for Egypt. The issue was never that they felt hunger. The issue was that they concluded God could not be trusted. We can bring our hunger to Him in honest prayer, as the psalmists do, without passing that verdict on His goodness.

For the believer walking through darkness, Psalm 30:5 and Psalm 91:1 belong together. Weeping may lodge for a night, but you are lodging under the shadow of the Almighty. The sorrow is the temporary guest, and God's favor is "for life."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 28:11 – Jacob, fleeing Esau, stops for the night at Bethel. On that unplanned night God reveals Himself in the dream of the ladder.

  • Genesis 32:13, 21 – Jacob lodges for the night before meeting Esau, and that night he wrestles with God.

  • Leviticus 19:13 – A hired worker's wages must not remain with the employer overnight. Delayed justice to the poor matters to God.

  • Joshua 9:18 – The congregation complains against the rulers over the treaty with Gibeon. The complaining sense appears again after the wilderness.

  • 2 Samuel 12:16 – David lies all night on the ground, pleading for his child.

  • Job 31:32 – Job insists that no stranger had to lodge in the street while he had a door to open.

  • Proverbs 15:31 – The ear that heeds life-giving correction will abide among the wise.
