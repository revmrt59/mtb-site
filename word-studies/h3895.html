JAW, JAWBONE, CHEEK - (lᵉchîy, Strong's H3895)

Summary

The Hebrew word lᵉchîy means the jaw or cheek, the lower side of the face of a person or an animal. It is an ordinary body word, but Scripture uses it at some of its most telling moments. A cheek can be struck in contempt, wet with tears, or admired in love. A jaw can be a weapon in Samson's hand, a predator's strength that God breaks, the place where a yoke rests on a working animal, or the place where God sets a hook in a proud empire.

Two themes rise above the rest. First, the cheek is the seat of a person's honor, so striking it is an act of contempt. Isaiah's Servant of the Lord says, "I gave... My cheeks to those who plucked out the beard," and in doing so He freely accepts the deepest insult, which points ahead to the shame Jesus bore. Second, the jaw is the seat of a beast's power, and God holds that power in His hand. He breaks the jaws of the wicked, hooks the jaws of Pharaoh, and, with surprising tenderness, lifts the yoke from the jaws of His own weary people so He can feed them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lᵉchîy is the Hebrew name for the jaw and the cheek. In Hebrew one word covers both the bone and the face that covers it. Context decides which English word fits best. The NKJV renders it "cheek," "cheeks," "jaw," "jaws," "jawbone," "cheekbone," and once "neck" (Hosea 11:4). In Judges 15 the place where Samson fought came to be called Lehi, meaning "Jawbone."

The word appears in law, history, psalms, love poetry, lament, and prophecy. In the ancient world, as in many cultures today, the face carried a person's honor. A slap on the cheek or a beard torn out was meant to shame the victim, and the pain was secondary.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The cheek of a human face, the visible side of the face where honor, beauty, and sorrow show. Lamentations 1:2 describes Jerusalem: "Her tears are on her cheeks."

2. The jaw or jawbone, especially of an animal, as a physical structure. It can be eaten, used as a tool, restrained with a hook, or fitted under a yoke. Samson takes up "a fresh jawbone of a donkey" (Judges 15:15, paraphrased).

The word itself means only the body part. The shame, the restraint, and the tenderness come from what happens to that part of the body in each passage.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's meaning shifts with what is done to the cheek or jaw and who does it.

When people strike the cheek, the act expresses contempt. Zedekiah, a false prophet, strikes the true prophet Micaiah on the cheek and mocks him by asking which way the Spirit went from him to speak to Micaiah (1 Kings 22:24; 2 Chronicles 18:23). Job's enemies "strike me reproachfully on the cheek" (Job 16:10).

When God strikes or hooks the jaw, the act shows His judgment and control over powerful enemies, who are pictured as beasts (Psalm 3:7; Ezekiel 29:4; 38:4).

When a yoke sits on the jaw, the setting is a working animal. That makes God's lifting of the yoke in Hosea 11:4 especially striking.

When the cheek is described in poetry, it displays the state of the heart. It may show beauty or grief.

4. Theological Significance

The jaw and cheek show God's justice toward the proud and His mercy toward the humble.

In Psalm 3, David is fleeing from his son Absalom, and he prays: "For You have struck all my enemies on the cheekbone; You have broken the teeth of the ungodly" (Psalm 3:7). When people strike a cheek, they mean to insult. When God strikes the cheekbone and breaks the teeth, He takes away the enemy's ability to bite. The picture is a predator that can no longer devour its prey. God does more than shame David's enemies. He disarms them.

The cheek also shows what has happened between God and His people. In the Song of Solomon the bride's "cheeks are lovely with ornaments" (Song 1:10). Her beloved's "cheeks are like a bed of spices" (Song 5:13). These are faces close enough to delight in each other. Lamentations gives the opposite picture. Jerusalem "weeps bitterly in the night, Her tears are on her cheeks; Among all her lovers She has none to comfort her" (Lamentations 1:2). The city that should have been a cherished bride has become a lonely widow. She turned to other "lovers," and they betrayed her. The cheek that should show the joy of being loved shows the grief of unfaithfulness and exile. Unfaithfulness to God leaves its mark on the face.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 50:6 and Micah 5:1 — the struck cheek of the Servant and the Judge. Micah foresees a day of siege when "They will strike the judge of Israel with a rod on the cheek" (Micah 5:1). The next verse turns to Bethlehem, from which the true Ruler of Israel will come. Isaiah's Servant goes further: "I gave My back to those who struck Me, And My cheeks to those who plucked out the beard; I did not hide My face from shame and spitting" (Isaiah 50:6).

The difference between these scenes and Micaiah's matters. Micaiah was struck against his will by a man trying to silence him. The Servant says "I gave." Tearing out a man's beard was one of the worst insults possible, and the Servant offers His cheeks for it. He does not turn His face away. In the surrounding verses He explains that He can do this because "the Lord GOD will help Me" (Isaiah 50:7, paraphrased). He entrusts His honor to God. In Him, the highest dignity accepts the lowest treatment on purpose.

Ezekiel 29:3–4 and 38:4 — hooks in the jaws of empires. God addresses Pharaoh as "O great monster who lies in the midst of his rivers, Who has said, 'My River is my own; I have made it for myself'" (Ezekiel 29:3). Pharaoh speaks as though he were the creator, and God answers with a fisherman's tool: "But I will put hooks in your jaws" (29:4). The king who claimed the Nile is dragged out of it like a river creature. The same language returns in the prophecy against Gog, whom God turns around and leads out with hooks in his jaws (Ezekiel 38:4).

This picture builds on Job 41:2, where God asks Job about Leviathan: "Can you put a reed through his nose, Or pierce his jaw with a hook?" No human can, but God can. Isaiah 30:28 adds that God sets "a bridle in the jaws of the people, Causing them to err." Even when hostile nations seem to choose their own course, God steers them toward His judgment. Proud powers become beasts on God's line. They are frightening to people, but they never move beyond His control.

Judges 15:15–19 — the jawbone and the spring. When the Spirit of the Lord comes on Samson, he takes up a fresh donkey's jawbone and strikes down a thousand Philistines (Judges 15:14–16). His weapon is a piece of a dead animal that could not even be eaten under the law (Leviticus 11). He then throws it away, and the place takes its name from it. Right after his victory, Samson nearly dies of thirst and calls on the Lord. "So God split the hollow place that is in Lehi, and water came out, and he drank; and his spirit returned, and he revived. Therefore he called its name En Hakkore" (Judges 15:19). En Hakkore means "spring of the one who called." Interpreters debate whether the water came from the bone itself or from a hollow in the ground at Lehi. Either way, the place named for the weapon becomes the place where God gives life. God won the battle with a worthless tool, and then He met His exhausted servant at the very spot where the fight had been fought.

Hosea 11:4 — the yoke lifted from the jaws. Throughout Scripture a yoke usually stands for hard labor and subjection to an enemy (for example, Deuteronomy 28:48). Hosea reverses that picture. God recalls Israel's youth: "I drew them with gentle cords, With bands of love, And I was to them as those who take the yoke from their neck. I stooped and fed them." The NKJV's "neck" translates lᵉchîy, the jaw. The image is a kind farmer who eases the yoke off his animal's jaw so it can eat, then bends down to feed it. God says this to a nation that kept running from Him (11:2). His heart toward them has always been to relieve their burden.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Gospels record the fulfillment of Isaiah 50 and Micah 5. At His trial Jesus was spat on, struck, and slapped (Matthew 26:67). Later soldiers struck His head with a reed (Matthew 27:30). The Judge of Israel was struck by those He had come to save. Like the Servant, Jesus did not hide His face. He took the shame that belonged to a bride whose cheeks were wet from her own unfaithfulness, so that she could be loved again.

The two pictures of the jaw meet at the cross. There Christ gave His cheek to those who struck Him, and there God set His hook in the jaws of every proud power that ruled through sin and death. Hosea's farmer who lifts the yoke points ahead to Jesus, who said His yoke is easy and His burden light (Matthew 11:29–30). The spring at Lehi offers a further connection, though it is a resonance and not a stated fulfillment. Out of a place marked by death and exhaustion, God opened water for the one who called on Him. Out of the death of His Son at the Place of a Skull, God pours out living water for all who call on Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Lamentations 3:30 gives counsel to someone suffering under God's discipline: "Let him give his cheek to the one who strikes him, And be full of reproach." The verses just before this one tell him to sit alone and keep silent, and the verses after it promise that the Lord will not cast off forever (3:28–31, paraphrased). Offering the cheek here is not weakness or approval of abuse. It is a refusal to defend one's own honor by force, because God will vindicate His people. Jesus carried this posture to completion, and He calls His disciples to turn the other cheek (Matthew 5:39). Believers can do this because their honor rests safely with the Father, as the Servant's did.

The same God who holds the jaws of the proud lifts the yoke from the jaws of His own people. A believer facing threats can remember that every hostile power works on God's line. A believer worn down can remember that God stoops to feed. Samson at Lehi shows that God does not abandon His servant after a victory. When Samson was exhausted and called out, God opened a spring where he stood.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 18:3 — "they shall give to the priest the shoulder, the cheeks, and the stomach." The jaw was part of the portion God gave from the people's sacrifices to support the priests who served Him.

  • Job 16:10 — "They strike me reproachfully on the cheek." Job, a righteous man, suffers the same contempt the Servant would later accept.

  • 1 Kings 22:24 — Zedekiah strikes Micaiah on the cheek for speaking the Lord's word. Faithful messengers often pay for the truth with their honor.

  • Song of Solomon 5:13 — "His cheeks are like a bed of spices." The bride delights in her beloved's face.
