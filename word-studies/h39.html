ABIMAEL - (ʼĂbîymâʼêl, Strong's H0039)

Summary

Abimael is one of the thirteen sons of Joktan, a descendant of Shem through Arphaxad, Salah, and Eber. He appears only twice in Scripture, both times as a single name in a list: once in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:28) and once in the Chronicler's matching genealogy (1 Chronicles 1:22). His name most likely means "My Father is God (El)," though the exact force of the middle syllable is debated. Some scholars read "Mael" as a tribal or regional name instead. Joktan's family settled "from Mesha as you go toward Sephar, the mountain of the east" (Genesis 10:30). That region is generally placed in southern Arabia, which would make Abimael the ancestor of a people living far from Israel's later borders.

Scripture records nothing Abimael said or did. Yet God placed his name in the record of the nations that came from Noah's sons after the Flood. That record is set just before the Lord calls Abram, through whom "all the families of the earth" would be blessed (Genesis 12:3). Abimael's quiet presence in the list is a small piece of a large truth. The God who chose one family never lost sight of the many families He had scattered across the earth, and the gospel's reach to "all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues" (Revelation 7:9) was in view from the beginning.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Abimael is an individual named in the line of Shem. Genesis 10:25 explains that Eber had two sons, Peleg, "for in his days the earth was divided," and Joktan. Abram's line runs through Peleg (Genesis 11:16–26), while Joktan fathered thirteen sons: Almodad, Sheleph, Hazarmaveth, Jerah, Hadoram, Uzal, Diklah, Obal, Abimael, Sheba, Ophir, Havilah, and Jobab (Genesis 10:26–29). Abimael is ninth in the list. Like most names in this part of Genesis 10, his name stands for both a man and the people descended from him. He should not be confused with Abimelech, a different name borne by Philistine kings and by Gideon's son.

The Hebrew name joins ʼab ("father") and ʼel ("God"), with the syllable ma between them. Read this way, the name probably means "My Father is God (El)." Some take the ma as a connecting particle known from ancient Semitic languages. Others suggest "Mael" was itself a tribal or local name, so that the name means something like "father of Mael." The first reading is more common, but the meaning is best described as probable rather than certain. Scripture offers no wordplay on the name.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Abimael's whole biblical record consists of his place in two genealogies. Genesis 10:28 reads simply, "Obal, Abimael, Sheba," and 1 Chronicles 1:22 gives the same sequence, "Ebal, Abimael, Sheba." The only difference is the alternate spelling of his brother's name. Genesis 10:30 gives the dwelling place of Joktan's sons, which is usually located in southern Arabia because of the Arabian associations of brothers such as Sheba, Ophir, and Hazarmaveth. The setting belongs to the generations after the Flood, when Noah's descendants spread out in obedience to God's word to "fill the earth" (Genesis 9:1).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Genesis 10 records the peoples that came from Noah's sons. Jewish and Christian tradition has often counted about seventy names in the list. The chapter comes right before God narrows His focus to Abram in Genesis 12. Its placement teaches something important: before the Lord set apart one family, He named the nations, located them, and traced their descent. Paul later states the principle directly, that God "has made from one blood every nation of men... and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26). Abimael's people were among those nations.

The Chronicler repeats the list centuries later, after the exile. Opening Israel's history with Adam and the nations reminded the returning remnant that their story belonged to God's dealings with the whole human race. Israel was not an isolated or forgotten tribe.

The name itself invites a careful observation. If it does mean "My Father is God," then a name honoring El was being given in a family outside Abram's line. That fits Scripture's account that humanity began with knowledge of the one Creator and later turned from Him (Romans 1:21–23). The name cannot prove what Abimael's family believed, though, because El was also used widely for gods across the ancient Near East.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never mentions Abimael. His importance lies in his membership among the nations of Genesis 10. These are the peoples Abram's offspring was meant to bless (Genesis 12:3). Paul identifies that offspring as Christ, through whom the blessing reaches the Gentiles (Galatians 3:8, 14, 16). John's vision of a multitude "of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9) shows the scattered nations gathered again around Jesus.

Abimael's brothers help fill in this picture. Ophir became known for gold brought to Solomon (1 Kings 9:28; 10:11). Psalm 72:10 pictures "the kings of Sheba and Seba" offering gifts to the righteous King. Two Shebas appear in Genesis 10 (verses 7 and 28), so it is not certain which one the psalm has in mind. Still, the broader pattern is clear. Distant peoples once listed only by name are pictured bringing their honor to God's King, and Revelation 21:24–26 describes "the glory and the honor of the nations" carried into the New Jerusalem. This is a thematic connection through his kin and region, not a direct statement about Abimael himself.

5. Spirit & Life

Abimael never speaks, fights, builds, or prays in the biblical record. His name survives because God chose to write it down, twice. The God who preserved this obscure name is the same God who sets the times and boundaries of every people (Acts 17:26). He did not forget the far-off families of Arabia while He worked out His promises through Abram. People who feel unnoticed can take courage from this. Our worth is not measured by how much of the story is about us. It rests on the God who knows us by name and who sent His Son for people from every nation.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:25–30: Places Abimael among Joktan's thirteen sons and describes where they lived.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:17–23: The Chronicler's parallel genealogy, which preserves his name for post-exilic Israel.

  • Genesis 12:3: God's promise to bless all the families of the earth through Abram.

  • Acts 17:26: God's sovereign ordering of every nation's times and boundaries.

  • Psalm 72:10; Revelation 21:24–26: The nations, including peoples linked with Joktan's line, bringing gifts and glory to God's King.

  • Revelation 7:9: People from all nations gathered before the Lamb.
