LEARN, TEACH, TRAIN - (lâmad, Strong's H3925)

Summary

The Hebrew verb lâmad is the Old Testament's main word for learning and teaching. In one form it means "to learn," and in another it means "to teach" or "to train." Scripture uses it for a soldier whose hands have been drilled for battle, a young animal broken to the yoke, and a child learning God's commandments at home. Biblical learning is never just storing information. A person has learned something when it has become part of how he lives, the way trained hands move without stopping to think.

The word carries some of the Bible's richest themes. In Deuteronomy, Moses ties Israel's future in the land to faithful teaching from God to Moses, from Moses to the people, and from parents to children, so that each generation might learn to fear the LORD. In the Psalms the word becomes a personal prayer: "Teach me Your statutes." The prophets warn that Israel learned the idolatry of the nations. They also promise a day when God's people will no longer need to tell one another to know Him, because all will know Him, and the nations will learn war no more. The God who was never taught by anyone becomes, through Christ and His Spirit, the Teacher of His people from the inside out.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lâmad is a common Hebrew verb. Its 86 uses cluster in Deuteronomy, the Psalms (especially Psalm 119), and Jeremiah. The same verb covers both sides of education. The student learns, and the teacher causes another to learn.

The settings are varied and practical. Some are military: tribesmen "skillful in war" (1 Chronicles 5:18) and Solomon's guards, "expert in war" (Song of Solomon 3:8). Some come from the farm, such as a heifer trained to thresh (Hosea 10:11). Others belong to the temple and the court. Musicians were "instructed" in songs for the LORD (1 Chronicles 25:7), and young captives in Babylon were chosen to be taught the language of the Chaldeans (Daniel 1:4). These everyday uses keep the spiritual uses grounded. When Israel is told to learn God's statutes, the word already suggests practice, repetition, and skill.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To learn, become practiced in. This is gaining knowledge or a skill through instruction and experience, as when Israel was to hear the law and "learn to fear the LORD" (Deuteronomy 31:12–13, paraphrased).

2. To teach, train. This is causing someone else to learn, whether a skill or a way of life: "Teach me Your paths" (Psalm 25:4).

3. Trained, skilled, expert. This describes a person or animal whose training is complete, as with soldiers "expert in war" (Song of Solomon 3:8) or a heifer trained to the work (Hosea 10:11).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What is being learned decides the moral weight of the word. Israel can learn the fear of the LORD (Deuteronomy 4:10), or it can learn "the abominations of those nations" (Deuteronomy 18:9, paraphrased). The verb itself is neutral. It describes how people are formed, and Scripture is deeply concerned with what forms them.

Who does the teaching also matters. Moses, Levites, parents, and Ezra the scribe all teach (2 Chronicles 17:9; Ezra 7:10). In the Psalms, however, the teacher is usually God Himself, and the verb most often appears as a plea: "Teach me." David can even credit his battle skill to the LORD, "who trains my hands for war, and my fingers for battle" (Psalm 144:1; compare Psalm 18:34). Training of the body and training of the soul come from the same Hand.

4. Theological Significance

The deepest truth behind lâmad is that God teaches and is never taught. Isaiah asks of the LORD who instructed Him and "taught Him in the path of justice" and who "taught Him knowledge" (Isaiah 40:14, paraphrased). Job asks, "Can anyone teach God knowledge, since He judges those on high?" (Job 21:22). The expected answer to both questions is that no one has. Paul draws the same conclusion: "For who has known the mind of the LORD? Or who has become His counselor?" (Romans 11:34).

Every human being has to learn. God alone has never needed instruction, so His knowledge is not borrowed or incomplete. That is exactly why the psalmist's prayers make sense. The One asked to "teach me Your statutes" is the only Teacher whose understanding has no gaps.

Scripture also shows that God keeps teaching people who will not listen. Through Jeremiah He says He taught Israel, "rising up early and teaching them," yet they would not receive instruction (Jeremiah 32:33, paraphrased). His teaching is patient and persistent, and it continues even when it is resisted.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy: the chain of instruction. On the edge of the Promised Land, Moses repeats this word again and again. God gave him statutes to teach (Deuteronomy 4:5, 14; 5:31; 6:1). Moses taught them to Israel so that the people might live and possess the land (4:1). Parents were to teach them to their children while sitting at home, walking by the way, lying down, and rising up (11:19). The goal was reverence: Israel was to learn to fear God all their days (4:10). Men, women, children, and the foreigner within the gates were all gathered to hear and learn (31:12). Even the king was to read God's law all his life so that he might learn to fear the LORD (17:19). No one was too young to be taught, and no one was too great to keep learning. The goal was never retention alone. Israel was to become a people who feared God and walked in His ways, and one generation's silence could cost the next generation its inheritance.

The Psalms: "Teach me." In the Psalms the word becomes a cry from a heart that knows the written law is not enough without the Lawgiver's personal help. "Teach me to do Your will, for You are my God; Your Spirit is good" (Psalm 143:10). The request is about doing, and the psalmist joins it to God's good Spirit. God's school also has an entrance requirement: "The humble He guides in justice, and the humble He teaches His way" (Psalm 25:9). Sometimes the classroom is hard: "It is good for me that I have been afflicted, that I may learn Your statutes" (Psalm 119:71). The psalmist does not call affliction good in itself. He calls it good because it moved God's statutes from his head into his life.

Learning the ways of the nations. God warned Israel not to learn the abominations of Canaan (Deuteronomy 18:9) and later said through Jeremiah, "Do not learn the way of the Gentiles" (Jeremiah 10:2, paraphrased). The record of what happened is painful: "But they mingled with the Gentiles and learned their works" (Psalm 106:35). Jeremiah 12:16 turns the matter around. God promises that if the surrounding nations will diligently learn the ways of His people, they will be established. The tragedy was that these nations had taught His people to swear by Baal. Israel had been thoroughly discipled, by the wrong teachers.

Jeremiah 31: the untrained calf and the new covenant. Ephraim confesses that he was disciplined like an untrained young bull that resisted the yoke (Jeremiah 31:18). That picture sums up Israel's history: commands from outside, met by a heart that would not bend. A few verses later, God promises a new covenant in which He writes His law on the heart. As a result, no one will need to teach his neighbor, "Know the LORD," because all will know Him, from the least to the greatest (31:33–34, paraphrased). The external chain of teaching that Deuteronomy commanded is not thrown away. Its goal is finally reached from within. The untrained heart becomes a taught heart because God Himself does the writing.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hebrews quotes Jeremiah's promise directly and applies it to the covenant Christ mediates: "None of them shall teach his neighbor, and none his brother, saying, 'Know the LORD,' for all shall know Me" (Hebrews 8:11). This is stated fulfillment, with the New Testament naming Jesus as the One through whom it comes.

Jesus also says, "It is written in the prophets, 'And they shall all be taught by God.' Therefore everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to Me" (John 6:45). The goal of all God's teaching is that people come to the Son. John adds that the anointing believers received from Christ "abides in you" and "teaches you concerning all things" (1 John 2:27). The Spirit the psalmist named in his prayer (Psalm 143:10) now lives within God's people as their Teacher. John's words do not remove the need for human teachers. They mean that no believer depends on a human go-between to know God.

The story reaches its end in the kingdom of peace. Isaiah and Micah both promise that the nations will beat swords into plowshares, "Neither shall they learn war anymore" (Micah 4:3; compare Isaiah 2:4). The same verb that described hands trained for battle now describes a lesson the world will finally unlearn under the Messiah's rule.

7. Spirit & Life

Because lâmad describes formation through practice, it carries a sober truth: everyone is being taught by something. Israel did not set out to learn Baal worship. They mingled with the Gentiles, and the learning followed (Psalm 106:35). A believer who is not deliberately learning God's ways is still being trained, by the habits, voices, and values around him. Paul's call is the same: "do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind" (Romans 12:2).

The military and farming uses give a helpful picture of what God's training aims at. David's hands were trained until the right movements came naturally (Psalm 144:1). Hebrews describes mature believers as "those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil" (Hebrews 5:14). That verse uses a different word, but the image matches. God's teaching works toward reflexes of faith, the kind of trust and obedience that respond rightly under pressure because they have been practiced.

This is why the Psalms' prayer still fits on a believer's lips. Humility opens the door, and hardship can become a lesson. The Teacher is the God who never had to learn anything, and He now teaches His people by His Spirit from within.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Isaiah 1:17 – God calls Judah to learn to do good, seek justice, and defend the fatherless and widow. Moral goodness is something His people must learn.

  • Ezra 7:10 – Ezra prepared his heart to seek the law of the LORD, to do it, and to teach it in Israel. He practiced the law before he taught it.

  • 2 Chronicles 17:9 – Under Jehoshaphat, officials and Levites carried the Book of the Law through the cities of Judah and taught the people, renewing Deuteronomy's chain of instruction.

  • Psalm 51:13 – After confessing his sin, David promises to teach transgressors God's ways. Forgiven sinners become teachers.

  • Isaiah 29:13 – The people's fear of God had been taught by human commandment, a hollow worship Jesus later exposed (Matthew 15:8–9).

  • Isaiah 48:17 – The LORD calls Himself the One who teaches His people to profit and leads them in the way they should go.
