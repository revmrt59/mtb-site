LAMECH - (Lemek, Strong's H3929)

Summary

Lamech is the name of two different men who lived before the Flood. They stand at turning points in two very different family lines. The first Lamech belongs to the family of Cain, and he is the seventh generation from Adam through that line (Genesis 4:18–24). He took two wives, fathered the founders of herding, music, and metalworking, and then composed a song boasting that he had killed a young man for wounding him. The second Lamech belongs to the line of Seth. He was the son of Methuselah and the father of Noah (Genesis 5:25–31; 1 Chronicles 1:3), and he is listed among the ancestors of Jesus in Luke 3:36. The meaning of the name is uncertain. Scholars have suggested an Arabic connection meaning something like "strong young man" and an Akkadian comparison with a title for a lower-ranking priest, but neither has been established. The name's significance comes from what Scripture records about each man, not from its etymology.

Genesis sets these two men side by side, and the contrast teaches a great deal. Cain's descendant turns God's protective word over Cain into a boast of unlimited revenge: "If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, Then Lamech seventy-sevenfold" (Genesis 4:24). Seth's descendant looks at the hard ground and the weariness of human labor. He names his son in hope that "This one will comfort us... because of the ground which the LORD has cursed" (Genesis 5:29). One man trusts his own strength and celebrates violence. The other grieves the effects of sin and waits for God's relief. Centuries later, when Peter asks whether forgiving seven times is enough, Jesus answers "up to seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22). His words echo Cainite Lamech's boast and turn it upside down. In the kingdom of heaven, forgiveness without limit replaces vengeance without limit.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name Lemek (Strong's H3929) refers to two separate men in Genesis. Readers must keep them distinct, because the text places them in opposing family lines and gives them opposite characters.

Lamech son of Methushael belongs to Cain's line: Adam, Cain, Enoch, Irad, Mehujael, Methushael, Lamech (Genesis 4:17–18). He is the seventh generation from Adam. His wives were Adah and Zillah. Adah bore Jabal and Jubal, and Zillah bore Tubal-Cain and a daughter, Naamah (Genesis 4:19–22).

Lamech son of Methuselah belongs to Seth's line: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Cainan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech (Genesis 5:3–25). He is the ninth generation from Adam and the father of Noah. He also appears in the Chronicler's genealogy (1 Chronicles 1:3). The Greek form of his name in Luke 3:36 is indexed separately (G2984) and is not counted among the Hebrew occurrences of H3929.

The two lines share some similar names, including Enoch in both and Lamech in both. Some critical scholars have argued that Genesis 4 and 5 are two versions of a single original list. The canonical text presents them differently. These are two distinct families, and the matching names help the reader notice how far apart the two lines have grown.

The meaning of Lemek is uncertain. One proposal links it to an Arabic word suggesting "strong young man," and some have connected it with the idea of a wild or forceful man. Another comparison, discussed by Richard Hess, points to an Akkadian title for a lower-ranking priest (lamakku or lumakku). All of these comparisons are tentative. Neither derivation is secure, and Genesis offers no wordplay on Lamech's own name. That is different from his son Noah, whose naming in Genesis 5:29 does carry a wordplay. Whatever Lemek originally meant, readers should not read either man's character from the name. The same name belongs to a violent boaster and to a man of hopeful faith.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Both men lived in the world before the Flood. In that period, Genesis records very long lifespans, the rapid spread of human culture, and the growth of violence that finally brought judgment (Genesis 6:5, 11).

Lamech of Cain's Line (Genesis 4:18–24)

Cain's genealogy moves quickly through names until it reaches Lamech. At that point the narrative slows down and expands into detail. The first thing recorded about him is that "Lamech took for himself two wives" (Genesis 4:19). This is the first recorded departure from the pattern of Genesis 2:24, where a man is joined to his wife and the two become one flesh. The phrase "took for himself" fits the man the rest of the passage reveals.

His children became the founders of major parts of human culture. Jabal was "the father of those who dwell in tents and have livestock." Jubal was "the father of all those who play the harp and flute." Tubal-Cain was "an instructor of every craftsman in bronze and iron" (Genesis 4:20–22). Genesis does not condemn herding, music, or metalwork. These are real goods, and they reflect the creativity God gave humanity. Yet they arise in a family that has gone out "from the presence of the LORD" (Genesis 4:16), and the next thing Genesis records is a song of bloodshed.

Lamech summons his wives: "Adah and Zillah, hear my voice; Wives of Lamech, listen to my speech! For I have killed a man for wounding me, Even a young man for hurting me. If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, Then Lamech seventy-sevenfold" (Genesis 4:23–24). It is an early poem in Scripture, the first recorded song of vengeance, and it celebrates killing. The punishment far outweighs the offense. A wound was answered with death. Lamech refers back to Genesis 4:15, where God promised sevenfold vengeance on anyone who killed Cain. That was a word of divine protection given to a guilty man. Lamech takes it and turns it into a claim of his own right to retaliate, multiplied far beyond what God had said. He never speaks to God or about God's judgment. He has made himself the judge.

The escalation from Cain is striking. Cain tried to hide what he had done ("Am I my brother's keeper?" Genesis 4:9). Lamech composes a song about it for an audience. Many readers have seen a connection between Tubal-Cain's bronze and iron and his father's confidence in violence. The text places the two side by side but does not state that Lamech used his son's weapons, so this is a reasonable inference rather than a stated fact.

Genesis traces Cain's line no further. The Flood that follows sweeps away all who were outside the ark. Genesis does not give the ancestry of the wives of Noah and his sons, so it does not tell us whether any Cainite descent survived through them.

Lamech of Seth's Line (Genesis 5:25–31)

Genesis 5 moves through its generations in a steady pattern: a man lived, fathered a son, lived more years, had other children, and died. The pattern breaks at Enoch, who "walked with God" (Genesis 5:24). It breaks again at Lamech, the grandson of Enoch and son of Methuselah. Lamech was 182 when his son was born, "And he called his name Noah, saying, 'This one will comfort us concerning our work and the toil of our hands, because of the ground which the LORD has cursed'" (Genesis 5:28–29).

These words look straight back to Genesis 3:17–19, where God cursed the ground because of Adam's sin and sentenced humanity to painful toil. Lamech names the cause of his weariness correctly. The ground is hard because the LORD cursed it. He uses God's covenant name, which shows that the calling on the LORD that began in Seth's day (Genesis 4:26) was still alive in this family. The name Noah sounds like the Hebrew word for rest, and Lamech connects it with comfort. He looked at his newborn son and hoped God would bring relief through him.

Lamech lived 595 more years and had other sons and daughters. His full lifespan was 777 years (Genesis 5:30–31). By the figures in Genesis, he died about five years before the Flood came in Noah's six hundredth year (Genesis 7:6). He did not live to see the judgment, or the rescue, that came through the son he had named in hope.

The comfort Lamech longed for did come in part. After the Flood, Noah offered sacrifices, and the LORD promised never again to curse the ground for man's sake in that way (Genesis 8:20–21). Noah himself "began to be a farmer, and he planted a vineyard" (Genesis 9:20), working the very ground that had been cursed. Even so, the deeper curse of sin remained.

Many generations later, the Chronicler opens Israel's history with Seth's line alone: "Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech," then Noah (1 Chronicles 1:3–4). Cain's Lamech is not mentioned there. Luke traces Jesus' ancestry back through "the son of Noah, the son of Lamech" to Adam and to God (Luke 3:36–38). Matthew's genealogy begins with Abraham, so it does not include Lamech.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states the facts plainly. One Lamech descended from Cain, took two wives, and boasted of killing. The other descended from Seth, named Noah in hope of comfort from the curse, and stands in the ancestry of Christ.

The meaning of the contrast is a careful inference, but it is strongly supported by the way Genesis arranges its material. Genesis 4 and 5 tell the story of two families moving away from each other after the Fall. Genesis 3:15 speaks of enmity between the seed of the serpent and the seed of the woman. The New Testament later says Cain "was of the wicked one" (1 John 3:12). Read against that background, the two Lamechs show where each path leads. Cain's line produces impressive cities, crafts, and music, and also a man who sings about murder. Seth's line produces no recorded inventions, but it keeps calling on the LORD and keeps hoping in Him.

A further detail adds to the contrast. In Seth's line, the seventh from Adam is Enoch, who walked with God and was taken (Genesis 5:24; compare Jude 14). In Cain's line, the seventh from Adam is Lamech, who boasted of bloodshed. This parallel is an observation from the genealogies rather than an explicit biblical statement, but it fits the deliberate design of these chapters.

Some readers also connect Cainite Lamech's "seventy-sevenfold" with Sethite Lamech's 777 years. The numbers are real and the echo is easy to see. Scripture does not interpret the match, so it is best treated as a striking feature of the text and not pressed into a hidden code.

The two Lamechs also show why the Flood came. Cainite Lamech is an early, vivid picture of the violence that later "filled" the earth (Genesis 6:11). Sethite Lamech shows that even in that dark world, God preserved people who knew His name and waited on Him. Through that remnant He saved the human race.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The strongest Christ-centered connection runs through Matthew 18. Peter asks, "Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Up to seven times?" Jesus answers, "I do not say to you, up to seven times, but up to seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:21–22). Jesus does not name Lamech. However, the Greek wording of His answer matches the Greek translation of Lamech's "seventy-sevenfold" in Genesis 4:24, and the movement from "seven" to a multiplied seven follows the same pattern as Lamech's boast. This is a well-supported canonical connection rather than an explicit New Testament interpretation.

The point is clear. Lamech took God's protective word over Cain and magnified it into unlimited revenge. Jesus takes Peter's limit and stretches it into unlimited forgiveness. Lamech's song called his household to admire his vengeance. Jesus calls His disciples to imitate the mercy of a King who has forgiven a debt they could never repay (Matthew 18:23–35). Whether the number is read as seventy-seven or seventy times seven, the meaning is the same in both places: without limit.

The Sethite Lamech connects with Christ in another way. Luke places him in the line that leads to Jesus (Luke 3:36). Lamech hoped for comfort from the curse on the ground, and Noah brought a real but partial answer. Scripture carries that same longing forward. Creation still "groans" under the effects of sin as it waits for redemption (Romans 8:20–22). The Bible's final vision promises that "there shall be no more curse" (Revelation 22:3). Christ, who became a curse for us (Galatians 3:13), is the One through whom the curse is finally removed. Lamech's hope that his son would bring rest resonates with Jesus' invitation to the weary to come to Him for rest (Matthew 11:28). That link is thematic rather than a stated fulfillment, but it follows the actual direction of Scripture's story.

5. Spirit & Life

The two Lamechs lived in the same world under the same curse. Both faced hard ground, hard work, and other people who could hurt them. What divided them was how they responded to God.

Cain's Lamech answered injury with escalation. He had been wounded, so he killed. He had heard of God's protection over Cain, so he claimed a far greater protection for himself, by his own hand. His story shows how quickly human gifts can be turned to serve pride. The same household that gave the world the harp and the forge gave it a song celebrating death. Genesis does not ask us to fear music or skill. It shows that progress without God does not make people better. It gives a proud heart stronger tools. Lamech's confidence also proved empty. His family's achievements could not shelter anyone outside the ark from the waters God sent.

Seth's Lamech teaches a different way. He did not pretend life was easy. He named "the toil of our hands" and traced it honestly to "the ground which the LORD has cursed." Faith in God does not require denying what sin has done to the world. It means bringing that weariness to God and expecting Him to act. Lamech's hope was attached to a child he could hold, and he died before seeing what God would do through him. Genesis records Lamech's words of hope rather than a separate promise God gave to him. Yet his hope looked to God's dealing with the cursed ground, and God answered it in part through Noah and fully in Christ, far beyond Lamech's lifetime.

Jesus brings both stories home to every disciple. Everyone has been wounded by someone. The instinct of Cain's Lamech lives in every human heart: to keep score, to answer harm with greater harm, and to tell others how justified we were. Jesus calls His people to stop counting. That command is not a burden laid on unforgiven people. It flows from the mercy of God, who has cancelled a debt of ten thousand talents for everyone who trusts Him (Matthew 18:24–27). Those who have received that mercy are free to forgive "seventy times seven." The parable also ends with a sober warning that forgiveness must come "from his heart" (Matthew 18:35).

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 4:16–18 — Places Cainite Lamech in the line that went out from the LORD's presence and identifies him as Methushael's son.

  • Genesis 4:19–22 — Records his two wives and the children who founded herding, music, and metalworking.

  • Genesis 4:23–24 — The Song of Lamech: an early poem and the first recorded song of vengeance, boasting of a killing and of seventy-sevenfold vengeance.

  • Genesis 4:15 — God's sevenfold protection over Cain, which Lamech twists into a claim of his own vengeance.

  • Genesis 5:25–31 — The Sethite Lamech: son of Methuselah, father of Noah, his prophetic naming of Noah, and his 777-year life.

  • Genesis 3:17–19 — The curse on the ground that Lamech names in Genesis 5:29.

  • Genesis 8:20–21; 9:20 — The partial fulfillment of Lamech's hope after the Flood.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:3 — The Chronicler includes Sethite Lamech in the line traced from Adam to Israel.

  • Luke 3:36 — Sethite Lamech in the genealogy of Jesus (Greek name indexed separately, G2984).

  • Matthew 18:21–22 — Jesus' "seventy times seven," which reverses Lamech's boast of unlimited vengeance.

  • Romans 8:20–22; Revelation 22:3 — The longing for release from the curse, carried to its final answer in Christ.
