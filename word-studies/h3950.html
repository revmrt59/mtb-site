GATHER, GLEAN - (lâqaṭ, Strong's H3950)

Summary

The Hebrew verb lâqaṭ means to gather, pick up, or glean. It describes collecting scattered things from the ground one piece at a time: stones for a heap, sticks for a fire, arrows after target practice, stalks of grain dropped behind the reapers. Scripture uses the word most often in two places. In Exodus 16, Israel goes out each morning to gather manna. In Leviticus and Ruth, the poor glean what harvesters leave behind. In both settings the gatherer bends low to pick up what someone else has provided, either God from heaven or a landowner who obeyed God's law.

That posture of stooping to receive carries much of the word's spiritual weight. Psalm 104 pictures every living creature gathering what God's open hand supplies. Boaz deliberately lets grain fall for a Moabite widow. Through Isaiah, God promises that He Himself will gather His scattered people "one by one." The word that describes the poor picking up leftovers becomes a picture of the Lord's careful, personal rescue of His own. Lâqaṭ also reveals the heart, because what people bend down to gather shows what they trust and whom they serve.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lâqaṭ belongs to the ordinary life of a farming and herding people. Its settings are fields, vineyards, wilderness camps, and household hearths. Jacob's relatives gather stones to build a heap of witness (Genesis 31:46). A boy picks up Jonathan's arrows (1 Samuel 20:38). A servant gathers herbs during a famine (2 Kings 4:39). Children collect wood for a fire (Jeremiah 7:18). Hebrew has other words for gathering, including words for assembling crowds or bringing in a harvest. Lâqaṭ usually keeps its eye on the ground and on small things picked up by hand.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to collect scattered items by picking them up. Context supplies the nuance:

1. To pick up or collect scattered objects or material, such as stones, money, arrows, wood, herbs, or manna (Genesis 31:46; 47:14; Exodus 16:16).

2. To glean, meaning to gather grain or grapes left behind after the main harvest (Leviticus 19:9–10; Ruth 2:2–3).

3. To gather people, either as a group drawn together (Judges 11:3, "banded together") or as individuals collected by God (Isaiah 27:12).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The two great clusters of the word are the manna narrative (nine uses in Exodus 16) and gleaning (three in Leviticus, twelve in Ruth 2). In the manna account, the gatherer receives directly from God. In the gleaning texts, the gatherer receives from God's provision through the obedience of a landowner. In both, the gatherer did not plant or produce what is picked up.

Who does the gathering changes the picture. When a landowner is forbidden to gather, the prohibition protects the poor. When the poor gather, the word describes survival. When idolaters gather, ordinary household work is turned toward a false goddess. When the Lord gathers, the humble work of a gleaner becomes a picture of divine care.

4. Theological Significance

The word teaches first that God is the Provider and His people are receivers. Psalm 104:28 states this for the whole creation: "What You give them they gather in; You open Your hand, they are filled with good." Lions, birds, sea creatures, and people all live like gleaners in God's field. None of them generates its own life.

The word also shows that God's provision invites response. Manna fell freely, yet Israel had to go out and pick it up. God made the daily gathering itself a test: "that I may test them, whether they will walk in My law or not" (Exodus 16:4). Grace is never earned by gathering, but grace that is refused is not received.

The gleaning laws reveal God's concern for people with no field of their own. Leviticus 19:9 forbids the owner to "gather the gleanings of your harvest," and verse 10 names the poor and the stranger as the ones those gleanings are for. By limiting the owner's gathering, God built mercy into Israel's economy. The land and its harvest belonged finally to Him, and He reserved part of every harvest for those who would otherwise go hungry.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 16: daily bread and daily trust. God promised to "rain bread from heaven," and the people were to "gather a certain quota every day" (Exodus 16:4). They could not stockpile. Leftover manna bred worms (16:20), except the double portion gathered on the sixth day for the Sabbath (16:5, 22). Those who went out to gather on the seventh day found nothing (16:26–27). Every detail trained Israel to trust God for today. Exodus 16:18 adds that whoever gathered much had nothing left over and whoever gathered little had no lack. Paul later quotes that line to urge generous sharing among believers (2 Corinthians 8:15). Numbers 11:8 shows that gathering continued even in the chapter where the people complained of "nothing at all except this manna before our eyes" (11:6). They bent down for God's provision while their hearts longed for Egypt.

Ruth 2: mercy beyond the minimum. Ruth asks to "glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor" (Ruth 2:2). She is poor, widowed, and a foreigner, exactly the person Leviticus had in mind. Boaz keeps the law and then goes past it. He tells her to stay in his field (2:8), feeds her at his own meal until she is satisfied (2:14), and instructs his young men: "Also let grain from the bundles fall purposely for her; leave it that she may glean, and do not rebuke her" (2:16). The law required leftover edges. Boaz arranged generous handfuls. Ruth came to the field to gather scraps, and through her gleaning she met the man who would redeem her family.

Judges 1:7: the tyrant's table. The defeated king Adoni-Bezek confesses that seventy maimed kings "used to gather scraps under my table." He used gathering to humiliate people, forcing them to grovel for crumbs. Set beside Boaz's field, the contrast is plain. The tyrant's scraps degraded the defeated. God's gleanings preserved the dignity of the poor, who worked openly in the field by right and were not left begging under someone's table.

Isaiah 27:12: God as the gleaner. "The LORD will thresh, From the channel of the River to the Brook of Egypt; And you will be gathered one by one, O you children of Israel." Gleaning belonged to the poorest people in Israel. Here the Lord Himself takes up the gleaner's work. After the threshing of judgment, He searches the whole region between the Euphrates and the border of Egypt and picks up His scattered people individually. Isaiah could have pictured a single sweeping ingathering. Instead he says "one by one." No exile is too small, too far, or too buried in the dust of the nations to be noticed and lifted.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus takes up the manna story directly. When the crowd recalled that God "gave them bread from heaven to eat," Jesus answered, "My Father gives you the true bread from heaven," and then said, "I am the bread of life. He who comes to Me shall never hunger" (John 6:31–35). Israel gathered manna and still died in the wilderness. Christ offers Himself as the bread that gives life to the world (John 6:33).

Ruth's story also moves toward Christ. The foreign gleaner becomes the great-grandmother of David (Ruth 4:17), and Matthew names her in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). Boaz's generosity toward an outsider is a fitting likeness of the grace that welcomes Gentiles into God's family. The Canaanite woman in Matthew 15:27 speaks from a gleaner's position: "even the little dogs eat the crumbs which fall from their masters' table." Jesus did not leave her under the table. He granted her request in full.

Isaiah's "one by one" finds its fullest resonance in the Good Shepherd, who calls His own sheep by name (John 10:3). After feeding the five thousand, Jesus told His disciples to gather up the fragments so that nothing would be lost (John 6:12). Later in the same chapter He said that of all the Father had given Him He would lose nothing (John 6:39). These are Greek texts, not uses of lâqaṭ, but they show the same heart. The Lord who once commanded mercy for gleaners now gathers His people Himself, and none are lost.

7. Spirit & Life

Believers live as gleaners. We did not plant the field of salvation, and we have nothing to bring but empty hands. Like Ruth, we are outsiders welcomed into a field that belongs to another, and we find that the Owner has let handfuls fall on purpose. Grace in Christ is more than the legal minimum.

The manna also teaches us to gather today. Israel ordinarily could not live on yesterday's portion, and the soul cannot live on last year's walk with God. Jesus invites a daily coming to Him, and the gathering is part of the trust.

What we bend down to gather reveals our hearts. In Jeremiah 7:18, a whole family works in perfect cooperation: "The children gather wood, the fathers kindle the fire, and the women knead dough, to make cakes for the queen of heaven." The diligence that once gathered manna was spent on an idol, and God says they did it "that they may provoke Me to anger." In 2 Kings 4:39, a servant gathered "a lapful of wild gourds" during a famine, "though they did not know what they were," and the stew turned deadly. Hunger without discernment led him to pick up poison. Believers face the same choices daily in what they read, watch, crave, and pursue. A family can be busy and united and still be gathering for the wrong god.

Finally, the believer is gathered as well as gathering. Isaiah's promise means that God's attention reaches the single person, picked up from the dust by hand. Song of Solomon 6:2 offers a gentler picture of the beloved going to his garden "to gather lilies." Many Christian readers have heard in that verse the delight of Christ in His people. That reading is an analogy rather than the poem's stated meaning, but it fits Isaiah's truth well. The Lord gathers His people because He values them, and He takes pleasure in them as His own.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 31:46 — Jacob tells his relatives to gather stones for a heap that will witness to the agreement between him and Laban. Ordinary picking up serves a solemn purpose.

  • Genesis 47:14 — Joseph gathers up all the money in Egypt and Canaan in exchange for grain during the famine. His gathering keeps two nations alive.

  • Judges 11:3 — Worthless men band together around Jephthah, an outcast whom God would still use to deliver Israel.

  • 1 Samuel 20:38 — Jonathan's lad gathers up the arrows, unaware that their placement carried a life-saving message to David.

  • Isaiah 17:5 — Israel's coming judgment is compared to someone gleaning ears of grain in the Valley of Rephaim. Only a few scattered stalks remain, a picture of a nation reduced to a remnant.
