TONGUE, LANGUAGE - (lâshôwn, Strong's H3956)

Summary

The Hebrew word lâshôwn means "tongue." Most often it names the tongue as the organ of speech. In Scripture, though, the tongue is rarely neutral. It shows what is in the heart. In the Psalms and Proverbs it can be a sword, an arrow, or a serpent's poison, or it can be "choice silver" and a source of healing. The same word can also mean a people's language, and it can describe things shaped like a tongue: a wedge of gold, a bay of the sea, or a flame of fire.

Through this word the Bible shows that speech is a matter of lordship. The wicked say, "With our tongue we will prevail; our lips are our own; who is lord over us?" (Psalm 12:4). God answers by claiming the tongue for Himself. He made the mouth. He forgives David so that his "tongue shall sing aloud of Your righteousness" (Psalm 51:14). He gives His Servant an instructed tongue to speak to the weary. And He swears that "every tongue shall take an oath" to Him (Isaiah 45:23), a promise the New Testament fulfills in every tongue confessing Jesus Christ as Lord. The nations once scattered by language are finally gathered, "all nations and tongues," to see His glory (Isaiah 66:18).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lâshôwn is the ordinary Hebrew word for the tongue. It appears across the Old Testament, from the family lists of Genesis to the prophets. Psalms and Proverbs together contain nearly half of its uses, so the word belongs especially to Israel's prayers and wisdom about speech. Like English, Hebrew could use "tongue" for a whole language. Hebrew also applied the word to objects with a tongue's long, projecting shape. The NKJV therefore translates it as "tongue," "language," "wedge," "bay," and, in a few places, with words such as "slanderer," "babbler," and "talkers."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The physical tongue. The organ in the mouth, as when Gideon's men lapped water with their tongues like dogs (Judges 7:5), or when a nursing infant's tongue clings to the roof of its mouth from thirst (Lamentations 4:4).

2. The tongue as speech. The tongue standing for what a person says and how they say it: "Death and life are in the power of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21).

3. A language. The speech of a nation or people, as in the Table of Nations, where the coastland peoples were separated, "everyone according to his language" (Genesis 10:5). The same sense appears in the royal decrees of Esther, which were sent to each people in its own language (Esther 1:22).

4. A tongue-shaped thing. Achan's "wedge of gold" (Joshua 7:21), a "bay" of the Salt Sea marking Judah's border (Joshua 15:2), and a tongue of fire. The NKJV renders that last phrase "flame" in Isaiah 5:24.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the tongue speaks in Scripture, it usually carries moral weight. Hebrew poetry often treats the tongue almost as a person in its own right. In Psalm 73:9 the proud "set their mouth against the heavens, and their tongue walks through the earth," and in Psalm 52:4 David addresses his enemy directly: "You deceitful tongue." In lines like these, the tongue stands for the whole person and the way that person lives. The tongue reveals what the heart already loves. Job 15:5 traces the link: "your iniquity teaches your mouth, and you choose the tongue of the crafty."

The tongue is often paired with "lips" in parallel lines (Psalm 12:3; 120:2), and the pairing presents speech as a whole. The word can even describe speech without words. In Isaiah 57:4 idolaters "make a wide mouth and stick out the tongue," a gesture of contempt aimed at God Himself.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that the tongue belongs to God, and every person must decide how to use it. Psalm 139:4 confesses that before a word is on my tongue, the Lord knows it completely. Speech is never hidden from Him. For that reason, a corrupted tongue is more than a social problem. It rebels against the One who made it.

God's own speech appears through this word as well. Isaiah 30:27 pictures the Lord coming against Assyria with lips full of indignation and a tongue like a devouring fire. The proud say their tongue will prevail. God's tongue judges, and nothing withstands it. The holy anger of the Lord is not empty talk; when He speaks against evil, the judgment is already burning.

Yet the God who judges also redeems the tongue. In Psalm 51:14, David's tongue is set free only after his guilt is dealt with: "Deliver me from the guilt of bloodshed… and my tongue shall sing aloud of Your righteousness." Forgiveness opens the mouth. A tongue that has been cleansed finds its created purpose, which is to "speak of Your righteousness and of Your praise all the day long" (Psalm 35:28) and to "speak of Your word" (Psalm 119:172).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 12 and the weaponized tongue. Psalm 12 shows the root of corrupted speech. The wicked do more than lie. They declare independence: "With our tongue we will prevail; our lips are our own; who is lord over us?" (12:4). The psalmist sees the real issue. Flattery and proud talk are a refusal to have a Lord. The Psalms and prophets then describe what such a tongue becomes. It is "a sharp sword" (Psalm 57:4) and "like a sharp razor" (Psalm 52:2), and the wicked "sharpen their tongue like a sword, and bend their bows to shoot their arrows—bitter words" (Psalm 64:3). Jeremiah sees a whole society failing this way: "Their tongue is an arrow shot out… One speaks peaceably to his neighbor with his mouth, but in his heart he lies in wait" (Jeremiah 9:8). Psalm 140:3 adds a serpent's venom. These images describe a weapon that strikes from a distance and leaves wounds the victim cannot trace. That is why the righteous pray, "Deliver my soul, O LORD, from lying lips and from a deceitful tongue" (Psalm 120:2), and why Job 5:21 promises shelter "from the scourge of the tongue."

Moses' slow tongue and the Servant's instructed tongue. At the burning bush, Moses protested that he was slow of speech and slow of tongue (Exodus 4:10). The Lord answered by asking who had made man's mouth. Moses' weakness was real, but it did not limit God. Isaiah carries the thought forward. In the age of restoration, the tongue of the mute will sing (Isaiah 35:6). Then the Servant of the Lord says that the Lord God has given Him the tongue of the learned (that is, of one who has been taught). With that tongue He knows how to speak a word in season to the weary, because each morning the Lord wakens His ear to listen (Isaiah 50:4, paraphrased). This tongue is the opposite of Psalm 12. The proud say, "Our lips are our own." The Servant's tongue is received, taught, and used for the tired and the hurting. Moses doubted his tongue, and the Servant receives His tongue as a gift. In both cases, God is the one who makes the tongue fit for His purpose.

The tongue that clings to the roof of the mouth. Scripture repeatedly describes a tongue stuck to the roof of the mouth. In judgment, God tells Ezekiel, "I will make your tongue cling to the roof of your mouth, so that you shall be mute and not be one to rebuke them, for they are a rebellious house" (Ezekiel 3:26). Losing the prophet's warning was itself a sentence on Israel. In fallen Jerusalem, "the tongue of the infant clings to the roof of its mouth for thirst" (Lamentations 4:4). Psalm 137:6 turns the same picture into a vow: "If I do not remember you, let my tongue cling to the roof of my mouth." The exile would rather lose his voice than forget Zion. In each case, a silent tongue marks the end of human strength, whether by judgment, by ruin, or by freely chosen loyalty.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word follows the human story from scattering to gathering. Genesis 10 divides the nations "according to their languages" (Genesis 10:20, 31; compare 10:5). The Babel account in Genesis 11 explains how this happened, using a different Hebrew word (lip). After the exile, language still marks the danger of losing one's identity: Nehemiah was grieved that children of Judah could speak the language of Ashdod but not the language of their own people (Nehemiah 13:24). The prophets, however, foresee God reversing the scattering. "I will gather all nations and tongues; and they shall come and see My glory" (Isaiah 66:18). Zechariah 8:23 pictures men from every language taking hold of a Jew's robe because they have heard that God is with His people. At Pentecost, the gospel was heard in many languages (Acts 2). John then sees a multitude "of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). God does not erase the variety of languages. He gathers them in worship.

Isaiah 45:23 leads to the same place: "Every knee shall bow, every tongue shall take an oath." Paul quotes this as a word about God (Romans 14:11). In Philippians 2:11 he applies it to the exalted Jesus: "every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord." The oath Isaiah reserved for the Lord alone is given to the Son. Jesus is also the Servant of Isaiah 50, whose taught tongue spoke rest to the weary (compare Matthew 11:28). And in Psalm 22:15, the sufferer's tongue clings to his jaws as he is brought into the dust of death. On the cross, the tongue given to comfort others dried with thirst (John 19:28). The voice that spoke a word in season was silenced for a time so that rebels' tongues could be forgiven.

7. Spirit & Life

James admits plainly, "no man can tame the tongue" (James 3:8). The Old Testament agrees, and it also points to the only remedy. The tongue changes when its owner has a Lord. Psalm 51 shows the order: first comes cleansing from guilt, and then comes the song. Believers do not master their speech by willpower alone. They bring their speech under the One who made the mouth and who teaches the Servant morning by morning.

Proverbs shows what the redeemed tongue becomes. The tongue of the wise brings healing, while reckless words pierce like a sword (Proverbs 12:18). A wholesome tongue is a tree of life (Proverbs 15:4), so it brings back something of what was lost in Eden. The woman of strength has the law of kindness on her tongue (Proverbs 31:26), which means that kindness rules her speech as steadily as a law. This is what maturity sounds like. Wisdom is more than avoiding lies. It speaks the life-giving word the weary person needs, as Christ does in Isaiah 50:4.

One contrast within the word itself helps make the choice clear. It is an illustration drawn from shared vocabulary rather than the teaching of either passage. Achan coveted a "wedge" (literally, a tongue) of gold and buried it in his tent, and it brought death on Israel (Joshua 7:21). Proverbs 10:20 says, "The tongue of the righteous is choice silver." One "tongue" was hidden treasure that cursed a household. The other is speech that enriches everyone who hears it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Samuel 23:2. David's last words testify that the Spirit of the Lord spoke by him and that God's word was on his tongue, a picture of inspired speech.

  • Psalm 39:1–3. David resolves to guard his ways so that he does not sin with his tongue. As he keeps silent, the fire burns within him until he speaks to God.

  • Isaiah 11:15. "The LORD will utterly destroy the tongue of the Sea of Egypt… and make men cross over dry-shod." Here a gulf shaped like a tongue becomes the setting for a new exodus.

  • Isaiah 28:11. God warns that He will speak to His scornful people through foreigners with stammering lips and another tongue. Paul cites this passage in 1 Corinthians 14:21.

  • Proverbs 21:23. Whoever guards his mouth and tongue keeps his soul from troubles.

  • Zephaniah 3:13. The restored remnant will speak no lies, and no deceitful tongue will be found in their mouth. This is the reversal of Jeremiah 9.
