VERY, GREATLY, EXCEEDINGLY - (mᵉʼôd, Strong's H3966)

Summary

The Hebrew word mᵉʼôd is the Old Testament's ordinary word for "very," "greatly," and "exceedingly." It turns up the volume on whatever it touches: a city's wickedness, a famine's severity, a father's grief, a king's terror, or the greatness of God. On its own it carries no moral weight. It intensifies, and the Bible's writers choose carefully what they intensify. The same word that marks God's verdict on finished creation as "very good" also marks Sodom as exceedingly wicked.

Because of that, mᵉʼôd traces the high points and low points of Scripture. God's creation is very good, sin is very grave, and the LORD is "greatly to be praised." In a few key places the word stands as a noun meaning something like "muchness" or "full capacity." That is how it appears in the great command of Deuteronomy 6:5, where Israel is told to love the LORD "with all your strength." God does not ask for a moderate response to a moderate God. He asks for everything a person has, and He promises Himself as the reward.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mᵉʼôd appears throughout the Hebrew Bible, from Genesis to Zechariah. It is most common in narrative books such as Genesis and 1 and 2 Samuel, where it measures the size of flocks, the scale of a battle, or the depth of someone's emotion. The Psalms use it often, both in praise and in cries of distress. Deuteronomy and Joshua attach it to commands about keeping God's words.

The word seems to come from a noun meaning "force" or "abundance." In most verses it works as an adverb. That older noun sense still shows in a few important passages.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is intensity or degree: much, very, exceedingly. NKJV renders it in many ways, including "very," "greatly," "exceedingly," "carefully," "diligently," "severely," and "strength." Context decides which English word fits.

1. An intensifier, "very" or "greatly," that raises the degree of an adjective or verb. Rebekah was very beautiful (Genesis 24:16), and Saul's heart trembled greatly (1 Samuel 28:5).

2. A noun meaning "might," "strength," or "full capacity." This is the sense in Deuteronomy 6:5 and 2 Kings 23:25, where it follows "with all your."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's nuance changes with what it modifies. Attached to emotion, it describes extremes such as Isaac trembling exceedingly when he learned he had been deceived (Genesis 27:33). Attached to quantity, it describes abundance, as in the grain Joseph stored in Egypt (Genesis 41:49). Attached to commands, it becomes "carefully" or "diligently." The point there is how wholeheartedly a person is to obey. In worship it describes God: "You are very great" (Psalm 104:1).

Two grammatical patterns help the reader. First, Hebrew sometimes doubles the word, mᵉʼôd mᵉʼôd, to express the highest degree. The floodwaters prevailed exceedingly over the earth (Genesis 7:19). Joshua and Caleb, standing against ten fearful spies, declared Canaan an exceedingly good land (Numbers 14:7). Second, when a preposition and "all" come before the word, it works as a noun, "all your muchness." That shift is what gives Deuteronomy 6:5 its force.

4. Theological Significance

Because mᵉʼôd intensifies, the passages that use it show what Scripture regards as extreme. Three realities stand out.

The first is the goodness of what God makes. Through Genesis 1 God pronounces each work good. After He forms humanity in His image and surveys everything together, His verdict is very good (Genesis 1:31). The intensifier falls on the whole, completed creation, every part in its place and humanity at its center, before sin entered. God was fully satisfied with what reflected His design.

The second is the gravity of sin. The men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinful against the LORD (Genesis 13:13), and the outcry against them was great because their sin was very grave (Genesis 18:20). Scripture does not soften rebellion against God into a small mistake. When God's people finally saw their unfaithfulness after the exile, "a very large assembly" gathered in brokenness before God (Ezra 10:1). Isaiah's prayer pleads with God not to be exceedingly angry and not to afflict His people very severely (Isaiah 64:9, 12). The prayer recognizes that God's anger against sin is real and deserved, and it asks for mercy anyway.

The third is the greatness of God. The psalmists reach for mᵉʼôd when ordinary words fall short. "For the LORD is great and greatly to be praised" (Psalm 96:4). He "is greatly exalted" (Psalm 47:9). Psalm 145:3 joins the same confession to a striking admission: "His greatness is unsearchable." The intensifier in worship marks the place where human language runs out. God's worth exceeds what praise can measure, so praise must stretch to its limit.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 6:5 and 2 Kings 23:25: Loving God with all your "muchness." The Shema's great command reads, "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength." The word translated "strength" is mᵉʼôd, the ordinary word for "very," used here as a noun. To love God with all your mᵉʼôd is to love Him with all your "very-ness": your full capacity, your energy, your resources, and everything you have that could be called "much." The command does not stop at heart and soul. It reaches to every ability and possession a person might hold back.

Centuries later, Scripture uses this same language for one king. Josiah "turned to the LORD with all his heart, with all his soul, and with all his might" (2 Kings 23:25). The narrator adds that before him there was no king like him, and none arose after him. Josiah heard the rediscovered Law, tore down idols across the land, and restored the Passover. His reforms show what the command looks like in an actual life. He brought the full weight of his throne and his resources under God's word.

Genesis 15:1: God as the exceeding reward. In Genesis 14 Abram refused to take even a thread or a sandal strap from the king of Sodom, so that no one could say a pagan king had made him rich. Right after this, the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision. God told him not to fear and promised to be his shield and his exceedingly great reward. Abram had just turned down a fortune. God answered that loss by offering Himself, and He used mᵉʼôd to describe the gift. What Abram forfeited could be counted. What he received could not.

1 Samuel 28 and 30: Two kings at their extremity. These chapters set two men side by side under crushing pressure. Facing the Philistines, Saul's heart trembled greatly (28:5). He told the spirit of Samuel he was deeply distressed (28:15), and he fell to the ground dreadfully afraid (28:20). Saul had already been cut off from God, and he had gone to a medium for answers.

Days later David came home to Ziklag and found the city burned and the families taken captive. His own men talked of stoning him. Scripture says David was greatly distressed (30:6), using the same intensifier. Then David strengthened himself in the LORD his God. Both men reached the limit of what they could bear. Saul turned to darkness, and David turned to God. Faith does not mean escaping extreme pressure. It means where a person runs when that pressure peaks.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus named Deuteronomy 6:5 the first and greatest commandment (Matthew 22:37–38; Mark 12:30). He also kept it as no one else has. Josiah came close, but he died, and the nation fell soon after. Christ loved the Father with all His heart, soul, and strength, all the way to the cross. Those who belong to Him are forgiven for loving God by halves. They also receive the Spirit, who begins to produce that whole love in them.

The word also appears at two prophetic moments that point to Christ. Isaiah says of the LORD's Servant that He shall be exalted and extolled and be very high (Isaiah 52:13). This exaltation comes before the description of His marred face and His wounds for our transgressions. The Servant who is lifted very high is the same one who was crushed. Zechariah calls Zion to rejoice greatly because her King comes, righteous, having salvation, lowly, and riding on a donkey (Zechariah 9:9). The Gospels see this fulfilled when Jesus enters Jerusalem (Matthew 21:5; John 12:15).

Genesis 15:1 also comes to completion in Christ. The God who offered Himself as Abram's exceedingly great reward has given Himself fully in His Son. In Christ, believers inherit God Himself.

7. Spirit & Life

Mᵉʼôd exposes a common habit of moderation. Many believers want a manageable faith, with some sorrow over sin, some praise, and some devotion. Scripture uses its intensifier on all three. Sin is very grave. God is greatly to be praised. Love for Him reaches to all your strength. Shrinking any one of these distorts the others. A person who thinks sin is small will find God's grace unremarkable. A person who thinks God is small will find wholehearted love unreasonable.

The commands to obey "diligently" and "carefully" show how that love is kept. Moses warned Israel to guard themselves diligently so they would not forget what their eyes had seen (Deuteronomy 4:9, 15). Joshua charged the tribes to take careful heed to love the LORD and walk in His ways (Joshua 22:5). The psalmist confesses, "You have commanded us To keep Your precepts diligently" (Psalm 119:4). Forgetting God takes no effort. Drift happens on its own. Remembering Him takes attention, intention, and daily care.

This kind of devotion does not run on willpower. It rests on what God has promised to be. Abram could let go of Sodom's wealth because God Himself was his reward. David could strengthen himself in the LORD on the worst day of his life because the LORD was his God. When distress is great, the believer still has a God who is very great, and the deepest trouble can become the place where faith takes hold of Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 4:5. Cain was very angry when God did not respect his offering. This is the first time Scripture applies the intensifier to human emotion, and the anger led to murder.

Numbers 12:3. Moses is described as very humble, more than anyone on earth. The intensifier highlights meekness in a man who held great authority.

Psalm 46:1. God is a very present help in trouble. His nearness is intensified at the moment it is needed most.

Psalm 119:140. The psalmist calls God's word very pure and says that is why he loves it. Its purity is measured in the highest degree.

Ezekiel 37:2, 10. The bones in the valley were very many and very dry. After the breath entered them, they stood up as an exceedingly great army. The same word measures both the hopelessness of death and the size of God's work to give life.
