EAT, DEVOUR, CONSUME - (ʼâkal, Strong's H0398)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʼâkal is the Old Testament's ordinary word for eating. It is used for a traveler's meal, a harvest supper, a king's table, and the food God gives His people. The same verb also describes what fire, the sword, wild animals, and locusts do. In those settings the NKJV translates it "consume" or "devour." One word covers both nourishment and destruction: you either eat, or you are eaten.

Scripture places this everyday verb at key turning points. The garden command concerns eating, and so does humanity's first sin. Israel ate the Passover lamb on the night of deliverance, ate manna in the wilderness, and ate before the LORD in joy. Fire from God consumed the accepted sacrifice and also devoured those who treated His holiness lightly. The prophets ate God's words. Again and again in Scripture, eating shows whom a person trusts, and the story leads to Jesus Christ, who lived by every word from God and gave Himself as the bread that brings life.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼâkal is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Old Testament. It appears in every major part of it. Genesis uses it in the garden and at patriarchal meals. Exodus through Deuteronomy use it for Passover, manna, sacrifices, and food laws. The historical books use it for royal tables and battle, wisdom literature for the rewards of a person's choices, and the prophets for judgment and restoration.

The NKJV renders it most often as "eat," "ate," and "eaten," but also as "devour," "consume," "feed," "dine," and "feast." These English choices follow the context. The Hebrew word stays the same whether a family is eating bread or a fire is burning a city.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to take something in and use it up, usually by eating. Three main senses appear:

1. To eat food. This is the most common sense. It covers physical eating, from ordinary meals to sacred feasts (Genesis 2:16; Ruth 2:14).

2. To consume or devour. Here the subject is fire, the sword, beasts, enemies, or insects that destroy what they touch (Leviticus 9:24; Genesis 37:20; Joel 1:4).

3. To take in or experience, figuratively. A person may "eat" the results of his own conduct (Proverbs 1:31; Proverbs 18:21), take God's words into himself (Jeremiah 15:16), or be "eaten up" by consuming zeal (Psalm 69:9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When people are the subject, the verb usually involves fellowship, worship, or obedience. Meals seal agreements (Genesis 26:30; 31:46), welcome guests (Genesis 18:8), and take place "before the LORD" (Deuteronomy 12:7). God's laws also set limits on eating, such as clean and unclean animals (Leviticus 11), blood (Leviticus 17:10–14), and holy portions reserved for priests (Leviticus 22:10). In Israel, what and where someone ate could show whose household they belonged to.

When fire, the sword, or enemies are the subject, the verb describes judgment. Isaiah 1:19–20 shows the contrast within a single passage. The willing and obedient will eat the good of the land, but those who refuse and rebel will be devoured by the sword.

Two grammatical patterns help the reader:

  • Doubled verb for emphasis. Hebrew sometimes repeats a verb to make it stronger. In Genesis 2:16 it literally reads eating you may eat, and the NKJV renders the emphasis as "freely eat." God's permission in the garden was generous and wholehearted.

  • To cause to eat, that is, to feed. One form of the verb means to make someone eat. With God as the subject, it describes His provision: He "fed" Israel with manna (Deuteronomy 8:3, 16) and would have fed them with the finest wheat (Psalm 81:16). The same form can also describe judgment, as when God feeds a faithless people with bitter wormwood (Jeremiah 9:15).

4. Theological Significance

Eating shows that human beings are creatures. Every meal confesses that our life comes from outside ourselves. Scripture presents God as the One who supplies that life, invites His people to His table, and decides what they may eat. For this reason, eating repeatedly becomes a test of whether people will trust His provision or take what He has withheld.

The verb also shows that God's holiness cannot be ignored. His glory appeared to Israel "like a consuming fire" on Sinai (Exodus 24:17), and Moses warned that the LORD is a consuming fire (Deuteronomy 4:24). Such holiness can receive an offering or destroy a rebel.

The covenant blessings and curses rely on this verb as well. Faithful Israel would eat its bread to the full (Leviticus 26:5). Rebellious Israel would plant and see enemies eat the harvest (Deuteronomy 28:33; Jeremiah 5:17), and the land of their enemies would eat them up (Leviticus 26:38). The worst curse reverses nourishment entirely: in siege, people would eat their own children (Deuteronomy 28:53). Disobedience turns the eater into what is eaten.

God also remains Lord over loss. Through Malachi He called Israel, "Return to Me, and I will return to you" (Malachi 3:7), and promised to rebuke "the devourer" so that it would not destroy the fruit of their ground (Malachi 3:11). Through Joel, after calling His people to return to Him with all their heart (Joel 2:12–13), He promised to restore the years the locusts had eaten, so that they would eat in plenty and praise His name (Joel 2:25–26).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 2–3: The garden permission and the first sin. God's command about the garden begins with a generous gift: "Of every tree of the garden you may freely eat" (Genesis 2:16). Only one tree is withheld. The serpent's strategy is to shrink the gift and enlarge the restriction: "Has God indeed said, 'You shall not eat of every tree of the garden'?" (Genesis 3:1). The woman saw "that the tree was good for food," so "she took of its fruit and ate... and he ate" (Genesis 3:6). The judgments that follow keep returning to eating. The serpent will "eat dust," and Adam will eat from cursed ground "in toil" (Genesis 3:14, 17). Afterward, the way to the tree of life is guarded so that fallen humanity cannot eat from it (Genesis 3:22–24). The test in the garden was whether people would receive food from God's hand or seize it apart from His word.

Exodus 12 and 24: Eating that binds people to God. On Passover night Israel did more than mark their doors with blood. They were commanded to eat the lamb's flesh that night, dressed and ready to leave (Exodus 12:8, 11). Eating the meal joined each household to the lamb whose blood protected them. Only those within the covenant community could share the meal (Exodus 12:43–48). At Sinai, Israel's elders saw the God of Israel, and God did not strike them. Instead, they ate and drank in His presence (Exodus 24:11). That meal was a sign of peace between God and those He had redeemed.

Leviticus 9–10: Holy fire, two outcomes. When Aaron first offered sacrifices, fire came out from the LORD and consumed the burnt offering. The people shouted and fell on their faces (Leviticus 9:24). Shortly afterward, Nadab and Abihu offered fire God had not commanded, and fire went out from the LORD and devoured them (Leviticus 10:2). Both verses use the same verb, and in both the fire comes from the LORD. The sacrifice God appointed was received, while worship that ignored His instructions was judged.

Deuteronomy 8: Hunger that teaches. Moses explains that God humbled Israel, let them hunger, and fed them with manna. He did this so they would know that people do not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD (Deuteronomy 8:3). God used both the hunger and the food to teach this lesson. Moses then warns that when Israel has eaten and is full in the good land, they must not forget the God who fed them (8:10–16).

Jeremiah 15:16 and Ezekiel 3:1–3: Eating God's words. Jeremiah says that when God's words were found, he ate them, and they became the joy and rejoicing of his heart. Ezekiel was commanded to eat a scroll filled with lament and woe. God caused him to eat it, and it tasted as sweet as honey in his mouth. Before these prophets spoke for God, His message had to become part of them. The words were not sweet because they were mild. Ezekiel's scroll was full of mourning. The sweetness came from receiving God's own speech.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament is written in Greek, so it uses different words for eating, but it carries these Old Testament patterns to their goal. Adam ate in a garden full of food and fell. Jesus fasted in the wilderness, and when Satan urged Him to make bread, He answered with Deuteronomy 8:3 (Matthew 4:4). Where Israel grumbled about bread, the Son trusted His Father's word.

Jesus then presents Himself as the true bread from heaven, greater than the manna. Those who eat of Him live forever (John 6:48–58). Jeremiah and Ezekiel ate God's words, and Christ is that Word made flesh. Jesus also gave His disciples bread and the cup at Passover (Luke 22:15–20), and Paul calls Him "our Passover" (1 Corinthians 5:7). The lamb Israel ate on the night of deliverance pointed to Him.

Two psalms that use this verb are applied directly to Jesus. The friend who ate His bread and lifted his heel against Him (Psalm 41:9) is fulfilled in Judas (John 13:18). The zeal for God's house that "has eaten me up" (Psalm 69:9) is fulfilled when Jesus cleanses the temple (John 2:17).

The storyline that began with a barred tree of life ends with an invitation. The overcomer is promised the right to eat from the tree of life (Revelation 2:7), and John, like Ezekiel, eats a scroll that is sweet in the mouth (Revelation 10:9–10).

7. Spirit & Life

Deuteronomy 8 changes how a believer can understand seasons of lack. God allowed Israel to hunger on purpose and then fed them with manna they could not explain or hoard apart from His instructions (Exodus 16:19–24). The aim was to bring them to depend on His word. Their hunger in the wilderness was not evidence that God had abandoned them. He was teaching them where life comes from.

The prophets show what it looks like to receive Scripture well. Jeremiah did not merely study God's words. He ate them, and they became his joy in the middle of suffering. Ezekiel found sweetness even in a scroll of woe. God's Word nourishes those who take it in deeply, including its hard parts.

Ordinary meals also belong to life with God. Israel was commanded to eat "before the LORD" and rejoice. In Scripture, a table shared with thanksgiving is a place of fellowship with the God who provides it.

Leviticus 9–10 brings both comfort and soberness. Fire from the same holy LORD that destroyed careless worship also received the sacrifice God appointed. Believers draw near through the Lamb God has given, which leaves no room either for terror or for casual disregard of His holiness. For those who have watched their labor or their years "eaten," Malachi 3:11 and Joel 2:25 show God's heart and power: He rebukes the devourer and restores what was lost. Both promises were spoken to Israel as part of His call to return to Him (Malachi 3:7; Joel 2:12–13). They do not guarantee that every loss will be given back in the same form, but they invite believers to turn to the Lord, trust Him, and pray boldly for His restoration.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ruth 2:14 — Boaz invites Ruth, a Moabite gleaner, to come and eat with his reapers, and she eats until she is satisfied. His hospitality reflects God's kindness to an outsider.

  • 2 Samuel 9:7 — David tells Mephibosheth, lame and from the house of the former king Saul, that he will eat bread at the king's table continually. Eating at the table is a sign of grace given to someone who could not earn it.

  • 1 Kings 19:5–8 — An angel twice tells the exhausted Elijah to get up and eat. In the strength of that food he travels forty days to Horeb. God cares for a discouraged servant's body as well as his calling.

  • Psalm 22:26 — The poor will eat and be satisfied, and those who seek the LORD will praise Him. This promise comes in the triumphant close of the psalm of the suffering one.

  • Isaiah 55:1–2 — God invites the thirsty and those without money to come, buy, and eat, and to delight in what is truly good. He offers food that satisfies, freely given.
