PRECIOUS THINGS, PLEASANT, EXCELLENCE - (meged, Strong's H4022)

Summary

The Hebrew word meged describes the very best of something: its choicest, most excellent part. The NKJV translates it as "precious things," "precious," or "pleasant." Scripture never uses it for gold, jewels, or anything made by human hands. It always describes the finest gifts of the living world, such as the dew of heaven, the deep springs, the harvest ripened by the sun and the months, the riches of the ancient hills, and the best fruit of a garden.

The word appears in only two places, and they belong together. In Deuteronomy 33 Moses uses it again and again as he blesses Joseph's land with the best that heaven and earth can give. He then crowns that whole list with the favor of the God who met him at the burning bush. In the Song of Solomon the same word describes the "pleasant fruits" of a bride's garden, which she has grown and saved for her beloved. Read together, these passages show a God who gives His best and who desires to delight in the fruit His goodness produces in His people.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Meged is a rare Hebrew noun found only in poetry. Standard lexicons connect it with a root meaning "to be eminent" or "to excel," and they note a related Arabic word meaning "to be glorious." Those connections are useful background, but the biblical passages themselves show how the word was actually used.

Both settings are agricultural and outdoors. Deuteronomy 33 is Moses' final blessing over the tribes before his death, and Joseph's portion describes a rich, well-watered land. The Song of Solomon pictures orchards, spices, and garden fruit. The word belongs to the world of harvest and growing things, and it marks that world at its peak.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Meged means excellence, choiceness, or the best of something. It usually stands before a phrase that names where the good thing comes from, such as "of heaven," "of the earth," or "of the hills." In those phrases it means "the choicest gifts that come from this source." A single definition covers all eight uses, with a slight difference in emphasis:

1. The choicest bounty of creation, the best gifts of sky, water, sun, seasons, hills, and land (Deuteronomy 33:13–16).

2. Choice, delightful fruit, the produce of a garden at its finest (Song of Solomon 4:13, 16; 7:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Deuteronomy, meged is stacked up in a cascade. Moses names the best of heaven, the best that the sun brings forth, the best produce of the months, the best of the everlasting hills, and the best of the earth and its fullness. The repetition is the point, because every part of creation is pictured yielding its finest gifts to Joseph.

In the Song, the word stays agricultural but becomes personal. The "pleasant fruits" are not a general harvest. They grow in one particular garden, and they are offered to one particular person. The setting moves from a tribal territory to a love relationship. The meaning of the word stays the same, but the direction of the gift changes.

4. Theological Significance

Both passages tie the best gifts of creation to God's generosity. In Deuteronomy 33:13, Joseph's land is "Blessed of the LORD." The dew, the deep waters, and the ripened crops did not come from chance or human skill. God gave them, and He did not give leftovers. The language Moses chose insists that God gives what is excellent.

Moses also refuses to let the gifts stand apart from the Giver. The list ends with God's own favor, which shows that what is truly valuable is not the produce but the goodwill of the One who sends it. Abundance that does not lead a person back to God has missed its purpose.

The Song adds the other half of the picture. Excellent things can also be cultivated, guarded, and given in love. When the two contexts are taken together, the word traces a pattern that runs through Scripture. God gives His best, and the life He has blessed bears fruit that brings Him delight.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 33:13–16 — Joseph's land and the God of the bush. Moses begins, "Blessed of the LORD is his land, With the precious things of heaven, with the dew, And the deep lying beneath" (33:13). The blessing reaches from the sky to the springs under the ground. The following verses continue the pattern. Moses speaks of the best fruits brought forth by the sun and the best produce of the months, then of the ancient mountains and everlasting hills. Finally he names the precious things of the earth and its fullness, together with the favor of Him who dwelt in the bush (33:14–16).

That last phrase is the key to the passage. Moses could have ended with the fullness of the earth. Instead, he reaches back forty years to the bush that burned without being consumed (Exodus 3:1–6). There the holy God came near, called Moses by name, and announced that He had seen His people's misery and would deliver them. By naming that God here, Moses identifies the source of all Joseph's abundance as the same God who rescued Israel from slavery. The finest produce of the land is the visible sign of His favor, and His favor is worth more than everything on the list.

This blessing echoes Jacob's earlier blessing of Joseph in Genesis 49:25–26, which also speaks of blessings of heaven above and of the deep that lies beneath. That passage uses different Hebrew wording, so it is not an occurrence of meged. It does show that God's generous care for Joseph's line ran from the patriarchs to the edge of the Promised Land.

Song of Solomon 4:12–16 — the garden opened to the beloved. The bridegroom describes his bride as an enclosed garden: "Your plants are an orchard of pomegranates With pleasant fruits, Fragrant henna with spikenard" (4:13). He goes on to list saffron, cinnamon, frankincense, myrrh, "all the chief spices," and "A well of living waters" (4:14–15). The garden is rich because it is well watered.

Then the bride answers him. She calls on the winds to blow so that the garden's fragrance will spread, and she invites him in: "Let my beloved come to his garden And eat its pleasant fruits" (4:16). She calls it "my garden" and then "his garden" in the same verse. What she has grown, she now gives. The choicest fruit exists for the joy of the one she loves.

Song of Solomon 7:13 — fruit laid up. Later she tells him that "at our gates are pleasant fruits, All manner, new and old, Which I have laid up for you, my beloved." "Laid up" means she has deliberately saved and guarded the fruit for him. Her love is patient and attentive, and it gathers the best of every season and keeps it for him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Deuteronomy's "precious things" describe the land God promised, and the land was always a pointer to something greater. In Christ, the Father has blessed His people "with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 1:3, paraphrased). The choicest gift is now a Person. Peter calls Jesus the living stone, "chosen by God and precious" (1 Peter 2:4–7, paraphrased). Peter uses a different Greek word, but the theme continues: God's most excellent gift is His Son. Moses linked the best of the earth to the favor of the God who drew near in a burning bush. That God has now drawn nearer still by taking on human flesh, and His favor is no longer seen only in harvests but in a Savior.

Christians have long read the Song, which is first of all a celebration of love between bride and bridegroom, as also picturing Christ and His people. The fruit image supports that reading. Jesus said, "I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit" (John 15:5), and "By this My Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit" (John 15:8). The garden that welcomes its beloved finds its fullest expression in a people who live in Christ and bear fruit for the Father's joy.

7. Spirit & Life

Taken together, the two settings of meged show both sides of life with God. In Deuteronomy, God gives excellent things. In the Song, a loved one gives excellent things back. The giving begins with God. The bride's garden is fruitful because it holds "A well of living waters" (Song 4:15), and Joseph's fields flourish because of "the dew" from heaven (Deuteronomy 33:13). Nothing excellent comes from us that was not first watered from above. James says the same: every good and perfect gift comes down from the Father (James 1:17, paraphrased).

God's generosity, however, is not meant to produce people who only consume. Paul urges believers "by the mercies of God, that you present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God" (Romans 12:1). The mercies come first, and the offering follows. The bride's words show what that offering looks like in practice. She did not give her beloved whatever happened to be left over. She tended the garden, gathered fruit "new and old," and saved it for him. A believer who has received God's best can do the same with time, attention, obedience, and worship, deliberately keeping the best for the Lord.

Moses' blessing also guards the heart against a quieter danger. A full barn can crowd out the One who filled it. Moses placed God's favor at the top of the list because the gifts are good, but the Giver is better. Even a well-watered life is only truly rich when it remains close to Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All eight occurrences of meged fall within the passages discussed above. Two of them add images worth noting on their own.

  • Deuteronomy 33:14 uses the word twice, once for what the sun brings forth and once for what the months produce. God's generosity reaches through every day and every season of the year.

  • Deuteronomy 33:15 applies the word to the ancient mountains and everlasting hills. Even the oldest and most lasting features of creation give their best under God's blessing.
