TOWER, WATCHTOWER - (migdâl, Strong's H4026)

Summary

The Hebrew word migdâl means a tower, a tall structure built to raise people above the ground around them. In the Old Testament it names city towers where frightened people fled during a siege, watchtowers where a guard could see trouble coming, towers in vineyards and pastures that protected the harvest and the flock, and once the wooden platform from which Ezra read God's Law to the returned exiles.

Scripture uses this ordinary building to ask where people place their trust. The Bible's first tower is Babel, raised so its builders could "make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:4), and the prophets warn that God will bring down every proud tower. Against that background Proverbs makes its bold claim: "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run to it and are safe" (Proverbs 18:10). Towers also show God's care. He builds a watchtower in His vineyard, He promises a future to the "tower of the flock," and His city's towers stand secure because He dwells there. The word moves from human self-exaltation to the safety found in God Himself.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Migdâl is a common Hebrew noun used for many kinds of raised structures. In the ancient Near East, towers were part of everyday life. Cities built them into their walls, and some cities had a stronger inner tower at their center. Kings built them at frontier posts and along travel routes. Farmers and herdsmen built simpler ones in vineyards and grazing lands. Most of the occurrences come from historical books such as Judges, Chronicles, and Nehemiah. Others appear in prophetic warnings and promises, and a handful in the poetry of Psalms, Proverbs, and the Song of Solomon.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

A migdâl is a tall, sturdy structure that gives height, protection, and a wide view. The particular kind of tower comes from the setting.

1. A defensive tower, either part of a city wall or a separate stronghold where people took refuge (Judges 9:51; Nehemiah 3:1).

2. A watchtower in a field, vineyard, or pasture, used to guard crops and flocks (Isaiah 5:2; Genesis 35:21).

3. A raised wooden platform, used once for Ezra's public reading of the Law (Nehemiah 8:4).

4. A poetic image of upright stature or of raised ground, as when the bride's neck is "like the tower of David" (Song of Solomon 4:4) or the beloved's cheeks are "Banks of scented herbs" (Song of Solomon 5:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of a tower in a passage depends on who builds it and who trusts it. When kings such as Uzziah, Jotham, and Hezekiah build towers, the Chronicler presents the work as part of a strong and blessed reign (2 Chronicles 26:9–10; 27:4; 32:5). When proud people build towers to secure themselves apart from God, those towers become targets of His judgment. A tower used for watching gives sight. In 2 Kings 9:17 "a watchman stood on the tower in Jezreel, and he saw the company of Jehu as he came." A tower used for refuge gives a last place of safety when other defenses fail.

Several towers carry proper names: the Tower of Hananel, the Tower of the Hundred, the Tower of David, and the "tower of Eder," which means "tower of the flock." These names tie the word to specific places in Israel's story. The prophets later take up those same places in their promises.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture sets two kinds of towers side by side. The first kind is built by human pride. Babel begins this line. Isaiah announces that on the Day of the LORD, God's judgment will fall on every high tower and every fortified wall, because these stand for human arrogance (Isaiah 2:15; see 2:11–12). Ezekiel applies the same judgment to Tyre, the wealthy trading city whose towers were manned by hired soldiers: "the men of Gammad were in your towers; They hung their shields on your walls all around; They made your beauty perfect" (Ezekiel 27:11). God declares that the enemy will "break down her towers" (Ezekiel 26:4). Wealth builds impressive defenses, but those defenses cannot stand against Him.

Judges shows the same weakness at the level of a town. Gideon tears down the tower of Penuel (Judges 8:9, 17). Abimelech burns the people who had taken shelter in the stronghold of Shechem (Judges 9:46–49). Towers people trusted proved no safer than the people inside them.

The second kind of tower reveals God. He builds towers to guard what He has planted. He is called a tower Himself. And He makes the towers of His city secure by His presence. In every one of these passages, real safety depends on God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Babel and the name of the LORD (Genesis 11:4–5; Proverbs 18:10; Psalm 61:3). Babel's builders said, "let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:4). They wanted a tower to win fame and to keep them from being scattered. Then the narrator says the LORD "came down" to see the city and the tower (Genesis 11:5). The tower meant to reach heaven was so small that God had to come down to look at it.

Proverbs 18:10 answers Babel in its own terms: "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run to it and are safe." At Babel people built a tower to make a name. In Proverbs the name itself is the tower. God's name stands for who He has shown Himself to be, His faithfulness, power, and presence. The righteous do not build anything. They run.

Judges 9:51 gives a picture of what that running looked like. When Thebez was attacked, "there was a strong tower in the city, and all the men and women—all the people of the city—fled there and shut themselves in." That inner tower was the last place to go after the outer walls had fallen. Proverbs says the LORD is that place for His people. David prays the same truth: "For You have been a shelter for me, A strong tower from the enemy" (Psalm 61:3). The tower of Thebez held, and a woman on its roof dropped a millstone that ended Abimelech's attack (Judges 9:53). How much more secure are those who run into the name of the LORD.

The tower in God's vineyard (Isaiah 5:2; Mark 12:1). In Isaiah's Song of the Vineyard, the owner clears the ground, plants choice vines, builds a tower in the middle, and cuts out a winepress. Then he looks for good grapes, and the vineyard produces wild ones. The tower is a mark of how much the owner cared. A watchman stationed there would guard the vines from thieves and animals all season. God then asks what more He could have done for His vineyard (Isaiah 5:4).

Jesus took up this picture: "A man planted a vineyard and set a hedge around it, dug a place for the wine vat and built a tower" (Mark 12:1; compare Matthew 21:33). The Gospels are written in Greek and use a Greek word for tower, but the picture clearly comes from Isaiah. By mentioning the tower, Jesus showed that the leaders who rejected God's servants and finally His Son had no excuse. The owner had provided everything they needed to bear fruit.

The tower of the flock (Genesis 35:21; Micah 4:8). After Rachel died and was buried on the way to Bethlehem, Jacob "pitched his tent beyond the tower of Eder" (Genesis 35:19–21), a shepherds' tower for watching over the flocks. Micah later addresses Jerusalem by the same name: "And you, O tower of the flock, The stronghold of the daughter of Zion, To you shall it come, Even the former dominion shall come" (Micah 4:8). A few verses later he turns to "Bethlehem Ephrathah," from whom "The One to be Ruler in Israel" will come (Micah 5:2). Jewish tradition connected Migdal Eder with the Bethlehem area and with the coming of the Messiah. That tradition cannot be proven, but Micah himself places the restored kingdom and the Ruler from Bethlehem side by side. The promised King is also a Shepherd who watches over His flock.

Ezra's platform (Nehemiah 8:4). When the exiles gathered at the Water Gate, Ezra stood on a wooden migdâl built for the occasion so that he could read the Book of the Law. This is the only place the NKJV translates the word "platform." The platform lifted Ezra so the whole crowd could see and hear him. When he opened the book, all the people stood up (Nehemiah 8:5). A restored people raised God's Word to a place where everyone could hear it, and they responded with reverence before they had heard a single line.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of towers runs from Babel's attempt to climb up to heaven to God coming down to His people. Babel tried to reach heaven and make a name. In Christ, God came down and gave His people His own name for refuge. Jesus is the Shepherd-King whom Micah's tower of the flock looked toward. On the night of His birth, shepherds near Bethlehem were "keeping watch over their flock by night" (Luke 2:8). Luke does not mention Migdal Eder, but the scene matches Micah's promise closely: shepherds keeping watch near Bethlehem when the promised Ruler is born.

Christ is also the Son whom the vineyard tenants rejected. In His parable the tower stands as proof that God's care was complete and that Israel's rejection of Him was without excuse.

The Tower of Hananel carries the larger story forward. Nehemiah records its rebuilding and its dedication after the exile (Nehemiah 3:1; 12:39). Jeremiah had promised that the city would be rebuilt for the LORD from the Tower of Hananel to the Corner Gate, and that it would never again be uprooted or thrown down (Jeremiah 31:38–40). Zechariah names the same landmark when he describes Jerusalem "raised up and inhabited in her place... from the Tower of Hananel to the king's winepresses" (Zechariah 14:10). A real tower that the returned exiles rebuilt became, in the prophets, a marker of the city God will make permanent.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 48 invites worshipers to "Walk about Zion, And go all around her. Count her towers" (Psalm 48:12). The psalm has already explained why the towers matter: "God is in her palaces; He is known as her refuge" (Psalm 48:3). Counting the towers was a way of remembering God's protection. The people looked at the stones and saw what God had done to keep them. They counted so they could tell the next generation (Psalm 48:13). Believers do the same thing when they recall God's specific acts of faithfulness and pass them on to their children.

Every believer has towers of his own to give up. Reputation, savings, competence, and moral record all promise safety. Babel and Tyre show how those towers end. Proverbs 18:10 points to the one tower worth running to, and running means admitting you cannot protect yourself.

The Song of Solomon adds a different note. The bride answers her brothers' worry about her youth by saying, "I am a wall, And my breasts like towers; Then I became in his eyes As one who found peace" (Song of Solomon 8:10). Her firm virtue leads to peace and to favor in her beloved's eyes. The groom praises her: "Your neck is like the tower of David, Built for an armory, On which hang a thousand bucklers, All shields of mighty men" (Song of Solomon 4:4). Ancient walls were often lined with shields that served as both protection and decoration. The Song is love poetry first. Read in the light of the whole Bible, it gives the church a picture of herself: standing upright and secure, adorned with shields she did not win, and found at peace in her King's eyes.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Kings 9:17 — The watchman on the tower in Jezreel sees Jehu's company coming before anyone else does. A tower gives sight, and God's people need watchfulness to see what is approaching.

  • 2 Kings 17:9 — Israel built pagan high places everywhere "from watchtower to fortified city." Their idolatry spread from the smallest outpost to the largest city.

  • 2 Chronicles 26:10 — Uzziah built towers in the desert to protect his many herds. Here the tower is a sign of good management under God's blessing.

  • 2 Chronicles 32:5 — Facing Assyria, Hezekiah repaired the broken wall and raised it up to the towers. In the following verses he also tells the people that the LORD is with them.

  • Song of Solomon 7:4 — The bride's neck is "like an ivory tower" and her nose "like the tower of Lebanon Which looks toward Damascus." These images express stately, watchful beauty.
