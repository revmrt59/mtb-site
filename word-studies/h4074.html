MEDIA (MADAI) - (Mâday, Strong's H4074)

Summary

Mâday names two closely linked things in the Hebrew Bible. First, it is Madai, a son of Japheth and grandson of Noah, listed among the founding peoples in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5). Second, and far more often, it is Media and the Medes, the people and land in the mountains of what is now northwestern Iran. Over time the Medes became a major world power and later partners with Persia. The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is probably of Indo-Iranian origin, but no reliable meaning can be given, and the Bible builds no wordplay on it. What matters in Scripture is what God does with this people.

The Medes enter Israel's story at a low point. When Assyria destroyed Samaria, the northern tribes were deported to "the cities of the Medes" (2 Kings 17:6). Later, Isaiah and Jeremiah announce that the LORD Himself will stir up the Medes against Babylon, a people who "will not regard silver" (Isaiah 13:17), sent as "the vengeance for His temple" (Jeremiah 51:11). In Esther and Daniel, Media stands beside Persia as one named people within the empire that replaced Babylon, known for laws that could not be changed. In Ezra, the record of Cyrus's decree to rebuild God's house is found in a palace in the province of Media (Ezra 6:2). At Pentecost, Luke lists "Medes" among those who heard the wonderful works of God in their own languages (Acts 2:9). The land where Israel was scattered in judgment became part of the audience of the gospel.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name Mâday names an ancestor and also the land and people of Media. Context distinguishes the man from the nation.

Madai the ancestor. In Genesis 10:2 and 1 Chronicles 1:5, Madai is the third-listed son of Japheth, listed with Gomer, Magog, Javan, and others. The Table of Nations traces peoples back to named ancestors, and Madai stands at the head of the Median line. Scripture records nothing more about him as a person.

Media and the Medes. In the other fourteen occurrences, the word names the land of Media or its people. Context decides whether "Media" (the region or kingdom) or "the Medes" (the people) is the better English rendering. Media lay east of Assyria and northwest of Persia, in mountainous country. In Esther and Daniel it is paired with Persia as a joint kingdom.

The name's meaning. The etymology of Mâday is debated. Most likely the name comes from the Medes' own Indo-Iranian language and was carried over into Hebrew as a foreign name. No verified meaning is available, and this study does not propose one. Scripture makes no play on the name.

Related names indexed separately. The Hebrew lemma H4074 is only part of the Bible's witness to the Medes. Related forms are listed under separate Strong's entries. H4075 is the Hebrew gentilic "Mede," used of Darius in Daniel 11:1. The Aramaic chapters of Daniel and Ezra use H4076, the Aramaic name for the people and land (as in Belshazzar's sentence in Daniel 5:28, the laws of Daniel 6, and Ezra 6:2), and H4077, the Aramaic gentilic for Darius the Mede (Daniel 5:31). The New Testament's "Medes" in Acts 2:9 is Greek (G3370). These passages are discussed below as related witnesses, not as occurrences of H4074.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

A name among the nations (Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5). Madai first appears in the long list of peoples who spread across the earth after the flood. The Chronicler repeats the line when he opens Israel's history with Adam. The Medes are part of the human family God made and watched over from the beginning, long before they had any role in Israel's affairs.

The land of Israel's exile (2 Kings 17:6; 18:11). The Medes first touch Israel's story as a place of loss. In Hoshea's ninth year, "the king of Assyria took Samaria and carried Israel away to Assyria, and placed them in Halah and by the Habor, the River of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes" (2 Kings 17:6). Second Kings 18:11 repeats the record during Hezekiah's reign in Judah. The writer explains why: Israel had refused God's warnings and served other gods (2 Kings 17:7–18). At this point Media does nothing in the story. It is simply far away, a foreign country at the eastern edge of Assyria's reach where the northern tribes vanish from the land God gave them.

The instrument against Babylon (Isaiah 13; 21; Jeremiah 51). In the prophets, the Medes are no longer a distant place but an army. Isaiah's oracle against Babylon names them directly: "Behold, I will stir up the Medes against them, who will not regard silver; and as for gold, they will not delight in it" (Isaiah 13:17). A conqueror who cannot be bribed cannot be sent away with a payment. The next verse is hard to read: "Also their bows will dash the young men to pieces, and they will have no pity on the fruit of the womb; their eye will not spare children" (Isaiah 13:18). In Isaiah 21:2 the prophet hears the summons, "Besiege, O Media!" The vision troubles him so deeply that he describes being bowed down and dismayed (Isaiah 21:3–4). Announcing judgment brought him no pleasure.

Jeremiah gives the reason most plainly: "The LORD has raised up the spirit of the kings of the Medes. For His plan is against Babylon to destroy it, because it is the vengeance of the LORD, the vengeance for His temple" (Jeremiah 51:11). Babylon had burned the house where God put His name. The Medes would be the means of answering that act. Later in the chapter, the LORD calls the nations, the kings of the Medes, and their governors and rulers to prepare against Babylon (Jeremiah 51:28).

Jeremiah does not make the Medes heroes. In Jeremiah 25, where the prophet takes the cup of the LORD's wrath to the nations, "all the kings of the Medes" are on the list of those who must drink it (Jeremiah 25:25). God uses them, and God also judges them.

Partner in a ruling empire (Daniel; Esther). Daniel lived to see Babylon fall. In the Aramaic account, Belshazzar hears the sentence: "PERES: Your kingdom has been divided, and given to the Medes and Persians" (Daniel 5:28). The kingdom then passes to Darius the Mede (Daniel 5:31). In the Hebrew vision of Daniel 8, the angel explains: "The ram which you saw, having the two horns—they are the kings of Media and Persia" (Daniel 8:20). One horn grows higher than the other, and the higher one comes up last (Daniel 8:3). This matches Persia rising to lead the partnership.

Daniel 9:1 dates Daniel's great prayer to the first year of Darius, "of the lineage of the Medes." In that year Daniel was reading Jeremiah and found the prophecy of seventy years of desolation (Daniel 9:2; Jeremiah 25:11–12). The same chapter of Jeremiah that lists the kings of the Medes was being fulfilled before his eyes, under a Median king. Who this Darius was historically is still debated.

The book of Esther is set in Persia's court, and its usual phrase is "Persia and Media." At Ahasuerus's feast "the powers of Persia and Media" were present (Esther 1:3). The king's closest advisors are "the seven princes of Persia and Media" (1:14), and the noblewomen are "the noble ladies of Persia and Media" (1:18). The book closes by pointing to "the book of the chronicles of the kings of Media and Persia" (10:2). Median nobility still held a place of honor, though Persia now led.

Laws that could not be changed. Esther and Daniel both stress one feature of this empire. Memucan advises the king to remove Vashti by a decree "recorded in the laws of the Persians and the Medes, so that it will not be altered" (Esther 1:19). The occasion is almost absurd. The empire's most rigid legal machinery is put to work over a wine-soaked king's wounded pride (Esther 1:10–12). Later, Haman's decree to destroy the Jews could not be revoked. Rescue came through a second decree that let the Jews defend themselves (Esther 8:8–11). In Daniel 6, Darius is trapped by his own unchangeable law and cannot save Daniel from the lions. God shuts the lions' mouths (Daniel 6:8, 14–15, 22).

The record of the decree in Media (Ezra 6:2). When the rebuilding of the temple was challenged, a search was made, "and at Achmetha, in the palace that is in the province of Media, a scroll was found." It held Cyrus's decree that the house of God in Jerusalem be rebuilt (Ezra 6:3–5). Jeremiah had called the Medes "the vengeance for His temple." Now, in a Median palace, the document that protected the temple's rebuilding came to light.

At Pentecost (Acts 2:9). The last biblical mention of the Medes is joyful. Among the "devout men, from every nation under heaven" in Jerusalem (Acts 2:5) were "Parthians and Medes and Elamites" (Acts 2:9). These were Jews and proselytes living in those lands (Acts 2:10), and they heard the apostles speak "the wonderful works of God" in their own tongues (Acts 2:11).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states several things plainly. God stirred up the Medes (Isaiah 13:17). He raised up the spirit of their kings for His own plan against Babylon (Jeremiah 51:11). The ram of Daniel 8 is Media and Persia (Daniel 8:20). The Medes are not portrayed as a covenant people, yet He moved them as freely as He moved Assyria and Babylon before them. Even so, Daniel 6:25–27 records Darius honoring "the living God," and Acts 2 shows devout Jews and proselytes from Media hearing the gospel.

Some conclusions follow from the larger story without being stated in so many words. The fall of Babylon to the Medes and Persians opened the way for Cyrus's decree and the return from exile (Ezra 1:1–4). The finding of that decree in Media's province (Ezra 6:2) suggests that God's answer to the burning of His temple reached beyond Babylon's ruin to the rebuilding of His house. It also seems fitting that Media's story begins with the scattering of Israel and ends with Pentecost, though Luke does not draw the line himself. The same land that took in the exiled northern tribes was represented when God poured out His Spirit in Jerusalem.

Scripture is also careful not to approve the Medes' cruelty. Isaiah 13:18 describes their brutality with horror, not praise. Jeremiah 25:25 places their kings under the same cup of wrath. God can use a ruthless nation to bring judgment while holding that nation accountable for what it is.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Daniel 8:20 explicitly identifies Media and Persia as the ram. Many interpreters also see Medo-Persia as the second kingdom in Daniel 2 and Daniel 7, the silver chest and arms and the bear. That reading is strongly supported, though not unanimous. Either way, Daniel's visions place this empire in a line of human kingdoms that rise and fall until "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom which shall never be destroyed" (Daniel 2:44, paraphrased). The Medes were mighty, but their kingdom was given to them (Daniel 5:28), and in time it was taken away by the goat from the west (Daniel 8:5–7, 21). Only the kingdom of the Son of Man lasts forever (Daniel 7:13–14). The New Testament proclaims that kingdom in Jesus.

Pentecost gives the clearest gospel connection. Acts 2 does not call itself a reversal of 2 Kings 17. Still, the canonical movement is hard to miss. Israel was scattered to the cities of the Medes because of its faithlessness, and people from that same region stood in Jerusalem to hear of Christ's resurrection and the promise of the Spirit (Acts 2:32–39). Luke does not say how many from each region believed. Peter's words fit them well: "the promise is to you and to your children, and to all who are afar off, as many as the Lord our God will call" (Acts 2:39).

The unchangeable law of the Medes and Persians also offers a thematic contrast. Human decrees claimed permanence but often trapped the people who made them. God's word truly stands forever (Isaiah 40:8), and His purposes save rather than ensnare.

5. Spirit & Life

The Medes' story shows how freely God rules over powers outside His covenant people. The kings of the Medes made their own plans and gathered their own armies. Yet Jeremiah could say that the LORD had raised up their spirit, and that the plan was His (Jeremiah 51:11). For believers under governments or cultural forces that seem far beyond their control, this is solid ground. God does not need the nations' permission to keep His word to His people.

God also takes the honor of His house seriously. The phrase "the vengeance for His temple" tells us He noticed what Babylon did to the place of His worship. He answered it, and then He made sure the house was rebuilt. The God who cared about Jerusalem's temple now dwells among His people by His Spirit (Ephesians 2:21–22). He has not grown indifferent to the holiness of His dwelling.

The laws of the Medes and Persians warn about a kind of pride that still shows up in human systems. Ahasuerus and Darius both found that their own irreversible words bound them. God was not bound. He rescued Daniel in the lions' den and the Jews in Esther's day. Nothing a human authority has sealed can close a door God intends to open.

The story also carries mercy. The northern tribes were carried off to Media as judgment, and for generations that land must have stood for loss and distance from God. Yet Media's last appearance in Scripture is at Pentecost, among people hearing the wonderful works of God. No place is too far from Jerusalem for the gospel to reach.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5 — Madai, son of Japheth, as ancestor of the Medes in the Table of Nations.

  • 2 Kings 17:6; 18:11 — Israel deported to "the cities of the Medes" after Samaria's fall.

  • Isaiah 13:17–18 — God stirs up the Medes, an army that cannot be bribed and shows no pity.

  • Isaiah 21:2 — Media summoned against Babylon, and the prophet's distress at the vision.

  • Jeremiah 25:25 — The kings of the Medes also drink the cup of God's wrath.

  • Jeremiah 51:11, 28 — The LORD raises up the Medes as "the vengeance for His temple."

  • Daniel 5:28 (Aramaic, related entry) — Babylon's kingdom given to the Medes and Persians.

  • Daniel 8:20 — The two-horned ram identified as Media and Persia.

  • Daniel 9:1 — Daniel's prayer in the first year of Darius the Mede, while reading Jeremiah's seventy years.

  • Esther 1:3, 14, 18, 19; 10:2 — "Persia and Media" as the joint empire, and its unchangeable law.

  • Daniel 6 (Aramaic, related entry) — The unchangeable law and God's rescue of Daniel.

  • Ezra 6:2 (related entry) — Cyrus's temple decree found in the province of Media.

  • Acts 2:9 (Greek, G3370) — Medes present at Pentecost.
