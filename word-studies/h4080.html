MIDIAN - (Midyân, Strong's H4080)

Summary

Midian is the name of a son of Abraham, of the people descended from him, and of the desert land where they lived, chiefly east of the Gulf of Aqaba and ranging across the wilderness south and east of Canaan. The name most likely comes from a Hebrew root meaning "strife," "contention," or "judgment." That derivation is probable rather than certain, and Scripture never builds a lesson on it. Midian was born to Abraham and Keturah and was later sent away eastward from Isaac, the son of promise. His descendants then stood near Israel's story for centuries. Midian sheltered Moses for forty years. In its wilderness God spoke from the burning bush, and from it came Jethro, a priest who blessed the LORD and offered sacrifices to Him. Yet Midian also helped hire Balaam, drew Israel into idolatry at Peor, and later swept over the land like locusts until God broke its power through Gideon's three hundred.

The prophets turned that victory into a phrase. Isaiah promised that the coming Child would break the oppressor's yoke "as in the day of Midian" (Isaiah 9:4), and the Gospels show that promise being fulfilled in Jesus' ministry in Galilee. Isaiah also saw a day when the camels of Midian would come to Zion carrying gold and incense, proclaiming "the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). Midian's story warns that being close to God's people is different from belonging to God. It also shows a God who saves by His own strength and who keeps a place in His purposes even for the descendants of those once sent away.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Midian names a man, his descendants, and their land. The passage usually makes clear which is meant. The first is a person: Midian, the fourth-listed of Keturah's six sons by Abraham (Genesis 25:1–2; 1 Chronicles 1:32). Genesis places the account after Sarah's death but does not date each birth. His five sons were Ephah, Epher, Hanoch, Abidah, and Eldaah (Genesis 25:4; 1 Chronicles 1:33). The second is the people descended from him, a loose confederation of tribes with their own elders, princes, and kings (Numbers 22:4, 7; 31:8; Judges 7:25; 8:5). The third is the land they occupied, "the land of Midian" (Exodus 2:15; Habakkuk 3:7).

That land is hard to map precisely. Midian's heartland appears to have lain east of the Gulf of Aqaba, but the Midianites were herders and caravan traders, so their camps reached into the wilderness near Horeb (Exodus 3:1), whose exact location is disputed, into the plains of Moab (Numbers 22:4; Genesis 36:35), and, in Gideon's day, into the Jezreel Valley in the heart of Israel (Judges 6:33). Scripture treats Midian as a people on the move, and its borders should not be drawn more sharply than the text allows.

The Hebrew lemma is מִדְיָן (Midyân). The name is most likely related to the Hebrew root dîn, which carries the ideas of strife, contention, or judgment. Because this derivation is probable rather than proven, the name's meaning should not be read as a verdict on Midian's character. Scripture makes no wordplay on it, and Midian's history includes both Jethro's hospitality and Peor's treachery.

Some related names are indexed separately and should be kept distinct from H4080. The gentilic form "Midianite(s)" (H4084) describes the traders who bought Joseph (Genesis 37:28) and Hobab "the son of Reuel the Midianite," whom Moses invited to guide Israel (Numbers 10:29). The New Testament uses a Greek form of the name when Stephen recalls that Moses lived as a sojourner in Midian (Acts 7:29). These passages fill out the picture, but they are not counted among the occurrences of this Hebrew entry. The text also links Midianites with Ishmaelites. Joseph's buyers are called both (Genesis 37:25–28), and Gideon's Midianite enemies wore gold earrings "because they were Ishmaelites" (Judges 8:24). This suggests intermarried desert peoples, or a broad label used for caravan tribes, but the Bible does not explain the exact relationship.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

A son sent eastward (Genesis 25). Midian's story begins inside Abraham's household. After listing Keturah's sons, Genesis records that Abraham gave all he had to Isaac and gave gifts to the sons of his concubines, sending them eastward, away from Isaac, while he was still living (Genesis 25:5–6). The arrangement was deliberate. Midian was a real son of Abraham, but the promise would run through Isaac. Midian's people would always be relatives of Israel, close in blood and often close in geography, yet outside the line through which God had promised to bless the nations.

Midian is next mentioned, in canonical order, in the record of Edom's early kings. Hadad son of Bedad "attacked Midian in the field of Moab" (Genesis 36:35; 1 Chronicles 1:46, paraphrased). The brief note shows that Midian was already a known power in the Transjordan.

A refuge for Moses (Exodus 2–4). The tone changes completely when Moses kills an Egyptian and runs for his life: "But Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh and dwelt in the land of Midian; and he sat down by a well" (Exodus 2:15). At that well he defended the seven daughters of the priest of Midian. He married one of them, Zipporah, and spent years tending his father-in-law's flocks (Exodus 2:16–21; 3:1). This priest is called Reuel (Exodus 2:18) and Jethro (Exodus 3:1). Stephen later says Moses was eighty when God sent him back, after forty years in Midian (Acts 7:23, 29–30).

Midian became the place where God prepared His deliverer. Moses led Jethro's flock to Horeb, the mountain of God, and there the LORD spoke from the bush that burned without being consumed (Exodus 3:1–6). The call that would bring Israel out of Egypt came to a fugitive shepherd caring for a Midianite priest's sheep. It was also in Midian that the LORD told Moses to go back to Egypt, because everyone who had sought his life was dead (Exodus 4:19).

Jethro's worship (Exodus 18). After the Exodus, "Jethro, the priest of Midian" heard what God had done and brought Zipporah and her sons to Moses in the wilderness (Exodus 18:1–5). When Moses told him the whole story, Jethro rejoiced and blessed the LORD for delivering His people. He declared that he now knew the LORD was greater than all the gods. Then he brought a burnt offering and sacrifices to God, and Aaron and the elders of Israel ate bread with him before God (Exodus 18:9–12). Jethro then counseled Moses to appoint capable, God-fearing men to judge the people, so that the burden of leadership would be shared (Exodus 18:13–26). A Midianite priest helped bring order to Israel's life under God. Later, Moses urged Hobab "the son of Reuel the Midianite, Moses' father-in-law" to come with Israel because he knew how to camp in the wilderness (Numbers 10:29–32). Whether "father-in-law" there refers to Reuel or to Hobab, and how Reuel, Jethro, and Hobab relate, is debated; Judges 4:11 calls Hobab Moses' father-in-law. The Kenites, identified with this family, went up with Judah into the land (Judges 1:16; 4:11) and kept friendly ties with Israel into Saul's day (1 Samuel 15:6).

Midian against Israel: Balaam and Peor (Numbers 22–25). Forty years later, Midian appears on the opposite side. As Israel camped on the plains of Moab, the Moabites said to "the elders of Midian" that this vast company would lick up everything around them, as an ox licks up grass (Numbers 22:4). Elders of Moab and Midian went together with the diviner's fee in hand to hire Balaam to curse Israel (Numbers 22:7). God turned every attempted curse into blessing (Numbers 23–24).

Where cursing failed, seduction succeeded. Israelite men joined in sexual immorality with Moabite women and bowed to Baal of Peor, and the LORD's anger burned against Israel (Numbers 25:1–3). In the middle of the plague, an Israelite leader brought a Midianite woman into the camp in open defiance. She was Cozbi, daughter of Zur, a head of a father's house in Midian (Numbers 25:6, 14–15). Phinehas's zeal ended the plague. The LORD then showed Moses where the scheme had come from:

"Harass the Midianites, and attack them; for they harassed you with their schemes by which they seduced you in the matter of Peor and in the matter of Cozbi, the daughter of a leader of Midian, their sister, who was killed in the day of the plague because of Peor." (Numbers 25:17–18)

Moab stands in the foreground of the Balaam story, but God names Midian's "schemes." Later Moses reveals that the plan had come from Balaam's counsel (Numbers 31:16), and the New Testament remembers Balaam as the one who taught Balak to put a stumbling block before Israel (Revelation 2:14).

The LORD's vengeance (Numbers 31; Joshua 13). Moses' last military campaign was against Midian. The LORD called it vengeance on Midian for Israel, and Moses called it "the LORD's vengeance" (Numbers 31:2–3, paraphrased). A thousand men from each tribe went out with Phinehas, carrying the holy articles and trumpets (Numbers 31:3–6). They killed the five kings of Midian, Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba, and they killed Balaam with the sword (Numbers 31:7–8). Joshua 13:21 adds that these Midianite princes had been allied with Sihon the Amorite. The chapter's severity, including Moses' anger that the women who had carried out Peor's seduction were spared (Numbers 31:14–16), troubles modern readers. It should be read in light of what Peor nearly did: it brought a deadly plague on the generation about to enter the land and struck at Israel's loyalty to the LORD itself. God treated the corruption of His people's worship as a mortal threat.

Locusts over the land (Judges 6). Midian was not finished. Generations later, "the children of Israel did evil in the sight of the LORD. So the LORD delivered them into the hand of Midian for seven years" (Judges 6:1, paraphrased). The oppression was crushing. Israel hid in mountain dens, caves, and strongholds (6:2). At every harvest, Midianites, Amalekites, and the people of the East swept in and destroyed the crops as far as Gaza, leaving no sheep, ox, or donkey (6:3–4):

"For they would come up with their livestock and their tents, coming in as numerous as locusts; both they and their camels were without number; and they would enter the land to destroy it." (Judges 6:5)

Israel was "greatly impoverished" and cried to the LORD (6:6). Before He sent a deliverer, God sent a prophet to remind them that He had brought them out of Egypt and that they had not obeyed His voice (6:7–10). God dealt with the cause before He removed the oppressor.

Gideon and the three hundred (Judges 6–8). The Angel of the LORD found Gideon threshing wheat in a winepress to hide it from Midian (6:11). Gideon's first response was an honest complaint: if the LORD was with them, why had all this happened? (6:13). The LORD did not argue. He sent him: Gideon was to go in this might of his and save Israel from the hand of Midian, because the LORD had sent him (6:14). He promised that Gideon would strike down the Midianites as though they were one man (6:16).

When Midian and its allies camped in the Valley of Jezreel (6:33), Gideon gathered an army. Then God made it smaller. Thirty-two thousand became ten thousand, and ten thousand became three hundred, because the LORD said that Israel would otherwise claim the glory, saying its own hand had saved it (7:2–8). The enemy lay in the valley "as numerous as locusts," with camels as countless as the sand (7:12). God let Gideon overhear a Midianite's dream of a barley loaf tumbling into the camp and overturning a tent. His companion interpreted it as the sword of Gideon, into whose hand God had delivered Midian (7:13–14). Gideon worshiped, and then he attacked with trumpets, torches, and pitchers. The LORD set each Midianite's sword against his companion, and the army fled (7:15–22).

The pursuit reached the Jordan. Ephraim captured and killed Midian's princes Oreb and Zeeb (7:24–25). Gideon soothed Ephraim's wounded pride with a gentle answer (8:1–3), then pursued and captured the kings Zebah and Zalmunna (8:5, 12). When Israel asked him to rule over them because he had saved them from Midian, Gideon refused and said the LORD would rule over them (8:22–23). Scripture summarizes the result: Midian was subdued and lifted its head no more, and the land was quiet for forty years (8:28). Decades later, Jotham could remind Shechem that his father had risked his life to deliver them from Midian (9:17).

The story has a dark postscript. Gideon asked for the gold earrings from the plunder and made an ephod from them. All Israel played the harlot with it, and it became a snare to Gideon and his house (8:24–27). Midian's armies had been defeated, but Midian's gold led Israel back into the same kind of false worship that Peor had introduced.

Later history and memory. Midian later appears as a route through the southern wilderness. Hadad the Edomite, Solomon's adversary, set out from Midian, passed through Paran, and reached Egypt (1 Kings 11:18). From then on, Midian lives on mainly in Israel's memory and prophecy, as the next section shows.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture plainly states several things about Midian. Midian descended from Abraham and was deliberately sent away from Isaac (Genesis 25:2, 6). God used Midian's wilderness to shelter and prepare Moses, and He revealed Himself there (Exodus 2–3). A priest of Midian confessed the LORD's greatness and worshiped Him (Exodus 18:10–12). Midian schemed to seduce Israel at Peor and was judged for it (Numbers 25:16–18; 31:1–8). God gave Israel into Midian's hand because of Israel's sin, and He delivered Israel by Gideon's tiny band so that the glory would be His (Judges 6:1; 7:2).

From these facts, some careful implications follow. Midian shows what it looks like to stand near God's promise without being shaped by it. The same people produced Jethro, who rejoiced at God's mercy to Israel, and Cozbi, whose presence in the camp served a plot against Israel. Kinship with Abraham gave Midian no immunity. In Numbers, blood ties and family history counted for nothing when Midian set itself against the LORD's people and the LORD's worship.

Midian also confronted Israel with two distinct dangers. At Peor, the threat came through invitation, feasting, and desire, drawing Israel away from the LORD from the inside (Numbers 25:1–2). In Judges, it came through force and fear, as raiders stripped Israel of harvests and livestock until people hid in caves (Judges 6:2–6). One danger corrupted Israel's worship; the other emptied its storehouses and broke its courage. Both are recorded, and both were answered by God.

Finally, the Midian chapters show that God controls the rise and fall of nations for the sake of His people's faithfulness. He handed Israel to Midian because of Israel's evil (Judges 6:1). He named Israel's disobedience through a prophet before raising a deliverer (6:8–10). He shrank the army so that no one could mistake who had won (7:2). Midian's defeat was meant to teach Israel to trust the LORD rather than its own numbers.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

"The day of Midian." Gideon's victory became a byword in Israel. Asaph prayed against a new coalition of enemies, "Deal with them as with Midian, As with Sisera, As with Jabin at the Brook Kishon" (Psalm 83:9), and asked God to make their nobles like Oreb and Zeeb and their princes like Zebah and Zalmunna (83:11). Isaiah promised Judah that the LORD would raise a scourge against Assyria "like the slaughter of Midian at the rock of Oreb" (Isaiah 10:26, paraphrased). For both writers, Midian's fall meant a crushing oppressor brought down suddenly by God.

Isaiah uses the phrase most memorably in his promise of the coming King:

"For You have broken the yoke of his burden And the staff of his shoulder, The rod of his oppressor, As in the day of Midian." (Isaiah 9:4)

The surrounding verses describe light dawning on Zebulun, Naphtali, and "Galilee of the Gentiles" (9:1–2) and the birth of a Child who will be called Wonderful, Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace, whose government will have no end on David's throne (9:6–7). Matthew explicitly quotes Isaiah 9:1–2 as fulfilled when Jesus began preaching in Galilee (Matthew 4:12–17). The New Testament does not mention Midian here, but it places Jesus at the center of the very passage that uses Gideon's victory as its model.

The connection deserves attention. Gideon's battle was fought in the Jezreel Valley, and among the tribes he summoned were Zebulun and Naphtali (Judges 6:33–35), the same northern territories Isaiah names. Isaiah's point is how the yoke will be broken. In the day of Midian, the oppressor fell to a handful of men with torches and jars, so plainly that the victory was God's alone. Isaiah promises a liberation of the same kind, accomplished by God through a Child. The Gospels show its fulfillment in a Messiah who conquered through weakness, rejection, and the cross, a victory no human army could claim (1 Corinthians 1:25; Colossians 2:15). This is strongly supported canonical development. Isaiah draws the pattern, and the New Testament identifies Jesus as the King of Isaiah 9, even though it nowhere calls Gideon a type of Christ.

The camels come home. Isaiah 60 pictures the nations streaming toward Zion's light:

"The multitude of camels shall cover your land, The dromedaries of Midian and Ephah; All those from Sheba shall come; They shall bring gold and incense, And they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD." (Isaiah 60:6)

The reversal is striking. In Judges, Midian's camels covered Israel's land "without number" to destroy it (Judges 6:5). In Isaiah, the camels of Midian cover the land to bring gifts and praise. Ephah, named alongside Midian, was Midian's own son (Genesis 25:4). The next verse adds Kedar and Nebaioth (Isaiah 60:7), sons of Ishmael (Genesis 25:13). A Sheba also appears among Keturah's descendants (Genesis 25:3), though Scripture names more than one Sheba. Taken together, these names suggest that Isaiah sees Abraham's other sons, the ones once sent eastward with gifts and away from Isaac (Genesis 25:6), returning to the city of God bearing gifts of their own and praising His name. This reading rests on the genealogies Isaiah's names recall, and it fits the chapter's vision of nations coming to the LORD's light (Isaiah 60:3).

Many readers have also heard an echo of this verse when wise men from the East bring gold, frankincense, and myrrh to the infant Jesus (Matthew 2:1, 11). Matthew does not cite Isaiah 60, so this should be held as thematic resonance rather than declared fulfillment. Even so, the gospel answers the hope of Isaiah 60. In Christ, the blessing promised to Abraham reaches the nations, including peoples who once stood outside Israel's story (Galatians 3:8, 14; Ephesians 2:12–13).

The LORD who comes from the south. Habakkuk's prayer recalls God marching out from Teman and Mount Paran in splendor (Habakkuk 3:3), the southern wilderness where Israel first met Him at Sinai. "I saw the tents of Cushan in affliction; The curtains of the land of Midian trembled" (3:7). The wilderness that once sheltered Moses and witnessed the burning bush now shakes at the approach of the God who revealed Himself there. Habakkuk draws on that memory to strengthen his own faith that God will again come to save His people (3:13, 18–19).

5. Spirit & Life

God does some of His most important work in places that look like exile. Moses arrived in Midian as a fugitive and sat down by a well (Exodus 2:15). For forty years he did ordinary work in a foreign land, keeping someone else's sheep. Yet the call that reshaped history came to him there, on the back side of the wilderness, from a bush that would not burn up. When life feels like a long detour, Midian is a reminder from the text itself that God had not set Moses aside. He was preparing him to lead.

Jethro shows that God's mercy to His people is meant to be heard and celebrated by others. Jethro did not see the plagues or the sea. He heard the story and rejoiced, blessed the LORD, and worshiped (Exodus 18:9–12). Faithful testimony to what God has done can bring an outsider to confess that the LORD is greater than all gods. The Kenites who descended from Jethro's household kept their place beside Israel for generations (Judges 1:16; 1 Samuel 15:6). In contrast, the Midianite leaders behind the scheme at Peor chose a hostile way (Numbers 25:17–18; 31:16). Shared ancestry did not make the difference; each household's response to the living God did.

Peor teaches that the enemy who cannot curse may try to seduce. Balaam could not speak a single curse God had not spoken (Numbers 23:8), but his counsel led Israel to sin until the plague broke out (Numbers 31:16). God's people are often less endangered by open opposition than by quiet invitations to compromise their loyalty to Him. It is also worth noticing that Moab dominates the story, yet God names Midian as the schemer (Numbers 25:18). Danger does not always come from the obvious front.

Gideon's story speaks to anyone who feels small, afraid, or stripped bare. God met a frightened man hiding grain in a winepress, listened to his honest question, and sent him anyway (Judges 6:11–14). Then He deliberately made Gideon's army too small to win, so that Israel could never say its own hand had saved it (7:2). God's strength is most clearly seen when human strength has nothing left to boast in. Paul learned the same lesson when the Lord told him that His strength is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9).

Victory, however, has its own dangers. Gideon defeated Midian's army and refused the rule Israel offered him (Judges 8:22–23), but Midian's gold became the ephod that snared Israel (Judges 8:27). The plunder of a defeated enemy led to the same spiritual unfaithfulness the enemy had once introduced. After God gives deliverance, the heart still needs guarding.

Finally, the last word on Midian belongs to grace. A people descended from the son sent away, an enemy that once seduced and plundered Israel, appears in Isaiah's vision bringing gold and incense and proclaiming "the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). The God who judged Midian's sin did not close His purposes to Midian's descendants. That is good news for every reader who once stood outside and far off, and whom Christ has brought near.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 25:1–6 — Midian born to Abraham and Keturah; his sons listed; Keturah's sons given gifts and sent eastward away from Isaac.

  • Genesis 36:35; 1 Chronicles 1:46 — An early Edomite king attacks Midian in the field of Moab, showing Midian's early presence in the Transjordan.

  • Exodus 2:15–21; 3:1; 4:19 — Moses' refuge in Midian, his marriage into the priest's household, and the burning bush at Horeb while tending Jethro's flock.

  • Exodus 18:1–27 — Jethro, priest of Midian, rejoices in the LORD's deliverance, worships with Israel's elders, and counsels Moses.

  • Numbers 22:4, 7 — The elders of Midian join Moab in hiring Balaam.

  • Numbers 25:1–18 — The seduction at Peor, Cozbi daughter of a Midianite leader, and the LORD's command to harass Midian for its schemes.

  • Numbers 31:1–16; Joshua 13:21 — The LORD's vengeance on Midian; its five kings and Balaam killed; Balaam's counsel exposed.

  • Judges 6:1–16 — Seven years of locust-like oppression, the prophet's rebuke, and Gideon's call.

  • Judges 7:1–25 — God reduces Gideon's army to three hundred and routs Midian; Oreb and Zeeb are killed.

  • Judges 8:1–28; 9:17 — Zebah and Zalmunna captured, Gideon refuses kingship, the gold ephod becomes a snare, and Midian lifts its head no more.

  • 1 Kings 11:18 — Midian as a route in Hadad the Edomite's flight to Egypt.

  • Psalm 83:9–11 — Midian's defeat becomes a model for Israel's prayers against later enemies.

  • Isaiah 9:1–7 — "As in the day of Midian": the coming Child breaks the oppressor's yoke; compare Matthew 4:12–17.

  • Isaiah 10:26 — The slaughter of Midian at the rock of Oreb as a pattern for God's judgment on Assyria.

  • Isaiah 60:6–7 — The camels of Midian and Ephah bring gold and incense and proclaim the LORD's praises.

  • Habakkuk 3:3, 7 — The land of Midian trembles at the LORD's coming from the southern wilderness.

  • Acts 7:29–30 (related Greek form) — Stephen recalls Moses' forty years as a sojourner in Midian and God's appearance in the wilderness.

Judges 7:13 And when Gideon had come, there was a man telling a dream to his companion. He said, “I have had a dream: To my surprise, a loaf of barley bread tumbled into the camp of Midian; it came to a tent and struck it so that it fell and overturned, and the tent collapsed.” (NKJV)

Judges 7:14 Then his companion answered and said, “This is nothing else but the sword of Gideon the son of Joash, a man of Israel! Into his hand God has delivered Midian and the whole camp.” (NKJV)

Judges 7:15 And so it was, when Gideon heard the telling of the dream and its interpretation, that he worshiped. He returned to the camp of Israel, and said, “Arise, for the LORD has delivered the camp of Midian into your hand.” (NKJV)
