STEEP PLACE, CLIFF - (madrêgâh, Strong's H4095)

Summary

The Hebrew word madrêgâh names a steep place: a sheer cliff, a rocky ledge, or high rough ground that few can reach. It appears only twice in the Old Testament, and the two uses could hardly be more different. In Song of Solomon 2:14 the beloved calls to his bride as a dove hidden "in the secret places of the cliff," safe in rock no predator can climb. In Ezekiel 38:20 God's presence comes in judgment, and "the steep places shall fall" along with mountains and walls.

The same kind of terrain is a shelter in one passage and a ruin in the other. The difference is not the rock. It is who is hiding there and why. A fragile dove tucked into the cliff is answered with tender longing: "Let me see your face, let me hear your voice." The world's strongest heights, the barriers that seem permanent to human eyes, collapse when the Lord arrives. Together the two verses ask a searching question: is my security something I hold against God, or something God has given me in Himself? Scripture's answer points to Christ, the Rock who shelters the weak and before whom every proud height comes down.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Madrêgâh is a Hebrew noun for rugged, steep terrain. It describes the kind of landscape found throughout the hill country and wilderness edges of Israel, where limestone cliffs drop sharply and ledges are reachable only by careful climbing, if at all. The word is often linked to a root that has to do with stepping, which fits the picture of terraced, stair-like rock faces. Even so, the word's meaning in Scripture comes from how it is used, and in both places it simply means a high, hard-to-reach steep place.

The word's two settings are very different. One is love poetry in the Song of Solomon. The other is prophetic vision in Ezekiel. That contrast is what makes such a small word worth studying.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Madrêgâh means a steep place, cliff, or rocky height. What defines it is how high it is and how hard it is to reach. Whether that makes it a place of safety or a place of defiance depends entirely on the context. The NKJV translates it "cliff" in Song of Solomon 2:14 and "steep places" in Ezekiel 38:20. Both translations describe the same physical reality, so the word has a single sense.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Song of Solomon 2:14 the steep place stands parallel to "the clefts of the rock." The poetry repeats the idea in a second line, as Hebrew poetry often does. The emphasis falls on hiddenness: these are "secret places," crevices high in a cliff where a dove can nest out of reach.

In Ezekiel 38:20 the word appears in a list of things that cannot stand before God: mountains, steep places, and walls. The list moves from natural heights to a man-made defense. That progression places the cliffs among the things people rely on for protection. Here the emphasis falls on how solid and immovable these heights seem, which is exactly what makes their fall so startling.

4. Theological Significance

These two verses show the two faces of God's presence. To those who oppose Him, His coming makes all creation "shake at My presence" (Ezekiel 38:20). Fish, birds, beasts, and people tremble, and the ground itself gives way. His holiness meets no barrier He cannot remove. To the one He loves, His presence comes as a call to draw near. The same God who throws down the cliffs speaks tenderly to the dove hidden in one.

Scripture consistently shows that what makes a high place safe is God's provision, not the height itself. David prays, "He shall hide me in His pavilion... He shall set me high upon a rock" (Psalm 27:5). God sets him there; David does not climb there to escape God. Edom, by contrast, lived "in the clefts of the rock" and trusted its height. The Lord's verdict was, "I will bring you down from there" (Jeremiah 49:16; compare Obadiah 1:3). Those passages use different Hebrew words, but they use the same image. A height God provides is a refuge. A height that pride claims becomes a trap.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:14 — the dove in the cliff. The verse follows the beloved's invitation to come away because winter is past and spring has arrived (2:10–13). He then pictures his bride as a dove "in the clefts of the rock, in the secret places of the cliff." Rock doves nest in exactly such crevices, high on sheer faces where predators cannot reach. The image speaks of safety and also of shyness, because the dove is hidden and hard to see.

The beloved does not stop at admiring her safety. He calls out to her: "Let me see your face, let me hear your voice; for your voice is sweet, and your face is lovely." The cliff protects her, but he does not want her to stay hidden from him. Her security becomes the place from which she can answer him. Many Christian readers have rightly heard in this poem a picture of the love between the Lord and His people, though the Song is first of all love poetry between a bride and groom. Read that way, the verse shows a God who shelters His people so that they can come before Him openly, not so that they can stay out of sight.

Exodus 33:22 adds depth to the scene. God tells Moses, "I will put you in the cleft of the rock, and will cover you with My hand while I pass by." Moses survives the nearness of God's glory only because God Himself hides him in the rock. The hiding place in Exodus is where weak human flesh is protected so that God's glory can be revealed. The cleft in the Song is where the beloved's longing can be answered. In both scenes, God provides the hiding place, and He provides it in order to draw near.

Ezekiel 38:20 — the steep places fall. Ezekiel 38 describes Gog's invasion of a restored Israel. God responds with a great shaking of the land (38:19). Verse 20 lists what gives way: "The mountains shall be thrown down, the steep places shall fall, and every wall shall fall to the ground." Cliffs were the ancient world's natural fortresses, barriers no army could cross and heights no attacker could take. Ezekiel places them beside the walls people build and shows both collapsing. The chapter ends with God's stated purpose: He will magnify and sanctify Himself so that the nations will know that He is the LORD (38:23). God brings down the steep places to make His own glory known.

Ezekiel's vision belongs to a larger prophetic pattern. Isaiah sees the day of the LORD coming "upon all the high mountains... upon every high tower, and upon every fortified wall," until "the LORD alone will be exalted in that day" (Isaiah 2:14–17). Micah and Nahum describe mountains melting and rocks thrown down before Him (Micah 1:3–4; Nahum 1:5–6). Isaiah 40:4 adds a further note: "every mountain and hill brought low... and the rough places smooth." The leveling of the heights is more than punishment for pride. It also clears away every barrier that stands between the Lord and the world He is coming to reign over.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Paul writes that Israel in the wilderness "drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them, and that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4). That statement gathers the rock imagery of the Old Testament around Jesus. He is the shelter God provides. Moses was hidden in a cleft so that he could see God's glory and live. In Christ, believers are hidden in the One who reveals the Father's glory.

The leveling of the heights also points to Christ. The Gospels apply Isaiah 40's promise that every mountain will be brought low to John the Baptist preparing the way for Jesus (Luke 3:4–6). Hebrews describes a final shaking of earth and heaven that will leave only a kingdom that cannot be shaken (Hebrews 12:26–28). Ezekiel's falling cliffs belong to that same story. Everything that has resisted God will come down, and what remains is what He has built.

So Christ stands at the dividing line between the two uses of madrêgâh. For those who trust their own heights, He is the coming Lord before whom every stronghold falls. For those who flee to Him, He is the Rock with a sheltering cleft. Christians have long meditated on the "clefts of the rock" as a picture of Christ's wounds. The risen Lord invited Thomas to touch His hands and side (John 20:27). That reading is a devotional reflection rather than the Song's original meaning, but it fits well, because the Rock was struck so that His people could be hidden in Him.

7. Spirit & Life

The dove's safety does not come from her own strength. She is small and easily frightened, and the cliff holds her. Believers who feel fragile are not asked to become cliffs themselves. They are asked to take refuge in the Rock God has provided. The hiding place does not depend on how strong the one who hides there is.

The Song also shows that being hidden in God is not meant to end in hiding from God. Some Christians treat their security in Christ as a place to retreat, safe but silent and keeping their faces turned away. The beloved's words break that silence: "Let me hear your voice; for your voice is sweet." The Lord's people are safe so that they can speak to Him freely, in prayer, confession, and praise. Being secure in Him should give them the boldness to answer Him.

Ezekiel adds a sober warning. Each person has some steep place they lean on: savings, reputation, skill, or a carefully built independence. Those heights feel permanent until God's presence shakes them. Edom thought, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (Obadiah 1:3). The safest place to stand is wherever God has placed you, and that place is in Christ. When everything else falls, the dove in the Rock is still there, and the Lord still calls to her.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Madrêgâh occurs only in Song of Solomon 2:14 and Ezekiel 38:20, and both are developed above. Readers will meet "steep place" in other English verses, such as Micah 1:4. Those verses should not be assumed to use this Hebrew word, though they draw on the same kind of imagery.
