WHAT, WHY, HOW - (mâh, Strong's H4100)

Summary

The Hebrew word mâh is the Old Testament's basic way of asking "what?" In the NKJV it also appears as "why," "how," and "whatever," because this one word can ask about a thing, a reason, or a degree. Often the speaker is not looking for information. When God asks Cain, "What have you done?" He already knows the answer. The question presses Cain to face his deed.

The same word carries many tones. It can be the grief of a lament, the sting of an accusation, an open-handed offer, or a burst of praise. In Malachi it becomes the stubborn pushback of people who could not see their own sin. Reading these passages well means asking two things: what kind of question is this, and who is asking it?

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Mâh opens a question by standing in for the piece that is missing. "What is His name?" leaves a gap where the name should be, and mâh marks that gap. Hebrew often attaches a short prefix to the word, and the prefix changes the question. With a prefix meaning "for," the question becomes "for what?", which English renders "why?" With a prefix meaning "in" or "by," it becomes "in what way?" or "how?"

Mâh can also leave the gap open instead of asking anyone to fill it. Then it means "whatever." And when a speaker is overwhelmed by something, it can turn into an exclamation: "how great!" In every case the surrounding sentence shows which job mâh is doing.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Asking about a thing, a deed, or an identity (NKJV "what")

God confronts Cain with a question: "What have you done? The voice of your brother's blood cries out to Me from the ground" (Genesis 4:10). God is not gathering facts. The question holds Cain accountable for what he did. At the burning bush, Moses imagines Israel asking, "'What is His name?' what shall I say to them?" (Exodus 3:13). In that verse mâh asks for real content: the name of the God who sent him.

Asking for a reason (NKJV "why")

"Why are you angry? And why has your countenance fallen?" (Genesis 4:6). Before Cain's sin, God asks him to examine his own heart. When Elkanah sees Hannah weeping, he asks, "Hannah, why do you weep? Why do you not eat? And why is your heart grieved?" (1 Samuel 1:8). The repeated question shows a husband's concern, though it also shows that he cannot fully understand her sorrow.

Leaving the matter open: "whatever"

Jonathan tells David, "Whatever you yourself desire, I will do it for you" (1 Samuel 20:4). Here mâh asks nothing. Instead it opens a blank space that David may fill however he wishes, which is a clear sign of Jonathan's loyalty. David speaks the same way to the Gibeonites: "Whatever you say, I will do for you" (2 Samuel 21:4).

Exclamations and questions that expect no answer (NKJV "how")

Zechariah looks ahead to the day of the Lord's salvation and exclaims, "For how great is its goodness And how great its beauty!" (Zechariah 9:17). This is praise, not an inquiry. Balaam asks, "How shall I curse whom God has not cursed?" (Numbers 23:8). Balaam is not asking for a method. He is admitting that the thing cannot be done, because no one can reverse God's blessing.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Many of the Bible's most searching moments come as questions that God already knows the answer to. He asks Cain why he is angry and what he has done. Later He asks Elijah, who has fled to a cave on Horeb, what he is doing there (1 Kings 19:9, 13). These questions are not for God's benefit. He asks them so the person will tell the truth about themselves. When God asks "what?", He is offering room to confess.

Malachi turns this pattern around. God declares, "I have loved you," and the people answer, "In what way have You loved us?" (Malachi 1:2). The same kind of reply comes again and again. In effect they ask how they have despised His name (1:6), how they should return (3:7), and how they have robbed Him (3:8). Their "what?" is not humble curiosity. It is self-defense. God answers each challenge with specific evidence. A question can open the heart to God, or it can be used to argue with Him.

4. Reading Guidance

Because English splits mâh into "what," "why," and "how," the English word you see is the translator's judgment about the question's purpose. Do not assume every question asks for information, and do not read an exclamation like Zechariah 9:17 as a real inquiry. Also, not every English "why" comes from mâh. Hebrew has a different word that is also rendered "why."
