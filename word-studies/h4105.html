MEHETABEL - (Mᵉhêyṭabʼêl, Strong's H4105)

Summary

Mehetabel is a Hebrew name meaning "God does good" or "God benefits." Two different people in Scripture bear it, living many centuries apart. The first is a queen of Edom, the wife of King Hadar (spelled Hadad in Chronicles). Genesis 36:39 and 1 Chronicles 1:50 both name her among the early kings of Esau's descendants. She is the only royal wife mentioned in that list, and the record also names two generations of her family: she was "the daughter of Matred, the daughter of Mezahab." The second Mehetabel appears only as an ancestor in Nehemiah 6:10. Shemaiah, the man who tried to frighten Nehemiah into hiding in the temple, is identified as "the son of Delaiah, the son of Mehetabel."

Neither Mehetabel acts in the biblical story, yet both passages are worth our attention. The Edomite queen shows that God's Word took careful notice of a nation outside Israel and recorded the names of its royal household. The later Mehetabel stands in the family line of a man who claimed to speak for God while taking money from Jerusalem's enemies. A good name in the family tree did not make Shemaiah faithful, and Nehemiah had to judge his words by the character of God, not by his pedigree.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H4105 covers two separate individuals. They should not be confused.

The first is Mehetabel, queen of Edom, the wife of Hadar, who reigned from the city of Pau (Genesis 36:39). First Chronicles 1:50 repeats the record with slightly different spellings, Hadad and Pai. In Hebrew script the letters d and r look very much alike, which helps account for Hadar and Hadad. She is called "the daughter of Matred, the daughter of Mezahab." That wording can be read two ways. Matred may be her mother, in which case the second "daughter" refers to Matred as the daughter of Mezahab. Or Matred may be her father, in which case Mehetabel is the granddaughter of Mezahab, since Hebrew often uses "daughter" or "son" for a later descendant. The text does not settle the question.

The second is Mehetabel, a post-exilic ancestor of Shemaiah son of Delaiah, who lived in Jerusalem in Nehemiah's day (Nehemiah 6:10). The verse does not say whether this Mehetabel was a man or a woman. Family lines in Nehemiah usually trace through fathers, which makes a grandfather more likely, but the name was borne by a woman in Genesis, so certainty is not possible.

The name joins the Hebrew verb yṭb, "to be good" or "to do good," with 'el, "God." It means "God does good," "God benefits," or, in a slightly freer sense, "one whom God makes glad." The meaning is well supported. Scripture does not make any wordplay on it.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The queen of Edom. Genesis 36 traces the family of Esau and lists the kings who reigned in Edom before any king reigned over Israel (Genesis 36:31). The list is brief and spare: each king dies, and another reigns in his place. When Baal-Hanan son of Achbor died, Hadar took the throne, and at that point the record slows down. It names his city, then his wife, then her parents' line: "His wife's name was Mehetabel, the daughter of Matred, the daughter of Mezahab" (Genesis 36:39). No other king in the list has his wife named. The Chronicler kept the same detail when he copied the genealogy centuries later (1 Chronicles 1:50). Mezahab appears to mean "waters of gold." Some later Jewish interpreters, including Rashi (Jarchi), Abarbanel, and Saadiah as summarized in McClintock and Strong's Cyclopaedia, suggested that this points to a wealthy family or one connected with gold working, but that is a proposal, not something the text states. What the text does show is that Mehetabel's family was remembered, and it was likely well known to the first readers.

The ancestor in Nehemiah. During the rebuilding of Jerusalem's wall, Nehemiah's enemies tried force, rumor, and intrigue to stop him (Nehemiah 4–6). Nehemiah 6:10 records that he went to the house of "Shemaiah the son of Delaiah, the son of Mehetabel, who was a secret informer." The NKJV's "secret informer" renders a Hebrew word that may also mean "shut in." Shemaiah urged Nehemiah to meet him inside the temple and shut the doors, "for they are coming to kill you." Nehemiah refused to flee into a place where, as a layman, he had no right to go. He then saw that God had not sent Shemaiah at all. Tobiah and Sanballat had hired him to make Nehemiah afraid, so that he would sin and give them grounds to discredit him (Nehemiah 6:11–13). Nehemiah names the prophetess Noadiah and other prophets as part of the same campaign of intimidation (6:14). Mehetabel's name appears only to identify Shemaiah's family.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states that Edom came from Esau and had kings early (Genesis 36:1, 31). The Lord had already told Rebekah that two nations were in her womb (Genesis 25:23). He later gave Mount Seir to Esau as his possession and told Israel not to take it (Deuteronomy 2:4–5). He even commanded, "You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7). Against that background, the careful naming of an Edomite queen fits a God who rules all nations and keeps track of their histories, including peoples outside the chosen line. The promise that kings would come from Abraham (Genesis 17:6) is seen even in Esau's branch of the family.

It is also worth noticing that a name built on 'El appears in Edom's royal house. El is a common Semitic word for God, so the name is consistent with some memory of the God of Abraham and Isaac among Esau's descendants, but it cannot by itself identify the deity meant. The name alone cannot tell us what Mehetabel herself believed, though, and Edom's later history became one of pride and hostility toward Israel that brought God's judgment (Obadiah 1–4; Malachi 1:2–4).

In Nehemiah, the significance lies in Shemaiah's position. The text gives his family line, so he was a known member of the community and not an outsider. One serious danger in chapter 6 came through a man with standing among God's people, using the language of prophecy and the setting of the temple.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament does not mention Mehetabel, and Scripture establishes no direct connection between either person and Christ. Their places in the larger story are limited to the two settings above: Edom's early royal history and the opposition to Jerusalem's restoration.

5. Spirit & Life

The queen's name in Genesis 36 is a small sign that God does not overlook people we might treat as footnotes. He had His servants record the wife of a foreign king, along with her mother's or father's line. His attention to the nations is real, even when His saving purpose runs through Israel.

Nehemiah 6:10 speaks more directly to God's people. Shemaiah came from a family whose names honored God. Mehetabel means "God does good," and the name Shemaiah itself refers to the Lord hearing. Even so, he sold his words to the enemies of God's work. Nehemiah did not test the message by the man's background. He tested it by what God had commanded, and he recognized that God would not send him into sin to save his life. Believers still need that discernment. A message that pushes us toward fear and disobedience has not come from the Lord, however spiritual its source appears.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:39: Names Mehetabel as Hadar's wife and gives her lineage through Matred and Mezahab. She is the only royal wife in Edom's king list.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:50: Repeats the record with the spellings Hadad and Pai, preserving her name in Israel's later history.

  • Genesis 36:31: Sets the list before any king reigned over Israel.

  • Deuteronomy 2:4–5; 23:7: Show God's care for Edom as Israel's brother nation.

  • Nehemiah 6:10–14: Names the second Mehetabel as Shemaiah's ancestor and records the hired false prophecy that Nehemiah saw through.
