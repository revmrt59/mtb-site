MOAB - (Môwʼâb, Strong's H4124)

Summary

Moab is the name of a man, the nation descended from him, and the land that nation occupied east of the Dead Sea. The first Moab was the son of Lot by his elder daughter, born in the cave after the destruction of Sodom (Genesis 19:37). His descendants became a settled kingdom across the Jordan from Israel. Its territory gave its name to the "plains of Moab," where Israel camped before entering Canaan. The name is commonly understood to mean "from father." That meaning is probable rather than certain, and it echoes the troubling account of the nation's birth. Moab appears across Scripture from Genesis to Zephaniah: as a relative God protected, as an enemy who hired Balaam to curse Israel, as the oppressor Eglon, as the homeland of Ruth, as the place where David hid his parents, and as the subject of some of the longest judgment oracles in the prophets.

Moab's story holds two truths together. God guarded Moab's land for Lot's sake and would not let Israel take it (Deuteronomy 2:9). Yet Moab repeatedly set itself against God's people, and its pride became proverbial in Isaiah and Jeremiah. The Law shut Moabites out of the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3), yet a Moabite widow named Ruth took refuge under the wings of Israel's God, became the great-grandmother of David, and is named in Matthew's genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). Even Jeremiah's long sentence of doom ends with God's promise to bring back Moab's captives "in the latter days" (Jeremiah 48:47). Moab shows how firmly God opposes pride, how far His mercy reaches, and how He has always meant His Messiah's kingdom to include people from the nations.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name Môwʼâb (Strong's H4124) names a man, his descendants, and their land. The name occurs about 180 times, all of them in the Old Testament.

  • Moab the ancestor. He was the son of Lot by Lot's firstborn daughter. Scripture says of him: "The firstborn bore a son and called his name Moab; he is the father of the Moabites to this day" (Genesis 19:37). He was therefore a grand-nephew of Abraham, and his half-brother Ben-Ammi became the father of the Ammonites (Genesis 19:38).

  • Moab the land. This was the high plateau east of the Dead Sea. The Arnon gorge often served as its northern border (Numbers 21:13; Judges 11:18), though control of the land north of the Arnon changed hands several times. The "plains of Moab" were the lowlands east of the Jordan opposite Jericho (Numbers 22:1; 33:48–50). Israel camped there in its last days before the conquest.

  • Moab the nation. These were the people descended from Moab, ruled by their own kings, such as Balak, Eglon, and Mesha, and devoted to their god Chemosh (Numbers 21:29; 1 Kings 11:7).

Several closely related words are indexed separately and are not counted among the occurrences of H4124. These include the gentilic Môwʼâbî, "Moabite" or "Moabitess" (H4125), which appears in Deuteronomy 23:3 and in Ruth's title, "Ruth the Moabitess" (Ruth 1:22). They also include Chemosh (H3645) and Balak (H1111). Because Moab's most important theological moments are told with these words, this study discusses them as related terms. The compound family name Pahath-Moab (for example, Nehemiah 7:11) is also a separately indexed related name (H6355). It is a family designation, not a reference to the land itself.

Name meaning. Môwʼâb is widely understood to mean "from father." On this reading, the name joins a Hebrew element meaning "from" with ʼâb, "father." The meaning fits the setting of Genesis 19, where Lot's daughters conceive by their father. It is best treated as a probable or traditional explanation rather than a certain one, because the Hebrew text of Genesis 19:37 records the naming without explaining the name. If the meaning is correct, the name itself recalls the nation's origin. Still, the meaning of the name should not be treated as a summary of Moab's spiritual identity. Scripture tells Moab's story through its history, not through its etymology.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Origins in the shadow of Sodom

Moab begins where Sodom ends. Lot had chosen the well-watered plain and settled near a wicked city. He escaped its fire with his two daughters and ended in a cave, where the daughters made him drunk and conceived by him (Genesis 19:30–38). Genesis records this without comment, and the silence is sobering. The nation that would later pride itself on its strength came from a family that had lost nearly everything. Moab's land is also mentioned early in the list of Edom's kings, where Hadad defeated Midian "in the field of Moab" (Genesis 36:35).

A relative God protected

When Israel came out of Egypt, the Song of the Sea already pictured "the mighty men of Moab" seized with trembling (Exodus 15:15). Yet when Israel actually reached Moab's border, God gave Moses a surprising order. Israel was not to harass Moab or fight with it, because the LORD had given the land of Ar to the descendants of Lot as their possession (Deuteronomy 2:9; see also 2:8, 18). Israel went around Moab (Numbers 21:11–15). Centuries later Jephthah reminded the Ammonites of this, pointing out that Israel never took the land of Moab (Judges 11:15–18, 25).

The land Israel did take north of the Arnon had already been taken from Moab by Sihon king of the Amorites. Numbers preserves a victory song from Heshbon that mourns Moab as the people of Chemosh, who had been devoured and scattered (Numbers 21:26–29). Israel won that land from Sihon, not from Moab.

Balak, Balaam, and Peor

Moab did not return Israel's restraint. Balak king of Moab saw Israel camped on the plains and feared them (Numbers 22:1–4). He sent the elders of Moab and Midian with payment to bring the diviner Balaam to curse Israel (Numbers 22:7). Three times Balaam stood with Balak on the heights, and three times God turned the intended curse into blessing (Numbers 23–24).

Balaam's fourth major oracle (Numbers 24:15–19) looked far ahead. A Star would come out of Jacob and a Scepter would rise out of Israel, and that ruler would crush the brow of Moab (Numbers 24:17). Moab could not curse a people God had blessed, and later Scripture returns to this episode often (Joshua 24:9; Micah 6:5).

What cursing could not do, compromise nearly did. Israel stayed at Shittim and "began to commit harlotry with the women of Moab," who invited them to the sacrifices of their gods. Israel joined itself to Baal of Peor, and a plague followed (Numbers 25:1–9). Midianite women were also involved (Numbers 25:6, 14–18). Later Moses explains that the scheme came through Balaam's counsel (Numbers 31:16), and the risen Christ says the same: Balaam taught Balak to put a stumbling block before Israel through idolatry and sexual immorality (Revelation 2:14).

When the Law later excluded Moabites from the assembly, it gave a specific reason: Moab and Ammon had not met Israel with bread and water on the way, and they had hired Balaam to curse Israel (Deuteronomy 23:3–4). The verse itself reads: "An Ammonite or Moabite shall not enter the assembly of the LORD; even to the tenth generation none of his descendants shall enter the assembly of the LORD forever" (Deuteronomy 23:3).

The plains of Moab: where Israel heard the Word

The ground of Israel's enemy became the place where God prepared His people for their inheritance. On the plains of Moab, Israel took its second census (Numbers 26:3, 63). There God gave laws about inheritance, cities of refuge, and the land (Numbers 35:1; 36:13), and the inheritance east of the Jordan was distributed (Joshua 13:32). Moses explained the Law "in the land of Moab" (Deuteronomy 1:5), and the LORD made a covenant with Israel there in addition to the covenant at Horeb (Deuteronomy 29:1).

From Mount Nebo in Moab, Moses saw the whole land of promise. He then died "in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD," and God buried him in a valley there, in a grave no one knows (Deuteronomy 32:49; 34:1–6). Israel wept for him thirty days on the plains of Moab (Deuteronomy 34:8). The great lawgiver's last sermon, his last view of the land, and his grave all lie in Moab.

Eglon and Ehud

In the period of the judges, Israel did evil, and "the LORD strengthened Eglon king of Moab against Israel" (Judges 3:12). For eighteen years Moab ruled Israel and took tribute. Then God raised up Ehud, a left-handed Benjamite, who killed Eglon and led Israel to strike down about ten thousand Moabites at the fords of the Jordan. The land then had rest for eighty years (Judges 3:12–30).

Moab here is God's rod of discipline. Israel's later sin included serving "the gods of Moab" along with other foreign gods (Judges 10:6). Samuel recalled the Moabite oppression as one of the ways God corrected His people (1 Samuel 12:9).

Ruth: a Moabitess comes home to God

Into this history of hostility comes the book of Ruth. During a famine, Elimelech of Bethlehem took his family to "the country of Moab" (Ruth 1:1–2). His sons married Moabite women, and then all three men died. When Naomi heard that the LORD had visited His people with bread, she turned toward home (Ruth 1:6). Her daughter-in-law Orpah went back "to her people and to her gods" (Ruth 1:15). Ruth refused to leave Naomi and bound herself to Naomi's people and Naomi's God (Ruth 1:16–17).

The narrator does not let us forget where Ruth came from: "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab. Now they came to Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest" (Ruth 1:22). The reapers call her "the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab" (Ruth 2:6). Boaz blesses her as one who has come to take refuge under the wings of the LORD God of Israel (Ruth 2:12). He then redeems Naomi's land and marries Ruth (Ruth 4:3–10). Their son Obed became the father of Jesse, the father of David (Ruth 4:17, 21–22). A woman from the nation barred from the assembly became an ancestor of Israel's king.

David, Saul, and the monarchy

Saul fought against Moab (1 Samuel 14:47). Yet when David was running from Saul, he took his father and mother to the king of Moab and asked him to shelter them until he knew what God would do for him (1 Samuel 22:3–4). David's Moabite ancestry may lie behind this request, though the text does not say so.

Later, as king, David defeated Moab severely. He made the captives lie down, measured them with a line, put two lines of them to death, and made the rest his servants who brought tribute (2 Samuel 8:2; 1 Chronicles 18:2). He dedicated their silver and gold to the LORD (2 Samuel 8:12; 1 Chronicles 18:11). Among David's mighty men, Benaiah killed "two lion-like heroes of Moab" (2 Samuel 23:20; 1 Chronicles 11:22). Balaam's word about a ruler from Jacob crushing Moab's brow found an early fulfillment in David's reign.

Chemosh on a hill east of Jerusalem

Solomon's heart was turned by his foreign wives. "Then Solomon built a high place for Chemosh the abomination of Moab, on the hill that is east of Jerusalem, and for Molech the abomination of the people of Ammon" (1 Kings 11:7). God named this worship of Chemosh among the reasons He would tear the kingdom from Solomon's son (1 Kings 11:33). The shrine stood within sight of the city of God's temple for generations.

It came down only under Josiah: "Then the king defiled the high places that were east of Jerusalem, which were on the south of the Mount of Corruption, which Solomon king of Israel had built for Ashtoreth the abomination of the Sidonians, for Chemosh the abomination of the Moabites, and for Milcom the abomination of the people of Ammon" (2 Kings 23:13). Moab's god Chemosh, distinct from the Baal of Peor to whom Israel had once been joined (Numbers 25:3), was welcomed into Jerusalem by Israel's own king, repeating the same kind of compromise.

Mesha's rebellion and Jehoshaphat's battle

After Ahab died, Moab rebelled (2 Kings 1:1; 3:5). Mesha king of Moab was a sheep-breeder who had paid Israel an enormous tribute of lambs and wool (2 Kings 3:4). Jehoram of Israel, Jehoshaphat of Judah, and the king of Edom marched against him and ran out of water in the wilderness. For Jehoshaphat's sake, Elisha gave the word of the LORD: the valley would fill with water, and God would also deliver Moab into their hands (2 Kings 3:10–18).

In the morning the Moabites saw the sun on the water, thought it was blood, and rushed in to plunder. They were routed instead (2 Kings 3:21–24). Trapped in his city, Mesha offered his eldest son as a burnt offering on the wall. Then "there was great indignation against Israel," and the armies withdrew (2 Kings 3:26–27). The account is grim. It shows how far Chemosh-worship would go, and it leaves the meaning of the "indignation" unexplained.

After Elisha died and was buried, "the raiding bands from Moab invaded the land in the spring of the year" (2 Kings 13:20). Later, bands of Moabites were among those the LORD sent against Judah to destroy it, according to the word of His prophets (2 Kings 24:2).

In Jehoshaphat's time, Moab, Ammon, and others came against Judah (2 Chronicles 20:1). Jehoshaphat's prayer recalled that God had not let Israel invade these nations when it came out of Egypt, and now they were coming to drive Israel out of its inheritance (2 Chronicles 20:10). Judah went out singing praise, and the LORD set ambushes. Moab and Ammon turned on their allies from Mount Seir and then destroyed one another (2 Chronicles 20:22–23). Moab also appears in Chronicles' genealogies, where some of Judah's and Benjamin's families once lived or ruled in Moab (1 Chronicles 4:22; 8:8).

Moab in the Psalms and the prophets

The Psalms place Moab firmly under God's hand. God says, in effect, that Moab is His washpot, the basin for menial use (Psalm 60:8; 108:9). Moab also appears in the coalition of nations that conspired to wipe Israel's name from memory (Psalm 83:6).

The prophets give Moab sustained attention. Isaiah devotes two chapters to it (Isaiah 15–16). He describes weeping on the high places, shaved heads, and fugitives fleeing to Zoar, and his own heart cries out for Moab (Isaiah 15:1–9). Then the LORD speaks a surprising word: "Let My outcasts dwell with you, O Moab; Be a shelter to them from the face of the spoiler. For the extortioner is at an end, Devastation ceases, The oppressors are consumed out of the land" (Isaiah 16:4). Immediately after this, Isaiah announces a throne established in mercy in the tabernacle of David, where One will sit in truth, judging and seeking justice (Isaiah 16:5).

The next verse names what keeps Moab from that refuge: its pride, haughtiness, and wrath (Isaiah 16:6). The prophet mourns again, saying his heart resounds like a harp for Moab (Isaiah 16:11). Moab's worship at its high places would not help it (Isaiah 16:12), and its glory would be brought low within three years (Isaiah 16:13–14). Isaiah also foresees God's people prevailing over Moab (Isaiah 11:14). He sets Moab's trampling right beside the LORD's feast for all peoples on Zion, where death is swallowed up forever (Isaiah 25:6–10).

Jeremiah gives the longest oracle concerning Moab (Jeremiah 48). He pronounces judgment on Moab's cities one after another: Nebo, Kirjathaim, Heshbon, Dibon, Kerioth, and many more. He announces that Moab will be ashamed of Chemosh, as Israel was ashamed of Bethel (Jeremiah 48:13). He identifies the root problem plainly: "We have heard the pride of Moab (He is exceedingly proud), Of his loftiness and arrogance and pride" (Jeremiah 48:29). Moab had magnified itself against the LORD (Jeremiah 48:26, 42).

One image explains how that pride took hold: "Moab has been at ease from his youth; He has settled on his dregs, And has not been emptied from vessel to vessel, Nor has he gone into captivity. Therefore his taste remained in him, And his scent has not changed" (Jeremiah 48:11). Yet God also says He will wail for Moab and that His heart will moan like flutes for its people (Jeremiah 48:31, 36). The oracle ends with an unexpected promise: the LORD will bring back the captives of Moab in the latter days (Jeremiah 48:47).

Jeremiah also lists Moab among the nations that must drink the cup of God's wrath (Jeremiah 25:21). In Jeremiah 9:25–26 Moab is among the nations God will punish; the NKJV says, "all these nations are uncircumcised, and all the house of Israel are uncircumcised in the heart." Many interpreters take this to mean that outward circumcision, practiced by some of these peoples, counts for nothing without a changed heart. Either way, all peoples answer to God. Moab's envoys were present in Jerusalem when Jeremiah wore the yoke of Babylon (Jeremiah 27:3). When Jerusalem fell, some Jews had found shelter in Moab and came home to Gedaliah (Jeremiah 40:11). Moab served as a refuge once again, as it had in the days of Elimelech and of David.

Ezekiel condemns Moab for saying Judah was "like all the nations." In saying this, Moab denied that Judah was distinct as God's people (Ezekiel 25:8–11). Amos condemns Moab for burning the bones of Edom's king to lime (Amos 2:1–2). That crime was committed against another pagan nation, not against Israel, so God holds the nations accountable for cruelty toward anyone. Daniel mentions Moab among those who escape a future king's hand (Daniel 11:41). Micah calls Israel to remember Balak and Balaam so that they may know the righteousness of the LORD (Micah 6:5). Zephaniah hears Moab's taunts against God's people and declares that Moab will become like Sodom (Zephaniah 2:8–9). With those words the story returns to where it began, in the ruins Lot fled.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

God rules the borders of the nations. Scripture states directly that the LORD gave Moab its land for Lot's sake (Deuteronomy 2:9). Israel's God is not a local deity limited to one people. He assigns territory, remembers family ties, and keeps His word even to a nation that would oppose His people. His care for Moab grew out of His relationship with Abraham, whose nephew Lot He had rescued. From this we can reasonably draw an implication: God's faithfulness to Abraham reached outward to Abraham's relatives, even through their failures.

Moab tested whether Israel would remain faithful. Moab's greatest threat was never its army. Balak's attempt to curse Israel failed completely, because God had blessed His people (Numbers 23:8, 20). The damage came at Peor, when Israel joined in Moab's feasts and worship (Numbers 25:1–3). Later Scripture confirms that this was a strategy (Numbers 31:16; Revelation 2:14). Solomon later repeated Israel's failure by honoring Chemosh in Jerusalem. Through Moab, Scripture shows that the people of God are most endangered when they voluntarily compromise their loyalty to Him.

Moab served as an instrument of God's discipline. Scripture says plainly that the LORD strengthened Eglon against a disobedient Israel (Judges 3:12). It also says He sent Moabite raiders against Judah in its final days (2 Kings 24:2). God used Moab to correct Israel without approving Moab's hostility.

God judges pride. The prophets single out Moab's arrogance more insistently than almost any other sin of the nations (Isaiah 16:6; Jeremiah 48:29; Zephaniah 2:8–10). Moab mocked God's people, magnified itself against the LORD, and trusted its strongholds, treasures, and god. The judgment oracles treat this pride as a rebellion against God Himself and not only as a personal flaw.

God's mercy reaches past His judgments. The same prophets who announce Moab's ruin also grieve over it (Isaiah 16:11; Jeremiah 48:36). They call Moab to shelter God's outcasts (Isaiah 16:4), and they promise restoration in the latter days (Jeremiah 48:47). Scripture does not spell out the form of that restoration. It does show that God's final word to Moab is not simply destruction.

Ruth reveals God's welcome to the nations. Deuteronomy 23:3 is a real and solemn exclusion, and the book of Ruth never denies it. Ruth's ancestry is mentioned again and again. Yet Ruth enters Israel's life by taking refuge under the LORD's wings (Ruth 2:12), and God places her in the line of David. Scripture never explicitly reconciles the law of Deuteronomy 23 with Ruth's story. The lesson from the canon as a whole is careful but clear: God receives the foreigner who comes to Him in faith. Isaiah later says the same of foreigners who join themselves to the LORD (Isaiah 56:3–7).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Explicit New Testament witness: Ruth in the genealogy of Jesus. Matthew writes that Boaz begot Obed by Ruth (Matthew 1:5). Ancient genealogies usually listed only fathers, and Matthew did not have to include her. He deliberately names a Moabitess, a woman from a people shut out of the assembly, in the opening lines of the story of God's Son. Through Ruth, Moab became part of the family line of the Messiah. The gospel of Jesus Christ is good news for nations once kept far off.

Strongly supported canonical development: the Star and Scepter. Balaam's prophecy of a Star from Jacob and a Scepter from Israel who would crush Moab (Numbers 24:17) was partly fulfilled when David subdued Moab (2 Samuel 8:2). Scripture's royal promises consistently look beyond David to a greater Son of David. Jesus calls Himself "the Bright and Morning Star" (Revelation 22:16), though that verse does not cite Numbers 24 directly. It fits the canon to see Christ as the final King who overcomes every enemy of God's people. That conclusion rests on the overall pattern of Scripture rather than on a single explicit New Testament quotation.

Isaiah's throne of mercy. Isaiah 16:4–5 sets two things side by side: God's call for Moab to shelter His outcasts, and the promise of a throne established in mercy in the tabernacle of David. On that throne One sits in truth, judging and seeking justice. The connection is striking. Moab is invited to show mercy, and the true refuge, the merciful King from David's house, is announced in the very next verse. The prophecy points to the Messiah's reign. Jesus, the Son of David, is that King, in whom justice and mercy meet.

Thematic resonance: the proud brought low and the humble received. Isaiah 25 places the LORD's feast for all peoples, where death is swallowed up, alongside Moab trodden down (Isaiah 25:6–10). Jeremiah ends a chapter of judgment with Moab's restoration (Jeremiah 48:47). Ruth, who came humbly to glean, is gathered into the people of God. These passages together reflect a pattern that Mary later celebrates: God scatters the proud and lifts up the lowly (Luke 1:51–52). Moab as a proud nation stands under judgment, while a Moabite who seeks refuge finds a place at the table. This is a thematic pattern, not a formal type, but it runs deep through the gospel.

Balaam's counsel in the church. The risen Christ uses the Moab episode directly. He warns the church at Pergamos about those who hold the teaching of Balaam, who taught Balak to put a stumbling block before Israel (Revelation 2:14). Peor was not only an episode from Israel's past. Jesus treats it as an ongoing danger for His people.

5. Spirit & Life

Moab shows us a God who keeps His word even when people do not deserve it. He protected Moab's land for Lot's sake while Moab was hiring a prophet to curse Israel. His faithfulness does not depend on whether others return it. The same faithfulness is the believer's security now. Balak could pay Balaam, climb three mountains, and build seven altars at each place, but he could not curse a people God had blessed (Numbers 23:8). The enemy's real hope was to draw Israel away from God, because he could not overcome God's blessing directly. That is why Christ's warning to Pergamos still matters. For most believers, the greater danger is not open attack but quiet compromise with what God has called idolatry.

Jeremiah's picture of wine settled on its dregs reaches deep into the heart. Moab had never been poured from vessel to vessel and had never gone into captivity, so its "taste" and "scent" never changed (Jeremiah 48:11). Long ease produced not maturity but an unchanged, self-satisfied pride. The verse is about Moab, but it helps us understand how God treats His own people. Sometimes He unsettles us, pouring us from one vessel to another through loss, change, or hardship, so that what is stale and proud in us can be drawn off. Disruption in a believer's life is not always a sign of God's absence. It may be His refusal to leave us as we are.

Moab also warns against the pride the prophets named so plainly. Moab trusted its works, treasures, strongholds, and god (Jeremiah 48:7, 13), and it mocked God's people (Zephaniah 2:8). The LORD took this personally, because Moab had magnified itself against Him (Jeremiah 48:42). Pride is not a minor weakness. It sets a person against God.

Yet God does not delight in the ruin of the proud. Isaiah's heart cries for Moab, and God says His own heart moans like flutes for Moab's people (Isaiah 15:5; 16:11; Jeremiah 48:36). His judgments are real, but they come from a God who grieves even over His enemies. That should influence how His people speak about those who oppose them.

Above all, Moab gives us Ruth. She came from a people with a shameful beginning, a hostile history, and a law that barred them from the assembly. She had nothing to offer but a widow's loyalty and a willingness to glean. She came to take refuge under the wings of the God of Israel, and He gave her a redeemer, a home, and a place in the line of the Messiah. No one's ancestry or past is beyond God's power to redeem when that person comes to Him for refuge. In Ruth's story, the gospel reaches into the very nation the Law had kept at a distance.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 19:30–38 — Moab's birth to Lot's elder daughter and the origin of the Moabites; the background for the name's probable meaning.

  • Deuteronomy 2:8–9, 18 — God gives Moab its land for Lot's sake and forbids Israel to attack it.

  • Numbers 22–24 — Balak hires Balaam; God turns the curse into blessing; the Star and Scepter prophecy (24:17).

  • Numbers 25:1–3; 31:16 — Israel's idolatry with the women of Moab at Peor, carried out on Balaam's counsel.

  • Deuteronomy 23:3–4 — Moabites excluded from the assembly, with the reasons given.

  • Deuteronomy 29:1; 34:1–8 — The covenant made in the land of Moab, and Moses' death and burial there.

  • Judges 3:12–30 — Eglon's oppression and Ehud's deliverance.

  • Ruth 1:1–22; 2:12; 4:13–22 — Ruth the Moabitess takes refuge in the LORD and becomes an ancestor of David.

  • 1 Samuel 22:3–4; 2 Samuel 8:2 — David shelters his parents in Moab and later subdues it.

  • 1 Kings 11:7; 2 Kings 23:13 — Solomon's high place for Chemosh and Josiah's destruction of it.

  • 2 Kings 3:4–27 — Mesha's rebellion, Elisha's word, and the sacrifice on the wall.

  • 2 Chronicles 20:1–23 — Moab's invasion turned back as Judah praises the LORD.

  • Isaiah 15–16; 25:6–10 — Lament over Moab, the call to shelter God's outcasts, the throne of mercy, and the judgment of pride.

  • Jeremiah 48:11, 29, 36, 47 — Moab settled on its dregs, its pride, God's grief over it, and its latter-day restoration.

  • Amos 2:1–2; Zephaniah 2:8–10 — Judgment for cruelty and for mocking God's people; Moab to become like Sodom.

  • Matthew 1:5; Revelation 2:14 — Ruth in the genealogy of Jesus; Christ's warning about Balaam's teaching.
