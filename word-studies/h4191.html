DIE, KILL, PUT TO DEATH - (mûwth, Strong's H4191)

Summary

Mûwth is the ordinary Hebrew verb for dying. It describes a person or animal coming to the end of life, and in other forms it means to kill, execute, or put to death. Scripture uses it in genealogies, battle reports, courtroom laws, priestly warnings, and the laments of Job and Ecclesiastes. Wherever life ends in the Old Testament, this word is usually present.

The Bible never treats death as simply the way things are. The word first appears in God's warning in Eden: "in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die" (Genesis 2:17). From then on, death is the consequence of turning from the God who gives life. The same God who pronounces that sentence also says He kills and makes alive. He declares that He takes no pleasure in anyone's death, and He promises that His dead will rise. That tension between a just sentence and a merciful heart runs through the Old Testament and is resolved in Jesus Christ, who died and rose so that those in Him "shall be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mûwth is a common Hebrew verb, used more than eight hundred times from Genesis to Zechariah. It appears in almost every kind of Old Testament writing. It ends the life stories of the patriarchs, records the deaths of kings, sets penalties in the law of Moses, and gives the prophets and wisdom writers their vocabulary for mortality.

Its close relative is the noun māweṯ (H4194), "death." Several of the best-known Old Testament verses about death use that noun instead of this verb, such as Isaiah's promise that God "will swallow up death forever" (Isaiah 25:8). The "shadow of death" in Psalm 23:4 is a related but separately indexed compound word (tsalmaveth, H6757). This study concentrates on the verb, which describes the event of dying or the act of bringing death about.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, mûwth means to cease living. Context and verb form determine whether someone dies, causes death, or is described as dead.

1. To die. This is the simple event of life ending, whether through age, illness, battle, or judgment. It is used of people, of animals such as the fish of the Nile in Exodus 7:21, and even of a tree stump in the ground in Job 14:8. Occasionally it is used figuratively, as when Nabal's heart "died within him" before he actually died ten days later (1 Samuel 25:37–38).

2. To kill or put to death. In its causative forms the verb means to bring about someone's death. That can be murder, as in Saul's attempts to kill David (1 Samuel 19), killing in battle, judicial execution, or God's own act of judgment. NKJV translates these uses with "kill," "slay," and "execute."

3. Dead, the dead one. As a participle it describes a dead person or "the dead," as when Abraham asks the sons of Heth for a place to bury his dead (Genesis 23:4).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In narrative the verb is usually plain and factual. It reports that someone died, often followed by burial and succession. In the law it becomes a sentence, and the recurring formula "shall surely be put to death" marks offenses that strike at human life or at God's holiness, such as murder (Exodus 21:12), cursing parents, or blasphemy (Leviticus 24:16). In the priestly instructions it works as a guardrail. Priests are to wash, dress, and approach as God directs "lest they die" (Exodus 28:35; 30:20–21), because coming carelessly near the Holy One is lethal. In the prophets, especially Jeremiah and Ezekiel, dying and living become the two roads set before the nation.

One grammatical pattern matters. Hebrew can intensify a verb by repeating it, roughly "dying you shall die." NKJV usually renders this "surely die" or "surely be put to death." The doubling gives the warning or sentence emphatic force. The form by itself does not make every warning irreversible; Ezekiel 33:14–15 pairs "You shall surely die" with the promise that the one who turns "shall surely live." It appears in God's word to Adam, in the capital laws, and in prophetic verdicts such as Elijah's message to King Ahaziah (2 Kings 1:4).

4. Theological Significance

Because the verb first appears in a divine warning, Scripture presents death as a verdict that entered God's good creation. It is never portrayed as a neutral part of nature. Paul states the theology directly: "through one man sin entered the world, and death through sin" (Romans 5:12). This is why the law treats death so seriously, and why the sanctuary warnings keep repeating that sinful people cannot approach a holy God on their own terms and live.

Death is also never independent of God. In the Song of Moses, God claims death as His own act: He kills and makes alive, wounds and heals, and no one can deliver from His hand (Deuteronomy 32:39). Hannah repeats the confession almost word for word in her prayer. The LORD kills and makes alive, brings down to the grave and brings up (1 Samuel 2:6). Israel's neighbors gave death its own gods. Israel was taught that death answers to the LORD. That truth is sobering, because there is no hiding from Him. It is also hopeful, because only the One who rules death can undo it. Hannah's song, sung over a child given to a barren woman, already joins "brings down" to "brings up."

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 2:17; 3:3–4; 5:5–31 — The warning, the denial, and the drumbeat. God's first use of the word is the emphatic double form: "you shall surely die" (Genesis 2:17). When the serpent speaks to Eve, he repeats God's exact construction and adds a single negative, telling her she will not surely die (Genesis 3:4). The first lie in Scripture is a direct contradiction of God's word about death. The event in Eden then needs Genesis 5 to complete it. Generation after generation, the genealogy closes with the same Hebrew refrain, "and he died." Adam lived 930 years, "and he died" (5:5). Methuselah lived 969 years, "and he died" (5:27). Long life did not cancel the sentence. Only Enoch breaks the pattern, because God took him (5:24), and that single exception shows that God alone can interrupt the rhythm. Genesis 5 proves the serpent wrong one name at a time.

Numbers 16:41–49 — Standing between the dead and the living. After Korah's rebellion, the whole congregation accused Moses and Aaron of killing the LORD's people (16:41, using this verb). God's judgment broke out as a plague, and people began to die. At Moses' word, Aaron took a censer with fire from the altar and incense, ran into the middle of the assembly, and made atonement. Numbers records that he stood between the dead and the living, and the plague stopped (16:48). The man accused of killing the people ran into the path of death to save them. Death was spreading through the camp as a just penalty, and it stopped at the place where God's appointed high priest stood with atonement in his hands. Fourteen thousand seven hundred died (16:49). The rest lived because a priest put himself in the gap.

Ezekiel 18 and 33 — "Why should you die?" The exiles complained that they were suffering for their fathers' sins. God answered with a principle of personal accountability: the soul who sins is the one who will die (Ezekiel 18:4, 20). The chapter does not end with a verdict, though. It ends with a plea. God asks Israel why they should die, says He has no pleasure in the death of one who dies, and calls them to turn and live (18:31–32). Ezekiel 33 sharpens the plea with an oath: "'As I live,' says the Lord GOD, 'I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live. Turn, turn from your evil ways! For why should you die, O house of Israel?'" (33:11). The living God swears by His own life that He does not want His people dead. He pronounces the sentence, and then He argues with sinners to escape it.

Isaiah 26:14, 19 — Your dead shall live. Isaiah sets two groups of the dead side by side. The foreign lords who ruled Israel are dead and will not live again (26:14); in context this declares their final defeat and that their rule will never be restored. It does not deny the later teaching that the unjust also rise to judgment (John 5:28–29; Acts 24:15). God's own dead are different. Isaiah says they will live, rise with his own dead body, and be called to awake and sing, they who dwell in the dust (26:19). The same verb names both groups. What separates them is whom they belong to. Here the Old Testament speaks plainly of bodily resurrection for God's people.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament leaves a question open. God decreed that sin brings death, and He does not lie, as the serpent did. Yet God says He takes no pleasure in that death and promises that His dead will live. How can both be true?

The New Testament answers in Jesus. "The wages of sin is death," Paul writes, "but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 6:23). Christ did not cancel the sentence of Genesis 2:17. He bore it. Aaron's run into the plague is a shadow of this. Aaron stood at the edge of death with incense. Jesus went all the way into death itself and came out the other side. Hebrews describes Him as the priest who "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). Aaron's intercession stopped one plague on one day. Christ's intercession continues without end.

His resurrection is also the firstfruits of Isaiah's hope. "Christ is risen from the dead, and has become the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. For since by man came death, by Man also came the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ all shall be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:20–22). The refrain of Genesis 5, "and he died," is answered by a new one: He is risen.

7. Spirit & Life

The serpent's lie is still told in many versions: sin is not that serious, death is only natural, God does not mean what He says. Genesis 5 is God's patient reply. Every grave proves His word was true. The believer can face death honestly, without pretending it is a friend, because Scripture calls it what it is.

Ezekiel 33:11 also lets us hear God's voice where many people expect to hear only anger. The same God who says "you shall surely die" asks, "why should you die?" His warnings are themselves acts of mercy, spoken so that people will turn. Anyone who thinks God is eager to destroy has not listened to Him swear by His own life that He is not.

Numbers 16 shows what that mercy costs and how it works. Rebellious people were saved by the very priest they had slandered, and they were saved where he stood. Believers today stand on the living side of that line because Jesus stands in the gap. That gives Christians a pattern for their own lives as well. Moses and Aaron answered accusation with intercession, falling on their faces before the LORD (16:45), and at Moses' word Aaron ran toward the dying instead of away from them. Aaron's action is a canonical analogy for Christ's priesthood, not an event Hebrews 7 names. Those who belong to Christ carry the same message into a world that is still dying: there is a Priest, and there is still time to turn and live.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 23:4 — Abraham asks to buy a burial place for his dead. His first recorded land purchase in Canaan is a grave. Genesis does not comment on his motive, but read with Hebrews 11 it can be seen as a quiet statement of faith that God would keep His promise beyond death.

  • Ruth 1:17 — Ruth vows that where Naomi dies, she will die and be buried. A Moabite widow binds her death, and so her whole life, to Naomi's people and Naomi's God.

  • 2 Samuel 12:23 — After his child dies, David says he will go to the child, but the child will not return to him. Grief and hope sit side by side.

  • 1 Kings 17:20 — Elijah cries out, asking whether the LORD has brought tragedy on the widow by killing her son. God answers by restoring the boy to life, a small picture of Hannah's confession.

  • 2 Kings 20:1 — Isaiah tells Hezekiah to set his house in order because he will die. Hezekiah prays, and God adds fifteen years to his life.

  • Psalm 118:17 — The psalmist declares that he will not die but live, and declare the works of the LORD. Rescue from death becomes a reason to testify.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H4194 (māweṯ), "death." It is the noun from the same root and is often used for death as a condition or a power, as in Isaiah 25:8. Psalm 23:4's "shadow of death" uses the separately indexed compound tsalmaveth (H6757).
