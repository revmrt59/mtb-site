ADVANTAGE, PROFIT, PLENTY - (môwthâr, Strong's H4195)

Summary

Môwthâr is a Hebrew word for what is left over: the surplus, gain, or advantage that remains when the account is settled. It appears only three times in the Old Testament, all in the wisdom books. In Proverbs it names the real "profit" and "plenty" that come from honest labor and careful planning, set against the "poverty" that follows empty talk and hasty shortcuts. In Ecclesiastes the same word takes on a heavier question. Measured against death, "man has no advantage over animals" (Ecclesiastes 3:19).

Taken together, these three uses tell the truth about human gain. God made a world where diligence bears fruit, and that fruit is a genuine gift. The grave, however, cancels every surplus a person can store up. The tension points beyond itself to the resurrection of Jesus Christ. Because He is risen, Paul can tell believers that "your labor is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58). Only a risen Lord turns our labor into a gain that outlasts the grave.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Môwthâr is a noun formed from the verb yathar (H3498), which means to remain over or be left. The prefix gives the noun the sense of the thing that remains: the surplus or margin. The word belongs entirely to Israel's wisdom literature. It appears twice in Proverbs and once in Ecclesiastes and nowhere else in the Old Testament. Both books ask practical questions about how life works and what it is worth, so a word for "what you have left at the end" fits naturally there.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, môwthâr means gain beyond what was spent: profit, abundance, or an edge over something else. Context brings out two related senses:

1. Practical surplus, the profit or plenty that results from wise action. "In all labor there is profit" (Proverbs 14:23).

2. Comparative advantage, the margin by which one thing exceeds another. "Man has no advantage over animals" (Ecclesiastes 3:19).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Proverbs the word always stands on the positive side of a contrast, and in both verses its opposite is machsor (H4270), "poverty" or want. Proverbs 14:23 sets labor against "idle chatter." Proverbs 21:5 sets "the plans of the diligent" against haste. The pairing teaches that surplus and lack are opposite outcomes of opposite ways of living.

Ecclesiastes uses the word as a measure. The Preacher asks what margin humanity holds over the animals when both face death, and he answers that there is none. The word itself has not changed. The scale of the question has.

4. Theological Significance

These three verses show that God made the world orderly. Work produces fruit, careful planning produces plenty, and those outcomes are not random. Proverbs presents this as the way God's world normally runs, and that gives ordinary work real dignity. A carpenter's steady hours and a farmer's careful planning take part in the order God built into creation.

The same Scriptures also refuse to let that order become a god. Ecclesiastes brings the word môwthâr to the grave and finds that it buys nothing there. This is mercy as well as warning. The plenty God gives through diligence sustains life, allows generosity, and reflects His wisdom. Because it cannot rescue anyone from death, it cannot become a savior either. Earthly gain is a tool for stewardship, and God makes sure it can never pass for salvation.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 14:23 and 21:5: the fruit of real work. "In all labor there is profit, But idle chatter leads only to poverty." The Hebrew behind "idle chatter" points to mere words of the lips: talk about work that never becomes work. Plans, boasts, and good intentions spoken aloud produce no surplus. Effort does. Proverbs 21:5 adds a second contrast: "The plans of the diligent lead surely to plenty, But those of everyone who is hasty, surely to poverty." The danger here is the opposite of laziness. The hasty person is busy but impatient, grabbing at shortcuts and skipping the slow work of thinking ahead. Proverbs places môwthâr between those two failures. Talking without doing produces nothing, and doing without thought ends in loss. Plenty comes to the person who both plans and labors.

Ecclesiastes 3:19: the advantage that disappears. "For what happens to the sons of men also happens to animals; one thing befalls them: as one dies, so dies the other. Surely, they all have one breath; man has no advantage over animals, for all is vanity." The surrounding verses explain the point. God tests people so they may see that, left to themselves, they are like beasts (3:18), and all go to one place, all from the dust and all returning to dust (3:20). The word that measured a farmer's harvest in Proverbs now measures the human race against the cattle, and the margin comes out to zero. Ecclesiastes is not denying that people bear God's image or that the spirit returns to God (12:7). The Preacher is looking at life "under the sun," at what can be observed from this side of the grave. From there, death levels everything. The wise and the diligent die like the fool and the ox. No accumulated profit can keep anyone from the dust.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Read side by side, these three verses leave an unresolved question. If God designed labor to yield a surplus, and death cancels every surplus, what becomes of a life's work? Looking under the sun, Ecclesiastes finds that no earthly gain can keep a person from the grave, even as the Preacher affirms that God will judge every work (3:17; 12:14) and that the spirit returns to God (12:7). The Old Testament wisdom books do not fully close that gap.

Paul answers the question directly in 1 Corinthians 15. Arguing for the resurrection, he asks, "If, in the manner of men, I have fought with beasts at Ephesus, what advantage is it to me? If the dead do not rise, 'Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die!'" (15:32). Paul writes in Greek, so this is not môwthâr, and his question is not identical to the Preacher's. Ecclesiastes weighs the mortality people share with animals. Paul asks what his apostolic labor and danger are worth if the dead do not rise, and the beasts he mentions belong to his own hardship at Ephesus rather than to Ecclesiastes. Both passages, though, ask about advantage in the face of death, and Paul grants that without resurrection his labor gains nothing.

Christ has been raised, and death has been swallowed up in victory (15:54–57). Paul's conclusion therefore answers the question Ecclesiastes leaves open: "Therefore, my beloved brethren, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that your labor is not in vain in the Lord" (15:58). The labor that Proverbs commends and Ecclesiastes sees cut off by death is gathered up into a life that does not end. Romans 8 widens the picture. Creation itself "was subjected to futility" in hope and "will be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God" (Romans 8:20–21). The "vanity" that cancels human advantage is not permanent. In the risen Christ, God secures the surplus that death could not touch.

7. Spirit & Life

Proverbs 14:23 cuts close for believers. It is easy to talk about prayer, discipleship, service, and Scripture reading, and to take the talking for the thing itself. Proverbs answers plainly that idle words of the lips lead to want. Fruitfulness in Christ comes through the quiet, often unnoticed obedience of ordinary days: actually praying, actually serving, actually doing what the Word says. Proverbs 21:5 guards the other side. Spiritual haste, which wants maturity without patience and results without faithfulness, ends in poverty too.

Ecclesiastes 3:19 is a mercy to a proud heart. Every person is tempted to think that enough diligence, wisdom, or achievement will secure his standing. The Preacher walks us to the graveside and shows that none of it holds. Being stripped of that illusion is painful, yet it is what makes room for the gospel.

Paul then hands the believer a different way to think about work. Because Christ is risen, faithful labor "in the Lord" is never wasted, even when it seems unseen, small, or unfinished. A mother's daily care, a teacher's patient lessons, and a believer's hidden obedience are not cancelled at the grave. In Christ the account does not come out to zero.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Môwthâr occurs only three times: Proverbs 14:23 ("profit"), Proverbs 21:5 ("plenty"), and Ecclesiastes 3:19 ("advantage"). All three are explained above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3498 (yathar), lexical relative: the verb meaning to remain over or be left, from which môwthâr is formed.

  • H4270 (machsor), canonical pairing: "poverty" or want, set directly against môwthâr in both Proverbs 14:23 and 21:5.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Proverbs states how God's world ordinarily works. It does not promise that every hard worker will prosper. Ecclesiastes itself notes that things do not always turn out as wisdom predicts. Proverbs 14:23 and 21:5 should therefore be read as faithful descriptions of God's order, not as guarantees of wealth. The deeper guarantee about the worth of our labor belongs to 1 Corinthians 15:58, and it rests on the resurrection.
