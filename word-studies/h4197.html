BLENDED BEVERAGE, MIXTURE - (mezeg, Strong's H4197)

Summary

The Hebrew word mezeg means "mixture" and refers to wine that has been blended or prepared, rendered by the NKJV as "blended beverage." It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 7:2, where the bride is praised in the language of a royal banquet: "Your navel is a rounded goblet; It lacks no blended beverage." Prepared wine of this kind was a drink for feasts and weddings, so the image speaks of delight, celebration, and rich satisfaction.

Within the Song, the word teaches that the love of husband and wife is meant to be a feast. It is a gift of God to be enjoyed with gladness, and the poet never treats it as a grudging duty. The phrase "lacks no" adds the picture of a cup that is never empty. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, this single image joins a larger pattern: Wisdom mixing her wine, the LORD's feast on His mountain, Jesus supplying wine at a wedding, and the overflowing cup of those who live in God's presence. God is not stingy with joy. He prepares it, and He keeps the cup full.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mezeg is a Hebrew noun found only in Song of Solomon 7:2. Because it occurs once, Scripture gives us no other passages that would show how the word was used by different writers. Its meaning comes from the plain sense "mixture" and from the setting of the verse, which pictures a goblet at a banquet.

In the ancient world, wine was "mixed" in more than one way. Sometimes it was blended with spices or honey to make it richer and sweeter. In other settings it was tempered with water. The Song itself speaks elsewhere of "spiced wine" made from the juice of pomegranates (Song 8:2), which suggests a prepared, festive drink. Either way, mixed wine took preparation. It belonged to the table of celebration more than to the ordinary meal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mezeg means a mixture, specifically mixed or blended wine. It names a prepared drink, one that someone has deliberately combined or seasoned. In its single use, the word fills a goblet in a love poem, and the NKJV conveys this with "blended beverage." One definition is enough for this word.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The only occurrence comes in the middle of a descriptive love song (Song 7:1–5). The bride is praised from her feet upward, and each part of her is compared to something precious, fruitful, or royal. Most readers hear the bridegroom's voice here. The imagery is frank but discreet, as is typical of the Song, and it honors the body rather than cheapening it.

Two pictures sit side by side in verse 2. First comes the goblet that "lacks no blended beverage," then the waist that is "a heap of wheat Set about with lilies." Grain and wine together were Scripture's familiar signs of a blessed harvest and a full house. The poet gathers the best of the table and the best of the field to say that his bride is abundance itself to him.

The phrase "lacks no" carries the weight of the line. This goblet is not simply filled once. The poet pictures it as never lacking.

4. Theological Significance

The Song of Solomon stands in Scripture as God's own affirmation that married love is good. By describing the bride with the language of festive wine, the poem places physical and emotional intimacy within the celebration God intends. The same spirit appears in the opening line of the Song, "For your love is better than wine" (Song 1:2), and in Proverbs, where a husband is urged to "rejoice with the wife of your youth" and to "always be enraptured with her love" (Proverbs 5:18–19). God, who made man and woman, is the author of their delight in one another. He does not merely tolerate it.

This also says something about God Himself. A God who fills His creation with feast-wine and harvest-wheat is generous by nature. Scripture's habit of describing His goodness through tables, cups, and banquets is consistent with His character as the Giver who delights to give.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 7:2 — the goblet that lacks nothing. The verse sets the bride before us as a "rounded goblet," a vessel shaped for the banquet table. Its contents are mezeg, wine that has been carefully prepared. The word itself implies craftsmanship: someone has measured, blended, and seasoned the drink. The love being celebrated has been cared for, and it is rich rather than plain. The claim that the goblet "lacks no blended beverage" moves the picture from a single glad moment to unfailing supply. In a poem about husband and wife, this means the love God gives in marriage is meant to keep satisfying. It is a fountain to be "blessed" (Proverbs 5:18), not a pleasure that fades after the wedding day.

Proverbs 9:2 — Wisdom's mixed wine. In Proverbs 9, Wisdom builds her house and prepares a feast: "She has slaughtered her meat, She has mixed her wine, She has also furnished her table." She then calls the simple to come, eat, and live. The same idea of mixed wine that delights the bridegroom in the Song is here offered by Wisdom as an invitation to life. Mixed wine is the sign of a host who has made ready and wants guests to come. The banquet image moves from the intimacy of marriage to God's welcome of anyone willing to turn from folly.

Isaiah 25:6 — the LORD's feast for all people. Isaiah looks forward to a day when "The LORD of hosts will make for all people A feast of choice pieces, A feast of wines on the lees... Of well-refined wines on the lees." These are wines aged and clarified, the finest of the cellar. The next verses speak of God destroying the veil over the nations and swallowing up death forever (Isaiah 25:7–8). The best wine is served at the table where death is defeated. What the Song pictures in one marriage, Isaiah announces for the redeemed of the whole earth.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Paul quotes the first marriage, "the two shall become one flesh," and then adds, "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32). Christians have long read the Song in that light. The New Testament never names Song 7:2 as a prophecy, but Paul's words give firm ground for seeing human marriage, and the Song's celebration of it, as a picture of Christ's love for His people.

That storyline runs through wine. At Cana, a wedding feast reached the point every human celebration reaches: "They have no wine" (John 2:3). Jesus had the servants fill six stone waterpots "up to the brim," and the master of the feast told the bridegroom, "You have kept the good wine until now!" (John 2:7, 10). The first sign of Jesus' ministry turned a failing feast into an abundant one, and the best came last.

At His final supper, Jesus gave His disciples the cup of the new covenant and said He would drink it new with them in His Father's kingdom (Matthew 26:27–29). Scripture's story ends at "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9), where Isaiah's feast is finally spread.

Scripture also knows another mixed cup. "In the hand of the LORD there is a cup, And the wine is red; It is fully mixed, and He pours it out" for the wicked to drain to the dregs (Psalm 75:8). In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed that "this cup" might pass from Him, yet He drank it (Matthew 26:39). The Bridegroom took the cup of judgment so that His bride could receive the cup that lacks nothing.

7. Spirit & Life

Earthly joys run out. Cana shows it, and every believer knows it from experience: the feast ends, strength fades, and even good gifts lose their sweetness. Song 7:2 pictures something else, a goblet that "lacks no blended beverage." David knew where that kind of supply is found: "In Your presence is fullness of joy; At Your right hand are pleasures forevermore" (Psalm 16:11). He also knew that it holds even in hard places: "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies... My cup runs over" (Psalm 23:5). The cup does not wait for trouble to leave before it overflows.

Mixed wine is also made on purpose. Nobody finds spiced wine in a stream. It is prepared by someone who cares about the guest. The joy God gives His people is like that. Wisdom "mixed her wine" before she ever sent out her invitation, and the LORD Himself makes Isaiah's feast. Believers do not have to manufacture their own gladness in God. They are invited to come to a table already furnished.

For husbands and wives, the verse is a gentle summons to treasure each other. The Song never sees marital love as a fading thrill. It sees a cup worth keeping full, through attention, honor, and delight that continue long after the wedding. When a marriage does that, it becomes a small, living picture of the Bridegroom who kept the good wine until last.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Mezeg occurs only in Song of Solomon 7:2, which is discussed above. The other verses cited in this study, such as Song 1:2, Proverbs 9:2, Isaiah 25:6, and John 2:1–10, use different words. They support the study as related images of wine and feasting. They are not further occurrences of mezeg.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H4538 (mesek), "mixture," mixed wine. This word is close to mezeg in meaning, and the two may be related forms of the same idea. Mesek appears in Psalm 75:8 for the "fully mixed" cup of God's judgment. Placed beside Song 7:2, it gives Scripture's two mixed cups: the cup of delight and the cup of wrath.
