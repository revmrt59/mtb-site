PSALM, MELODY - (mizmôwr, Strong's H4210)

Summary

The Hebrew word mizmôwr means a psalm, a song set to music. The NKJV translates it "Psalm," and every one of its occurrences is in the Book of Psalms, always in the heading above a psalm, never inside the poem itself. When a heading reads "A Psalm of David," this is the word at work. It tells the reader what kind of text follows: a composition meant to be sung, most likely with instruments.

That small label carries a great deal. It sits over David's flight from Absalom and over Israel's shouts of victory. It sits over David's confession after Bathsheba and over Psalm 88, a prayer that ends in darkness. It also sits over Psalms 22, 40, and 110, the songs the New Testament reads as speaking of Christ. Israel sang its joy, its grief, its repentance, and its hope of the coming King, and God gave all of it a place in His worship.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mizmôwr belongs to the family of the Hebrew verb zamar, "to sing praise" or "make music." That verb appears in Psalm 33:2: "Praise the LORD with the harp; Make melody to Him with an instrument of ten strings." Some scholars connect the root with plucking strings. That link is debated, but in actual use the word family plainly belongs to music offered to God, often with instruments.

The setting is Israel's worship. First Chronicles describes David appointing Levites "to be the singers accompanied by instruments of music, stringed instruments, harps, and cymbals" (1 Chronicles 15:16). The psalm headings come from that world of organized temple song, where singers, leaders, and instruments served together.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mizmôwr has one sense: a psalm, meaning a song composed for musical performance in worship. It names the kind of composition rather than describing an action or idea within a sentence. The word does not tell us whether the song is happy or sad. That comes from the psalm itself. What the label says is that this prayer was written to be sung.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Because the word appears only in headings, its usage depends on the company it keeps. It is often joined to a name: David most often, but also Asaph and the sons of Korah. It frequently appears with "To the Chief Musician," a note handing the psalm to the leader of temple music (Psalms 4, 51, 109). Sometimes it stands beside the ordinary word for "song." Psalm 30 is "A Psalm. A Song" for the dedication of David's house. Psalm 92 carries both labels as a song for the Sabbath. Some headings add the occasion, as Psalm 3 does in recalling David's flight from Absalom. Psalm 98 bears no author's name and is simply called "A Psalm."

4. Theological Significance

The word shows that God wants to be worshiped with music as well as with words. The pattern runs from David's organized Levitical singers (1 Chronicles 15:16) to the command of Psalm 33:2 to praise with harp and strings. Melody, skill, wood, strings, and breath can all be turned toward the Creator.

The headings also show that Israel's worship was ordered. A psalm given "To the Chief Musician" was not left as a private journal entry. One man's prayer became something a whole congregation could learn and sing for generations. God gathered individual experience into the common worship of His people, and the people learned to pray by singing what others had prayed before them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 98 and Psalm 88: the same label over joy and darkness. Psalm 98 is called simply "A Psalm." It calls for a new song because the LORD has done marvelous things and revealed His salvation before the nations. It summons harp, trumpets, the sea, the rivers, and the hills to rejoice before the coming Judge. Turn ten psalms back, and Psalm 88 carries the same word in its heading. Heman the Ezrahite cries day and night, feels counted among those going down to the pit, and says God has put loved ones far from him. Unlike most laments, Psalm 88 never turns to praise. It ends in darkness.

Both prayers are labeled mizmôwr. Both were handed to musicians, and both were meant to be sung in the sanctuary. The God who received the shout of Psalm 98 also received the unanswered ache of Psalm 88. Lament was not something the worshiper finished before entering God's presence. It was itself a form of worship, set to music and given to the congregation. Psalm 38 belongs in the same company. It is David's psalm "to bring to remembrance," written under the weight of his sin and God's discipline. Psalm 22 opens with the cry of abandonment before it moves to praise in the great assembly. The melody was wide enough for every condition of the human heart.

Psalm 51: repentance handed to the choir leader. The heading of Psalm 51 ties it to Nathan's visit after David's sin with Bathsheba, and it is addressed "To the Chief Musician." The king's most humiliating moment and his plea for a clean heart were turned into a song for public worship. David's confession did not stay hidden. It became a way for all of Israel to learn how a sinner returns to God. Psalm 109, David's cry against those who repaid his love with hatred, was likewise handed to the Chief Musician. God's people were given words for betrayal as well as for guilt.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

On the evening of His resurrection, Jesus told His disciples that "all things must be fulfilled which were written in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms concerning Me" (Luke 24:44). Several of the songs labeled mizmôwr sit at the center of that claim.

Psalm 22 begins with the cry Jesus spoke from the cross and moves through mockery, pierced hands and feet, and divided garments to praise among God's people. Psalm 40 supplies the words Hebrews places in the mouth of Christ coming into the world: "Sacrifice and offering You did not desire, But a body You have prepared for Me" and "Behold, I have come... To do Your will, O God" (Hebrews 10:5–7). Psalm 110 gives the words Peter preached at Pentecost: "The LORD said to my Lord, 'Sit at My right hand, Till I make Your enemies Your footstool'" (Acts 2:34–35).

Israel sang these songs for centuries before Jesus was born, and so the Messiah's suffering, His willing obedience, and His enthronement were lodged in the people's musical memory. When the apostles preached Christ, they were explaining songs the people already knew. The Greek Old Testament rendered mizmôwr as psalmos, the source of our word "psalm." So when Paul tells the church to speak "to one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:19), the church carries Israel's songbook forward. Now it sings these psalms knowing whom they were about.

7. Spirit & Life

The headings of the Psalms answer a question many believers carry quietly: is there anything I cannot bring to God in worship? The same word stands over Psalm 98's rejoicing and Psalm 88's darkness, over Psalm 51's confession and Psalm 109's wounds. God did not give Israel a hymnal of cheerful songs only. He gave words for the night when the light has not yet come, and He called those words worship.

This means a believer need not pretend in God's presence. Grief sung honestly before Him is faith, because it is still addressed to Him. Heman kept praying to the God he could not feel. David brought his sin into the open where God's people could learn from it. And because these prayers were given to the Chief Musician, they remind us that no one prays alone. The church still sings the struggles of believers who went before, and in time someone will sing ours.

These songs also teach us to look for Christ. The Son of God made Israel's psalms His own, from the cry of Psalm 22 to the royal promise of Psalm 110. Whoever sings these psalms walks a road He has already walked, and He meets His people there.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 3 (heading): The word labels David's prayer while fleeing from his son Absalom. Even in exile and family betrayal, the king wrote a song.

  • Psalm 19 (heading): A psalm that hears God's glory in the heavens and His perfection in His law, sung as one composition.

  • Psalm 23 (heading): The best-known shepherd psalm is a mizmôwr, a song for the congregation as well as for private comfort.

  • Psalm 100 (heading): A psalm of thanksgiving calling all the earth to come before the LORD with joy.

  • Psalm 139 (heading): David's meditation on God's knowledge of him, given to the Chief Musician for shared worship.
