ELAH - (ʼÊlâh, Strong's H0425)

Summary

Elah is a Hebrew name meaning "terebinth," a large, long-lived tree common in the land of Israel and sometimes rendered "oak" in English Bibles. The name belongs to one famous place and several different people, and keeping them separate is essential. The best known is the Valley of Elah, a broad valley in the foothills of Judah where Saul's army faced the Philistines and young David killed Goliath (1 Samuel 17:2, 19). The name also belongs to Elah son of Baasha, a king of northern Israel who reigned two years before his own officer murdered him while he was drunk (1 Kings 16:8–10). Another Elah is remembered only as the father of Hoshea, the last king of the northern kingdom (2 Kings 17:1). Three more appear in genealogies: an Edomite chief descended from Esau, a son of Caleb in the tribe of Judah, and a Benjamite whose family lived in Jerusalem (Genesis 36:41; 1 Chronicles 4:15; 9:8).

Read together, these entries set two kinds of kingship side by side. In the Valley of Elah, God's newly anointed shepherd goes out to face the enemy alone, trusting the LORD, and an entire army shares his victory, a pattern that the rest of Scripture carries forward to the Son of David. In Tirzah, King Elah stays home from the war, drinks himself senseless, and dies at the hands of his own servant, fulfilling God's word of judgment on his father's house. Later, when David is running for his life, the priest hands him the sword of Goliath, "whom you killed in the Valley of Elah" (1 Samuel 21:9). That sword had been kept behind the ephod, in the place of worship, as a lasting witness to what God had done.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name ʼÊlâh (אֵלָה) is the same word used in ordinary speech for the terebinth tree, catalogued separately as the common noun H0424. The terebinth is a sturdy, deeply rooted tree that can live for centuries, and English translations sometimes call it an "oak." The meaning "terebinth" is well established. Some connect the word to Hebrew roots associated with strength, but that derivation is a proposal rather than a settled fact. Scripture never comments on what the name means for any person who bears it, so any irony drawn from the meaning is the reader's observation rather than the biblical author's stated point.

This Strong's entry covers six referents:

  • The Valley of Elah, a valley in the Shephelah, the low hill country between the Philistine coastal plain and the Judean highlands (1 Samuel 17:2, 19; 21:9).

  • Elah son of Baasha, the fourth king of the northern kingdom of Israel, who reigned from Tirzah (1 Kings 16:6, 8, 13, 14).

  • Elah, father of Hoshea, who is named only as the father of Israel's last king (2 Kings 15:30; 17:1; 18:1, 9). His son Hoshea reigned well over a century after King Elah. Nothing suggests that this Elah was a king or related to Baasha's family.

  • Elah the Edomite, one of the chiefs of Esau listed "according to their families and their places" (Genesis 36:41; 1 Chronicles 1:52). The name may stand for a clan or district as well as for an individual.

  • Elah son of Caleb, the son of Caleb son of Jephunneh in the tribe of Judah (1 Chronicles 4:15).

  • Elah son of Uzzi, a Benjamite listed among the families living in Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 9:8).

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Valley of Elah

The Philistines gathered at Sochoh in Judah and camped at Ephes Dammim, while Saul and the men of Israel "encamped in the Valley of Elah" (1 Samuel 17:1–2, paraphrased). The two armies stood on opposite hills with the valley between them. The location mattered. Valleys like this one were natural roads from the coast into the highlands, so whoever held them guarded the way into Judah's heartland.

For forty days, Goliath of Gath came out morning and evening to challenge Israel, and Saul's army was "dismayed and greatly afraid" (17:11, 16, paraphrased). Saul was the tallest man in Israel and the one people expected to fight their battles (9:2; 8:20), yet he did nothing. David arrived on an errand from his father (17:17–19), heard the giant's taunts, and saw them as an insult to the living God (17:26). His brother Eliab dismissed him, Saul doubted him, and Goliath scorned him, but David went down into the valley with his sling and five stones. He told Goliath that the whole earth would know there is a God in Israel and that the battle belongs to the LORD (17:45–47). The stone struck, the giant fell, and the army that had stood frozen for forty days rose up with a shout and pursued the Philistines (17:52).

The valley appears once more. Fleeing from Saul, hungry and unarmed, David came to Ahimelech the priest at Nob. He deceived Ahimelech about his mission (21:2) and asked for a weapon. The priest answered, "The sword of Goliath the Philistine, whom you killed in the Valley of Elah, there it is, wrapped in a cloth behind the ephod. If you will take that, take it. For there is no other except that one here." And David said, "There is none like it; give it to me" (21:9). Scripture does not explain how the sword came to Nob, only that it was kept there. The episode ends darkly. David carried Goliath's sword straight to Gath, Goliath's own city, where he had to feign madness to escape (21:10–15). Doeg the Edomite then reported Ahimelech's help to Saul, and Saul had the priests of Nob slaughtered (22:9–19). David later admitted that he had caused it (22:22).

Elah son of Baasha, King of Israel

Baasha had seized the throne by killing Nadab son of Jeroboam while Israel was besieging the Philistine town of Gibbethon (1 Kings 15:27). God sent the prophet Jehu son of Hanani to announce that Baasha's house would be swept away like Jeroboam's because Baasha walked in Jeroboam's sins (16:1–4, 7). When Baasha died, "Elah his son reigned in his place" (16:6, paraphrased). The text dates his reign precisely: "In the twenty-sixth year of Asa king of Judah, Elah the son of Baasha became king over Israel, and reigned two years in Tirzah" (16:8).

The only scene from his reign is his death. "Now his servant Zimri, commander of half his chariots, conspired against him as he was in Tirzah drinking himself drunk in the house of Arza, steward of his house in Tirzah. And Zimri went in and struck him and killed him in the twenty-seventh year of Asa king of Judah, and reigned in his place" (16:9–10). Meanwhile the army was once again camped against Gibbethon (16:15). Gibbethon had also been the scene of Baasha's murder of Nadab (15:27), though Baasha's twenty-four-year reign (15:33) and Elah's two years lie between the two campaigns there. Israel's army was besieging the same town when each of these dynasties fell. Zimri wiped out Baasha's whole family and friends, "according to the word of the LORD" spoken through Jehu (16:11–12, paraphrased). The narrator gives the reason: the sins of Baasha and of Elah his son, by which they made Israel sin and provoked the LORD God of Israel to anger with their idols (16:13). Zimri lasted seven days before the army made Omri king and Zimri died in the burning palace (16:15–18). The usual closing formula (16:14) is all that remains of Elah's two years.

Elah, Father of Hoshea

The name returns at the very end of the northern kingdom. "Hoshea the son of Elah led a conspiracy against Pekah," killed him, and took the throne (2 Kings 15:30, paraphrased). "In the twelfth year of Ahaz king of Judah, Hoshea the son of Elah became king of Israel in Samaria, and he reigned nine years" (17:1). Hoshea did evil, though not to the degree of the kings before him (17:2). He became a vassal of Assyria, secretly turned to Egypt for help, and was imprisoned. Samaria endured a three-year siege and fell, and Israel was carried away into exile (17:3–6; 18:9–11). The writer explains that this happened because Israel had sinned against the LORD who brought them out of Egypt, worshiped other gods, and refused the prophets' warnings (17:7–23). Elah himself does nothing in the story. His name survives only as the patronymic of the king who saw the end.

Three Genealogical Figures

Elah the Edomite is listed among the chiefs of Esau (Genesis 36:41; 1 Chronicles 1:52), part of Scripture's careful record of the nation descended from Jacob's brother. Elah son of Caleb belongs to the household of Caleb son of Jephunneh (1 Chronicles 4:15), the spy who trusted God when the rest of Israel feared the giants of Canaan (Numbers 13–14) and who later drove the Anakim out of Hebron (Joshua 15:13–14). Elah son of Uzzi, the son of Michri (1 Chronicles 9:8) appears among the Benjamite families living in Jerusalem in a list that follows the record of Judah's exile to Babylon (9:1–3). His name testifies that God preserved a people in the city after judgment.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The Valley of Elah marks the moment God publicly revealed the king He had chosen. Scripture states that the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from the day Samuel anointed him (1 Samuel 16:13) and that the LORD had rejected Saul (16:1). The valley is where that private anointing became visible. Saul's fear and David's faith show what each king relied on, and David's victory begins the path toward the throne and the promise of an everlasting house (2 Samuel 7).

King Elah's story shows what happens when a king forgets whose people he rules. Scripture states that his death fulfilled God's word through Jehu and that it came because of idolatry (1 Kings 16:7, 12–13). The contrast with the valley is an observation drawn from the texts, but it is a fair one. In one place, God's anointed goes out to the battle and saves the people. In the other, the king sits drinking in his steward's house while his army is in the field, and his own officer kills him.

The two Elahs of the northern kingdom also mark stages in a long decline. Under King Elah, the throne passes by murder for the second time in a generation. Under Hoshea son of Elah, the whole nation is uprooted. 2 Kings 17 explicitly states that the exile was God's judgment for persistent unfaithfulness, the outcome Moses had warned of (Deuteronomy 28). The genealogies show God's other purposes. He keeps record of Esau's descendants, Caleb's household, and returning Benjamites, because His dealings with every line, both outside Israel and within the remnant, are part of one history.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never directly cites the Goliath story as a picture of Christ. Even so, the connection rests on strong canonical development rather than mere resonance. David is the anointed king whose line God promised to establish forever, and the New Testament presents Jesus as the Son of David who fulfills that promise (2 Samuel 7:12–16; Luke 1:32–33). In the valley, one anointed representative fights the enemy that has held God's people in fear, and the whole army shares in the victory he wins alone. The New Testament describes Christ's work in similar terms. Through death He destroyed the one who had the power of death and released those held in bondage by fear (Hebrews 2:14–15), and He disarmed the powers and triumphed over them (Colossians 2:15). Like David, He was dismissed by those closest to Him and scorned by the enemy before the victory came.

A quieter canonical thread runs through Caleb's family. The Anakim were not wiped out everywhere. Joshua 11:22 notes that some remained in Gath, Goliath's city. Caleb, whose son was named Elah, trusted God against giants that terrified Israel, and David did the same in the valley. Scripture does not link the son's name with the valley's name. The connection is thematic, and it lies in the shared faith that the size of the enemy does not limit the LORD.

The meaning of the name invites one more connection, though it remains a reader's observation. Northern kings who bore or descended from the name "Terebinth" were cut down and uprooted. Isaiah, who prophesied in the same era, warned the "drunkards of Ephraim, Whose glorious beauty is a fading flower" (Isaiah 28:1). Against that background, Isaiah's promise stands out: God would make His people "trees of righteousness, The planting of the LORD, that He may be glorified" (Isaiah 61:3). What lasts is what God plants.

5. Spirit & Life

The forty days in the valley show that fear spreads. Saul was afraid, and so was the army he led (17:11). David looked at the same giant and saw something else, a man defying the living God. The difference was not size or training but what each man believed about the LORD. Faith did not make David careless. It made him willing to go where everyone else refused to go, because he trusted that the outcome belonged to God.

The sword behind the ephod speaks to anyone in a dark stretch. The weapon from David's greatest victory had been kept in the place of worship, and in his hunger and danger it was handed back to him. "There is none like it; give it to me." What God has done before is not a mere memory. It is testimony kept in His presence, and it can strengthen us again. Scripture is honest that David was not at his best that day. He lied to the priest, he ran to Gath, and innocent people died. Psalms 34 and 56 are linked by their titles to this season, and they show David learning to cry out to God and trust Him in fear. God's past faithfulness did not excuse David's failures, but it did not fail him either.

King Elah's death offers a sober warning. He did not fall in battle. He fell while comfortable, careless, and drunk, surrounded by servants he assumed were loyal. David himself would later stay home while his army fought and fall into his gravest sin (2 Samuel 11:1). The safety of leaders, and of God's people generally, does not lie in position or comfort but in staying awake to God. The genealogical Elahs add a final note of grace. Kings rose and fell, but God kept writing names into His record, including a family in Jerusalem after the exile.

6. Key Scriptures

  • 1 Samuel 17:1–11, 19 — The Valley of Elah as the setting of Israel's forty-day standoff with Goliath and Saul's paralysis.

  • 1 Samuel 17:45–52 — David's confession of the LORD and the victory that the whole army shared.

  • 1 Samuel 21:9; 22:9–22 — Goliath's sword kept behind the ephod at Nob, given to David, and the costly aftermath.

  • 1 Kings 16:1–14 — Jehu's prophecy against Baasha, King Elah's two-year reign, his murder while drunk, and the reason God gives for the judgment.

  • 1 Kings 15:27; 16:15 — Two sieges at Gibbethon that frame both Nadab's death and Elah's.

  • 2 Kings 15:30; 17:1–23; 18:9–11 — Hoshea son of Elah and the fall of Samaria, with God's explanation of the exile.

  • Genesis 36:41; 1 Chronicles 1:52 — Elah among the chiefs of Edom.

  • 1 Chronicles 4:15; 9:8 — Elah of Caleb's household and Elah the Benjamite in Jerusalem.

  • Hebrews 2:14–15; Colossians 2:15 — Christ's victory over the enemy on behalf of His people, developing the pattern of the anointed champion.

  • Isaiah 28:1; 61:3 — The fading pride of Ephraim's drunkards set against the lasting "planting of the LORD."
