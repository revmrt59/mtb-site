DESIRE, PRECIOUS THING, LOVELY - (machmâd, Strong's H4261)

Summary

The Hebrew word machmâd names something that draws the heart and the eye: a prized possession, a cherished person, or anything that is a delight to look at. The NKJV translates it as "pleasant things," "valuables," "precious possessions," "darlings," "the desire of your eyes," and, in one memorable line, "altogether lovely." The word describes the thing loved rather than the act of loving it. In itself the word is positive. Whether that love is healthy or ruinous depends on what the treasure is and what place it holds in a person's heart.

Most of the time Scripture uses the word in scenes of loss. Enemies loot treasured things, starving people trade them for bread, temple treasures burn, and children are taken. The clearest example is Ezekiel 24. God takes the prophet's wife, "the desire of your eyes," and then tells Israel He will profane His own sanctuary, which had become their "desire" and their "arrogant boast." Two passages stand apart from this pattern. In Joel, God calls the temple treasures "My prized possessions" and defends them. In the Song of Solomon, the bride says of her beloved that "he is altogether lovely." Read together, these passages ask what our hearts treasure most, and they point toward the one Beloved whose loveliness no enemy can carry away.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Machmâd belongs to the family of the Hebrew verb châmad, "to desire" or "to delight in." The same verb appears in the commandment "You shall not covet" (Exodus 20:17). The verb describes the wanting. Machmâd names what is wanted, the object that holds a person's gaze.

Most uses of the word come from the prophets and from records of Jerusalem's fall to Babylon. Only the Song of Solomon uses it in a setting of joy. Because of this distribution, readers usually meet the word just as something precious is about to be lost.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Machmâd means "a desirable or precious thing," something valued because it delights the one who looks at it. Context shows what kind of treasure is meant:

1. Valued possessions or sacred objects, such as household goods, silver, or the furnishings of the temple (1 Kings 20:6; Lamentations 1:10; Joel 3:5).

2. Cherished people, such as a wife, young people, or children (Ezekiel 24:16; Lamentations 2:4; Hosea 9:16).

3. Delightfulness or loveliness as a quality of a person (Song of Solomon 5:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word is often tied to the eyes. Ben-Hadad threatens to take "whatever is pleasant in your eyes" (1 Kings 20:6). Lamentations speaks of those "pleasing to His eye" (2:4), and Ezekiel three times uses the phrase "the desire of your eyes" (24:16, 21, 25). The pairing fits the word's meaning. A machmâd is something a person keeps looking at because it gives joy.

The owner changes the tone of the word. When it belongs to Jerusalem, Samaria, or a besieged king, it is usually lost. When God calls the treasures "My prized possessions" (Joel 3:5), the same word becomes the basis for His defense of what belongs to Him.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God takes seriously what His people treasure. He does not leave their prized things untouched when those things have replaced Him. Silver, homes, and even the temple He gave are presented as things He is willing to let go to fire and plunder so that His people will stop trusting them. The losses are not random. In Lamentations 2:4 it is the Lord who "has bent His bow."

The word's most painful uses involve people. Lamentations 2:4 says God "has slain all who were pleasing to His eye." Hosea 9:16 warns rebellious Ephraim, "I would kill the darlings of their womb." These verses show that a nation's long turning from God does not cost only money and buildings. It reaches into families and touches the next generation. They do not teach that every family grief is a punishment. They are prophetic warnings about Israel's persistent rebellion, and they show that sin is never a private matter.

God also has His own machmâd. He is not only the one who removes treasures. He values things, guards them, and treats their misuse as an offense against Himself.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Ezekiel 24:15–27 — The wife and the sanctuary. God tells Ezekiel, "I take away from you the desire of your eyes with one stroke; yet you shall neither mourn nor weep" (24:16). His wife dies that evening, and the next morning he does as he was commanded (24:18). When the exiles ask why he is not mourning, God explains the sign: "I will profane My sanctuary, your arrogant boast, the desire of your eyes, the delight of your soul" (24:21). The exiles in Babylon were holding on to the temple as their guarantee that Jerusalem could not fall. God uses the same word for the temple that He used for Ezekiel's wife. Their attachment to the building was as deep as a husband's love for his wife, and their loss would be just as sudden. Their grief would be too stunned for ordinary mourning rites, so they would "pine away in your iniquities" instead (24:23).

Ezekiel's wife is not presented as sinful. She was a real treasure, and that is the point of the sign. The temple was also God's own gift and a good thing. It became deadly when the people treated it as their "arrogant boast" and their "stronghold" (24:25) while ignoring the God who lived there. When a sacred thing takes God's place, God is willing to remove even the sacred thing.

Lamentations 1:10–11 and Isaiah 64:11 — Treasures lost and treasures traded. Lamentations 1:10 grieves that "the adversary has spread his hand over all her pleasant things," as foreign nations walk into the sanctuary they were forbidden to enter. One verse later the word appears again in a darker scene: "They have given their valuables for food to restore life." The things people once displayed with pride are now traded for a loaf of bread. Isaiah 64:11 laments the same loss, "all our pleasant things are laid waste," and then turns the grief into prayer by asking whether the Lord will hold back (64:12). These passages model the right response to stripped-away treasures. The people bring their loss to God and do not try to rebuild it on their own.

Joel 3:5 — God's prized possessions. God charges Tyre, Sidon, and Philistia: "you have taken My silver and My gold, and have carried into your temples My prized possessions." These nations not only robbed Jerusalem. They also put God's holy things to work in the worship of idols, and Joel 3:6 adds that they sold the people of Judah as slaves. God treats this as a personal wrong and promises to repay it. The God who allowed His temple to be plundered as discipline still calls its treasures His own and will judge those who abuse them.

Song of Solomon 5:16 — Altogether lovely. After describing her beloved feature by feature, the bride sums him up: "His mouth is most sweet, yes, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, and this is my friend." In the prophets this word names what burns, what is looted, and what dies. Here it describes a person who fully deserves the heart's desire. Nothing in the passage suggests that his loveliness is under threat.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word's story moves from treasures that are lost toward a Treasure that cannot be lost. Ezekiel's sanctuary fell, and Jesus later spoke of His own body as a temple that would be destroyed and raised up in three days (John 2:19–21). The house that Israel prized as "the desire of your eyes" gave way to the Son in whom God truly lives with His people. Christ is a temple that no Babylonian army can profane.

Christians have long read the Song of Solomon as an image of Christ and His bride. This is a fruitful reading, although the New Testament does not quote the Song directly. Read this way, "altogether lovely" describes the Bridegroom whom believers love, and He answers the losses in Lamentations and Ezekiel. Haggai 2:7 uses a different word from the same Hebrew family to promise that the nations will come to "the Desire of All Nations" when God fills His temple with glory. The connection is a resonance of related words, not the same word, but it points the same way. The desire that idols and temples could not satisfy finds its true object in the Lord who comes to His house.

Joel 3:5 shows that God has His own treasures. In Christ, that treasure includes a people redeemed at great cost.

7. Spirit & Life

Every human heart has a machmâd, something it looks at with longing. The question is not whether we desire but what we desire most. Jesus asked the same question: "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). The prophets show what happens when the heart settles on something that "thieves break in and steal" (6:19). That can happen through siege, through famine, or through one sudden stroke.

Ezekiel 24 warns religious people especially. The exiles did not treasure a pagan idol. They treasured God's own house, the very place where His name lived. It is possible to love the house of God more than the God of the house: our church, our ministry, or the forms of our worship. Good gifts become dangerous when we lean on them in place of Him. God's willingness to remove even the sanctuary is severe, but it is also mercy. He would rather take a treasure from us than lose our hearts to it.

When the heart's desire rests on Christ, the one who is "altogether lovely," it rests on something that cannot be plundered. Family, possessions, and the places where we worship are still loved, often more freely, because they no longer carry a weight they were never meant to bear.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Kings 20:6 — Ben-Hadad raises his demand to a search of every house to seize "whatever is pleasant in your eyes." The word marks the line between what a person might give up under pressure and what they hold dear.

  • 2 Chronicles 36:19 — Babylon burns the house of God and "destroyed all its precious possessions." This is the historical record of the loss that Lamentations mourns.

  • Hosea 9:6 — "Nettles shall possess their valuables of silver." Weeds grow over the wealth of a scattered people, an image of how worthless such treasure becomes once the people are gone.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H2530 (châmad), "to desire, covet, delight in," the verb behind machmâd (Exodus 20:17).

  • Canonical pairing: In Ezekiel 24:21, "the desire of your eyes" stands beside "the delight of your soul." Together the two phrases describe an attachment that holds both the eyes and the inner life.
