MATRED - (Maṭrêd, Strong's H4308)

Summary

Matred was a member of an Edomite family remembered in Scripture only through a royal marriage. When Hadar (spelled Hadad in Chronicles) became king of Edom, the record names his wife Mehetabel as "the daughter of Matred, the daughter of Mezahab" (Genesis 36:39; compare 1 Chronicles 1:50). Matred is most often understood to be Mehetabel's mother, though the wording has been read in more than one way. The name is usually traced to a Hebrew root meaning "to pursue" or "to drive," so it may mean something like "pursuing" or "driven." That etymology is a reasonable explanation rather than a certainty, and nothing in the text connects the name's meaning to Matred's life.

Matred's importance comes from where Scripture places her. Genesis lists the kings "who reigned in the land of Edom before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). Edom had thrones, royal cities, and prominent families long before Israel had a king. Including a queen's mother and grandparent in that record shows that God's Word keeps careful account of the nations around His people, not only of Israel itself.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Matred is a single Edomite individual, attested only in Genesis 36:39 and its parallel in 1 Chronicles 1:50. Matred is the parent of Mehetabel, wife of King Hadar/Hadad, and the child of Mezahab. Mezahab is a separately indexed name (H4314) that is commonly explained as "waters of gold"; H4325, "water," is only one component of that name. The gloss does not by itself prove the family's wealth.

The phrase "daughter of Matred, daughter of Mezahab" is most naturally read as a chain of three generations. In that reading, Matred is female, the mother of Mehetabel. Some interpreters have instead taken Matred as Mehetabel's father, with the second "daughter of" referring back to Mehetabel. The first view is the common reading, but the text does not settle the question beyond dispute.

The name Maṭrêd is generally connected with the root ṭārad, which means "to pursue" or "to drive," and also "to be continuous," as in the continual dripping of Proverbs 19:13. On that basis the name is often rendered "pursuing," "propelling," or "driven." This derivation is probable rather than proven, and Scripture makes no wordplay on it.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Matred appears in no event of her own. Genesis 36:31–39 traces the succession of Edom's kings: Bela, Jobab, Husham, Hadad son of Bedad, Samlah, Saul of Rehoboth-by-the-River, and Baal-Hanan. When Baal-Hanan died, Hadar reigned from his city Pau. At that point the list does something it has not done for any earlier king. It names the king's wife and traces her family back two generations (Genesis 36:39). First Chronicles 1:50 repeats the record with the spellings Hadad and Pai.

Edom lay in the region of Mount Seir, south and southeast of the Dead Sea, and its people descended from Esau. The family of Matred clearly carried some standing in Edom, since its lineage was worth recording beside a king's name. Whether that standing came from wealth, influence, or something else is an inference the text does not explain.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Edom had kings before Israel did (Genesis 36:31). Numbers 20:14 also shows that Edom had a king during Israel's wilderness years, when Moses sent messengers to "the king of Edom," though it does not date Matred's family, and God told Israel He had given Mount Seir to Esau as a possession (Deuteronomy 2:5). Matred belongs to this picture of an established nation, which is a partial outworking of Isaac's words over Esau about his earthly lot (Genesis 27:39–40).

Readers can also draw a careful implication from the record. Israel had received promises that kings would come from Abraham and Jacob (Genesis 17:6; 35:11), yet it was Esau's line that gained royal houses first. Genesis sets these two families side by side and lets the reader feel how long Israel had to wait. God's timetable for His promises is not measured by who becomes established first.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No later Scripture mentions Matred, and no Christ-centered connection rests on her directly. She belongs to the wider story of Jacob and Esau, which the New Testament does take up (Romans 9:10–13; Hebrews 12:16–17). Romans 9:10–13 stresses God's choice of Jacob before either brother had done good or evil, and Hebrews 12:16–17 warns about Esau's own choices. Neither passage says anything about Matred personally. She should not be treated as a type.

5. Spirit & Life

The detail is small but telling. A list of foreign kings stops to name a queen's mother and grandparent. God's knowledge of human history is not limited to His own people. He gave Esau his land, He knew Edom's kings, and Scripture preserves even the families who married into its royal house.

For Israel, this chapter also brought a quiet test of faith. Genesis lists Esau's descendants with crowns before Israel had a king, while the promise of kings from Jacob still waited. Those who belong to God are still called to trust His promises when others seem to prosper sooner.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:31 — Identifies the list as Edom's kings before Israel had a king.

  • Genesis 36:39 — The primary attestation of Matred, giving Mehetabel's lineage through Matred to Mezahab.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:50 — A parallel record, with the spellings Hadad and Pai.

  • Genesis 27:39–40; Deuteronomy 2:5 — God's dealings with Esau and his land.

  • Genesis 35:11 — The promise of kings from Jacob, which places Edom's earlier kings in context.
