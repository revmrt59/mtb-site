WATER, WATERS - (mayim, Strong's H4325)

Summary

Mayim is the ordinary Hebrew word for water. It names the water a traveler drinks, the water a priest washes with, the rain that falls on the fields, the river Israel crossed, and the deep that covered the earth before God spoke light into being. The word itself means nothing more than "water." Scripture, however, returns to it so often and in so many settings that water becomes one of the Bible's richest pictures of how God gives life, judges sin, cleanses His people, and rescues them.

Water appears at nearly every turning point of the biblical story. The Spirit of God moves over the waters at creation. The Flood waters bring judgment. The sea parts for Israel, and water pours from a rock in the desert. The Lord calls Himself a spring of living water and grieves that His people have dug broken cisterns instead. The prophets then promise a day when He will pour water on the thirsty and His Spirit on their children, and when a river will flow from His house to heal a dead land. Jesus takes up that promise when He offers living water. To trace mayim through Scripture is to learn that God Himself is the source of life, and that thirsty people are meant to come to Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mayim is a common Hebrew noun used across almost every book of the Old Testament. Hebrew treats it as a plural-form noun. That suits water well, because water is something you measure by volume rather than count one by one. English translations alternate between "water" and "waters" for the same Hebrew word, usually depending on whether a cup or a sea is in view.

The setting matters. Israel lived in a dry land where rain was seasonal, wells were fought over, and a dry wadi could mean death for a flock. Abraham rebuked Abimelech over a well his servants had seized (Genesis 21:25), and later the herdsmen of Gerar quarreled with Isaac's herdsmen over water (26:20). A city under siege had to protect its water supply, which is why Hezekiah cut his tunnel to bring water into Jerusalem (2 Kings 20:20). An ancient reader felt both the life and the danger in this word: water kept a household alive, and floodwater or a raging sea could destroy it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mayim means water. Context decides whether a small amount or a great mass is in view, and whether the use is literal or figurative.

1. Water as a substance for drinking, washing, cooking, and ritual cleansing. Rebekah gives Abraham's servant water from her pitcher (Genesis 24:17), and the unclean person bathes in water (Leviticus 15:5).

2. Bodies or masses of water, such as seas, rivers, springs, floodwaters, and the primeval deep. The Spirit moves over "the waters" in Genesis 1:2, and the Lord drives back "the waters" of the sea in Exodus 14:21.

3. Figuratively, anything that flows, pours, or overwhelms like water. Examples include tears running from the eyes (Lamentations 1:16), strength draining away (Psalm 22:14), and danger rising to the neck (Psalm 69:1).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Several recurring phrases change how the word is heard.

"Living water" is the Hebrew way of describing running or spring-fed water, as opposed to collected rainwater. The NKJV usually renders it "running water," as with the well Isaac's servants found (Genesis 26:19) and the water used to cleanse a leper (Leviticus 14:5). The same everyday expression stands behind the Lord's self-description in Jeremiah 2:13 and the waters flowing from Jerusalem in Zechariah 14:8. When the prophets speak of God as "living water," they are drawing on an ordinary image: water that keeps flowing and does not go stale.

"Many waters" is a poetic phrase for overwhelming power. It can describe danger, as in "He drew me out of many waters" (Psalm 18:16). It can also describe glory, as when the voice of God's glory sounds like the noise of many waters (Ezekiel 43:2), or love that many waters cannot quench (Song of Solomon 8:7).

Similes often turn on water's inability to hold its shape. Death is like water spilled on the ground that cannot be gathered up again (2 Samuel 14:14). The sufferer of Psalm 22:14 is poured out like water. Mayim also appears in place names such as the waters of Merom (Joshua 11:5), the waters of Meribah (Numbers 20:13), and Jerusalem's Water Gate, where Ezra read the Law (Nehemiah 8:1).

The same word can carry opposite associations. Water rescues in one passage and drowns in the next. For that reason no single symbolic meaning can be read into every occurrence, and context must govern.

4. Theological Significance

Water first teaches that God rules what no human can tame. Before there is light, there is a dark, formless deep, and the Spirit of God is moving over the face of the waters (Genesis 1:2). God's first creative acts separate the waters above from the waters below and gather the seas so dry land appears (1:6–9). The wisdom books celebrate this boundary. God drew a circle on the face of the waters (Job 26:10) and gave the sea its limit so the waters would not pass His command (Proverbs 8:29). Water that could destroy the earth stays where God tells it to stay.

When God lifts that restraint, the result is judgment. In the Flood the waters rose until they covered the mountains (Genesis 7:17–24), which reversed the order of creation. Afterward God swore that the waters would never again become a flood to destroy all flesh (9:15). The psalmists carry the Flood's terror into personal suffering: "Save me, O God! For the waters have come up to my neck" (Psalm 69:1). Jonah, sinking, cries out that "The waters surrounded me, even to my soul" (Jonah 2:5). In these passages water pictures human helplessness, and God is the only one who can reach down and lift a drowning servant out.

Water also reveals God as the giver of life. He sends rain on the earth (Job 5:10), leads His sheep beside still waters (Psalm 23:2), and plants the righteous like a tree by rivers of water (Psalm 1:3; Jeremiah 17:8). Isaiah pushes the image toward its fullest meaning. God promises to pour water on the thirsty and in the same breath to pour His Spirit on Israel's descendants (Isaiah 44:3). He invites everyone who thirsts to come to the waters, even those with no money (55:1). He promises that His people will draw water with joy from the wells of salvation (12:3). The physical gift becomes a picture of God giving Himself.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Jeremiah 2:13 and 17:13: the fountain and the broken cisterns. God brings a charge against Judah in two parts. They have forsaken Him, the spring of living waters, and they have dug cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that cannot hold water. The contrast would have been plain to anyone in the land. A cistern was a plastered pit carved out to catch runoff. It was laborious to dig, its water was stale, and a single crack could empty it. A spring flows on its own. Judah had walked away from a running spring to work hard at holes that leak. In context the cisterns are the idols and foreign alliances Judah trusted (2:18). Jeremiah 17:13 repeats the charge: those who depart from the Lord have forsaken the fountain of living waters. Sin here is folly as well as rebellion, because it trades an endless supply for an empty pit.

Exodus 17:1–6 and Numbers 20:2–13: water from the rock. Israel camped where "there was no water" (Exodus 17:1), and the people quarreled with Moses and nearly stoned him. God answered with mercy. He told Moses to strike the rock at Horeb, and water came out for the people to drink. Psalm 105:41 remembers it this way: "He opened the rock, and water gushed out; It ran in the dry places like a river." Years later at Meribah the scene repeats. This time Moses was told to speak to the rock, but in anger he struck it twice. Water still came out abundantly for the congregation, even though Moses lost his entry into the land (Numbers 20:11–12). God's provision did not depend on the people's gratitude or on their leader's obedience. Deuteronomy 8:15 adds that God led Israel through that thirsty land on purpose, so they would learn where life comes from.

Leviticus 14–16 and Numbers 19: water that cleanses. Priests washed at the bronze laver before ministering, so that they would not die (Exodus 30:20). A healed leper, a person with a bodily discharge, and the high priest on the Day of Atonement all had to bathe in water (Leviticus 14:8–9; 15:5–27; 16:4, 24). Numbers 19 goes further. Contact with a corpse, the most serious defilement, required running water mixed with the ashes of a red heifer that had been burned outside the camp (19:9, 17). Water alone did not answer death; the cleansing came through water joined to a sacrifice. Hebrews 9:13–14 draws this exact comparison. If the ashes of a heifer could purify the flesh, the blood of Christ will much more cleanse the conscience from dead works.

Ezekiel 47:1–12: the river from the temple. Ezekiel sees water trickling from under the threshold of the temple. A man measures it. First it is ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then waist-deep, and at last it is a river too deep to cross (47:3–5). It flows down into the sea, and the waters there are healed. Everything the river reaches lives, fishermen spread their nets where nothing could survive before, and trees on its banks bear fruit every month with leaves for medicine (47:8–12). Ezekiel mentions no streams joining it. The river deepens miraculously as it flows out from God's sanctuary. Zechariah 14:8 sees living waters flowing out from Jerusalem, and Joel 3:18 says, "A fountain shall flow from the house of the LORD And water the Valley of Acacias."

2 Samuel 23:15–17: water poured out to the Lord. David, hiding in his stronghold while Philistines held Bethlehem, said he longed for a drink from the well by the gate of his hometown. Three of his mighty men broke through the enemy camp to bring it to him. David would not drink it. He poured it out to the Lord and called it the blood of men who had gone at the risk of their lives. Ordinary well water had become too costly to drink because of the love that had carried it. The only fitting place for it was before God.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of water runs from the first page of the Bible to the last. The waters of creation, the Flood, the parted Red Sea and Jordan (Exodus 14; Joshua 3), the rock in the wilderness, and the washings of the tabernacle all leave Israel physically sustained but still thirsty in heart. The prophets point past every well and laver to God Himself and to the gift of His Spirit.

Jesus steps directly into that promise. At Jacob's well He tells a Samaritan woman, "Whoever drinks of this water will thirst again, but whoever drinks of the water that I shall give him will never thirst" (John 4:13–14). The water He gives "will become in him a fountain of water springing up into everlasting life." That is the language of Jeremiah's spring, now flowing inside the believer. At the Feast of Tabernacles He cries out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink," and John explains, "this He spoke concerning the Spirit" (John 7:37, 39). Isaiah's pairing of water and Spirit is fulfilled in Him.

Paul says of the wilderness that Israel "drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them, and that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4). The cleansing of the Law finds its reality in "the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit" (Titus 3:5), so believers can draw near "having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water" (Hebrews 10:22). Ezekiel's river reaches its end in "a pure river of water of life, clear as crystal, proceeding from the throne of God and of the Lamb," whose tree bears leaves "for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:1–2). The river now flows from the throne of God and of the Lamb.

7. Spirit & Life

Jeremiah's cisterns describe the tired restlessness of much of life. People rarely stop wanting water. What they do is dig for it in places that cannot hold it, such as success, approval, money, or someone else's affection, and then they dig again when those run dry. God's grief in Jeremiah 2 is that He was there all along, flowing freely. Coming back to Him means stopping the digging and drinking.

The wilderness rock shows that God's kindness runs ahead of our faithfulness. Israel complained, and Moses lost his temper, yet the water still came. When a believer comes to God parched and ashamed, the story of Meribah says the rock is still there.

Ezekiel's river answers the fear that our own dryness is too deep for God. The water went into the most lifeless body of water the prophet knew, and it brought fish and fruit. It also kept deepening, from ankles to a river no one could cross. The life of God in His people is meant to grow past our depth, so that we stop wading and start being carried.

God also wants that life to flow out through His people. In Amos 5:24 He rejects noisy festivals and empty offerings and says, "But let justice run down like water, And righteousness like a mighty stream." A wadi runs full in the rainy season and dries up in summer. God asks for righteousness that keeps flowing in the heat, the kind that keeps others alive the way a perennial stream keeps a valley green.

David's poured-out water speaks to how we receive costly love. Some gifts, such as the sacrifice of a parent or the faithfulness of friends, are too costly to treat lightly. The blood of Christ is the costliest gift of all, and He gives it to be received, not refused. The fitting response is to receive it with gratitude and to pour out our worship before the Lord.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:2. The Spirit of God hovers over the face of the waters. In Scripture's first use of the word, God's presence is already at work over a dark and formless world.

  • Psalm 42:1. The deer pants for the water brooks, and the psalmist's soul pants for God. Thirst becomes the language of longing for His presence.

  • Isaiah 43:2. When God's people pass through the waters, He will be with them, and the rivers will not overflow them. The God who parted the sea promises His company in every flood.

  • Habakkuk 2:14. The earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the Lord as the waters cover the sea (compare Isaiah 11:9). The image is of God's glory filling the whole earth.

  • Ezekiel 36:25. God promises to sprinkle clean water on His people and cleanse them from all their idols. This is the prophetic hope behind the washings of the Law.

  • Zechariah 9:11. Because of the blood of the covenant, God sets His prisoners free from the waterless pit. Rescue is pictured as release from the dry cistern.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Symbolic Use

Explicit biblical symbolism. Proverbs uses water with specific meanings the text itself makes clear. A father urges his son to drink water from his own cistern and running water from his own well (Proverbs 5:15–16), meaning he should find delight and faithfulness within his own marriage. Folly answers with the claim that stolen water is sweet (9:17), which is the lure of adultery, and the next verse reveals that her guests are in the depths of the grave. The same book observes that as face answers face in water, so a person's heart reveals the person (27:19). Still water was the ancient mirror, and the heart shows who a person truly is. These are local images inside wisdom poetry and do not set a general meaning for water elsewhere.

Established biblical imagery. Bitter water appears as a place of testing. At Marah Israel found water too bitter to drink. Moses cried to the Lord, and God showed him a tree. When he threw it into the waters, they became sweet (Exodus 15:23–25). In Numbers 5:17–28, the "bitter water that brings a curse" worked differently. It exposed hidden unfaithfulness and brought the curse on a guilty wife, while an innocent wife was cleared and remained free (5:27–28). Both passages present bitter water as a test, but only at Marah does God turn the bitter water sweet. Many believers have seen in Marah's tree a picture of the cross, which takes the bitterness of the curse and turns it into grace. That reading is a resonance drawn from the whole of Scripture, not something Exodus states.

Pastoral association. After Abraham died, the Philistines filled his wells with earth. Isaac dug them again and called them by the names his father had given them (Genesis 26:18). The text records history, not allegory. Still, the account offers a fitting pastoral picture. Wells of truth that an earlier generation opened can be stopped up, and each new generation must dig again to reach the same water. They do not need new springs. They need to return to the old ones.
