UPRIGHTNESS, EQUITY - (mêyshâr, Strong's H4339)

Summary

The Hebrew word mêyshâr describes what is straight, level and even. It names a path with no bend in it, a judgment that leans toward no one, and speech that says exactly what is true. The NKJV renders it "uprightness," "equity," "uprightly," "right things," and even "smoothly." That last rendering shows the word's concrete root: wine that slides down with nothing to hinder it.

Scripture uses mêyshâr most of all for the way God rules. The Psalms ask whether human rulers ever judge "uprightly" (Ps 58:1), and then announce that the Lord is coming to judge "the peoples with equity" (Ps 98:9). The prospect of His coming makes the seas roar and the rivers clap their hands, because God's judgment straightens what this world has bent. The same word describes the heart God delights in (1 Chr 29:17), the level path He makes for those who trust Him (Isa 26:7), and the fairness that true wisdom produces (Prov 2:9). God is straight, and He makes His people straight.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mêyshâr is a Hebrew noun built on the idea of straightness or levelness. It appears almost entirely in poetry and prophecy. Most uses are in Psalms and Proverbs, with others in Isaiah and the Song of Solomon and single uses in 1 Chronicles and Daniel. The word does not appear in the Law or in most of the historical books.

Its home is the language of worship, wisdom and hope, where Israel sang about God's reign and taught its children how to live. The physical picture of something level or unobstructed stays close to the surface. That is why the same word can describe a just verdict and a smooth swallow of wine.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, mêyshâr means straightness or evenness: the absence of crookedness, slant or obstruction. Context determines which of several related senses is in view:

1. Equity or fairness in judgment, meaning a ruling that tilts toward no one: "And the peoples with equity" (Ps 98:9).

2. Uprightness of heart and conduct, meaning a life and inner motive without hidden bends: "You test the heart and have pleasure in uprightness" (1 Chr 29:17).

3. Right or truthful speech, meaning words that match reality: Wisdom promises that her lips will speak right things (Prov 8:6).

4. Smoothness of movement, meaning something that flows without friction: wine that "swirls around smoothly" (Prov 23:31).

In Daniel 11:6 the NKJV translates the word as "agreement," meaning a treaty on supposedly even terms between kings.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word shifts noticeably depending on who is acting.

When God is the subject, mêyshâr describes His rule, and it carries complete confidence. When human rulers are the subject, the word usually appears in a challenge or a failure. Psalm 58:1 asks whether rulers judge uprightly, and the "agreement" between kings in Daniel 11:6 collapses with betrayal.

In Proverbs, equity is the goal of instruction. The book's opening states its purpose: to give the instruction of wisdom, justice, judgment and equity (Prov 1:3). Chapter 2 shows how a person arrives there. A son who receives his father's words, cries out for understanding, and searches for wisdom as for hidden treasure discovers that "the LORD gives wisdom" (Prov 2:6). Then he will understand "equity and every good path" (Prov 2:9). In Proverbs, wisdom is meant to show up in fair dealing with real people.

The word also reaches into love. In Song 1:4 the maidens say of the king, "Rightly do they love you." Interpreters debate whether the phrase means rightly or the upright ones love you. Either way, the picture is devotion without pretense or hidden agenda: love that runs straight to its object.

4. Theological Significance

The passages using mêyshâr reveal a God whose character has no bend in it. His judgments do not slant toward the powerful (Ps 9:8). He speaks openly. In Isaiah 45:19 He insists that He has not spoken in secret, in some dark place, the way idol oracles did, and that He declares what is right. His words can be trusted because they are as straight as He is.

God also loves straightness in others. David prayed, "You test the heart and have pleasure in uprightness" (1 Chr 29:17). God gives the wisdom that produces equity as well (Prov 2:6–9). Across these passages a pattern emerges. God does not merely measure people against His straightness. He works it into them.

The background to this pattern is a world gone crooked. The Teacher of Ecclesiastes concluded, "What is crooked cannot be made straight" (Eccl 1:15). Within this fallen world, that is true. Human justice bends, treaties break, and hearts hide motives. The hope of the mêyshâr passages is that God Himself will do what no one under the sun can do.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 58 and Psalms 96–99: Crooked rulers and the coming King. Psalm 58 opens by confronting the powerful: "Do you judge uprightly, you sons of men?" The psalm then accuses them of weighing out violence in the earth (58:2). Their scales are rigged.

Against this, Psalms 96, 98 and 99 announce a different Judge. "The LORD reigns," and "He shall judge the peoples righteously" (Ps 96:10). "You have established equity" (Ps 99:4). What stands out is creation's response. The heavens rejoice, the sea roars, the trees of the woods sing, and the rivers clap their hands, "For He is coming, for He is coming to judge the earth" (Ps 96:11–13; 98:7–9).

People usually dread judgment. Creation celebrates it here because God's judgment is straight. His verdicts will not be bought or bent, and they will finally set right what crooked rulers twisted. For the oppressed and for a groaning creation, the coming of an equitable King is the best news imaginable. Psalm 75:2 adds that God chooses the time: "When I choose the proper time, I will judge uprightly." His delay is not indifference.

1 Chronicles 29:17: Straightness in the heart. At the end of his life, David watched Israel give freely for the temple his son would build. He prayed, "I know also, my God, that You test the heart and have pleasure in uprightness." David had just confessed that everything given already belonged to God (29:14–16), so the gifts themselves could not impress Him. What God tests and enjoys is the heart behind them. David could say, "in the uprightness of my heart I have willingly offered all these things." In this passage uprightness means giving without a hidden motive, reluctance or wish to be seen. David's joy then spilled over at seeing others give the same way.

Isaiah 26:7: The level path. "The way of the just is uprightness; O Most Upright, You weigh the path of the just." The righteous walk a straight way, and God, named here as "Most Upright," attends to the path itself. Several translations render "weigh" as make level, which fits the image of God smoothing the road ahead. This verse follows the promise that God keeps in perfect peace the one whose mind is fixed on Him because he trusts in Him (26:3). The believer's straightness is not a solo achievement. It is the life of someone walking with the Upright One, who evens out the way.

The contrast with Psalm 58 is striking. Corrupt rulers weigh out violence, while God weighs the path of the just.

Proverbs 23:31 and Song 7:9: Smoothness that deceives and smoothness that delights. The physical sense of the word sheds light on the moral one. Proverbs warns, "Do not look on the wine when it is red... When it swirls around smoothly." The danger lies in that smoothness. The wine meets no resistance, and only afterward does it bite like a serpent (23:32). Sin often works this way, slipping past our defenses before we feel its sting.

The Song of Solomon uses the same word with the opposite effect: "The wine goes down smoothly for my beloved." Here the absence of friction belongs to faithful love freely given. The same word warns against a smoothness that destroys and celebrates a smoothness that blesses. The difference lies entirely in what is flowing and where it leads.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story told by this word moves from a crooked world toward a coming King. Ecclesiastes admits that the crooked cannot be straightened, and Psalm 58 exposes rulers who bend justice. Isaiah then hears a voice cry, "Make straight in the desert A highway for our God" (Isa 40:3). The Gospels apply that cry directly to John the Baptist preparing the way for Jesus (Matt 3:3). The straightening God promised begins with the arrival of His Son.

Jesus fulfills every dimension of mêyshâr. He is the Wisdom of God (1 Cor 1:24), whose words are entirely true. He is the King whose love is never feigned, and He is the One who keeps the Psalms' promise of equitable judgment. Paul told the Athenians that God has appointed a day on which He will judge the world in righteousness by the Man He has ordained, and that He proved it by raising Him from the dead (Acts 17:31). The coming Judge of Psalms 96 and 98 has a name and a face.

This Judge has already done something no human ruler could. He has made crooked people straight. He did not bend justice to do it. He bore its full weight Himself.

7. Spirit & Life

God tests the heart and takes pleasure in uprightness. That truth reaches into ordinary moments: the gift given quietly, the apology without excuses, the motive no one else sees. David's joy at seeing God's people give willingly shows that a straight heart is a glad heart. It has nothing to hide and nothing to protect.

Our speech is meant to share God's straightness as well. The one who speaks uprightly is the one Isaiah says will dwell on high (Isa 33:15), and a father's heart rejoices when his son's lips speak what is right (Prov 23:16). Flattery, spin and half-truths are crooked roads, and believers are called off them.

Proverbs 23:31 also offers a sober warning. Temptation rarely announces itself with friction. It goes down smoothly. Spiritual alertness means asking where a pleasant thing leads, not only how it feels right now.

Above all, the level path is the trusting path. God does not hand us a map and wish us well. The Most Upright walks with those who keep their minds fixed on Him, and He weighs and levels their way. Because His coming judgment is straight, believers can wait for it with something closer to the trees' song than to dread.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 9:8 — "He shall administer judgment for the peoples in uprightness." God's fairness is pictured as the ongoing rule of a just King over every nation.

  • Psalm 17:2 — David asks God to look on what is upright and to let his vindication come from His presence. He trusts a straight Judge to see his case truly.

  • Psalm 75:2 — "When I choose the proper time, I will judge uprightly." God's timing and His fairness belong together.

  • Isaiah 33:15 — The person who speaks uprightly is among those who can dwell secure with God. Straight speech belongs to a life near Him.

  • Proverbs 23:16 — A father rejoices deeply when his son's lips speak right things. Truthful words are a parent's joy.

  • Daniel 11:6 — A royal "agreement" between the king of the South and the king of the North breaks down in betrayal. Human attempts at even terms prove fragile.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3474 (yāshar) — the related verb meaning to be straight or level, or to make straight. It belongs to the same Hebrew word family as mêyshâr.

  • H3477 (yāshār) — the related adjective "upright" or "straight," frequently used of people who walk rightly before God. It also belongs to the same word family.

  • Canonical pairing with righteousness — In Psalm 9:8 and Psalm 98:9, mêyshâr stands in poetic parallel with "righteousness." Together the two words describe the full justice of God's rule.
