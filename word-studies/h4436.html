QUEEN - (malkâh, Strong's H4436)

Summary

The Hebrew word malkâh means queen. It is the feminine partner of the ordinary Hebrew word for king, and in Scripture it names two kinds of royal women. One is a ruler in her own right, like the queen of Sheba, who governs her land and travels with her own wealth. The other is a king's chief wife, like Vashti and Esther in the Persian court, who holds the highest rank among the royal women but still lives under the king's authority. The word is rare and concentrated: it appears only in the Sheba account (1 Kings 10; 2 Chronicles 9), throughout the book of Esther, and twice in the Song of Solomon.

The significance of these few uses is substantial. In every major setting the highest human rank a woman could hold is tested by its relation to God's purposes. The queen of Sheba spends her wealth to hear wisdom, then blesses the LORD who set Solomon on his throne. Jesus later holds her up as a witness against those who would not listen to Him. Esther discovers that her crown was given to her so she could risk it to save her people. In the Song of Solomon, sixty queens fade beside one beloved bride. Taken together, these passages show that God rules over thrones, and that a crown is a trust from Him rather than a place to hide.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Malkâh is a Hebrew noun built as the feminine form of melek, king. Ancient royal courts had several ranks of royal women, and Hebrew used different words for them. Malkâh should not be confused with the word for "queen mother," the king's mother. She often held lasting influence in Judah, as when Asa "removed Maachah his grandmother from being queen mother, because she had made an obscene image of Asherah" (1 Kings 15:13). Nor is malkâh the word behind the idolatrous "queen of heaven" in Jeremiah 7 and 44. In the Old Testament, malkâh is always an ordinary earthly title, never the name of a goddess.

The settings are few but vivid: a foreign queen's state visit to Jerusalem, the palace intrigues of Persia, where many Jews still lived scattered after the exile, and a royal love poem.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Biblical usage shows two distinct senses:

1. A woman who reigns as monarch over her own kingdom, with authority to command wealth and conduct relations with other nations, as the queen of Sheba does (1 Kings 10:1).

2. A king's principal wife, the royal consort who holds the highest rank among the women of the court while remaining subject to the king, as Vashti and Esther are in Persia (Esther 1:9; 5:2) and as the "sixty queens" are in Song of Solomon 6:8.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Kings and Chronicles, all eight uses refer to the queen of Sheba. She never appears beside a husband, and she acts on her own initiative and with her own treasury.

Esther holds most of the occurrences, and there the word usually works as a formal title placed before a name: "Queen Vashti," "Queen Esther." The frequent title matters for the story. In chapter 1, Vashti's title makes her refusal a matter of state, and the king's advisers worry that her behavior "will become known to all women" and spread through the empire (Esther 1:16–18). In chapters 5–9 the narrator keeps calling Esther "Queen" at precisely the moments she uses her position, so the reader never forgets what she is risking.

In the Song of Solomon the word is plural, "queens," and it serves as a point of comparison. The women of highest rank in a royal court are named to show how far above them the beloved stands.

4. Theological Significance

These passages reveal God's rule over the summit of human power. A malkâh stood near the top of ancient society, yet each queen in Scripture is measured by how she relates to the purposes of God. Royal women are often introduced by their beauty, wealth, or rank. They are finally judged by what they do with the power they hold.

The Sheba account places a Gentile ruler before the God of Israel. Her praise in 1 Kings 10:9 goes beyond Solomon to "the LORD your God, who delighted in you," and she grasps the reason for his throne: "Because the LORD has loved Israel forever, therefore He made you king, to do justice and righteousness." A foreign queen states Israel's theology of kingship more clearly than many of Israel's own kings would.

Esther shows God's providence working through an office that seems entirely secular. The book never names God, yet the queenship of a Jewish orphan in a pagan court becomes the means by which His people survive. God does not need a sacred title to accomplish a sacred work.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The queen of Sheba (1 Kings 10:1–13; 2 Chronicles 9:1–12). She came because she "heard of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD" (1 Kings 10:1). Her interest was more than political, because Solomon's fame was bound up with the LORD's name. She brought "camels that bore spices, very much gold, and precious stones" (10:2) and "spoke with him about all that was in her heart." This was a ruler with every material advantage who knew she lacked something. When she saw his wisdom and the order of his house, "there was no more spirit in her" (10:5). She confessed that she had doubted the reports: "indeed the half was not told me" (10:7). Her gifts were unmatched, since "There never again came such abundance of spices" (10:10). Yet she went home having received more than she gave: Solomon "gave the queen of Sheba all she desired, whatever she asked" (10:13).

Esther: the crown given for such a time (Esther 1–9). The title first passes from Vashti, who refused to be paraded before the king's guests (1:11–12), and then comes to Esther. The palace seems to offer safety. When her maids and eunuchs tell her that Mordecai is mourning in sackcloth, though, "the queen was deeply distressed" (4:4). She sends him clothes, and when he refuses them she sends Hathach to learn what is wrong (4:4–5). Only then does Mordecai send her a copy of the decree to destroy the Jews and urge her to plead with the king for her people (4:7–8). Her royal rank could not shield her from her people's danger. Mordecai then warns that the palace will not save her and asks: "Yet who knows whether you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (4:14). Esther's answer is to fast and then approach the king uninvited, against the law, accepting that she may die (4:16). The king receives her, calls her "Queen Esther," and offers her up to half the kingdom (5:2–3). At the second banquet she finally names her request, which is her own life and the life of her people (7:3–4), and she identifies Haman as the enemy. By this point the title has taken on a new meaning. A rank won in a beauty contest has become a position from which she pleads for others.

The sixty queens and the one bride (Song of Solomon 6:8–9). The king lists the splendor of a royal court: "There are sixty queens And eighty concubines, And virgins without number" (6:8). Against that crowd he sets his beloved, whom he calls his dove and his perfect one. She is the only one. He adds that the queens and concubines themselves see her and praise her (6:9). The women of highest status in the land honor the one the king has chosen.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus takes up the Sheba account directly. "The queen of the South will rise up in the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and indeed a greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42; compare Luke 11:31). A Gentile queen who crossed great distances for Solomon's wisdom will testify against people who had the Wisdom of God standing in front of them and turned away. Her journey also anticipates the promise that "The Gentiles shall come to your light, And kings to the brightness of your rising" (Isaiah 60:3).

Esther's story points toward the gospel by resemblance rather than by any stated fulfillment. A royal figure of Israel's people stands in the place of danger before the throne and pleads for a people under a sentence of death. Esther risked her life and was spared. Christ "loved the church and gave Himself for her" (Ephesians 5:25), and He went all the way to death.

Scripture also presents a false queen. Babylon "says in her heart, 'I sit as queen, and am no widow, and will not see sorrow'" (Revelation 18:7). That boast echoes the proud "Lady of Kingdoms" of Isaiah 47:5–8, who said, "I am, and there is no one else besides me." Revelation uses a Greek word, and Isaiah a different Hebrew one, but the picture is the same: queenship seized for self-glory, ending in sudden judgment. The true answer to Babylon is the Bride: "the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7). The singular bride of Song 6:9 fits this pattern well. Ephesians 5:27 describes the church as one He will present "not having spot or wrinkle," chosen and loved above all the splendor of the world.

7. Spirit & Life

The queen of Sheba had gold, spices, and a kingdom, and none of it answered the questions in her heart. She went looking and asked hard things. When she found the answers, she gave generously and blessed the LORD. Jesus' warning in Matthew 12 is pointed. She traveled from the ends of the earth for a lesser wisdom. Many people today have the Scriptures within arm's reach and the risen Christ offering Himself, and they never ask Him anything.

Esther's crown speaks to anyone God has placed somewhere with influence, whether a job, a platform, a family role, or a seat at a table. Position can tempt a person to stay quiet and stay safe. Mordecai's question cuts through that temptation. Esther's place in the palace was God's provision for others, and using it faithfully meant being willing to lose it. Her fasting before she acted shows where she knew her help would actually come from.

Babylon claims the crown and believes she is untouchable. The bride receives her honor from the King who chose her. The difference between them is where each one's glory comes from.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Esther 1:9 — "Queen Vashti also made a feast for the women in the royal palace." The title appears at the height of Persian luxury, just before it is lost.

  • Esther 1:17 — The advisers fear that Vashti's refusal will become known to all women. A queen's conduct was treated as a matter of empire-wide consequence.

  • Esther 2:22 — Mordecai reports a plot against the king to Queen Esther, who tells the king. This quiet act of loyalty later turns the story in chapter 6.

  • Esther 8:1 — On the day Haman falls, the king gives Queen Esther the house of Haman. The enemy's estate passes to the one he planned to destroy.

  • Esther 9:29, 31 — Queen Esther writes with full authority, together with Mordecai, to confirm the days of Purim. The queen who once interceded now establishes a lasting remembrance of deliverance.

  • 2 Chronicles 9:12 — The Chronicler repeats that Solomon gave the queen of Sheba all she desired, more than she had brought him.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H4428 (melek), king, the masculine word of which malkâh is the feminine form (1 Kings 10:9).

  • Distinct Hebrew word, contrast: H4446 (meleketh), the "queen of heaven" worshiped as an idol in Jeremiah 7 and 44. It is not an occurrence of malkâh.

  • Distinct role, contrast: H1377 (gevirah), the Hebrew word for "queen mother," used of Maachah in 1 Kings 15:13.

  • Canonical pairing: G0938 (basilissa), the Greek word for queen in Matthew 12:42 and Revelation 18:7, which carries the Sheba account and the Babylon contrast into the New Testament.
