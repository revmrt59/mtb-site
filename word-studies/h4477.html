SWEET, SWEETNESS - (mamtaq, Strong's H4477)

Summary

The Hebrew word mamtaq means "sweetness" or "something sweet." It appears only twice in the Old Testament, and the NKJV translates it "sweet" both times. The two passages could hardly be more different. In Nehemiah 8:10, the word names the sweet drinks of a holy feast. In Song of Solomon 5:16, it describes the mouth of a beloved bridegroom. In both verses the word is plural, as if one sweetness were not enough.

The two passages show how God uses sweetness to lead His people from grief into joy. In Nehemiah, exiles weeping over the Law are told to stop mourning, "drink the sweet," and share with those who have nothing, "for the joy of the LORD is your strength." In the Song, a bride who has searched for her beloved concludes that "his mouth is most sweet." Together the verses describe a sweetness that belongs to the whole gathered people and to the individual heart. That sweetness reaches its fullest meaning in Jesus Christ, whose "gracious words" amazed those who heard Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mamtaq is a Hebrew noun built from the verb mataq (H4985), "to be sweet." That family of words is about taste, the pleasant sensation of honey or sweet drink, and it easily extends to anything delightful. In English Bibles, "sweet" sometimes describes a fragrance, such as the "sweet aroma" of an offering. Those passages use different Hebrew words. Mamtaq stays within taste and the mouth.

The word appears in two very different settings. One is a historical scene in Jerusalem after the exile. The other is a love poem. In both places the word is plural, so it literally means "sweet things" or "sweetnesses."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mamtaq refers to sweetness or to things that are sweet. The two uses show two shades of meaning:

1. Sweet things to drink or eat. In Nehemiah 8:10, the plural noun names actual sweet drinks served at a festive meal: "eat the fat, drink the sweet."

2. Sweetness as a quality, heightened by the plural. In Song of Solomon 5:16, the bride says of her beloved, "His mouth is most sweet." The Hebrew plural, "sweetnesses," works as intensification. His mouth is sweetness itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The grammar of the second verse affects its meaning. In Nehemiah, the plural simply counts: there were various sweet drinks on the table. In the Song, the plural concentrates the idea instead of counting. The bride is not listing several sweet qualities. She means that his mouth is sweet through and through. The NKJV's "most sweet" captures this.

The mouth in Song 5:16 is the source of both kisses and speech, and the poem seems to have both in view. Just before this verse, she has described his lips as "lilies, dripping liquid myrrh" (5:13). Sweetness therefore becomes a picture of how delightful he is to be near and to hear.

4. Theological Significance

Both passages reveal a God who wants His people to know delight. Neither scene is about mere pleasure. In Nehemiah, the sweet drink is commanded on a day declared "holy to our Lord." In the Song, sweetness describes the one the bride has longed for and searched for. In both, sweetness belongs to holiness and to love.

Scripture makes a related point with other "sweet" words. God's judgments are "sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb" (Psalm 19:10). The psalmist exclaims, "How sweet are Your words to my taste" (Psalm 119:103). When Ezekiel eats the scroll God gives him, "it was in my mouth like honey in sweetness" (Ezekiel 3:3). Bible readers have long described the reception of God's Word in terms of taste. Obedience can be a delight to the one who obeys.

Scripture also exposes a counterfeit. Folly calls out, "Stolen water is sweet" (Proverbs 9:17). Bread gained by deceit tastes sweet, "but afterward his mouth will be filled with gravel" (Proverbs 20:17). Job 20:12–14 describes evil kept "sweet in his mouth" that turns to "cobra venom within him." True sweetness and false sweetness both please the mouth. The difference is what each one becomes after it is swallowed.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Nehemiah 8:8–12: sweetness instead of mourning. The exiles had rebuilt Jerusalem's wall. Now they gathered on the first day of the seventh month to hear Ezra read the Law. The Levites "gave the sense, and helped them to understand the reading" (8:8). As the people understood, they wept, because the Law showed how far they and their fathers had fallen (8:9). The leaders' response may seem surprising. They did not tell the people to keep weeping. They said, "Do not sorrow." Instead: "Go your way, eat the fat, drink the sweet... for the joy of the LORD is your strength" (8:10).

The order of these words matters. The sweet drink comes after the Law has convicted the people, and it is given as part of God's holy day. The weeping was real and appropriate. On this day, though, God wanted His people to taste His goodness. That joy would strengthen them to obey, and their tears could not. Scripture often pictures exile and judgment as bitterness: "He has filled me with bitterness, He has made me drink wormwood" (Lamentations 3:15). Here the God who once filled His people with bitterness hands them something sweet. The scene recalls Marah, where the LORD made bitter waters "sweet" for a complaining people (Exodus 15:23–25).

The same verse includes a second command: "send portions to those for whom nothing is prepared." The sweetness was not for private enjoyment. Verse 12 reports that the people did both: they went "to eat and drink, to send portions and rejoice greatly, because they understood the words." Their generosity reflected the Law they had just heard. At Israel's feasts, God commanded that "the stranger and the fatherless and the widow" rejoice alongside everyone else (Deuteronomy 16:11, 14). These people had just learned that they deserved nothing. Having received grace they had not earned, they shared their portions with those who had nothing prepared.

Song of Solomon 5:10–16: the sweetness of the beloved. Earlier in the chapter, the bride delayed when her beloved knocked, and he was gone when she opened the door (5:2–6). She searched the city for him. When the daughters of Jerusalem ask what makes her beloved better than any other, she answers with a long description: "chief among ten thousand" (5:10), with head like gold, eyes like doves, and lips dripping myrrh (5:11–15). She ends with his mouth: "His mouth is most sweet, Yes, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, And this is my friend" (5:16).

This is the language of someone who has felt his absence. The sweetness of his mouth is the conclusion of her search. The sentence that follows is also important. "Beloved" is joined to "friend." The one whose mouth is sweet is someone she knows and who speaks with her, not a distant figure she only admires.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song of Solomon is first of all a poem about human love. Jewish and Christian readers have long heard in it the love between God and His people, and the New Testament presents Christ as the Bridegroom of His church. Read in that light, the bride's words find their fullest object in Jesus. When He first spoke in Nazareth, the people "marveled at the gracious words which proceeded out of His mouth" (Luke 4:22). He told His disciples, "The words that I speak to you are spirit, and they are life" (John 6:63). The sweetness the bride found in her beloved's mouth corresponds to the grace in Christ's words. The New Testament does not cite Song 5:16 as fulfilled, but the connection is fitting. Peter uses the language of taste for the same reality: "if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is gracious" (1 Peter 2:3, echoing Psalm 34:8).

Nehemiah's feast also points forward. The prophets foresaw a day when "the mountains shall drip with sweet wine" (Amos 9:13), and when the LORD would make "for all people a feast of choice pieces, a feast of wines on the lees" (Isaiah 25:6), the same passage in which He wipes away tears (25:8). Nehemiah's people moved from weeping to sweet drink for one day. In Christ, God promises a feast where weeping ends forever. The returned exiles' meal gave them a small foretaste of that table.

7. Spirit & Life

Nehemiah 8 shows how God treats people who have just been convicted by His Word. Some believers assume that ongoing sorrow proves seriousness. On this holy day, however, God sent His weeping people to a meal. Conviction that leads to repentance should then lead to the joy of the LORD, and that joy gives strength. A believer who never moves past grief over sin to rejoice in God's mercy will find obedience exhausting.

Taste is a fitting picture because no one can taste on someone else's behalf. The psalmist calls God's words "sweet... to my taste" (Psalm 119:103). That describes personal experience, not secondhand information. Scripture is also honest that the Word does not always stay sweet. John found the little book "sweet as honey" in his mouth, but it made his stomach bitter (Revelation 10:9–10). The message brought judgment as well as delight. Sin works the opposite way. It tastes sweet at first and turns to gravel and venom later (Proverbs 20:17; Job 20:14). Learning to know the difference is part of walking with God.

The two verses also hold together what Christians often separate. One sweetness is shared at a crowded table, with portions sent across town to people who had nothing. The other is spoken by a bride about her own beloved and friend. Delight in Christ that never leads to generosity has missed Nehemiah. Shared religious celebration that never knows Him personally has missed the Song.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Mamtaq occurs only in Nehemiah 8:10 and Song of Solomon 5:16, and both are developed in Section 5. The passages about sweetness cited in Sections 4 and 7 use other Hebrew and Greek words. They add canonical context but are not additional occurrences of this word.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H4985 (mataq), "to be sweet," the verb from which mamtaq is formed.

  • Thematic contrast: the bitterness of exile and of Marah (Lamentations 3:15; Exodus 15:23) is set against the sweetness God gives in restoration. This is a contrast in imagery, not a relationship between words.
