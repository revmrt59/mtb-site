FROM, OUT OF, OF, BECAUSE OF - (min, Strong's H4480)

Summary

The Hebrew word min is one of the small, ordinary words that hold the Old Testament together. It usually means "from" or "out of," and it answers the question "Where did this come from?" Depending on its setting, it can also mean "because of," "since," "among," "some of," or "more than." It tells where something starts, what caused it, what it has been separated from, or what larger whole it belongs to.

Small as it is, this word carries much of Scripture's teaching about God. The LORD formed man "of the dust of the ground" (Genesis 2:7). David told his soul, "my expectation is from Him" (Psalm 62:5). In judgment God hides His face "from" the rebellious (Micah 3:4), and in mercy He promises, "I will take you from among the nations" (Ezekiel 36:24). Zechariah looks ahead to the One who would come from Judah: "From him comes the cornerstone" (Zechariah 10:4). Following this little word teaches a basic question of faith: where do our life, our hope, and our rescue actually come from?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Min is a Hebrew preposition, a connecting word like the English "from" or "of." It is among the most frequent words in the Hebrew Bible and appears thousands of times. It often stands alone, but it is also commonly shortened and joined directly to the front of the next word as mi- or me-. For that reason English readers rarely notice it, even though it sits underneath many familiar phrases.

Strong's notes that the word may first have meant "a part of." Its use in Scripture, however, settles its meaning more securely than any guess about its origin. It appears in every kind of biblical writing: creation accounts, laws about sacrifices, battle reports, psalms of trust, and prophecies of exile and return.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, min marks a starting point: the place, person, cause, or time that something moves out from. Context determines which of several related senses is in view:

1. Source or origin — where something comes from. The LORD formed man "of the dust of the ground" (Genesis 2:7), and the tree of life grew "out of the ground" (Genesis 2:9).

2. Separation or removal — movement away from a place or person. Living things "were destroyed from the earth" (Genesis 7:23).

3. Cause or reason — what prompts an action, often translated "because of," "through," or "at." Israel "groaned because of the bondage" (Exodus 2:23).

4. Part of a whole, or a point in time — "some of" or "among" a larger group (Genesis 25:30; Leviticus 11:13), or a starting moment such as "From the day" or "since" (Deuteronomy 9:7; Exodus 9:18).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most of the time min simply traces motion or origin. Water drains from a skin bottle (Genesis 21:15), fat is taken from the sin offering (Leviticus 9:10), and captives go from their homeland into exile (Micah 1:16). The meaning changes with what follows it. When a person follows the word, it usually speaks of relationship: help from someone, or distance from someone. When an event or condition follows it, it usually explains a cause.

Two grammatical patterns are especially useful for readers. First, Biblical Hebrew has no separate form for words like "stronger" or "greater." To compare two things, it says one is strong "from" the other, meaning "more than" it. When 1 Samuel 17:50 says David "prevailed over the Philistine," the word "over" translates min. David was mightier than Goliath, though he carried only a sling and a stone.

Second, when min is joined to a verb, it can mean "away from doing" something, so that the action is prevented. Moses prays that Levi's enemies would be struck down so "that they rise not again" (Deuteronomy 33:11). In this use, "from" becomes a barrier.

4. Theological Significance

Because min tracks origins, causes, and distances, the passages that use it keep returning to a basic truth: God is the true source, and everything else is derived. Humanity is drawn from the dust and depends on the breath God gives. Hope that lasts is received from God, not produced from within. Even creation's obedience is traced to His voice.

The word also brings out the moral seriousness of distance. Sin can cause God to hide His face from His people. Yet the same word describes how God brings His people out of the places their sin took them. Judgment and mercy both involve separation. In judgment, the wicked are removed from the earth. In mercy, God's people are taken out of exile and brought home. What matters is the direction God is moving them and the purpose behind it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 62:5 and Zechariah 10:4: Where hope and leadership come from. David writes, "My soul, wait silently for God alone, For my expectation is from Him." Throughout the psalm, men are attacking him and lying about him, and his response is to quiet himself. The reason he gives turns on min: he is quiet because his expectation does not come from his enemies, his circumstances, or himself. Zechariah 10:4 uses the same word four times in a row: "From him comes the cornerstone, From him the tent peg, From him the battle bow, From him every ruler together." The verse before it names "the house of Judah" as the flock the LORD will visit. Israel's leaders had failed and its people had been ruled by others. The prophet answers that the foundation stone, the peg that holds the tent firm, and the strength for battle will all come from within the people God Himself has chosen.

Genesis 2:7 and Deuteronomy 9:7: Where we came from, and how long we have strayed. "The LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life." Here min names humanity's material: we are made from dust and kept alive by God's breath. In Deuteronomy, Moses uses the word to mark the starting point of a long record of rebellion: "From the day that you departed from the land of Egypt until you came to this place, you have been rebellious against the LORD." Israel's waywardness was not a single lapse; it began on the day of deliverance and continued. Read together, these verses show creatures made of dust whose failures stretch back to the beginning. Psalm 103:14 shows how God responds to such people: "For He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust."

Genesis 7:23, Micah 3:4, and Ezekiel 36:24: Taken away, and taken out. In the flood, living things "were destroyed from the earth," while "Only Noah and those who were with him in the ark remained alive." Micah describes judgment in personal terms. When the corrupt leaders cry out, God "will even hide His face from them at that time, Because they have been evil in their deeds." The worst separation is being cut off from God's favor. Ezekiel then turns the same word toward rescue: "For I will take you from among the nations, gather you out of all countries, and bring you into your own land." In the verses that follow, God promises clean water, a new heart, and His own Spirit within His people (Ezekiel 36:25–27). God removes His people from exile in order to bring them near.

Exodus 2:23, Psalm 104:7, and Isaiah 28:7: What moves people, and what moves creation. Israel "groaned because of the bondage, and they cried out; and their cry came up to God." The next verse says God heard and remembered His promise to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Their suffering drove them to cry out, and their cry reached God. In Psalm 104:7, the waters that covered the earth fled: "At Your rebuke they fled." God's word alone was enough to make the waters retreat. Isaiah 28:7 shows the opposite kind of cause. The priest and prophet "have erred through intoxicating drink... They err in vision, they stumble in judgment." The men responsible for seeing clearly on behalf of the nation were blinded by what they drank.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Zechariah does not name the Messiah. Still, his cornerstone from Judah fits closely with what the New Testament says of Jesus. Hebrews 7:14 says our Lord arose from Judah, and Paul writes that the church is built with "Jesus Christ Himself being the chief cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20). The leadership Israel's failed shepherds could not supply came, in the end, from God's own promised line.

Other passages that use min lead toward Christ as well. Paul contrasts the first man, who was of the earth and made of dust, with the second Man, who is from heaven (1 Corinthians 15:47). The One who rescues dust-made people came from above and took on our frame. Ezekiel's promised gathering from among the nations widens in the gospel, as Christ gathers a people out of every nation, cleanses them, and gives them His Spirit. Micah's warning about God hiding His face is answered at the cross, where Jesus bore our separation from God so that those who belong to Him would never be finally turned away.

7. Spirit & Life

David's quieted soul in Psalm 62 shows that peace often depends on where a person expects help to come from. Much anxiety comes from waiting on people, outcomes, or our own resources to give what only God can give. When David says "my expectation is from Him," he is not suppressing his feelings. He is sending his hope back to its true source. The same discipline is available to any believer: to ask honestly what we are really looking to, and then turn our waiting toward God.

Isaiah 28:7 adds a sober warning. What we take in affects how clearly we see. The priests did not intend to lose their vision; they were "swallowed up" gradually by what they had allowed to influence them. Anything we let shape our judgment ahead of God's word will eventually distort it.

Deuteronomy 9:7 and Psalm 103:14 together give the believer an honest place to stand. God is not surprised by our long history of failure. He remembers that we are dust, and He has still chosen to breathe life into us, hear our groaning, and gather us home.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 10:11 — The LORD reminds Israel that He delivered them from the Egyptians, from the Amorites, from the people of Ammon, and from the Philistines. The repeated "from" lists His faithfulness one rescue at a time.

  • 2 Kings 21:15 — God says His people have provoked Him since the day their fathers came out of Egypt. This echoes Moses' charge in Deuteronomy 9:7 many generations later.

  • 1 Samuel 30:19 — After the raid on Ziklag, David recovered everything, so that nothing was lacking, small or great. Here min marks the full range of what God restored.

  • Genesis 25:30 — Esau asks for some of the red stew. The word "some of" is small, yet he traded his birthright for it.

  • Psalm 39:13 — The grieving psalmist asks God to turn His gaze away from him so that he may recover strength. His honest lament still speaks directly to God.
