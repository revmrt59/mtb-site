BOLT, LOCK - (manʻûwl, Strong's H4514)

Summary

The Hebrew word manʻûwl names the bolt or lock that fastens a door shut. It is a small, practical word that appears only a handful of times in Scripture, and it appears in two very different settings. In Nehemiah 3, Jerusalem's gates are rebuilt one by one, and the record keeps noting that the builders hung the doors "with its bolts and bars." In Song of Solomon 5:5, the same word describes the lock on a bedroom door, which the bride finally reaches to open for her beloved.

The two settings teach two sides of life with God. Nehemiah's bolts show that rebuilding a broken city meant more than raising walls. The gates had to close, or Jerusalem stayed open to its enemies. The bride's lock shows a door that should have been opened sooner. Her beloved knocked while she hesitated, and when she finally touched the lock, it was dripping with myrrh. A healthy spiritual life needs both: firm gates against what would destroy us, and a door that opens readily when the Lord calls.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Manʻûwl is a Hebrew noun for a door fastener. Lexicons connect it with a verb meaning to bolt or to lock. That verb appears where Ehud locks the doors of the king's upper room (Judges 3:23), and its passive form gives the bride her title "a garden enclosed" in Song of Solomon 4:12. These links help confirm the word's basic idea of something shut and secured. They do not add hidden meanings to it.

The word appears in two kinds of places. One is the city gate, where massive doors guarded a whole population. The other is the private door of a home. Ancient gates were secured with both bolts and heavier bars, which is why Nehemiah keeps naming the two together.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Manʻûwl means a bolt or lock: the device that holds a door closed and keeps it from being pushed open. The NKJV translates it "bolts" in Nehemiah and "lock" in Song of Solomon. The meaning is the same in both books. Only the size of the door changes.

The word should be distinguished from "bars" (Hebrew beriyach), which appears beside it in Nehemiah. Bars were usually heavy beams laid across the doors to resist force from outside. The bolt was most likely the catch or locking mechanism that fastened the doors themselves. When both were in place, the gate could hold.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Nehemiah 3 the word is always plural and always part of a repeated building formula. The builders laid the beams, hung the doors, and installed the bolts and bars. That pattern appears at the Fish Gate (3:3), the Old Gate (3:6), the Valley Gate (3:13), the Refuse Gate (3:14), and the Fountain Gate (3:15). The repetition carries the point: each gate was finished only when it could be locked.

In Song of Solomon 5:5 the word is singular, and the setting is personal. The bride speaks of her fingers dripping "on the handles of the lock." The device is ordinary, but here the drama is about whether she will open the door, not whether it will keep enemies out. One small reading caution: "my locks" in verse 2 refers to the beloved's hair. That is a different word that happens to share the English spelling.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God cares about protecting what He restores. Jerusalem's walls lay broken and its gates burned (Nehemiah 1:3) until God moved a Persian king's heart and sent Nehemiah to rebuild. God did not restore His city only to leave it exposed. The faithfulness that brought the exiles home also gave them gates that could shut.

The Song of Solomon shows the other side of God's ways. The one who protects His people also seeks them out personally. He knocks and waits. A door that closes against an enemy is wisdom. A door that stays closed against the Beloved is a loss. Scripture uses one word for both, and the contrast between the two settings carries the lesson.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Nehemiah 3: gates that can close. Nehemiah 3 reads like a work roster, and that is part of its value. Priests, goldsmiths, perfumers, district leaders, and ordinary families each took a section of wall or a gate. The NKJV introduces the repeated formula at the Fish Gate: "Also the sons of Hassenaah built the Fish Gate; they laid its beams and hung its doors with its bolts and bars" (3:3). The same careful note follows at the Old Gate, the Valley Gate, the Refuse Gate, and the Fountain Gate. Even the Refuse Gate, used for carrying waste out of the city, received its bolts. No gate was considered too humble to secure.

The surrounding chapters show why this mattered. Sanballat and Tobiah mocked the work and plotted against it. Nehemiah 6:1 notes that the wall was rebuilt with no gaps left, yet the doors had not yet been hung in the gates, and that is exactly when the enemies renewed their schemes against Nehemiah (6:2). A wall with an unlocked gate is still an open city. Once the gates were finished, Nehemiah ordered them kept shut until the sun was high and guarded by watchmen (7:3). Later he shut them against Sabbath traders who were drawing the people into compromise (13:19). The bolts were the final, practical step that let Jerusalem live as God's city again.

Song of Solomon 5:2–6: the lock and the delayed response. The scene opens at night: "I sleep, but my heart is awake; It is the voice of my beloved! He knocks, saying, 'Open for me, my sister, my love, My dove, my perfect one; For my head is covered with dew'" (5:2). He has come through the cold night for her, and he calls her by four tender names. Her answer is drowsy reluctance: "I have taken off my robe; How can I put it on again? I have washed my feet; How can I defile them?" (5:3). There is nothing wicked in her excuses. They are the reasons of someone comfortable and settled for the night.

Then "my beloved put his hand By the latch of the door, And my heart yearned for him" (5:4). His reach toward the door stirs her heart where his words alone had not. "I arose to open for my beloved, And my hands dripped with myrrh, My fingers with liquid myrrh, On the handles of the lock" (5:5). The text does not say plainly where the myrrh came from. Many readers understand that the beloved left it on the handles when he reached in, so that his fragrance waited on the very barrier she had been slow to lift. That reading fits the movement of the scene well. When she opens the door, though, he has gone (5:6), and she spends the rest of the night searching for him.

The garden of 4:12, "a garden enclosed," was shut to everyone except the beloved. In chapter 5 the same bride keeps the door shut against him. The lock was never meant for him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Bible's story of gates moves from burned gates, to bolted gates, to gates that never close. Nehemiah's Jerusalem needed bolts because enemies surrounded it. In the New Jerusalem, John sees gates that are never shut by day, and there is no night there (Revelation 21:25). The bolts of Nehemiah were a mercy for a city still under threat. The open gates of the coming city show a people who are finally safe, because God has removed every enemy.

Christ also stands at a door. To a church grown comfortable and lukewarm, much like the drowsy bride, the risen Lord says, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20). Revelation does not quote the Song, but the likeness is striking: a voice, a knock, a closed door, and an offer of shared intimacy. Jesus does not force the lock. He calls, and He waits for His people to open.

The myrrh adds a further connection, which is resonance rather than stated fulfillment. Myrrh was among the gifts brought to the child Jesus (Matthew 2:11), and it was used for His burial (John 19:39). The Bridegroom who knocks at His people's door is the One whose love led Him to the grave. That costly love still clings to the threshold of a hesitant heart.

7. Spirit & Life

Nehemiah's builders teach that restoration has to be secured. When God rebuilds a life after sin or ruin, the gates need bolts. These are settled convictions about what we will not let back in, practical boundaries, and watchfulness at the weak points. Sanballat renewed his schemes while the doors were still unhung. The enemy often does the same with a believer who has repented but has not yet decided what to shut out. Nehemiah later shut the gates against the Sabbath traders because their goods were drawing the people back toward the sins that had once ruined the city. Guarding what God has rebuilt is part of honoring Him for rebuilding it.

The bride teaches a different lesson. The door that should open to Christ is often held shut by something small, such as comfort or a settled routine, rather than by open rebellion. "I have taken off my robe" is the excuse of a person who loves the Lord and still does not want to be inconvenienced. Her story is honest that delay costs something: by the time she opened, he had withdrawn, and she had to seek him. Yet the beloved's hand at the latch and the myrrh on the handles show that he had not given up on her. His nearness awakened her longing, and his fragrance met her at the door.

A believer needs both kinds of doors. Bolts and bars belong at the gates where sin and compromise press in. The lock of the heart should give way quickly when His voice is heard.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Nehemiah 3:6 — Jehoiada and Meshullam repaired the Old Gate and installed its doors with bolts and bars. The old entrance was secured with the same care as the new work.

  • Nehemiah 3:13 — Hanun and the people of Zanoah repaired the Valley Gate with its bolts and bars and also rebuilt a long stretch of wall. One team took responsibility for both the opening and the barrier.

  • Nehemiah 3:14 — Malchijah, a district leader, repaired the Refuse Gate with its bolts and bars. Even the city's humblest gate was made secure.

  • Nehemiah 3:15 — Shallun repaired the Fountain Gate, roofed it, and hung its doors with bolts and bars. The gate near the city's water supply was finished as completely as the rest.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1280 (beriyach), bar — a distinct word paired with manʻûwl throughout Nehemiah 3. The bar reinforced the gate against force, while the bolt fastened its doors (Nehemiah 3:3).

  • Thematic connection: Revelation 3:20 — Christ knocking at a closed door. This is an image-family parallel to Song of Solomon 5:2–5, not a lexical relative.
