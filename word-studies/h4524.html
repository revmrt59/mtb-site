AROUND, SURROUND, TABLE - (mêçab, Strong's H4524)

Summary

The Hebrew word mêçab describes what surrounds something: the area around a city, a ring of carvings on a wall, clouds circling through the sky, a circle of enemies, or the circle of guests around a king. The NKJV translates it "around," "about," "surround," and once "table." Because the English words differ, readers can easily miss that all five uses share one picture: something enclosed by what stands around it.

The word appears only five times, and each use answers the same question differently: what surrounds a person or place? Solomon's temple was ringed with carvings of cherubim, palm trees, and flowers. Jerusalem was ringed with idolatrous shrines until Josiah cleared them away. Storm clouds circle the earth under God's direction. David was encircled by enemies whose own words would fall back on them. And in the Song of Solomon, the bride says that "while the king is at his table," her perfume fills the room. Taken together, these passages teach that the people around us, the pressures around us, and the influences around us are never the final word. God rules every circle, and He draws His people into a circle where He Himself is the center.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mêçab is a Hebrew noun from the common verb sâbab, "to turn, go around, or surround." The noun names the result of that action: the surrounding area, the circuit, or the circle itself. It appears in temple description (1 Kings), royal reform (2 Kings), wisdom poetry (Job), a prayer for rescue (Psalms), and love poetry (Song of Solomon). The word supplies the shape of the scene. The passage supplies its meaning, because everything depends on what forms the circle and who stands inside it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is "that which surrounds" or "round about." Context brings out three related senses:

1. Surrounding area or all-around position. The carvings run "all around" the temple walls (1 Kings 6:29), and the shrines stand in "the places all around Jerusalem" (2 Kings 23:5).

2. Circling movement or encirclement. Clouds "swirl about" (Job 37:12), and enemies are "those who surround me" (Psalm 140:9).

3. A circle of people gathered around someone. In Song of Solomon 1:12 the word refers to the king's company reclining together, which the NKJV renders "table."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word itself is morally neutral. A ring of carved cherubim and a ring of pagan altars are described with the same word. Context decides whether the circle protects, threatens, or welcomes.

One grammatical detail matters in Psalm 140:9. David's enemies are described with a form that means "those who surround me." The word does more than give their location. It defines who they are by what they do to David: they close in on him from every side.

4. Theological Significance

These five passages together teach that God is Lord over every circle. He designed the ring of beauty around His sanctuary. He directs the clouds as they circle the earth. He turns the enemies' ring back on itself. He calls His king to clear away the ring of false worship around His city. And in the Song, love creates a circle of nearness around the king.

People are always surrounded by something. The question Scripture presses is whether that circle is the presence of God or something that competes with Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:12, the king's circle. "While the king is at his table, My spikenard sends forth its fragrance." The "table" here is the king's mêçab, the place where he reclines with his guests gathered around him. A few verses earlier the bride rejoices that "The king has brought me into his chambers" (1:4). In verse 12 she is inside the king's circle, near enough that her perfume reaches him and fills the room.

Spikenard was a rare and costly fragrance. The bride does not bring it out to earn her place. She is already welcome, and the king's nearness draws her offering out of her. To be seated in the king's company meant to share his favor, his protection, and his attention. The order matters for worship: the king first brings her near, and her costly love follows.

Psalm 140:9, the ring that collapses. David prays against violent men who "sharpen their tongues like a serpent" (v. 3) and hide snares, cords, and nets along his path (v. 5). He is hemmed in on every side, with no human way out. So he looks up: "O GOD the Lord, the strength of my salvation, You have covered my head in the day of battle" (v. 7).

Two verses later he prays, "As for the head of those who surround me, Let the evil of their lips cover them" (v. 9). The two verses mirror each other. God covers David's head with protection, and the enemies' heads are covered by their own words. The lies they spoke against David come back on them. The circle around David proves weaker than the God above him.

1 Kings 6:29, the circle of life. "He carved all the walls of the temple all around, both the inner and outer sanctuaries, with carved figures of cherubim, palm trees, and open flowers." Anyone who entered saw no bare wall. Cherubim had guarded the way back to Eden (Genesis 3:24), and trees and flowers recall the garden itself. God's house surrounded the worshiper with reminders of the life and holiness that sin had lost and that God was opening again. The carvings stood in the house where God promised to dwell among His people (1 Kings 6:11–13).

2 Kings 23:5, the circle that had to be cleared. Josiah "removed the idolatrous priests... in the places all around Jerusalem," along with those who burned incense to Baal and the stars. The holy city had become ringed with competing worship. The rest of the chapter shows that the corruption had not stayed at the edges, because objects of pagan worship had been brought into the house of the LORD itself (vv. 4, 6). Josiah's reform had to reach both the center and the surroundings. Compromise allowed to remain around the edges rarely stays there.

Job 37:12, the circling storm. Elihu describes the clouds: "they swirl about, being turned by His guidance, That they may do whatever He commands them." From the ground, a storm looks like aimless churning. Elihu sees a guiding hand turning every cloud toward an assigned purpose: "Whether for correction, Or for His land, Or for mercy" (v. 13). The same circling sky may discipline, water the land, or bless, and God decides which.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pictures carried by this small word come together in Jesus. Jesus Himself was surrounded by hostile men, and Psalm 22 describes the Sufferer as hemmed in by enemies (Psalm 22:12, 16), using different Hebrew words. At the cross the enemies' circle seemed to win, yet their scheme fell back on them as Psalm 140 prays, and the One they surrounded rose.

Jesus is also the true dwelling place of God. What the temple carvings pictured in wood and gold, the Word made flesh fulfills: God living among His people.

The king's circle in the Song finds an especially striking parallel in the Gospels. While Jesus reclined at table in Bethany, Mary broke open costly spikenard and anointed Him, and the house was filled with its fragrance (John 12:3; Mark 14:3). Nard appears rarely in Scripture, and here it is poured out at the true King's table. The Gospels do not cite the Song, but the echo is hard to miss.

At the Last Supper, the disciple "whom Jesus loved" was "leaning on Jesus' bosom" (John 13:23), close within the circle at His table. That circle widens until it reaches the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9). God's own table, prepared even "in the presence of my enemies" (Psalm 23:5), is where the story is heading.

7. Spirit & Life

Look honestly at what surrounds you. Some circles press in, like hostile words, schemes, and accusations. Psalm 140 does not teach you to break out by your own strength. It teaches you to say, "You are my God" (v. 6), and to trust the One who covers your head while lying lips undo themselves.

Some circles are subtler. The shrines around Jerusalem did not seize the temple in a day. Josiah's thoroughness is a model for the believer: deal with the small compromises at the edges of your habits, friendships, and entertainment before they reach the center of your worship.

Some circles feel like storms. Job 37 does not promise that every cloud brings mercy, since some bring correction. It does promise that every cloud is "turned by His guidance." The swirl around you has a Director.

Finally, there is the King's circle. He brings you near before you bring Him anything. Once you are seated with Him, your worship begins to rise like the bride's spikenard and Mary's nard. It is costly, but it is given freely because you are close to Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All five occurrences of mêçab are discussed in Section 5. Read together, they show how one Hebrew idea lies behind English words as different as "around" (1 Kings 6:29; 2 Kings 23:5), "about" (Job 37:12), "surround" (Psalm 140:9), and "table" (Song of Solomon 1:12).

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H5437 (sâbab), lexical relative.** This is the verb "to turn, go around, surround" from which mêçab comes. It is used widely of encircling, whether marching around a city or enemies closing in.

  • **H7979 (shulchan), thematic connection.** This is the ordinary word for "table," used in Psalm 23:5, "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies." The two words are distinct. They belong together thematically because both picture the king's table as a place of favor, provision, and safety.
