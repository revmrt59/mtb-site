NUMBER, FEW, WITHOUT NUMBER - (miçpâr, Strong's H4557)

Summary

The Hebrew word miçpâr means "number," the count or total of something. Scripture uses it for an army roster, the days of a festival, the months of a man's life, or the years of an exile. Two common phrases stretch its range. People who are "men of number" are so few they can be counted at a glance, which is why the NKJV often translates the phrase "few." Something "without number" cannot be counted at all, and the NKJV renders it "innumerable," "immeasurable," or "infinite."

What makes this ordinary accounting word rich is who is counting and why. When God numbers His people, as in the census of Numbers 1, He claims them, ransoms them, and gives each a place. When David counts Israel to know his own strength, judgment follows. The God who "counts the number of the stars" also has understanding without number (Psalm 147:4–5). He sets the number of a person's months (Job 14:5) and keeps His word to the exact number of years (Daniel 9:2). Judgment can shrink a proud people to a countable few, yet God promises that Israel will become "as the sand of the sea, which cannot be measured or numbered" (Hosea 1:10). That promise opens onto the multitude no one could number standing before the Lamb.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Miçpâr is a Hebrew noun drawn from the vocabulary of counting and recounting. It is most at home in Numbers and 1 Chronicles, where tribes, clans, Levites, and soldiers are listed. It also appears often in Job, where it measures the brief span of human life against the greatness of God.

The word is not a key to hidden number codes. It works like a ledger entry. The spiritual weight comes from the setting: a census commanded by God, a census demanded by a king, a lifespan held in God's hand, or a promise that outruns every tally.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Number, count, total. This is the sum of people or things. Numbers 1:2 orders a census "according to the number of names, every male individually," and 1 Chronicles 23:3 records "the number of individual males was thirty-eight thousand."

2. "Of number," meaning few. A small group can be counted easily. Jacob fears his neighbors "since I am few in number" (Genesis 34:30).

3. "Without number," meaning countless. When the word is negated, it describes what cannot be counted. Joseph gathered grain "until he stopped counting, for it was immeasurable" (Genesis 41:49).

4. A telling or recounting. In Judges 7:15, Gideon hears "the telling of the dream." Here the word refers to going through a story in order, the way one goes through a count.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word most often counts people: tribes in Numbers 1, the Levites in Numbers 3, the returning exiles in Ezra 2. It also counts time. The tenure of David at Ziklag (1 Samuel 27:7), the sacrifices for each day of the Feast of Tabernacles (Numbers 29), and the years left until Jubilee in a land sale (Leviticus 25:15–16) are all measured by "number."

The two idioms in Section 2 change the feel of the word. In "few in number," the word tells the reader how vulnerable or diminished a group is. Jacob's household could be wiped out, and exiled Israel would be scattered and small (Deuteronomy 4:27). In "without number," the word points to overwhelming abundance. That abundance may be threatening, like the locust-like hordes of Midian (Judges 6:5). It may be generous, like Joseph's grain. In the highest uses it is worshipful, as in the "wonders without number" of Job 9:10.

4. Theological Significance

The first truth is that God counts what belongs to Him. Israel's tribes are numbered by name, the Levites by household, and the stars by name. In Scripture, God's counting is a form of knowing and caring for what He has made.

The second truth is that God cannot be counted. Elihu says, "Nor can the number of His years be discovered" (Job 36:26), and Job adds that God does "wonders without number" (Job 9:10). The same Lord who keeps perfect account of creation has an understanding that is "infinite," literally "without number" (Psalm 147:5).

The third truth is that God keeps His word to the exact count. Daniel "understood by the books the number of the years specified by the word of the LORD through Jeremiah" (Daniel 9:2). Seventy years meant seventy years, and Daniel began to pray because the count was nearly complete. Jeremiah warned the refugees in Egypt that only a small number would escape and return, and that they would learn whose word stands (Jeremiah 44:28, paraphrased).

The fourth truth concerns human life. Job prays, "The number of his months is with You; You have appointed his limits, so that he cannot pass" (Job 14:5). Elsewhere he speaks of his few remaining years (Job 16:22) and of the wicked man whose months are cut short (Job 21:21). Human life is a counted span, and the One who holds the count is God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 1 and 26 with Exodus 30: the Lord's roster. Numbers opens with God's command to count "all who are able to go to war in Israel... by their armies" (Numbers 1:3). The count arranges a redeemed people into the Lord's camp for the march to Canaan. Exodus 30 had already explained what being counted meant: "every man shall give a ransom for himself to the LORD, when you number them, that there may be no plague among them" (Exodus 30:12). Rich and poor gave the same half-shekel (30:15). Each counted person stood before God on equal terms and at the same price.

The book of Numbers is framed by two censuses. The first generation's count ends in death. Their bodies were to fall in the wilderness "according to your entire number," and they would bear their guilt one year for each of the forty days of spying (Numbers 14:29, 34, paraphrased). The second census in Numbers 26 counts a new generation, and the land is divided "according to the number of names" (26:53, paraphrased). God's count therefore records judgment on unbelief and also secures an inheritance for each family.

David's census and Gideon's army: counting strength. Set against this, David tells Joab, "count the people, that I may know the number of the people" (2 Samuel 24:2; compare 1 Chronicles 21:2). The phrase "that I may know" exposes the motive. David was measuring his own strength. Chronicles adds that God had promised to multiply Israel like the stars. Joab's census went unfinished, wrath fell, and the number was never entered into David's official account (1 Chronicles 27:23–24, paraphrased). The plague that followed recalls the warning of Exodus 30:12, though the text does not tie the two together explicitly.

Gideon's story moves the opposite direction. God said, "The people who are with you are too many for Me... lest Israel claim glory for itself against Me" (Judges 7:2). He cut the army down to "the number of those who lapped," three hundred men (7:6). Meanwhile Midian's camels were without number, like sand on the seashore (7:12). Then Gideon heard "the telling of the dream" of a barley loaf flattening the Midianite tent (7:15), and he worshiped. God deliberately made His people countable so that the victory could be counted as His.

1 Chronicles 22: the unmeasured gift. The plague after David's census stopped at the threshing floor of Ornan. There David declared, "This is the house of the LORD God" (1 Chronicles 22:1, paraphrased). On the ground where his counting brought judgment, David began gathering "cedar trees in abundance" (22:4), literally "without number." He later says of the gold, silver, bronze, and iron, "there is no limit" (22:16, paraphrased). The man who had counted soldiers for his own security now gave to God's house beyond counting.

Psalm 147:4–5 and Isaiah 40:26: the God who counts the stars. Psalm 147 first speaks of God gathering the outcasts of Israel and healing the brokenhearted (147:2–3). Then it says, "He counts the number of the stars; He calls them all by name. Great is our Lord, and mighty in power; His understanding is infinite" (147:4–5). The psalm puts the wounded and the galaxies side by side because the same attentive God tends both.

Isaiah makes the same point to exiles who complained that their way was hidden from the LORD (40:27). Isaiah tells them to look up. God brings out the starry host by number, calls each by name, and not one is missing (40:26, paraphrased). If God never misplaces a star, He has not lost track of His people in Babylon.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word follows a clear movement. Abraham's descendants were promised to be as numerous as the stars and sand (Genesis 15:5; 22:17). Unfaithfulness reversed that promise. Moses warned that a people once as many as the stars would be "left few in number among the nations" (Deuteronomy 4:27; compare 28:62). God spared "a few" through sword and famine as witnesses (Ezekiel 12:16). Even Assyria, the proud instrument of judgment, would be cut down until the trees of its forest were "so few in number that a child may write them" (Isaiah 10:19). The very next verses promise that a remnant of Israel will return (10:20–22).

Hosea announces the reversal: "the number of the children of Israel shall be as the sand of the sea, which cannot be measured or numbered," and those called "not My people" will be called "sons of the living God" (Hosea 1:10). Paul quotes this promise to show God calling a people from Jews and Gentiles alike (Romans 9:24–27).

Christ fulfills both sides of God's counting. As the Good Shepherd, He knows each of His own: "he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out" (John 10:3). This is the census of Numbers made personal and complete. Yet His kingdom overflows every ledger. In Revelation 7, John first hears the tribes of Israel counted and then sees a great multitude from every nation that no one could number, standing before the throne and before the Lamb (Revelation 7:4–9). The remnant God once reduced to a countable few becomes, in Christ, a people beyond counting.

7. Spirit & Life

Much human anxiety comes from counting: savings, years left, strength available, people who stand with us. David's census shows how quickly that counting turns into trust in the total. Gideon's story shows God sometimes reducing our numbers on purpose, until all we can boast in is Him. A believer who feels outnumbered may be standing exactly where Gideon stood.

Job 14:5 gives a steadier comfort. The number of our months is "with You." The count is not hidden from God or left to chance. Accepting this limit is not fatalism. It frees a person to use the days given rather than grasp at days never promised. Jesus brings the same care down to the hairs of the head (Matthew 10:30).

David's turn from the census to the temple offering shows what grace does to a calculating heart. On the ground where his counting was judged, he began to give without counting. Worship that keeps careful score of its costs has not yet understood the God whose understanding has no number. Psalm 147 also places the stars beside the brokenhearted. The God who knows every star by name is not too vast to notice one wounded person. His greatness is the reason He can attend to each of His own.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 41:49 — Joseph's grain grew "as the sand of the sea, until he stopped counting, for it was immeasurable." God's provision in the famine outran Egypt's bookkeeping.

  • Genesis 34:30 — Jacob fears destruction "since I am few in number," a picture of the patriarchs' fragile beginnings before God multiplied them.

  • Exodus 23:26 — God promises to fulfill the number of His people's days, so that a full life is His gift (paraphrased).

  • Joshua 4:5, 8 — The twelve stones from the Jordan were taken according to the number of the tribes, so every tribe was represented in the memorial (paraphrased).

  • 1 Kings 18:31 — Elijah builds the altar on Carmel with twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes of Jacob. He did this in a divided kingdom, declaring that Israel was still one people before God (paraphrased).

  • Jeremiah 2:28, 32 — Judah's gods are as many as its cities, and God's people have forgotten Him days without number. Jeremiah sets countless idols against a countless forgetting (paraphrased).
