REFUGE, DEN, DWELLING PLACE - (mᵉʻôwnâh, Strong's H4585)

Summary

The Hebrew word mᵉʻôwnâh means a dwelling, but it is usually a wild one. Most of its uses describe a lair, the den where lions and other beasts lie down, hide, and rest. It names a place of retreat that no one dares to disturb. Because the word appears only nine times, each use stands out, and its rugged sense carries into the passages where it is applied to people and to God.

Moses uses this word in his final blessing over Israel: "The eternal God is your refuge, And underneath are the everlasting arms" (Deuteronomy 33:27). God does not simply supply His people with a stronghold. He is that stronghold, and from it He drives their enemies away. Psalm 76:2 uses the same word for God's "dwelling place in Zion," the place where He broke the weapons of war. The prophets use it as a warning. Jerusalem boasted that no one could enter its "dwellings," and Nineveh filled its "dens" with prey like a lion. God set Himself against both. Scripture places two kinds of dens side by side: the proud, violent lairs that people build for themselves, and the eternal God who is His people's home and protection.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mᵉʻôwnâh is a feminine Hebrew noun. It is closely related to the more common masculine word māʻôwn (H4583), which also means "dwelling" or "habitation." Both forms describe where someone or something lives. However, the nine uses of mᵉʻôwnâh lean strongly toward the natural world. Six of them describe animal dens, most of them belonging to lions.

The word appears almost entirely in poetry and prophecy: Job, Psalms, the Song of Solomon, Jeremiah, Amos, and Nahum, along with the poetic blessing of Moses in Deuteronomy 33. These are books that use pictures to teach, and the picture of a den was familiar to people who lived near the thickets and mountains where lions hunted.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is a place where a creature dwells and takes shelter. Context decides whether the dwelling is a beast's lair, a human stronghold, or God Himself.

1. An animal's den or lair. This is the place where wild animals retreat to rest or wait in hiding: "When they crouch in their dens, Or lurk in their lairs to lie in wait?" (Job 38:40).

2. A dwelling or refuge, applied to people or to God. This sense describes a secure place in which to live and take shelter: "The eternal God is your refuge" (Deuteronomy 33:27); "Who shall enter our dwellings?" (Jeremiah 21:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The literal uses set the word's tone. In Job 37:8, a winter storm drives animals into shelter: "The beasts go into dens, And remain in their lairs." Psalm 104:22 shows the daily rhythm God built into creation. The young lions hunt by night, and "when the sun rises, they gather together And lie down in their dens." A mᵉʻôwnâh is where a creature withdraws when it is finished hunting or wants to hide. It is concealed, defended, and dangerous to anyone who intrudes.

The English translation changes with the subject. The NKJV uses "dens" and "lairs" for animals, "dwellings" for Jerusalem, "dwelling place" for Zion, and "refuge" for God. Behind all five English words stands the same Hebrew word. Because the word's other uses are so strongly tied to the lair, its use for God and for human cities carries a sense of security that is fierce rather than merely comfortable. This undertone comes from the company the word keeps across its few occurrences, and it should not be read as a rule built into every use.

4. Theological Significance

The passages that use this word teach that security belongs to the one who guards the dwelling. A den is safe because of the lion that lives in it, not because of its walls. Scripture applies that logic directly to God. When God is the dwelling, nothing can overthrow it. When people make themselves the guardians of their own stronghold and set themselves against God, their den offers no protection at all.

These passages also show that God's protection is active. The God who is Israel's refuge thrusts out the enemy, and the God who dwells in Zion breaks the bow and the sword. His presence among His people is not a quiet retreat while the battle goes on elsewhere. His dwelling is the place from which He fights for them. The passages also show that God opposes predatory power. The lion's den becomes a picture of empires that grow fat on violence, and God declares Himself against them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 33:26–29: the eternal God as His people's refuge. Moses' last words to Israel reach their height in this verse: "The eternal God is your refuge, And underneath are the everlasting arms; He will thrust out the enemy from before you, And will say, 'Destroy!'" (Deuteronomy 33:27). Moses does not point to a fortified city or a mountain stronghold. The refuge is a Person. The word Moses chose usually describes a den that no creature dares to enter, and he applies it to God Himself. That refuge is not passive. Verse 26 pictures God riding the heavens to help His people, and verse 27 has Him driving the enemy out from before them.

The two words "eternal" and "everlasting" also matter. A den gives shelter for a night or a season, but this refuge has no end, and the arms beneath it never tire. Moses prays in a similar way in Psalm 90:1–2, using the related word māʻôwn: "Lord, You have been our dwelling place in all generations… from everlasting to everlasting, You are God." A dwelling that lasts from everlasting to everlasting comes close to the hope of life with God that does not end.

Psalm 76:1–5: God's stronghold in Zion. Asaph writes, "In Salem also is His tabernacle, And His dwelling place in Zion. There He broke the arrows of the bow, The shield and sword of battle" (Psalm 76:2–3). The word "there" ties God's dwelling to His victory. The enemies who come against Zion are plundered and sink into sleep (v. 5). Amos uses the same picture when he says that "the LORD roars from Zion" (Amos 1:2). God's home among His people is the stronghold of the Divine Warrior, and it is terrifying to anyone who attacks it.

Jeremiah 21:12–14: the den that could not save. In Zedekiah's day, Jerusalem trusted its location. God quotes the city's boast and answers it: "'Behold, I am against you, O inhabitant of the valley, And rock of the plain,' says the LORD, 'Who say, "Who shall come down against us? Or who shall enter our dwellings?"'" (Jeremiah 21:13). The people saw their city as an untouchable lair. Their question assumed that the only threat was Babylon. But the God they had ignored was the one standing at the door, and He promised to punish them according to the fruit of their doings (v. 14). A stronghold built in defiance of God becomes a trap when He is the one who comes against it.

Nahum 2:11–13 and Amos 3:4–8: two lions. Nahum mocks Nineveh as a lion that "filled his caves with prey, And his dens with flesh" (Nahum 2:12). Assyria had stocked its lair with the spoils of the nations. Nahum asks where that den is now (v. 11), and God's verdict follows in verse 13. In words much like those spoken to Jerusalem, the Lord of hosts declares Himself against Nineveh and promises to destroy its young lions. The empire's den would become a ruin.

Amos turns the image toward God. "Will a young lion cry out of his den, if he has caught nothing?" (Amos 3:4). A lion's roar means it has already seized its prey, and the roar is not a bluff. Amos draws the conclusion: "A lion has roared! Who will not fear? The Lord GOD has spoken!" (Amos 3:8). When the true Lion speaks, His word is certain. Nineveh's den was full of stolen flesh, but God's roar decides what happens to every den.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story told by mᵉʻôwnâh moves from creation's lairs, to the proud dens of nations, to God as the only refuge that cannot fall. The New Testament carries this hope forward in Christ. Paul tells believers, "For you died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:3). The life of a believer is now kept in the same place that Moses called Israel's refuge, in God, and Paul says it is kept there with Christ.

Revelation adds a striking connection. Heaven announces, "Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has prevailed" (Revelation 5:5). No passage directly links Revelation 5 to Deuteronomy 33, but the two images fit together naturally. Through most of this word's history, lions belonged to the hunters and the predatory empires. In Christ, the conquering Lion is the Redeemer, and the place where His people are hidden is His own unassailable life. The Lion who prevails is also the Lamb who was slain (Revelation 5:6), so the refuge He provides was won by His own sacrifice.

7. Spirit & Life

Every person lives in some kind of den. Jerusalem's den was made of geography and pride. Nineveh's was made of plunder. Both said in effect, "Who shall come down against us?" People still build these strongholds out of savings, reputation, control, or a carefully defended independence from God. These dens can feel safe for a long time. Jeremiah shows what that confidence is worth when the Lord says, "I am against you." Jerusalem's real danger came from the God it had refused to trust, not from Babylon.

Moses offers the alternative, and it is far better than a stronger wall. The eternal God Himself is the dwelling place. A believer's security does not depend on being strong, because the One who holds the refuge is. Beneath every weakness are "the everlasting arms," and in front of every enemy is a God who drives them out. Faith is the act of leaving the dens we have built and coming to live in Him.

The Song of Solomon offers a related picture. The bridegroom calls his bride "from the lions' dens, From the mountains of the leopards" (Song of Solomon 4:8). In its setting, this is a lover calling his beloved down from dangerous wild country. Many Christians have heard in it Christ's call to His people to come away from the predators of a fallen world. Read as an analogy, the picture fits the whole pattern of this word: He calls us out of dangerous dens and into Himself.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 37:8 — "The beasts go into dens, And remain in their lairs." The storm that God sends drives every creature into hiding. The verse shows the den as a shelter from forces far greater than the animals themselves.

  • Job 38:40 — God asks Job who provides for lions as they "crouch in their dens." Even the lair of the fiercest hunter lies within God's care and knowledge.

  • Psalm 104:22 — At sunrise, the lions "lie down in their dens." The predators' rest is part of the order God set in creation, and their retreat makes room for people to go out to work (v. 23).

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • **H4583 (māʻôwn), dwelling, habitation** — This is the masculine form of the same word. Moses uses it in Psalm 90:1, "Lord, You have been our dwelling place in all generations," which closely parallels Deuteronomy 33:27.

  • **H4631 (mᵉʻārâh), cave** — This is a distinct word, not a relative of mᵉʻôwnâh. It stands behind the "den of thieves" in Jeremiah 7:11. Jesus quotes that phrase in Matthew 21:13, where the Greek word spēlaion (G4693) also means "cave."

10. Other Relevant Insights

The famous "den of thieves" (Jeremiah 7:11; Matthew 21:13) uses a different Hebrew word, but it carries a related warning. Nineveh made its den a storehouse of violence. Jerusalem's leaders turned the house called by God's name into a hideout where sinners felt safe from consequences. In both cases, people tried to make a stronghold where they could do wrong and remain secure. God says of the temple, "Behold, I, even I, have seen it," and no den, whether a city or a sanctuary, is hidden from Him.
