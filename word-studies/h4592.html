LITTLE, FEW, SOON - (mᵉʻaṭ, Strong's H4592)

Summary

The Hebrew word mᵉʻaṭ means "little" or "few." It measures something as small compared with something larger. That something may be a quantity, such as a little water, a few people, or a small remnant. It may be a span of time ("a little while," "soon"), a degree ("almost," "scarcely"), or a matter of importance ("a small thing"). The NKJV uses more than a dozen English words to translate it, which shows how often Hebrew speakers reached for it in ordinary life.

In Scripture, this ordinary word also shows how God measures things. God set His love on Israel even though Israel was the least of all peoples. Jonathan trusted that the LORD could save by few as easily as by many. God promised to drive out Israel's enemies "little by little," and He promised His scattered exiles that He Himself would be "a little sanctuary" for them. The same word also exposes sin. Korah, Dathan, and Abiram called God's gifts "a small thing," and Proverbs warns that ruin comes through "a little sleep, a little slumber." Through mᵉʻaṭ, Scripture teaches God's people to see what He calls small and what He calls brief, and to look for Christ, who made Himself small for our sake.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mᵉʻaṭ is an everyday Hebrew word. It appears in nearly every part of the Old Testament: the Law, the histories, the psalms and wisdom books, and the prophets. Abraham offers his visitors "a little water" (Genesis 18:4). Ruth rests "a little" in the field (Ruth 2:7). The widow of Zarephath has only "a little" oil left (1 Kings 17:12). Because the word is so common, its theological uses carry particular weight. The same word a person would use about a cup of water is the word God uses to describe the nation He chose and the brief time before He acts.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mᵉʻaṭ describes something that is small relative to something else. It does not mean "none." Context shows what kind of smallness is meant:

1. Small in amount or number: a little, few, small, least. Jacob tells Pharaoh his days have been few (Genesis 47:9), and Ezekiel binds "a small number" of hairs in his garment (Ezekiel 5:3).

2. Short in time: a little while, soon. "For yet a little while and the wicked shall be no more" (Psalm 37:10).

3. Near in degree: almost, nearly, scarcely, on the verge. "My feet had almost stumbled" (Psalm 73:2).

4. Slight in importance: a small thing, a small matter, especially in questions such as "Is it a small thing...?" (Numbers 16:9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most often the word simply counts or measures: a few men at Ai (Joshua 7:3), a little food in Egypt (Genesis 43:2), smaller tribes receiving smaller inheritances (Numbers 26:54). Its significance grows when the thing measured is God's people, God's gifts, or God's timing.

Two patterns change the sense in ways a reader should notice.

The first is doubling. When the word is repeated, mᵉʻaṭ mᵉʻaṭ, it means "little by little," a gradual process. This is how God describes the conquest of Canaan (Exodus 23:30; Deuteronomy 7:22).

The second is the indignant question, "Is it a small thing...?" (Genesis 30:15; Numbers 16:9, 13; Isaiah 7:13; Ezekiel 16:20). A speaker uses this question to charge someone with belittling something weighty. The question exposes ingratitude or arrogance, and it often reveals more about the person being rebuked than about the thing they belittled.

4. Theological Significance

The word mᵉʻaṭ shows that God's scale of value differs from ours. Human beings tend to equate size with significance. Scripture repeatedly reverses that assumption.

Wisdom literature applies this reversal to possessions: "A little that a righteous man has Is better than the riches of many wicked" (Psalm 37:16). Proverbs teaches the same thing in two places. A little with the fear of the LORD is better than great treasure with trouble (Proverbs 15:16), and a little with righteousness is better than vast revenue gained through injustice (Proverbs 16:8). In each case, the spiritual condition of the owner determines the worth of what he owns. A little held under God's blessing can bring peace that great wealth gained unjustly cannot buy.

The word also measures human life truthfully. Jacob, after one hundred thirty years, called his days few and evil (Genesis 47:9). Job pleaded, "Are not my days few?" (Job 10:20). Even the proud "are exalted for a little while, Then they are gone" (Job 24:24). Moses expresses the same truth about human life with different Hebrew wording: "it is soon cut off, and we fly away" (Psalm 90:10). If our days are few, then our hope must rest in the eternal God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 7:7 and 1 Samuel 14:6: God chooses and saves through the few.

Moses tells Israel that the LORD did not set His love on them because they outnumbered other nations. In fact, they were the least of all peoples. The reason for God's choice, Moses explains, lies entirely in God: He loved them, and He kept the oath He had sworn to their fathers (Deuteronomy 7:8). Israel's smallness was part of the backdrop that made God's grace unmistakable.

Generations later, Jonathan stood before a Philistine garrison with only his armor bearer. He reasoned that nothing could hinder the LORD from saving, whether by many or by few (1 Samuel 14:6). Jonathan was acting on what Deuteronomy taught. If God chose Israel when it was the least, then God is not limited by the size of His army either. The doctrine of Deuteronomy 7 became courage on a cliffside. Paul teaches the same principle: God chose "the weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty... that no flesh should glory in His presence" (1 Corinthians 1:27, 29).

Exodus 23:30 and Deuteronomy 7:22: Little by little.

God promised, "Little by little I will drive them out from before you, until you have increased, and you inherit the land" (Exodus 23:30). Deuteronomy gives the reason: "you will be unable to destroy them at once, lest the beasts of the field become too numerous for you" (Deuteronomy 7:22). God paced the victory to match Israel's ability to settle the land. An empty land, cleared too quickly, would have turned wild. The slow pace was an act of mercy and wisdom on God's part. It did not mean His power was limited.

Numbers 16:9, 13: Calling God's gift "a small thing."

Moses asked Korah whether it seemed a small thing that God had set the Levites apart to serve at the tabernacle and to come near to Him (Numbers 16:9). Korah already held a holy calling, but he wanted the priesthood. Dathan and Abiram then turned the same question back on Moses. They called Egypt "a land flowing with milk and honey" and complained that it was a small thing to have been brought out of it to die in the wilderness (Numbers 16:13). Their words show how deeply ingratitude can distort a person's view. These men called the place of their slavery a paradise and treated their deliverance as a disaster. Deuteronomy 7 shows God lifting up what is small. Numbers 16 shows rebellious people belittling what God has given.

Isaiah 1:9; Ezekiel 5:3; Ezekiel 11:16: A small remnant and a little sanctuary.

"Unless the LORD of hosts Had left to us a very small remnant, We would have become like Sodom" (Isaiah 1:9). The survival of the few was entirely God's doing. Ezekiel acted out this truth by binding "a small number" of hairs into the hem of his garment (Ezekiel 5:3), a picture of the few whom God would preserve through judgment.

Ezekiel 11:16 goes further. The people left in Jerusalem claimed that the exiles were far from the LORD and that the land now belonged to those who remained (Ezekiel 11:15). God answered: "Although I have cast them far off among the Gentiles... yet I shall be a little sanctuary for them in the countries where they have gone." The phrase can mean a sanctuary in small measure or a sanctuary for a little while. Both readings fit the exiles' situation. The people who had lost the temple did not lose God. He Himself became their place of meeting in a foreign land.

Isaiah 26:20; Haggai 2:6; Psalm 37:10: The little while.

Isaiah calls God's people to "enter your chambers, And shut your doors behind you; Hide yourself, as it were, for a little moment, Until the indignation is past" (Isaiah 26:20). The picture recalls the Passover night, when Israel stayed indoors while judgment passed over. Haggai announced, "Once more (it is a little while) I will shake heaven and earth" (Haggai 2:6). The psalmist assures the troubled believer that the wicked will vanish in "a little while" (Psalm 37:10). In each case, the delay feels long to the people waiting, but God calls it short.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pattern of God working through the small reaches its fullest expression in Jesus. Psalm 8:5 uses mᵉʻaṭ to say that God made man a little lower than the angels. Hebrews 2:9 applies this psalm to Jesus, who was made lower than the angels for the suffering of death and is now crowned with glory and honor. The eternal Son entered the smallness of human life, with its few and fragile days.

Isaiah 7:13 adds another link. King Ahaz refused God's offer of a sign, and Isaiah asked whether it was a small thing for him to weary men, and whether he would weary God as well. God answered that unbelief with the sign of Immanuel (Isaiah 7:14), which Matthew 1:23 sees fulfilled in the birth of Jesus.

The "little sanctuary" of Ezekiel 11:16 points toward the hour Jesus announced, when true worshipers would worship the Father "in spirit and truth," whether or not they were in Jerusalem (John 4:21–23). It also points toward the church, which Paul calls "the temple of God," indwelt by His Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16). This is a canonical resonance, since the New Testament does not quote Ezekiel 11:16 directly, but the movement is real. God's presence was never confined to a building.

Hebrews also takes up the "little while." It quotes Haggai's promise of a final shaking and concludes that "we are receiving a kingdom which cannot be shaken" (Hebrews 12:26–28). It then encourages weary believers: "For yet a little while, And He who is coming will come and will not tarry" (Hebrews 10:37). The church now lives in the little while before Christ returns.

7. Spirit & Life

This word teaches believers to measure things the way God measures them. When God looks at the few, the weak, or the leftover remnant, He sees people He can use to display His grace. When people look at God's gifts and call them small, they are drifting toward the spirit of Korah. The person who serves faithfully in an unnoticed place should take courage from Deuteronomy 7. The person who resents his assigned portion should hear Moses' question as addressed to him.

God's "little by little" applies to growth in the Christian life as well. Believers often want instant victory over a sin or a weakness. God may grant victory gradually, so that the newly cleared ground of the heart does not become overgrown with pride or fresh temptation. The same gradual principle can also work toward ruin. "A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands" (Proverbs 6:10; 24:33) brings poverty like an armed man. People rarely fall through one great decision; they drift through many small ones. Faithfulness and neglect both grow by small steps.

Finally, mᵉʻaṭ gives believers language for honest struggle. Asaph confessed that his feet "had almost stumbled" when he envied the wicked (Psalm 73:2). Yet he found that God held him by the right hand (Psalm 73:23). "Almost" is the word of someone who came near to falling and was kept. For those who are suffering now, God calls the present affliction "a little while," and He is the one who measures it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 17:28: Eliab mocks David for leaving "those few sheep" in the wilderness. The young shepherd of a small flock is about to defeat Goliath.

  • 1 Kings 17:12: The widow has only a little oil and flour. God sustains her household through the famine by multiplying what little she had.

  • Psalm 2:12: The nations are warned to "Kiss the Son... When His wrath is kindled but a little." Even a small measure of God's anger is beyond anyone's strength to endure.

  • Psalm 94:17: Had the LORD not been his help, the psalmist says, his soul would soon have settled in silence. Death was that close.

  • Haggai 1:6, 9: The people sowed much and brought in little because they neglected God's house. A little harvest came as a result of misplaced priorities.

  • Zechariah 1:15: God says He was "a little angry" with His people, but the nations went beyond His purpose and added to their affliction. God's discipline is measured, and He holds accountable those who exceed it.
