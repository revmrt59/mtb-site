WORK, DEED, WORKMANSHIP - (maʻăseh, Strong's H4639)

Summary

The Hebrew word maʻăseh means a "work": something done or made. It can describe the woven curtains of the tabernacle, a shepherd's daily occupation, a person's moral deeds, an idol carved by a craftsman, or the heavens God set in place with His fingers. The word names the real result an action leaves behind. Whether that result is glorious or shameful depends on whose hands made it and for what purpose.

Through this word Scripture draws a sharp contrast. God's works reveal His character, and His people praise Him by remembering them: "The works of His hands are verity and justice" (Psalm 111:7). Human works may be offered to God, as when skilled artisans built His dwelling place. They may also be turned into idols, wasted "under the sun," or stored up as evidence for judgment. The deepest comfort the word offers comes when God's people say of themselves, "We are the clay, and You our potter; and all we are the work of Your hand" (Isaiah 64:8). The New Testament carries that confession forward when it says that believers are "His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus" (Ephesians 2:10).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Maʻăseh is a common Hebrew noun built on the verb ʿasah, "to do" or "to make." Because the verb is so ordinary, the noun covers the whole range of human and divine activity, from the work itself to the finished thing it produces.

The word clusters in a few settings. Exodus uses it again and again for the craftsmanship of the tabernacle and the priests' garments, and 1 Kings uses it for the bronze work of Solomon's temple. Deuteronomy applies it to farming and daily labor. The Psalms use it to praise God's works in creation and history. Ecclesiastes uses it to examine everything people do "under the sun." The prophets use it to condemn Israel's deeds and to ridicule idols as "the work of men's hands."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, maʻăseh means what someone does or makes. Context decides which kind of "work" is meant:

1. A crafted object or skilled workmanship. The tabernacle curtains carried "artistic designs of cherubim" (Exodus 26:1), and the NKJV also translates the word as "woven," "network," or "workmanship."

2. Labor or occupation. When Joseph's brothers came before Pharaoh, they were asked about their "occupation" (Genesis 46:33; 47:3). The same sense covers the six days of ordinary work in Exodus 23:12.

3. Deeds or conduct, the moral actions of a person or nation. Abimelech told Abraham he had done "deeds" that ought not to be done (Genesis 20:9).

4. God's acts in creation and history. These include the heavens, "the work of Your fingers" (Psalm 8:3), and the "great act of the LORD" that Israel saw in the Exodus (Deuteronomy 11:7).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The phrase that matters most is "the work of [someone's] hands." The word stays the same, but the owner of the hands changes everything. Psalm 8:6 says God made humanity "to have dominion over the works of Your hands." Isaiah 2:8 describes people who "worship the work of their own hands, that which their own fingers have made." In the first verse, people stand over God's handiwork as stewards. In the second, they bow before their own handiwork as worshipers. Creation's proper order has been turned upside down.

In the plural, especially in the prophets, "works" often describes a pattern of conduct. Jeremiah warns Judah against provoking the LORD "with the works of your hands" (Jeremiah 25:6–7). In those warnings "works" includes both the idols themselves and the way of life that produced them.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture lets us know God by what He has done. The psalmists rarely praise God in the abstract. They point to His works and read His character in them: "The LORD is righteous in all His ways, gracious in all His works" (Psalm 145:17). Because His works are real events and a real creation, praise can be handed down: "One generation shall praise Your works to another" (Psalm 145:4). Paul makes a similar point when he says God's invisible attributes are "understood by the things that are made" (Romans 1:20).

Human works carry moral weight before God, because a deed done does not simply disappear. Leviticus 18:3 forbade Israel to copy the "doings" of Egypt and Canaan. After the exile, Ezra confessed what had come "upon us for our evil deeds and for our great guilt" (Ezra 9:13). Ecclesiastes closes by saying God will bring every work into judgment, including secret things, whether good or evil (Ecclesiastes 12:14). God's response to deeds also works in the other direction. When Nineveh repented, God "saw their works, that they turned from their evil way" (Jonah 3:10, paraphrased), and He relented.

Deuteronomy adds that God blesses ordinary labor, and it ties that blessing to mercy. Israel was to share the tithe with the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow "that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hand" (Deuteronomy 14:29, paraphrased). The same promise follows giving to the poor without a grudging heart (15:10) and leaving gleanings in the field (24:19). God does not bless work simply because it is productive. He blesses the hands that open toward the vulnerable as He does.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The tabernacle's workmanship (Exodus 26–39; 1 Kings 7). No book uses maʻăseh more than Exodus, and most of those uses describe weaving, engraving, and metalwork for God's dwelling. The curtains were made "with artistic designs of cherubim" (Exodus 26:1). The ephod band, the breastplate, and the gemstones were all skilled work (28:8, 11, 15). Moses was told to build according to the pattern shown him on the mountain (25:40), and God filled Bezalel with His Spirit for craftsmanship (31:1–5). The word that praises God's heavens as His "handiwork" (Psalm 19:1) also describes an artisan's woven linen. The cherubim stitched into the curtains recall the cherubim who guarded Eden, and Hebrews later calls the earthly sanctuary a copy of heavenly things (Hebrews 8:5). When human skill follows God's pattern and is empowered by His Spirit, it becomes the setting where His glory dwells. Beauty, precision, and physical materials matter to God.

The irony of idols (Deuteronomy 4:28; Jeremiah 10; Psalm 115). Moses warned that in exile Israel would serve "gods, the work of men's hands, wood and stone, which neither see nor hear nor eat nor smell" (Deuteronomy 4:28). Jeremiah describes the process step by step. A tree is cut, decorated with silver and gold, and fastened "with nails and hammers so that it will not topple" (Jeremiah 10:3–4). The idol "must be carried, because they cannot go by themselves" (10:5). Jeremiah sets this beside the LORD, who "has made the earth by His power" (10:12), and concludes that idols are "a work of errors" (10:15). Psalm 115 adds a sobering note: "Those who make them are like them" (115:8). People become like what they trust. This is why Hosea's picture of repentance includes the vow, "Nor will we say anymore to the work of our hands, 'You are our gods'" (Hosea 14:3). The same verse also gives up trust in Assyria and in horses. "The work of our hands" stands for every attempt to build our own security, whether it is carved from wood or put together from alliances and armies.

The audit of Ecclesiastes. The Preacher built houses, planted vineyards, and made gardens. Then he looked over all the works his hands had done and found them vanity and grasping for the wind (Ecclesiastes 2:4, 11). The problem was not that work is evil. Work done only "under the sun" cannot carry the weight of ultimate meaning. Ecclesiastes points the reader past human works to God's: He has made everything beautiful in its time (3:11), yet no one can fully find out His work (8:17). The book then ends at the judgment seat, where every work will be weighed (12:14).

The clay pleads with the Potter (Isaiah 29:16; 64:8; Job 14:15). Isaiah rebukes schemers who act as if God cannot see: "Shall the thing made say of him who made it, 'He did not make me'?" (29:16). Later the same image turns into prayer. Israel, crushed by its sins, says, "But now, O LORD, You are our Father; We are the clay, and You our potter; And all we are the work of Your hand" (64:8). The people offer no merit. Their appeal rests entirely on whose they are. Job reaches for the same hope in his suffering when he looks beyond death: "You shall call, and I will answer You; You shall desire the work of Your hands" (Job 14:15). Being God's handiwork means being dependent on Him, and it also means being wanted by Him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah 59:5–6 pictures the wicked weaving spiders' webs and says their webs will not become garments and they cannot cover themselves with their works. The image fits the whole story of human maʻăseh. Our works may be intricate, but they cannot clothe our shame. Scripture's answer is a covering God supplies, which Isaiah later calls "the garments of salvation" and "the robe of righteousness" (Isaiah 61:10). The gospel brings that covering to fullness in Christ.

Paul's words in Ephesians gather the Old Testament threads together. Salvation is "not of works, lest anyone should boast" (Ephesians 2:9). He continues, "For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them" (2:10). Ephesians is written in Greek, so this is not the Hebrew word itself, but the pattern is the same as Isaiah 64:8. The clay does not save itself. It belongs to the Potter, who makes something good from it. Paul is confident that "He who has begun a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6).

The story ends with work restored. Creation was "subjected to futility... in hope" (Romans 8:20). Isaiah sees new heavens and a new earth where God's chosen ones will long enjoy the work of their hands (Isaiah 65:22). The vanity Ecclesiastes lamented will be lifted, and human labor, redeemed in Christ, will last and satisfy. The dead will still be "judged according to their works" (Revelation 20:12), but those who are God's workmanship in Christ will stand there clothed in what He has made.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 90 is Moses' prayer about how short human life is. It ends by asking God to let His work appear to His servants and then to establish the work of our hands (Psalm 90:16–17). The order of that prayer matters. Our work becomes lasting only after God's work has been seen. A believer can bring a job, a household, or a ministry to God and ask Him to make it last, because He is already at work.

The same order frees us from the anxiety of Ecclesiastes. In Psalm 92:4–5 the psalmist says God has made him glad through His work, and he cries, "O LORD, how great are Your works!" Joy comes from looking at what God has done before measuring what we have produced. The tabernacle shows that ordinary skills such as stitching, engraving, and metalworking can be holy when done by God's pattern for His dwelling. Deuteronomy adds that open-handed generosity is the kind of work God promises to bless.

When failure or suffering leaves us with nothing to offer, Isaiah 64:8 and Job 14:15 give us words to pray: we are the work of Your hand. God does not throw away what He has formed. He desires it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 5:29 — Lamech names Noah in hope of comfort from the toil of their hands on the cursed ground. The word's story begins under the curse.

  • Exodus 32:16 — The tablets of the Law were the work of God, and the writing was His writing. The commandments themselves were God's handiwork.

  • Psalm 139:14 — David praises God because he is fearfully and wonderfully made, and he says, "Marvelous are Your works." Each person is part of God's craftsmanship.

  • Isaiah 28:21 — The LORD rises to do His work, His "awesome work," against His own people. Judgment is real, though Isaiah calls it unusual for Him.

  • Isaiah 32:17 — The work of righteousness will be peace. Right living produces fruit God values.

  • Habakkuk 3:17 — Even if the labor of the olive fails, Habakkuk resolves to rejoice in the LORD. His joy does not depend on the harvest of his work.
