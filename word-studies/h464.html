ELIPHAZ - (ʼĔlîyphaz, Strong's H0464)

Summary

Eliphaz is the name of two different men in the Old Testament, and both belong to the world of Edom. The first is Esau's firstborn son by his wife Adah. His sons became chiefs of Edom, and through his concubine Timna he became the father of Amalek, whose descendants were Israel's bitter enemy for centuries (Genesis 36:10–12; 1 Chronicles 1:35–36). The second is Eliphaz the Temanite, the first and leading speaker among Job's three friends (Job 2:11). The name is usually understood to mean "My God is fine gold" or "God of gold," joining El (God) with a word for fine gold. Some scholars propose "My God is strength" instead, so the meaning is probable rather than certain. Scripture never builds a lesson on the meaning of either man's name.

Eliphaz the Temanite is the more important of the two. He came to Job in genuine grief and sat with him in silence for seven days. When he spoke, though, he insisted that Job's suffering must be the result of sin. Across three speeches he grew harsher, until he was accusing Job of cruelties Job never committed (Job 22:5–9). God's verdict was plain: "you have not spoken of Me what is right, as My servant Job has" (Job 42:7). The ending is full of mercy. God told Eliphaz to bring a sacrifice to Job and let Job pray for him, and Eliphaz obeyed. The man who wounded God's servant was restored through that servant's prayer, which points ahead to the greater Righteous Sufferer who intercedes for those who wronged Him. Paul later quoted one of Eliphaz's own sentences as Scripture (1 Corinthians 3:19, quoting Job 5:13). True words can still be wrongly applied, and correct doctrine cannot stand in for listening to God and loving a hurting person.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H0464 covers one Hebrew name borne by two distinct individuals. They should not be merged.

Eliphaz son of Esau was born to Esau and Adah in the land of Canaan, before Esau moved his household to the mountains of Seir (Genesis 36:2–8). He is listed first among Esau's sons (Genesis 36:4, 10). His sons were Teman, Omar, Zepho, Gatam, and Kenaz (Genesis 36:11), and Timna, his concubine, bore him Amalek (Genesis 36:12). His line appears again among the "chiefs" of Edom (Genesis 36:15–16) and in the Chronicler's genealogy (1 Chronicles 1:35–36).

Eliphaz the Temanite is Job's friend (Job 2:11; 4:1; 15:1; 22:1; 42:7, 9). "Temanite" connects him to Teman, a well-known city or district of Edom that carried the name of the first Eliphaz's son. It is natural to think of him as belonging to that Edomite clan, perhaps as a descendant of Esau's son, but Scripture does not give his genealogy. The book of Job also does not date its events precisely, so the time gap between the two men cannot be measured. The shared name and the Temanite homeland suggest that Job's friend came from the same Edomite region and culture as Esau's line, but Scripture does not show that he descended from the first Eliphaz.

The name. ʼĔlîyphaz is commonly explained as a compound of El, "God," and paz, "fine gold," giving "My God is fine gold" or "God of gold." The first element is clear. The second is less certain, and some propose a meaning such as "My God is strength." The best way to state it is that the name probably means "My God is fine gold." Scripture offers no wordplay on the name for either man. English readers may notice that Eliphaz himself tells Job that if he repents, "the Almighty will be your gold" (Job 22:25). The text does not link this line to his name, though, and it should be read as an echo rather than a deliberate pun.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Eliphaz, Firstborn of Esau

Genesis 36 is the record of "the genealogy of Esau, who is Edom" (Genesis 36:1). It shows the promise to Isaac being fulfilled in an unexpected direction. Esau, the brother who did not receive the birthright blessing, still became a nation, and Eliphaz stands at the head of his sons. Eliphaz's children became "chiefs," leaders of Edomite clans, which shows Edom organizing into a settled people in Mount Seir while Jacob's family was still a band of shepherds (Genesis 36:15–16, 31).

One detail stands out. "Timna was the concubine of Eliphaz, Esau's son, and she bore Amalek to Eliphaz" (Genesis 36:12). Timna is probably the same woman named as the sister of Lotan, a Horite chief of the land (Genesis 36:22). In that case Amalek came from a union between Esau's family and the older inhabitants of Seir. First Chronicles compresses the line and lists Amalek right after Eliphaz's other sons (1 Chronicles 1:36).

That single verse opens a long story. The Amalekites were the first people to attack Israel after the Exodus, striking at Rephidim (Exodus 17:8–16). Moses later recalled how they preyed on the weary stragglers at the back of the column, and that they "did not fear God" (Deuteronomy 25:17–19, paraphrased). The conflict ran into the days of Saul and Agag king of the Amalekites (1 Samuel 15). Many interpreters trace it further still to "Haman the Agagite" in Esther (Esther 3:1), though the book does not spell out his ancestry. Eliphaz himself is never blamed for any of this. He is simply the father through whom this hostile people entered history.

Eliphaz the Temanite, Job's Friend

The arrival. When Job's three friends heard of his calamities, "each one came from his own place... For they had made an appointment together to come and mourn with him, and to comfort him" (Job 2:11). Eliphaz is named first, probably as the oldest or most respected. The friends wept aloud, tore their robes, threw dust on their heads, and sat on the ground with Job for seven days without a word, "for they saw that his grief was very great" (Job 2:12–13, paraphrased). Their friendship was real, and this was the best thing they did.

The first speech (Job 4–5). Eliphaz begins courteously. He reminds Job that Job has strengthened others and asks him to take his own advice. He soon states his principle, though: the innocent do not perish, and those who plow iniquity reap trouble (Job 4:7–8, paraphrased). He supports it with a night experience: "Now a word was secretly brought to me, And my ear received a whisper of it... Fear came upon me, and trembling, Which made all my bones shake. Then a spirit passed before my face; The hair on my body stood up" (Job 4:12, 14–15). The voice asked, "Can a mortal be more righteous than God? Can a man be more pure than his Maker?" (Job 4:17). Much of what Eliphaz goes on to say is true and even lovely. God does great things without number, and He sends rain on the earth (Job 5:9–10). "He catches the wise in their own craftiness" (Job 5:13). Yet the whole speech is built to lead Job to one conclusion: you must have sinned.

The second speech (Job 15). The gentleness is gone. Eliphaz charges Job with undermining reverence for God and says that Job's own mouth condemns him (Job 15:4–6, paraphrased). He leans on tradition, the things "wise men have told, Not hiding anything received from their fathers" (Job 15:18). He then paints a long portrait of the wicked man who lives in terror and ends in ruin, and he clearly intends Job to see himself in it.

The third speech (Job 22). Here Eliphaz reaches his lowest point. His theology requires Job to be guilty, so he supplies the guilt himself: "Is not your wickedness great, And your iniquity without end? For you have taken pledges from your brother for no reason, And stripped the naked of their clothing. You have not given the weary water to drink, And you have withheld bread from the hungry" (Job 22:5–7). He goes on to accuse Job of sending widows away empty and crushing orphans (Job 22:9). None of it was true. Job 1:1 calls him "blameless and upright," and in chapter 29 Job describes himself as a father to the poor. Eliphaz still closes with an earnest call to return to God and find peace (Job 22:21–30), and the words are sound. They are aimed at a sin that does not exist.

God's verdict and Eliphaz's restoration (Job 42:7–9). After the LORD answered Job from the whirlwind, He turned to Eliphaz by name: "My wrath is aroused against you and your two friends, for you have not spoken of Me what is right, as My servant Job has" (Job 42:7). In the same breath He provided the remedy: "take for yourselves seven bulls and seven rams, go to My servant Job, and offer up for yourselves a burnt offering; and My servant Job shall pray for you. For I will accept him, lest I deal with you according to your folly" (Job 42:8). Eliphaz did not argue. "So Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite went and did as the LORD commanded them; for the LORD had accepted Job" (Job 42:9). Scripture then adds that the LORD restored Job's fortunes when he prayed for his friends (Job 42:10, paraphrased).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Several things are stated directly in Scripture. Esau's line became a nation through Eliphaz and his brothers. Amalek came from Eliphaz. Eliphaz the Temanite spoke wrongly of God. God was angry, appointed a sacrifice and an intercessor, and accepted Job's prayer for him. Paul quotes Job 5:13 with the words "it is written" (1 Corinthians 3:19).

From these facts some careful conclusions follow. The first Eliphaz shows how God's dealings with the nations around Israel are woven into the family history of the patriarchs. Edom and Amalek are not strangers to Israel but relatives, and that makes their later hostility all the more painful.

The second Eliphaz is central to the message of Job. His mistake was not that every sentence was false. Paul's quotation shows he could speak truth that belongs in God's Word. His error was building a rigid system, in which suffering always proves sin, and then forcing God's servant into it. When the facts did not fit, he changed the facts. God's rebuke protects a truth His people badly need: innocent suffering is real, and God Himself will vindicate it. The book does not explain all of Job's pain, but it plainly declares that Eliphaz's explanation was wrong.

Later prophets speak of the reputation of Teman for wisdom. Jeremiah asks, "Is wisdom no more in Teman?" (Jeremiah 49:7), and Obadiah warns that God will destroy the wise men of Edom (Obadiah 8–9). These passages do not mention Eliphaz. They do show that the wisdom of his homeland, admired as it was, finally failed to stand before God. Eliphaz offers an early portrait of that kind of wisdom.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament explicitly uses Eliphaz's words. Paul writes, "For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God. For it is written, 'He catches the wise in their own craftiness'" (1 Corinthians 3:19). The quotation carries a certain irony. Eliphaz himself was a wise man caught in his own reasoning. Paul uses his sentence to humble the Corinthians' pride in human cleverness, the same pride that kept Eliphaz from seeing what God was doing.

The ending of Job also forms a strong canonical pattern, even though the New Testament never calls Job a type of Christ. A righteous sufferer, falsely accused and declared right by God, prays for his accusers, and God accepts them because He accepts him: "My servant Job shall pray for you. For I will accept him." Scripture shows this pattern in its fullest form at the cross. Jesus, condemned on false charges, prayed for those who crucified Him (Luke 23:34). He now "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). Eliphaz could not stand before God on his own record. He needed a sacrifice and a mediator God had already accepted. Every believer stands in the same place.

5. Spirit & Life

God's anger with Eliphaz was not about a wrong opinion in a seminar. Eliphaz misrepresented God to a suffering man. This shows how seriously God takes the way His people speak of Him, especially to the broken. Eliphaz knew real truths, and he had sat in silence for a week out of love. He still did harm because he trusted his system more than he was willing to listen. Anyone who teaches or counsels is in danger of this. When a hurting person's experience does not fit our explanation, the temptation is to adjust the person rather than humbly question the explanation. Eliphaz's path from gentle hints in chapter 4 to invented crimes in chapter 22 shows where that temptation leads if it is not checked.

His night vision also deserves attention. Eliphaz rested much of his argument on a whispered word, a passing spirit, and bone-shaking fear (Job 4:12–16). Scripture never says what that spirit was. Its message contains truth, since no mortal is more righteous than God, yet in Eliphaz's mouth that truth only crushed a man who was already crushed. Some readers see a deceiving influence behind the vision, and that question is open. What the book makes clear is that a vivid spiritual experience did not make Eliphaz right. When God actually spoke, out of the whirlwind, He corrected every one of them. A spiritual impression must always answer to God's revealed Word and character.

Most of all, Eliphaz's story ends in grace. God did not leave him in his folly. He named the sin, provided the offering, and sent Eliphaz to the very man he had wronged. Restoration meant humility: bringing a sacrifice to Job and receiving Job's prayer. Eliphaz obeyed, and God received him. God's correction of His people is meant to bring them home, and He has provided the Mediator through whom it happens.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:4, 10–12 — Eliphaz as Esau's firstborn by Adah, his five sons, and the birth of Amalek through Timna.

  • Genesis 36:15–16; 1 Chronicles 1:35–36 — Eliphaz's line among the chiefs of Edom and in the Chronicler's genealogy.

  • Exodus 17:8–16; Deuteronomy 25:17–19 — Amalek's attack on Israel, showing the later weight of Eliphaz's line.

  • Job 2:11–13 — Eliphaz the Temanite comes to mourn with and comfort Job.

  • Job 4:12–17 — The night vision on which Eliphaz builds his first argument.

  • Job 5:13 — "He catches the wise in their own craftiness," later quoted by Paul.

  • Job 15:18 — Eliphaz's appeal to the tradition of the fathers.

  • Job 22:5–9 — Eliphaz's false accusations against Job.

  • Job 42:7–10 — God's rebuke, the sacrifice, Job's intercession, and Eliphaz's obedience and restoration.

  • 1 Corinthians 3:19 — Paul quotes Eliphaz's words as Scripture against worldly wisdom.

  • Jeremiah 49:7; Obadiah 8–9 — The prophets' judgment on the wisdom of Teman and Edom.
