FIND, FOUND, BE PRESENT - (mâtsâʼ, Strong's H4672)

Summary

The Hebrew verb mâtsâʼ means to find. It covers the ordinary experience of coming upon what was sought or hidden, such as a lost donkey, a stolen cup, or water in the desert. It also means to meet someone, to have enough of something, or simply to be found, meaning to be present. Scripture uses this everyday word for some of its weightiest realities. Noah "found grace in the eyes of the LORD." Moses promised scattered Israel, "you will find Him if you seek Him with all your heart." God says He "found" Israel in a howling wilderness. Sin, too, is said to "find you out."

Taken together, these uses show a God who can be found and who goes out to find. Grace is something people find in His eyes and cannot manufacture. God lets Himself be found by the wholehearted seeker, yet His ways can never be fully "found out" by human reason. When God searched for someone to stand in the gap, He "found no one." Scripture's answer to that empty search is the ransom God Himself provides, fulfilled in Jesus Christ. In Him the restless finally find rest for their souls.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mâtsâʼ is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Old Testament. It appears in family stories, laws, battle reports, prayers, proverbs, love poetry and prophecy. In law it describes a thief who is "found" or stolen goods located in someone's house (Exodus 22:2–8). In narrative it describes Joseph's servants finding the silver cup in Benjamin's sack (Genesis 44:12) and Samson's guests "solving" his riddle (Judges 14:12, 18). The NKJV renders the word in many ways, including "find," "present," "befall," "enough," "meet," and "solve," because the Hebrew word stretches wider than any single English term. That range is part of its value. A word used for finding lost animals is the same word Scripture uses for finding God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To find or discover what is sought, lost or hidden. Examples include the cup "found" in Benjamin's sack (Genesis 44:12) and Saul's search for his father's donkeys (1 Samuel 9:4, 20).

2. To come upon, meet or happen to someone. People meet one another on the road (1 Samuel 10:3), and troubles can "befall" a person, which in Hebrew means they find him (Deuteronomy 31:17).

3. To obtain or have enough. When Moses asks whether all the fish of the sea could be gathered to feed Israel, the question is literally whether they could be found in sufficient supply (Numbers 11:22).

4. To be found, meaning to be present or available. Saul counted "the people who were present" with him (1 Samuel 13:15). God is "a very present help in trouble" (Psalm 46:1); the psalmist is saying that God is readily found when trouble comes.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The weight of the word changes with who is doing the finding. When people find objects, the word is ordinary. When people find God, it describes a restored relationship. When God finds people, it shows His initiative. When sin or trouble "finds" someone, consequences arrive that cannot be dodged (Numbers 32:23).

One idiom deserves special attention: to "find grace" or "find favor in the eyes of" someone. Jacob uses it before Esau (Genesis 33:8, 10), and Ruth uses it before Boaz: "Why have I found favor in your eyes?" (Ruth 2:10). The phrase describes receiving kindness from someone who has the power to give it or to withhold it. That ordinary human usage helps readers see what it means when Noah and Moses find grace in the LORD's eyes.

The passive form "be found" matters too. When God says, "I will be found by you" (Jeremiah 29:14), He presents Himself as the One who allows Himself to be reached. Isaiah urges people to seek the LORD "while He may be found" (Isaiah 55:6).

In Job and Ecclesiastes, "find out" means to grasp something completely. Ecclesiastes says that no one can find out the work God does from beginning to end (Ecclesiastes 3:11).

4. Theological Significance

Mâtsâʼ reveals a God before whom nothing stays hidden. Moses warns the tribes east of the Jordan, "be sure your sin will find you out" (Numbers 32:23). Sin is pictured as something that tracks a person down. Judah, standing before a brother he did not recognize, confessed, "God has found out the iniquity of your servants" (Genesis 44:16). He understood that the cup in Benjamin's sack was bringing an older guilt to light: what the brothers had done to Joseph. When Elijah met Ahab in Naboth's stolen vineyard, the king said, "Have you found me, O my enemy?" Elijah answered, "I have found you, because you have sold yourself to do evil in the sight of the LORD" (1 Kings 21:20). God's prophet located the king at the scene of the crime, and no royal power could hide him.

The same word also marks the limit of human searching. Zophar asks, "Can you search out the deep things of God? Can you find out the limits of the Almighty?" (Job 11:7). In Hebrew, both questions use mâtsâʼ. Ecclesiastes repeats the point (Ecclesiastes 3:11; 7:24). These two truths belong together. God promises to be found by those who seek Him, but He never promises that anyone will master His ways. A believer can know God personally while still being unable to explain His providence. Scripture asks us to trust the Person when His methods are out of view.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 4:29 and Jeremiah 29:13–14: God promises to be found. Before Israel ever entered the land, Moses foresaw the nation's future idolatry and exile. Then he added a promise: "But from there you will seek the LORD your God, and you will find Him if you seek Him with all your heart and with all your soul." The words "from there" point to the far country of judgment. Centuries later, Jeremiah wrote to exiles in Babylon in nearly the same words: "And you will seek Me and find Me, when you search for Me with all your heart." He then gave God's pledge: "I will be found by you, says the LORD, and I will bring you back from your captivity" (Jeremiah 29:14). Finding God and being brought home are joined in one sentence. What the exiles found was the presence of the God they had lost, not new information about Him. Israel's history confirms the pattern. Under King Asa, the people sought the LORD wholeheartedly, "He was found by them," and He gave them rest all around (2 Chronicles 15:2, 4, 15). David gave Solomon the same counsel, with a warning that forsaking God means being cast off (1 Chronicles 28:9).

Genesis 6:8 and Exodus 33:12–17: finding grace. The LORD looked at a corrupt earth and grieved (Genesis 6:5–7). Then comes the turn: "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD." This is the first time Scripture speaks of grace, and it is something Noah found, not something he earned. Grace was already present in God's gaze, and Noah received it. Moses later stood on this same ground. After the golden calf, God threatened to send Israel on without Him. Moses pleaded, "For how then will it be known that Your people and I have found grace in Your sight, except You go with us?" (Exodus 33:16). Moses understood that favor with God means having God's presence with His people. God answered that Moses had indeed found grace in His sight (Exodus 33:17).

Deuteronomy 32:10 and Hosea 9:10: God finds His people. Moses' song describes the LORD's care for Jacob: "He found him in a desert land And in the wasteland, a howling wilderness." The verse continues with God encircling him and keeping him "as the apple of His eye." Hosea adds God's own memory: "I found Israel Like grapes in the wilderness." Grapes in a desert are an unexpected delight to a traveler. Israel had nothing to commend itself, yet God found the nation and treasured it. Hosea's next words carry God's grief: "But they went to Baal Peor." The God who found Israel was abandoned by the people He found. Isaiah later records God saying that He was found by those who did not seek Him (Isaiah 65:1).

2 Kings 22:8 and Jeremiah 15:16: finding the Word. During temple repairs, Hilkiah the priest "found the Book of the Law." When it was read to King Josiah, he tore his clothes (2 Kings 22:11). The finding led to repentance, renewed vows and the cleansing of the land (2 Kings 23). For Jeremiah the discovery was personal. In the middle of rejection and suffering, he says that God's words were found, that he ate them, and that they became the joy of his heart (Jeremiah 15:16). In one case the found Word overturned a nation's worship. In the other it kept a lonely prophet going.

Song of Songs 3:1–4; 5:6–8: seeking the beloved. The bride searches at night: "I sought him, but I did not find him" (3:1). She rises, goes through the streets, and asks the watchmen. Then comes relief: "I found the one I love. I held him and would not let him go" (3:4). Later the pattern returns more painfully. She hesitated to open the door, and when she finally did, "I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer" (5:6). Read within the larger biblical picture of God's love for His people, the Song gives words to a believer's experience of felt distance from the Lord, and to the longing that will not rest until He is found again.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Proverbs personifies Wisdom calling out in the streets. Wisdom promises that those who seek her diligently will find her (Proverbs 8:17), and that whoever finds her finds life and obtains favor from the LORD (Proverbs 8:35). The New Testament gives that wisdom a name. Christ Jesus "became for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30), the One "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3). Proverbs does not prophesy Christ directly, but the pattern points to Him: life is found in a Person.

The prophets record a search that came up empty. God told Jeremiah to run through Jerusalem to see "If you can find a man" who seeks the truth (Jeremiah 5:1). Through Ezekiel He says, "I sought for a man among them who would make a wall, and stand in the gap before Me on behalf of the land, that I should not destroy it; but I found no one" (Ezekiel 22:30). Isaiah describes the answer: seeing that there was no one to intercede, the LORD's own arm brought salvation (Isaiah 59:16). Revelation shows the same search on a heavenly scale. John "wept much, because no one was found worthy to open and read the scroll" (Revelation 5:4), until the Lamb who was slain stepped forward (Revelation 5:5–9).

Elihu supplies a related picture. God, being gracious to a man near death, says, "Deliver him from going down to the Pit; I have found a ransom" (Job 33:24). Elihu does not name the ransom. Still, the image of God Himself providing the price of rescue finds its fullness in Jesus, who came to give His life a ransom for many (Mark 10:45).

Psalm 89:20 records God saying, "I have found My servant David." Paul quotes this when he preaches that God brought Israel a Savior, Jesus, from David's line (Acts 13:22–23). Jesus also took God's finding upon Himself. The shepherd in His parable goes after the lost sheep "until he finds it," then calls out, "Rejoice with me, for I have found my sheep which was lost!" (Luke 15:4–6). God found Jacob in the wilderness, and in Christ He goes after the lost Himself.

7. Spirit & Life

The order matters. Noah found grace before he built the ark, and Moses pictures God's care for Israel as finding them in a howling wilderness. Our seeking is a response to the God who found us first. That frees seeking from anxiety. The command to seek "with all your heart" is not a test that might fail; it comes with God's own pledge, "I will be found by you."

The search for rest runs through this word. Noah's dove "found no resting place for the sole of her foot," so it returned, and Noah "put out his hand and took her" into the ark (Genesis 8:9). Naomi prayed that her widowed daughters-in-law might find rest (Ruth 1:9). Judah in exile "finds no rest" (Lamentations 1:3). Through Jeremiah, God pointed to the old paths: "Then you will find rest for your souls" (Jeremiah 6:16). Israel refused, and Jesus later repeated those words to weary people: "Take My yoke upon you and learn from Me... and you will find rest for your souls" (Matthew 11:29). Like the dove, a restless soul finds a resting place only when it comes back to the One who reaches out His hand.

Sin will find us out, and that is a mercy if it drives us to be found by God now. David says the godly pray to God "in a time when You may be found" (Psalm 32:6), in a psalm that celebrates confessed and forgiven sin. It is far better to come into the light willingly than to be found by Elijah in Naboth's vineyard.

When the Lord seems absent, the Song of Songs gives permission to keep searching through the night. The bride's lament became pursuit, and her pursuit ended in "I found the one I love." Josiah and Jeremiah show where that search often leads: back to the Word of God, opened and taken in.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:20 — Among all the animals, no helper comparable to Adam was found. The first "finding" in Scripture is a failure that God Himself answers by creating the woman.

  • Psalm 119:162 — The psalmist rejoices at God's word like someone who finds great treasure. Finding Scripture brings joy as well as instruction.

  • Proverbs 18:22 — The man who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD. A good marriage is presented as a discovered gift.

  • Daniel 12:1 — In the time of great trouble, everyone found written in the book will be delivered. Being "found" in God's record means salvation.

  • Malachi 2:6 — Of the faithful priest, injustice was not found on his lips. God looks to find truth in His servants' words.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2580 (chên, grace/favor) — Canonical pairing. The phrase "found grace in the eyes of" joins this noun with mâtsâʼ (Genesis 6:8; Exodus 33:16).

  • H1245 (bâqash, to seek) — Canonical pairing. Seeking and finding are linked in God's promise to Israel (Deuteronomy 4:29; Jeremiah 29:13).

  • G2147 (heuriskō, to find) — Thematic New Testament counterpart, not a lexical relative. It is the Greek verb behind the shepherd's finding of the lost sheep in Luke 15:4–6.
