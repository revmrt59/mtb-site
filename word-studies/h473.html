ELISHAH - (ʼĔlîyshâh, Strong's H0473)

Summary

Elishah appears only three times in the Bible, but those three verses reach from the scattering of the nations after the Flood to the prophetic lament over Tyre. In Genesis 10:4 and 1 Chronicles 1:7, Elishah is the first-named son of Javan, a grandson of Japheth and great-grandson of Noah. His line is among those from whom "the coastland peoples of the Gentiles were separated into their lands" (Genesis 10:5). By Ezekiel's day, Elishah names a maritime region known as "the coasts of Elishah," which supplied blue and purple cloth to the wealthy port city of Tyre (Ezekiel 27:7). The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is most likely the Hebrew form of an ancient place or people name rather than a Hebrew word with a clear meaning. Many scholars connect it with Alashiya, an ancient kingdom generally identified with Cyprus, but Scripture itself does not make that identification.

Elishah's brief record still has spiritual weight. Genesis places this distant people within God's ordering of the nations. Ezekiel then shows them caught up in Tyre's proud commerce. The blue and purple they sold were the same colors God had commanded for the tabernacle curtains and the high priest's garments (Exodus 26:1; 28:5). Israel used such colors in worship at God's command; Tyre used them to display its own beauty. If the link to Cyprus is correct, the story does not end in Tyre's judgment. The same Mediterranean coastlands later heard the gospel, when the Holy Spirit sent Barnabas and Saul to Cyprus (Acts 13:4).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Elishah refers to two related things. The first is a person, the son of Javan, listed with his brothers Tarshish, Kittim, and Dodanim: "The sons of Javan were Elishah, Tarshish, Kittim, and Dodanim" (Genesis 10:4). The second is the people and territory descended from or associated with him, which Ezekiel calls "the coasts of Elishah" (Ezekiel 27:7). Like many names in the Table of Nations, Elishah moves easily from an ancestor to the land and nation that bear his name.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. Some older commentators tried to connect it with El, the Hebrew word for God. That link has little support. In a list of Japhethite peoples, Elishah is far more likely a Hebrew rendering of a foreign geographic or ethnic name. For that reason, no reliable "meaning" should be read into the person or the region.

The most widely held identification connects Elishah with Alashiya, a Mediterranean kingdom named in ancient records from the late second millennium BC and generally located on the island of Cyprus or part of it. Other proposals have included Carthage, southern Italy or Sicily, and Elis in the Peloponnese of Greece. The Cyprus connection is the strongest of these, but it rests on evidence outside the Bible. Scripture requires only a coastal or island people in the Mediterranean world with access to the costly blue and purple dyes.

Elishah should not be confused with the prophet Elisha (H0477), whose name means "God is salvation." The two names look similar in English, but they are spelled differently in Hebrew and refer to entirely unrelated subjects.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Among the scattered nations. Elishah first appears in the Table of Nations, Genesis 10, which traces how Noah's descendants spread across the earth after the Flood. Javan's sons are listed, and then comes the summary: "From these the coastland peoples of the Gentiles were separated into their lands, everyone according to his language, according to their families, into their nations" (Genesis 10:5). Elishah belongs to the westward branch of humanity, the peoples of the Mediterranean islands and shores.

Preserved in Israel's memory. First Chronicles 1:7 repeats the list for the people of Israel after the exile: "The sons of Javan were Elishah, Tarshishah, Kittim, and Rodanim." The spellings Tarshishah and Rodanim differ slightly from Genesis. Dodanim and Rodanim differ only in their first letter, and the Hebrew letters for d (daleth) and r (resh) look very much alike, which likely explains the variation. The Chronicler begins Israel's story with Adam and the nations. That choice placed a returning, humbled people within the whole human family that God had made and still ruled.

Supplier to Tyre. Centuries later, Elishah reappears in Ezekiel's lament over Tyre, the great Phoenician trading city. Ezekiel pictures Tyre as a magnificent ship built from the finest materials of the known world: cedars of Lebanon, oaks of Bashan, and linen from Egypt. Tyre boasted that it was perfect in beauty (Ezekiel 27:3). Elishah's contribution is specific: "Fine embroidered linen from Egypt was what you spread for your sail; Blue and purple from the coasts of Elishah was what covered you" (Ezekiel 27:7). This covering was likely an awning or canopy over the deck. Dyed with the most expensive colors of the ancient world, it was a sign of display more than of seaworthiness. The lament goes on to describe the ship broken in the heart of the seas, with all its wares and merchandise sinking with it (Ezekiel 27:26–27).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture plainly states that Elishah's people took their place among the nations "according to their families" (Genesis 10:5). The Table of Nations comes just before the call of Abraham, through whom God promised to bless "all the families of the earth" (Genesis 12:3). Elishah is one of those families. Its name in the record means that even the far coastlands were never outside God's knowledge or His purposes.

Ezekiel 27 adds a sobering second note. A people named in Genesis as part of God's ordered world appears in Ezekiel as a supplier to a proud commercial power that God was about to bring down. The prophet does not accuse Elishah directly. Still, the vision makes clear that God sees the whole web of trade that fed Tyre's self-glory, down to where its fabric came from. His rule over the nations reaches far past Israel's borders.

The connection between Elishah's blue and purple and the colors of the tabernacle is a comparison of uses rather than a direct statement in Ezekiel. The comparison is grounded in the text, though. Israel knew these colors from the curtains of God's dwelling: "fine woven linen and blue, purple, and scarlet thread" (Exodus 26:1), and from the high priest's garments (Exodus 28:5). A Hebrew reader hearing that Tyre was "covered" in blue and purple would recognize the same kind of costly colors God had commanded for His house now covering a city that praised its own beauty. Ezekiel does not say these were sanctuary materials or colors reserved only for sacred use; the point is the contrast between two uses.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No New Testament passage names Elishah, so any connection to Christ comes through the larger story rather than a direct fulfillment. Two lines of connection are well supported.

The first follows the coastlands. Isaiah foresees that the coastlands will wait for the Servant's law (Isaiah 42:4) and that God's glory will be declared among distant nations and coastlands (Isaiah 66:19). The peoples scattered in Genesis 10 are among those the Messiah came to reach. If Elishah is Cyprus, the Book of Acts shows this happening on Elishah's own shores. Barnabas, a key early missionary, was "a Levite of the country of Cyprus" (Acts 4:36). Believers from Cyprus were among the first to preach to Greeks in Antioch (Acts 11:19–20). And when the Spirit launched the first missionary journey, Barnabas and Saul "sailed to Cyprus" (Acts 13:4). Whatever its exact location, Elishah lay within the Mediterranean world the gospel crossed in those early years.

The second follows the colors. The blue and purple of the tabernacle belonged to the place where God dwelt among His people. John writes that the Word became flesh and dwelt among us (John 1:14), revealing in Christ the presence the tabernacle had only pictured. Ezekiel shows colors like those of God's dwelling used to display human pride, and the gospel shows God bringing His presence to the very nations that had traded in them. This is a thematic resonance rather than a stated type, but it rests on real passages. A similar pattern appears again in Revelation 18, where purple appears among the cargo of a fallen commercial city whose merchants mourn its loss.

5. Spirit & Life

Elishah reminds us that God's record of the nations is personal and specific. A people on the edge of Israel's map, known mainly for selling dye, is named by God in Genesis and noticed by Him in Ezekiel. No people is too far away for His attention, and no trade route is hidden from His judgment.

Ezekiel 27:7 also reveals how God regards the good things of His creation. The blue and purple were not evil. God Himself chose them to adorn His dwelling. The problem in Tyre was what they were used for. The gifts, skills, and wealth God places in the world can be offered back to Him in worship, or they can be spent dressing up human pride. When Tyre sank, the costly covering went down with it.

Yet the coastlands that once supplied Tyre's vanity became places where the gospel was preached. God's answer to a world spending His gifts on itself was not only judgment. He sent His Son, and then He sent messengers across the sea. The fitting response is to bring to Him what Tyre kept for itself: our resources, our work, and our lives, offered to the One whose dwelling Israel adorned with those colors at His command.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:4–5 — Identifies Elishah as Javan's son and places his line among the coastland peoples of the Gentiles.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:7 — Repeats the genealogy for post-exilic Israel, with minor spelling variations among the brothers' names.

  • Ezekiel 27:7 — The only appearance of Elishah as a region: "the coasts of Elishah" supply blue and purple to cover Tyre's ship.

  • Ezekiel 27:3, 26–27 — Sets the context of Tyre's boast of beauty and its coming ruin.

  • Exodus 26:1; 28:5 — Establishes blue and purple as colors God commanded for the tabernacle and priestly garments.

  • Genesis 12:3 — God's promise to bless all the families of the earth, including the families listed in Genesis 10.

  • Isaiah 42:4; 66:19 — Prophetic hope for the coastlands to receive God's law and glory.

  • Acts 4:36; 11:19–20; 13:4 — The gospel's early reach to Cyprus, relevant if Elishah is identified with that island.
