APPEARANCE, SIGHT, VISION - (marʼeh, Strong's H4758)

Summary

The Hebrew word marʼeh means "appearance" or "sight," the way something looks when it is seen. Scripture uses it for ordinary things: Rachel's beauty, Joseph's good looks, the fat and gaunt cows in Pharaoh's dream, and a sore on the skin that a priest had to examine. It also describes the most overwhelming sights a human being has ever been given. The glory of the LORD on Sinai looked "like a consuming fire." Ezekiel saw blazing brightness around the throne of God. Daniel received visions that left him faint and sick.

Because the word is about what meets the eye, it carries one of Scripture's most searching lessons. Appearances attract us, mislead us, and tempt us. The LORD told Samuel that people look at the outward appearance, but He looks at the heart. That lesson reaches its depth in Isaiah's Servant, who had "no beauty that we should desire Him" and whose "visage was marred more than any man." The God whose glory blazed like fire revealed His love most fully in a face disfigured by suffering. To know Him, we must learn to see differently.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Marʼeh is a Hebrew noun formed from the common verb "to see." It names whatever is seen: a face, a figure, a visible condition, or a vision. The word appears in many kinds of writing. It occurs in family stories in Genesis, in priestly instructions in Leviticus, in the court narratives of Samuel and Esther, and in poetry. It is used most heavily in the prophets. Ezekiel accounts for more than a third of its occurrences, fifteen of them in his opening vision alone, and Daniel adds twelve more. That concentration matters. The word Israel used for a beautiful face became the word the prophets reached for when they tried to describe God's glory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Outward look or visible condition. This sense covers how a person, animal, or object appears, whether attractive, ordinary, or diseased. Rachel was "beautiful of form and appearance" (Genesis 29:17), and the priest judged whether a sore "appears to be deeper than the skin" (Leviticus 13:3).

2. Semblance or likeness. The word can describe something that looks like something else. It is often joined with "like," as in "the appearance of fire" over the tabernacle (Numbers 9:16). It can also mean a visible model, as in the "pattern" the LORD showed Moses for the lampstand (Numbers 8:4).

3. Vision. The word can refer to a revelation that God gives a prophet to see. Gabriel is told to make Daniel understand the vision (Daniel 8:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the narratives, marʼeh often marks a person's beauty just before that beauty becomes a test. Sarah's beauty endangered Abram in Egypt (Genesis 12:11), and Isaac feared for his life because of Rebekah's looks (Genesis 26:7). Joseph's handsome appearance is mentioned immediately before Potiphar's wife pursues him (Genesis 39:6). David's fall begins when "he saw a woman bathing, and the woman was very beautiful to behold" (2 Samuel 11:2). Ecclesiastes allows the young to walk in the sight of their eyes but reminds them that God will bring them into judgment (Ecclesiastes 11:9). Appearance itself is not the problem. God made every tree in Eden "pleasant to the sight" (Genesis 2:9). The danger comes when sight governs desire and loyalty, which John calls "the lust of the eyes" (1 John 2:16).

In Leviticus 13–14 the word becomes a working term for the priest. He must look closely at the appearance of a sore, a spot, or mold on a house wall. His verdict decides whether a person may remain in the camp where God dwells. Holiness required careful attention, and corruption could not simply be overlooked.

One construction deserves notice. The prophets repeatedly write "like the appearance of." Ezekiel says the living creatures' "appearance was like burning coals of fire, like the appearance of torches" (Ezekiel 1:13). This doubled language deliberately keeps a distance. The prophet describes what he saw as faithfully as he can while admitting that his words only approximate it.

4. Theological Significance

The passages using this word reveal a God who sees truly and who is Himself beyond ordinary sight. He is never fooled by appearances. People can be misled even by an altar (Joshua 22:10), but God weighs what lies beneath.

Even so, God makes Himself visible in ways human eyes can bear. Fire is the most consistent medium. Israel saw "the sight of the glory of the LORD" as "a consuming fire on the top of the mountain" (Exodus 24:17). Over the tabernacle the cloud became "the appearance of fire by night" (Numbers 9:16). Ezekiel saw fire and lightning around the throne. That fire both guides and warns. It led Israel through the wilderness night, and it told them that this holiness could consume them. God lets Himself be seen while shielding people from more than they could survive.

God also reveals Himself in different ways to different servants. When Miriam and Aaron challenged Moses, God said, "I speak with him face to face, Even plainly, and not in dark sayings" (Numbers 12:8). The word translated "plainly" is marʼeh: Moses received openly what others received in symbols. To other prophets God made Himself known "in a vision" (Numbers 12:6). Genesis 46:2 describes Jacob hearing God "in the visions of the night." Numbers 12:6 and Genesis 46:2 use the closely related Hebrew noun marʼah (H4759), meaning vision, and describe the same kind of prophetic encounter. Seeing what God shows is always a gift He chooses to give.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Samuel 16:7 — The LORD looks at the heart. Samuel saw Jesse's eldest son Eliab and assumed he must be God's anointed. The LORD stopped him. In substance He said, "Do not look at his appearance or his height, because I have refused him; people look at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart." The choice fell on the youngest son, who had been left out with the sheep. Even David's appearance later misled an enemy. Goliath despised him as a ruddy, good-looking youth (1 Samuel 17:42). Isaiah later promised a King from Jesse's line who would not judge by the sight of His eyes (Isaiah 11:3). Jesus applies the same standard to His hearers: "Do not judge according to appearance, but judge with righteous judgment" (John 7:24).

Ezekiel 1 — The appearance of the likeness of the glory. By the river Chebar, Ezekiel saw living creatures, wheels, a crystal expanse, and above them a throne with a figure like a man, surrounded by fire and the brightness of a rainbow. He uses marʼeh again and again. At the climax he does not claim to have seen the LORD Himself. He says he saw the appearance of the likeness of the LORD's glory (Ezekiel 1:28), and then he fell on his face. Each added word, "appearance" and "likeness," moves God's glory one step further beyond human description. Ezekiel's careful wording shows reverence for God, and it is no evidence of a weak experience.

Daniel 1 and 10 — Human splendor undone. Daniel was chosen for the king's service as one of the young men "in whom there was no blemish, but good-looking" (Daniel 1:4). After ten days on vegetables, his appearance was better than that of those who ate the king's food (Daniel 1:15). Yet when a heavenly messenger came, "his face like the appearance of lightning, his eyes like torches of fire" (Daniel 10:6), Daniel's strength drained away. He recovered only when one having the likeness of a man touched him and strengthened him (Daniel 10:18). The best human appearance cannot stand before heaven's reality. God sent a touch shaped like a man to raise him.

Isaiah 52:14–53:2 — The marred visage. Isaiah's Servant passage turns every human standard of appearance upside down. "His visage was marred more than any man, And His form more than the sons of men" (Isaiah 52:14). In His ordinary life "there is no beauty that we should desire Him" (Isaiah 53:2), and the word for "beauty" there is again marʼeh. The chapter goes on to explain the disfigurement: He was wounded for our transgressions, and by His stripes we are healed (Isaiah 53:5). The ruined appearance was the visible cost of bearing other people's sin.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

When this word's uses are traced across Scripture, they lead to a surprising place. Throughout the Old Testament, God's appearance is fire on the mountain, fire over the tabernacle, and fire and lightning around the throne in Ezekiel's vision. The Servant's appearance is a face marred beyond recognition. The New Testament joins the two. The Son of God, "being found in appearance as a man, He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross" (Philippians 2:8). That verse uses a different, Greek word, but it describes the same movement downward. Heaven's final vision keeps the paradox. John looked toward the throne and saw "a Lamb as though it had been slain" (Revelation 5:6). The wounds remain visible in glory.

The Gospels supply another quiet fulfillment. Under the Law, the priest examined the appearance of a leper's skin and could only pronounce him clean or unclean. When a leper knelt before Jesus, He reached out, touched him, and made him clean (Mark 1:40–41). The Law could tell what a disease looked like. Jesus is the King of Isaiah 11, who sees past appearance and has power to change what He sees. The lampstand made according to the "pattern" shown on the mountain (Numbers 8:4) points the same direction. Hebrews calls that tabernacle "the copy and shadow of the heavenly things" (Hebrews 8:5). The visible forms of worship pointed beyond themselves to a reality fulfilled in Christ.

7. Spirit & Life

This word asks believers to retrain their eyes. David's eyes on the roof, Samuel's eyes at Jesse's house, and Israel's eyes looking at the Servant all went wrong in the same way: they trusted what looked impressive or desirable. Many of us still do this with people, with churches, and with our own spiritual progress. We rank by looks, gifts, and visible success. God chose a shepherd boy, and He saved the world through a Man whose face people turned away from. If we cannot see glory in a cross, we will keep missing God where He is most clearly at work.

The same lesson calls for honest watchfulness. Ecclesiastes 11:9 does not tell the young to close their eyes. It tells them to remember that God will judge where their eyes lead them. Guarding what we look at is part of loving Him.

Daniel's experience also offers comfort. When heaven's reality left him without strength, God did not leave him lying on the ground; a touch in the likeness of a man lifted him up. In Christ, God has done this for us fully. Paul says God has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of His glory in the face of Jesus Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6). The fire Ezekiel could only describe as an appearance of a likeness has become a face we can know.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:9 — God made every tree "pleasant to the sight." Beauty belongs to His good creation before it ever becomes a temptation.

  • Exodus 3:3 — Moses turned aside to see the "great sight" of the bush that burned without burning up. His curiosity led him to the place where God called him.

  • Joshua 22:10 — The eastern tribes built an "impressive" altar by the Jordan. The other tribes misread it as rebellion and nearly went to war before they learned its true purpose (22:11–34).

  • Song of Solomon 2:14 — The beloved longs to see the other's face, because it is lovely. Seeing someone can be an act of delight and love.

  • Ezekiel 43:3 — Ezekiel says the vision of God's glory returning to the temple was like the earlier visions he had seen. The glory that departed in judgment returns to dwell with His people.
