HEALING, HEALTH, REMEDY - (marpêʼ, Strong's H4832)

Summary

The Hebrew noun marpêʼ means healing, health, cure, or remedy. It describes the condition of something that has been made whole again, and Scripture applies it to a sick body, a troubled heart, a strained relationship, and a nation broken under God's judgment. English versions translate it in several ways: "health," "healing," "remedy," "wholesome," "sound," and once, in a conflict with a ruler, "conciliation."

The word gathers in three places. In Proverbs, wise and gentle words bring health to those who hear them. In the history and prophecy of Judah, the word often appears in the negative, as in "no remedy," describing people who refused God's warnings until nothing could be done for them. In the prophets, God promises the healing His people cannot produce for themselves, and the Old Testament ends with "the Sun of Righteousness" rising "with healing in His wings" (Malachi 4:2). Taken together, the word moves from a hopeless diagnosis to a Healer who comes in person.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Marpêʼ is a noun built from the common Hebrew verb for healing, rāphāʼ, the verb behind God's self-description as the LORD who heals His people. The noun names the result of that action: the cure, the restored health, the remedy.

It occurs sixteen times in fifteen verses, all of them in 2 Chronicles, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Jeremiah, and Malachi. Half of these occurrences are in Proverbs. The rest sit at turning points in Israel's story: the collapse of Judah before Babylon, Jeremiah's laments and promises, and the last chapter of Malachi.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its center, marpêʼ is the state of being healed or the means by which healing comes. Context determines which aspect is in view.

1. Health or soundness. This is the restored, life-giving condition of a person, including body and inner life, as when a sound heart gives life to the body (Proverbs 14:30).

2. Cure or remedy. This is what heals. It most often appears in the negative to say that no cure exists, as in "till there was no remedy" (2 Chronicles 36:16).

3. Calming or conciliation. This is a gentle, settling response that soothes anger: "conciliation pacifies great offenses" (Ecclesiastes 10:4). In this verse and a few others in Proverbs, the sense leans toward calmness and gentleness. Some scholars think a related Hebrew verb meaning "to relax" or "to let go" stands behind these uses.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Proverbs, marpêʼ is usually the fruit of wisdom. The father's words are health to the son's whole body (4:22). The tongue of the wise brings health where reckless words cut like a sword (12:18). A faithful messenger brings health (13:17), and pleasant words are health to the bones (16:24). The verses repeatedly connect healing with speech, so that what comes out of one person's mouth either wounds or restores another.

The most important grammatical pattern is negation. When the text says there is "no" marpêʼ, the word stops describing restoration and instead marks a point beyond restoration. This is how Proverbs speaks of the sudden ruin of the wicked (6:15; 29:1), how the Chronicler describes King Jehoram's fatal disease (2 Chronicles 21:18), and how he describes the fall of Jerusalem itself. In Jeremiah, the negation becomes a cry from the people themselves, who ask why there is no healing for them.

4. Theological Significance

The word shows that God regards sin as a sickness and Himself as the only cure. Proverbs speaks of health that follows from walking in God's wisdom. The historical books show what happens when that wisdom is refused again and again. Health and ruin are both treated as outcomes of how people respond to God's word.

The word also shows the shape of God's patience. Judgment that ends in "no remedy" arrives only after long and repeated mercy. Even then, God's last word in the prophets is a promise to heal. Healing here belongs to God's initiative. Judah could not produce it, ritual could not secure it, and optimism could not declare it into existence. God would give it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

2 Chronicles 36:16 and Proverbs 29:1 — when there is no remedy. The Chronicler's account of Judah's end begins with compassion. The LORD sent His messengers "rising up early and sending them, because He had compassion on His people" (36:15). The response was contempt: "they mocked the messengers of God, despised His words, and scoffed at His prophets, until the wrath of the LORD arose against His people, till there was no remedy" (36:16). The sequence carries the meaning. Warning after warning was itself the treatment, and each refusal hardened the patient further. Proverbs 29:1 applies the same pattern to the individual. The person who stiffens his neck after repeated correction will be broken suddenly, without remedy. The disease that proves fatal is the refusal to be corrected.

Jeremiah 8:15; 14:19; 33:6 — the cry and the promise. Jeremiah gives the word its most painful setting. The people say they looked for peace and for a time of health, and trouble came instead (8:15). Their prophets had promised exactly that kind of peace. In 14:19 the word occurs twice. The people ask God why He has struck them so that there is no healing, and they repeat that they waited for a time of healing and found trouble. They knew they were sick, but they could not cure themselves. Then, with Jerusalem under siege and Jeremiah in prison, God speaks over the same ruined city: He will bring it healing, heal its people, and reveal to them an abundance of peace and truth (33:6). The healing they had looked for and could not find, God promises to bring Himself, and He joins it to the true peace the false prophets had only claimed.

Malachi 4:2 — the Sun of Righteousness. The final occurrence comes as the Day of the LORD approaches, burning "like an oven" against the proud (4:1). For another group the same day brings something very different: "But to you who fear My name The Sun of Righteousness shall arise With healing in His wings; And you shall go out And grow fat like stall-fed calves." The wings are the spreading rays of the rising sun. Healing comes the way light comes at dawn, reaching everything it touches. The result is health and gladness, calves let out of the stall and leaping. Healing is now attached to a figure who rises, and Malachi connects His coming with the arrival of a forerunner like Elijah (4:5).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Following marpêʼ through the Old Testament traces the outline of the gospel. People who refuse God's word reach a state where there is no remedy. They discover they cannot heal themselves. God promises to heal them. In Malachi, that healing rises as a Person.

The New Testament picks up Malachi's dawn imagery. The angel says John the Baptist will go before the Lord "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17), which echoes Malachi's promise of an Elijah figure. At John's birth his father Zechariah praises God for "the tender mercy of our God, With which the Dayspring from on high has visited us; To give light to those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death, To guide our feet into the way of peace" (Luke 1:78–79). Luke does not quote Malachi 4:2, but the forerunner, the rising light, and the way of peace all point toward it. Jesus then says it plainly: "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12). His healing of bodies throughout the Gospels showed in visible form the deeper cure He came to bring, the forgiveness of sin and the life of God's new day. What Jeremiah promised to a ruined city arrives in a Savior, and the peace and truth God promised there are found in Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Second Chronicles 36 should be read with soberness. It does not describe a God who loses patience quickly. It shows a God who sent warnings early and often because He had compassion. The danger lies in how a heart grows used to refusing Him. Hebrews gives the same warning to believers: "exhort one another daily, while it is called 'Today,' lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin" (Hebrews 3:13). While God is still correcting us, the remedy is still being offered, and His rebuke is a mercy.

Jeremiah's lament teaches honesty. The people's question, "Why have You stricken us so that there is no healing for us?" (Jeremiah 14:19), is the point where real healing can begin, because it gives up the false peace that said all was well. God answers people who admit they are sick.

Those who have received healing from Christ become people through whom healing reaches others. Proverbs locates much of this in speech: "A wholesome tongue is a tree of life, But perverseness in it breaks the spirit" (Proverbs 15:4). The tree of life takes us back to Eden, and a gentle word can give someone a small taste of what was lost there. Ecclesiastes 10:4 brings this into a tense situation, an angry superior: "Do not leave your post; For conciliation pacifies great offenses." The wise servant does not storm out or fight back. His calm becomes a remedy. Paul gives the same counsel: "If it is possible, as much as depends on you, live peaceably with all men" (Romans 12:18). A believer's calm answer in a heated room is a small, real share in the healing the Sun of Righteousness brings.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Chronicles 21:18 — King Jehoram, who had led Judah into idolatry and murdered his brothers, is struck with a disease the NKJV calls "incurable." His fatal sickness pictures in one man what would later happen to the whole nation.

  • Proverbs 4:22 — The father's words are life to those who find them and health to all their flesh. Wisdom received into the heart reaches the whole person.

  • Proverbs 6:15 — The troublemaker who sows discord is broken suddenly, without remedy. Calamity comes on him without warning.

  • Proverbs 12:18 — Rash speech pierces like a sword, but the tongue of the wise brings health. The same organ can wound or heal.

  • Proverbs 14:30 — A sound heart is life to the body, while envy rots the bones. Inner peace and inner corruption both work their way outward.

  • Proverbs 16:24 — Pleasant words are like a honeycomb, sweet to the soul and health to the bones. Kindness nourishes people as well as pleasing them.
