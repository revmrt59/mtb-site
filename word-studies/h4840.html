SCENTED, SPICE - (merqâch, Strong's H4840)

Summary

The Hebrew word merqâch means a spice or perfume, and it carries the sense of fragrance that has been skillfully blended. It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 5:13. There the bride describes the man she loves: "His cheeks are like a bed of spices, Banks of scented herbs." The NKJV translates merqâch as "scented." The word belongs to the family of Hebrew terms for the perfumer's craft. That is the same craft God commanded for the holy anointing oil and incense of the tabernacle. Fine perfumes were costly, and they were usually associated with kings and the wealthy.

The bride says this while searching for a beloved she has lost. Others have asked her what makes him better than any other man, and she answers by describing his worth feature by feature. The Church has long read the Song as a picture of Christ and His people. Read that way, the fragrance of the Bridegroom's face expresses His supreme worth and the delight of His nearness. His lips, which drip with myrrh, point to words that give life. The New Testament takes up the same fragrance language. It speaks of Christ's self-giving as "a sweet-smelling aroma" to God and calls believers "the fragrance of Christ."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Merqâch is a Hebrew noun related to the verb râqach (H7543), which describes blending spices and oils. This is the work of a perfumer or apothecary. Hebrew uses words from this family for the "art of the perfumer" (Exodus 30:25) and for the person who practices it.

In the ancient world, spices such as myrrh, aloes and cinnamon were carried long distances and sold at high prices. Blending them well took skill. Song of Solomon is a poem celebrating love between a bride and her bridegroom. It is full of gardens, spices and royal imagery, and it uses that language to express how precious each lover is to the other.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Merqâch has one sense:

1. A spice, perfume or aromatic preparation: fragrance associated with the perfumer's skill rather than simply wild plants. In Song of Solomon 5:13, the bride compares her beloved's cheeks to "banks of scented herbs."

The exact image in the phrase is debated. The word the NKJV renders "banks" can also mean "towers." For that reason, some translators picture raised garden beds and others picture heaped-up perfumes. Either way, the picture is the same: an abundance of rich fragrance.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The only occurrence of merqâch sits inside a carefully built portrait. In Song of Solomon 5:10–16, the bride praises her beloved from head to foot. She begins with his head and hair and then moves to his eyes, cheeks, lips, hands and legs.

The verse pairs two similar pictures, "a bed of spices" and "banks of scented herbs." The second phrase reinforces and heightens the first. The verse then moves straight to his lips, "Dripping liquid myrrh." Because the fragrance belongs to his face and mouth, it is the fragrance of someone near enough to speak with. The bride is describing a man she knows up close.

4. Theological Significance

Because this word comes from the perfumer's world, it links the Song's language of delight to the language of the sanctuary. In Exodus 30:25, God commanded Moses to make "a holy anointing oil, an ointment compounded according to the art of the perfumer." The sacred incense was also to be blended by the perfumer's skill (Exodus 30:35). God then forbade anyone to copy these blends for ordinary use.

The Song does not call the beloved's face holy. Still, it draws on the vocabulary God used for the fragrance of His own dwelling. This tells us something about God. He is the Maker of fragrance and the One who takes delight in it. He set apart costly, carefully blended aromas for His presence. He also gave the gift of love between husband and wife and made it rich with delight. Scripture does not set holiness against delight. When the bride takes pleasure in her beloved, she uses words that also belong to worship.

The setting adds another layer. The fragrant beds of 5:13 recall the garden of Song 4:14–16, which is filled with "all the chief spices." In that passage the bride calls on the winds to blow on her garden "that its spices may flow out." Genesis 3:18 describes the ground under the curse: "Both thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you." The Song pictures the opposite: a cultivated, fragrant place where love is welcomed and enjoyed. This image does not mean that every spice in Scripture points back to Eden. It does reveal God's purpose to restore what sin spoiled, so that fellowship once again bears fruit and gives off fragrance.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:13 in its setting. The verse belongs to one of the most moving scenes in the Song. In 5:2–8, the bride hesitates when her beloved knocks at night. When she finally opens the door, he is gone. She searches the city, is beaten by the watchmen, and begs the daughters of Jerusalem to tell him she is "lovesick." They respond by asking what makes her beloved better than any other (5:9). Verse 13 is part of her answer.

This setting matters. The bride praises him while she is missing him. She does not wait until she has found him again before she speaks of his worth. Her memory of his face, fragrant and costly, keeps her searching. She ends her description by declaring that he is wholly lovely (5:16).

The value of the fragrance. Elsewhere the Song connects fragrance with a person's name and reputation. In Song of Solomon 1:3, the bride says, "Your name is ointment poured forth; Therefore the virgins love you." Psalm 45, a wedding song for a king, describes his robes this way: "All Your garments are scented with myrrh and aloes and cassia, Out of the ivory palaces" (Psalm 45:8). Rich perfume belonged to royal splendor. When the bride uses merqâch for her beloved's cheeks, she is saying he is a treasure beyond anyone else. The Church's long tradition of reading the Song as a picture of Christ and His people finds its center here. The Bridegroom is supremely worthy, and His presence satisfies.

Cheeks and lips. In the second half of verse 13, the fragrance moves from his cheeks to his lips, which are "Dripping liquid myrrh." The fragrance is connected to breath and speech. The bride treasures what this man says as much as how he looks.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song does not name Christ, and the New Testament never quotes 5:13. Even so, the fragrance imagery of the Bible's story comes together in Him.

The perfumer's art was first given for the anointing oil and the incense of God's dwelling. In Christ, God came to dwell with His people in person. Christ's offering of Himself is described in fragrance language: Christ "has loved us and given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma" (Ephesians 5:2).

A further connection deserves careful attention, and it is drawn as an analogy rather than stated by the text. Spices are crushed and ground before they are blended, because that is how their fragrance is released. The cheeks the bride praises as fragrant can be set beside the Servant's words in Isaiah 50:6: "I gave My back to those who struck Me, And My cheeks to those who plucked out the beard; I did not hide My face from shame and spitting." In the ancient world, pulling out a man's beard was an act of deep humiliation. The face that is the Church's delight was struck and shamed. Out of that crushing came the fragrant offering of Ephesians 5:2. The thorns of Genesis 3:18 appear again at the cross, pressed onto His head, so that the cursed ground could become a garden again.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride's example teaches believers to dwell on the worth of Christ even when He seems far away. She was hurt and searching when she described her beloved's face. Christians also pass through seasons when they cannot sense the Lord's nearness. In those seasons, the right response is to recall what Scripture shows of Him: His mercy, His truth, His holiness, His self-giving. Speaking of His worth, as she did, strengthens desire for Him rather than letting it fade.

The pairing of fragrant cheeks with lips dripping myrrh also points to His word. Jesus said, "The words that I speak to you are spirit, and they are life" (John 6:63). His people come close to Him by receiving what He says. That fragrance does not stay with the Bridegroom. Paul writes that God "through us diffuses the fragrance of His knowledge in every place," and that "we are to God the fragrance of Christ" (2 Corinthians 2:14–15). Those who spend time near Christ begin to carry something of His fragrance into ordinary places.

Finally, the fragrance came at a cost. If His beauty came through His being struck, then delight in Christ and gratitude for the cross belong together. Believers love Him as the Lord who was crushed for them, not only as someone attractive to admire.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Merqâch occurs only in Song of Solomon 5:13, which is discussed above. Related fragrance passages that use other Hebrew words, such as Exodus 30:25, Psalm 45:8 and Song of Solomon 4:14–16, are treated in Sections 4 and 5.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7543 (râqach), lexical relative: the verb meaning to blend or prepare spices and perfumes. Words from this family describe the perfumer's art in Exodus 30:25.

  • H1314 (besem), canonical pairing: the common word for spice, used for the garden's spices in the Song (compare Song of Solomon 4:14–16). It is a different word from merqâch, but it belongs to the same fragrant imagery.
