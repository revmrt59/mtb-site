MASSA - (Massâʼ, Strong's H4854)

Summary

Massa was the seventh-listed of the twelve sons of Ishmael, which makes him a grandson of Abraham through Hagar the Egyptian. Scripture names him only twice, both times in the family record of Ishmael (Genesis 25:14; 1 Chronicles 1:30). Like his brothers, he gave his name to a people, one of the "twelve princes according to their nations" who settled in the deserts of northern Arabia (Genesis 25:16). His name is spelled exactly like the Hebrew word massa, which means "burden" or "load," something lifted and carried. The name probably carries that meaning. Scripture never explains why it was given, and it attaches no lesson to it.

A single name in a list can still tell us something about God. Years before Massa was born, God promised Abraham that Ishmael would father twelve princes and become a great nation (Genesis 17:20). Massa's place in that list of twelve shows that God kept His word to the son who was sent away with his mother. Some scholars also propose that Agur and King Lemuel, whose words close the Book of Proverbs, came from this Ishmaelite people. That reading depends on a separately indexed Hebrew word and remains debated. If it is correct, the God of Abraham allowed the descendants of Ishmael to contribute to the wisdom of His Word.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Massa (H4854) is one individual, the seventh-listed son of Ishmael. The full order of the brothers is Nebajoth, Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam, Mishma, Dumah, Massa, Hadar, Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah (Genesis 25:13–15). The Chronicler repeats the list, writing "Hadad" where Genesis has "Hadar" (1 Chronicles 1:30). That small difference in spelling is common in parallel genealogies and does not change Massa's position. Massa belongs to the generation after Ishmael; he was a cousin of Jacob and Esau. Genesis ties his family directly to the promise God made about Ishmael.

The Hebrew name Massâʼ has the same spelling as the common noun massa (H4853). That noun comes from the verb nasa, "to lift up" or "to carry," and it means a burden, a load carried, or, in prophetic books, a weighty "oracle" that a prophet lifts up and delivers. Because the name and the noun are identical in form, the name probably means "burden" or "load." This is a likely meaning, not a certainty. The Bible offers no wordplay on Massa's name and gives no account of why he received it. Readers should not treat "burden" as a description of his character or his life.

The name later belonged to a people as well as to a man. Genesis 25:16 describes Ishmael's sons as heads of "their towns and their settlements, twelve princes according to their nations." Massa therefore stands for both a person and the North Arabian tribe that traced its descent from him. A royal inscription of the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser III lists a people called Mas'a together with Tema, another name in Ishmael's line. Identifying that people with Massa's descendants is a scholarly proposal, and it fits the way Genesis groups Ishmael's sons.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Scripture tells no story about Massa himself. His appearance in the Bible is entirely genealogical. The setting of that genealogy still matters. The record opens, "Now this is the genealogy of Ishmael, Abraham's son, whom Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah's maidservant, bore to Abraham" (Genesis 25:12). It comes right after Abraham's burial, when Isaac and Ishmael stood together at their father's grave (Genesis 25:9). Before Genesis turns to Isaac's line and the story of Jacob, it pauses to record Ishmael's sons by name.

The pause is deliberate. God had promised Hagar in the wilderness that her descendants would be too many to count (Genesis 16:10). He had told Abraham that Ishmael would "beget twelve princes" (Genesis 17:20). When Hagar and the boy were sent away, He repeated that He would make Ishmael a great nation (Genesis 21:13, 18). Genesis 25:12–16 shows those words fulfilled. Twelve sons are listed, and the passage ends by calling them "twelve princes according to their nations."

Centuries later, after the exile, the Chronicler copied the same names into his record of the nations descended from Abraham (1 Chronicles 1:28–31). By then Israel was a small, chastened community trying to understand its place in God's purposes. The Chronicler began with Adam and included Ishmael's sons, so the record of Israel opened with the whole human family, Massa among them.

A related name in Proverbs. Two verses in Proverbs use the separately indexed noun massa (H4853). The NKJV renders them: "The words of Agur the son of Jakeh, his utterance" (Proverbs 30:1), and "The words of King Lemuel, the utterance which his mother taught him" (Proverbs 31:1). The word translated "utterance" is spelled like Massa. Some scholars and some translations, such as the RSV, read it here as the place name, making Agur a man "of Massa" and Lemuel "king of Massa." The NKJV keeps the meaning "utterance." These verses are not counted as occurrences of H4854. Their connection to the tribe of Massa is a scholarly proposal, not a settled fact, but it is worth knowing.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

What Scripture states plainly is that Massa is one of the twelve princes God promised to Ishmael, and that God kept that promise. The main line of the story runs through Isaac, but the Bible does not quietly drop Ishmael. It names his sons one by one. God's word to a frightened slave woman in the desert carried the same weight as His word to Abraham, and Massa's name is part of the evidence.

A careful implication follows. God's faithfulness is not limited to the people through whom He chose to bring redemption. He made particular promises to Ishmael out of mercy, and He fulfilled them exactly. The line of promise remains Isaac's (Genesis 17:21), and Ishmael's blessing is a different kind of blessing. Both, however, rest on the same trustworthy God.

If Agur and Lemuel were Massaites, the significance reaches further. Proverbs would then close with wisdom from outside Israel that has been brought under the fear of the Lord. Agur confesses that he lacks understanding and asks who has gone up to heaven or gathered the wind in His fists (Proverbs 30:2–4). Lemuel's mother teaches her son to stay sober and to speak up for the poor and needy (Proverbs 31:4–9). This pattern appears elsewhere in Scripture. Melchizedek, Jethro, and Job all show non-Israelites who knew and honored the true God. Even so, the Massaite authorship of Proverbs 30–31 should be presented as a possibility and not as something the text directly says.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No New Testament passage mentions Massa, and nothing in Scripture makes him a type of Christ. The connections that do exist are canonical and thematic.

The prophets show interest in Ishmael's descendants. Isaiah speaks of the flocks of Kedar and the rams of Nebaioth, two of Massa's brothers, being brought to the Lord's altar in the day of Zion's glory (Isaiah 60:7). Massa is not named there, but his family is drawn into the hope of nations coming to worship Israel's God. Revelation completes the picture: "And the nations of those who are saved shall walk in its light, and the kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into it" (Revelation 21:24). This is thematic resonance, not a prophecy about Massa. Still, it fits the gospel's reach. In Christ, the descendants of Ishmael are welcome alongside the descendants of Isaac (Galatians 3:28–29).

If Proverbs 30–31 does preserve Massaite wisdom, it also points forward to Christ. In Him "are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3), and Paul calls Him "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24). Agur's honest question about who has ascended to heaven, and what His Son's name is, finds its answer in the One who came down from heaven (John 3:13). The New Testament does not link that answer to Massa, but the question is in the canon and Christ answers it.

5. Spirit & Life

Massa's name teaches us that God does not forget what He has said. Hagar had been used and then sent away, and God spoke to her twice in the wilderness. Generations later, twelve princes stood as proof that He meant it. A believer who feels overlooked can take this seriously. The God who keeps His smaller promises to Ishmael will certainly keep His greater promises in Christ (2 Corinthians 1:20).

If Agur belonged to Massa, his prayer adds a further lesson. He asks God to keep falsehood far from him and to give him neither poverty nor riches, only the food he needs. He fears that wealth might lead him to deny the Lord and that poverty might tempt him to steal (Proverbs 30:7–9). True wisdom, wherever it appears, begins with this kind of humble dependence on God.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 16:10; 17:20; 21:13, 18 — God's promises to Hagar and Abraham that Ishmael would become a great nation and father twelve princes.

  • Genesis 25:12–16 — The genealogy of Ishmael, naming Massa seventh and calling the sons "twelve princes according to their nations."

  • 1 Chronicles 1:28–31 — The post-exilic record that repeats Massa among Abraham's descendants.

  • Proverbs 30:1; 31:1 — The separately indexed word massa (H4853), which some scholars read as a reference to the tribe of Massa.

  • Isaiah 60:7; Revelation 21:24 — Ishmael's descendants and the nations brought to worship God in the final hope of Scripture.
