DRAW, PROLONG, EXTEND - (mâshak, Strong's H4900)

Summary

The Hebrew verb mâshak means to draw, in the sense of pulling something toward yourself or stretching it out. It is an ordinary working word. Brothers pull Joseph up out of a pit, a soldier draws back a bowstring, priests sound a long blast on the ram's horn, and a farmer draws seed out of his bag as he sows. The same word can also stretch time. Hope can be "deferred," God can have "patience" for many years, and a psalmist can ask the Lord to "continue" His lovingkindness.

The word becomes most precious when God is the one drawing. "With lovingkindness I have drawn you," the Lord says through Jeremiah (Jeremiah 31:3). Through Hosea He says, "I drew them with gentle cords, with bands of love" (Hosea 11:4). Against that tender pull Isaiah sets people who harness themselves to their own sin "as if with a cart rope" (Isaiah 5:18). The word therefore asks every reader two questions: what is pulling you, and which way? In the New Testament, Jesus declares that when He is lifted up He "will draw all peoples to Myself" (John 12:32).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mâshak belongs to the everyday life of ancient Israel. People used it on the farm, on the battlefield, at the well, and in worship. Its picture is simple: something is grasped and pulled, or something is drawn out to greater length. The word appears 36 times, from Genesis to Amos. It is spread across narrative, poetry, wisdom, and prophecy, so readers meet it in rescue stories, battle reports, prayers, and prophetic warnings.

Because the word is so ordinary, its meaning in any passage depends on two things: who is doing the pulling, and what is being pulled. A rope in the hands of a rescuer means one thing. A net in the hands of a predator means something very different.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To pull, drag, or draw physically. Examples include pulling Joseph from the pit (Genesis 37:28), drawing a bow (1 Kings 22:34), dragging a victim into a net (Psalm 10:9), and leading troops out to battle, which the NKJV renders "deploy" (Judges 4:6–7).

2. To draw out or lengthen, whether in sound, size, or time. The word describes a "long" horn blast (Exodus 19:13), a people "tall" in stature (Isaiah 18:2), patience stretched over years (Nehemiah 9:30), and hope "deferred" (Proverbs 13:12).

3. To draw a person along, attracting or leading someone toward oneself. Examples are the bride's plea "Draw me away!" (Song of Solomon 1:4) and God's drawing of His people (Jeremiah 31:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines the sense of the word. At Sinai the horn "sounds long" as the signal that the people may come near the mountain (Exodus 19:13). At Jericho the priests "make a long blast with the ram's horn," and the walls fall (Joshua 6:5). In both scenes the drawn-out sound marks the moment God acts.

The word often takes on a predatory tone. The wicked man "lies in wait secretly, as a lion in his den" and catches the poor "when he draws him into his net" (Psalm 10:9). Scripture turns that image back on the powerful: "God draws the mighty away with His power" (Job 24:22). David prays, "Do not take me away with the wicked" (Psalm 28:3). In that prayer the verb pictures being dragged off to the judgment that falls on evildoers.

4. Theological Significance

When Scripture applies mâshak to God's character, the word shows a Lord who lengthens His mercy. Nehemiah's prayer confesses, "Yet for many years You had patience with them, and testified against them by Your Spirit in Your prophets" (Nehemiah 9:30). God's patience was drawn out year after year, and it was never silent, because He kept sending prophets with His warnings. David asks for the same lengthening of grace: "Oh, continue Your lovingkindness to those who know You" (Psalm 36:10). The opposite prayer appears in Psalm 85:5: "Will You prolong Your anger to all generations?" God's people know which of His works they long to see extended.

The word is also paired with lovingkindness (chesed, God's loyal, faithful love). In Psalm 36:10 David asks God to draw it out. In Jeremiah 31:3 God draws His people with it. Psalm 109:12 shows the dark reverse, where the curse falls on a man for whom there is "none to extend mercy." To withhold drawn-out mercy is to leave someone abandoned.

The word's most ordinary uses also show God's providence. The brothers "pulled Joseph up and lifted him out of the pit" to sell him (Genesis 37:28). Their hands were full of malice, yet God used that pull to bring Joseph to Egypt and later to save the family. A soldier "drew a bow at random, and struck the king of Israel between the joints of his armor" (1 Kings 22:34). The archer aimed at no one in particular, yet the arrow carried out God's announced judgment on Ahab. People pull the rope and the string, and God directs where the pulling ends.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Jeremiah 31:3, Hosea 11:4, and Song of Solomon 1:4: the God who draws. Jeremiah speaks to a people broken by exile. God's word to them begins with the oldest fact about them: "I have loved you with an everlasting love; therefore with lovingkindness I have drawn you." The word "therefore" carries the point. God's drawing grows out of His everlasting love and depends on nothing His people did to earn it.

Hosea supplies the picture. Israel is like a young work animal, and God handles it as a kind farmer would: "I drew them with gentle cords, with bands of love, and I was to them as those who take the yoke from their neck. I stooped and fed them." He loosens the yoke, bends down, and feeds the animal. The God who pulls His people toward Himself also lowers Himself to care for them. He does all this for a nation that kept turning away.

Song of Solomon 1:4 gives the answering voice: "Draw me away! We will run after you." The beloved does not try to run first and hope to be noticed. She asks to be drawn, and running follows. The order is the same as in Jeremiah and Hosea. Love draws first, and the loved one responds with eager desire.

Isaiah 5:18: the other rope. "Woe to those who draw iniquity with cords of vanity, and sin as if with a cart rope." Isaiah pictures people who have put themselves in harness like oxen and are dragging a heavy load of sin behind them. The next verse shows their attitude. They mock God, daring Him to hurry up and do His work so they can see it (Isaiah 5:19). The cords are "vanity," meaning empty and worthless, yet they bind as tightly as any rope. Hosea's gentle cords and Isaiah's cart rope are the two harnesses this word offers. God's cords lift the yoke, while sin's rope only adds weight.

Ezekiel 12:25, 28 and Proverbs 13:12: delay and certainty. Proverbs states plainly what waiting does to a person: "Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but when the desire comes, it is a tree of life." In Ezekiel's day, rebellious Israel turned delay into an excuse. They told themselves that the days dragged on and that the prophets' visions came to nothing (Ezekiel 12:22). God answered with this same verb: "the word which I speak will come to pass; it will no more be postponed" (Ezekiel 12:25). He repeats it in verse 28. God may draw out His patience, but His people may not mistake that patience for forgetfulness. When He says the delay is over, it is over.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament teaches that God draws His people with love, and the Gospel shows where that drawing leads. Jesus said, "And I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all peoples to Myself" (John 12:32). John's Gospel is written in Greek, so the Hebrew mâshak does not appear there. Still, the movement matches Hosea and Jeremiah closely. The cross is where God's bands of love take their fullest form. The drawing now reaches beyond Israel to "all peoples."

Hosea's farmer who removes the yoke points ahead to Jesus' invitation for the weary to come to Him and take His yoke, which is easy and light (Matthew 11:28–30). The one who dragged sin like a cart rope is offered a different harness.

The word's future uses add color to this hope. Amos looks to restoration days when "the treader of grapes [shall overtake] him who sows seed" (Amos 9:13). The sower drawing seed from his bag is overtaken by the harvest, because God's abundance arrives faster than the work can keep up. The long horn blasts at Sinai and Jericho also find a resonance, though not a stated fulfillment, in "the trumpet of God" at Christ's return (1 Thessalonians 4:16). That will be the final moment when God comes near and death's walls fall.

7. Spirit & Life

Every life is being pulled by something. Isaiah's scoffers felt free while they strained against a cart rope. The person God draws is the one who is truly freed, because God's cords take the yoke off. When temptation feels like strength, Isaiah 5:18 shows what is actually happening: a person is hauling the load that will crush them.

The Song's order is good news for anyone who feels too slow to follow Christ. "Draw me away! We will run after you." We can ask to be drawn before we have the strength to run. This is the right order, because Jeremiah says God's drawing began with "an everlasting love."

Nehemiah 9:30 adds a sober note. God had patience "for many years," and yet the people "would not listen." Drawn-out mercy is time given for returning to God, and it is not permission to stay away. Proverbs 13:12 shows that Scripture understands how heavy waiting can be, and Ezekiel 12:25 shows that God's word does not fade during the wait. In the meantime, believers can pray David's prayer with confidence: "Oh, continue Your lovingkindness to those who know You."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 12:21 — Moses tells the elders to "pick out and take lambs" for the Passover. The word here describes drawing out the lamb chosen for the night of deliverance.

  • Jeremiah 38:13 — Jeremiah is pulled up from the dungeon by ropes. Ebed-Melech's rescue echoes the pulling of Joseph from his pit, but this time the hands are merciful.

  • Job 41:1 — God asks whether Job can "draw out Leviathan with a hook." What humans cannot pull, God rules.

  • Deuteronomy 21:3 — The elders take a heifer "which has not pulled with a yoke." The animal is set apart because it has never been harnessed for work.

  • Isaiah 13:22 — Babylon's days "will not be prolonged." God sets the length of the time given to proud empires.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2617 (chesed), lovingkindness or mercy — a canonical pairing, not a lexical relative. It is joined with mâshak in Psalm 36:10, Jeremiah 31:3, and Psalm 109:12.

  • G1670 (helkō), to draw — a thematic, cross-language parallel. The Greek verb is used in John 12:32 for Christ drawing all peoples to Himself.
