MESHECH - (Meshek, Strong's H4902)

Summary

Meshech first appears in Scripture as a son of Japheth and grandson of Noah (Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5). It then becomes the name of the people descended from him, a distant northern nation usually paired with Tubal. The meaning of the name is uncertain. It sounds like a Hebrew verb meaning "to draw" or "to drag," and some have connected it with drawing a bow. It is more likely the Hebrew form of a foreign people's name, so no meaning should be pressed. One further occurrence, 1 Chronicles 1:17, lists a Meshech in Shem's line who appears as "Mash" in Genesis 10:23. He should be kept separate from Japheth's son.

Israel never fought Meshech in any recorded battle, yet the name carries real weight in Scripture. Ezekiel remembers Meshech as a people who traded "human lives and vessels of bronze" (Ezekiel 27:13) and spread terror in the land of the living before going down to the grave (Ezekiel 32:26). In Ezekiel 38–39, Meshech stands with Gog in a great coalition from the far north against God's restored people, and the LORD Himself destroys it so that the nations will know Him. Revelation 20:8 takes up this "Gog and Magog" picture for the final rebellion against Christ's people. In Psalm 120 the pilgrim cries, "Woe is me, that I dwell in Meshech," and gives words to every believer who longs for peace while living among people who want war.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The lemma Meshech refers to two individuals and one people group, and they should be kept distinct.

Most passages concern Meshech, son of Japheth, and his descendants. He is listed among Japheth's sons with Gomer, Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, and Tiras (Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5). From him came the nation of Meshech, which appears in Psalms and Ezekiel. Scripture places this people in the far north from Israel's point of view and almost always pairs it with Tubal. Scholars commonly identify Meshech with a people known in ancient Assyrian and Greek sources, located in the region of Anatolia, modern Turkey, and later farther north toward the Black Sea. That identification is widely held. The details of where they lived and how they moved are still debated.

Another man named Meshech appears in a passage whose wording differs from the parallel account. 1 Chronicles 1:17 reads, "The sons of Shem were Elam, Asshur, Arphaxad, Lud, Aram, Uz, Hul, Gether, and Meshech." The parallel list in Genesis says, "The sons of Aram were Uz, Hul, Gether, and Mash" (Genesis 10:23). Chronicles compresses Shem's and Aram's sons into one list, and the name in the Aram position, which corresponds to Mash in Genesis, appears as Meshech. Mash is indexed separately (H4851). This Semitic Meshech is a different person from Japheth's son. The difference in form most likely reflects a copying variation or an assimilation to the better-known name. Scripture says nothing more about him.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. The Hebrew spelling (מֶשֶׁךְ) matches a root meaning "to draw, drag, or stretch out." Some have linked this to archery, as in "drawing" a bow. Because Meshech was a foreign people known by their own name in other ancient records, the name is better understood as a Hebrew rendering of a non-Hebrew name than as a Hebrew word with a fixed meaning. Scripture builds no wordplay on it, and nothing about Meshech's role should be read out of the sound of the name.

The NKJV wording in Ezekiel 38:2–3, "the prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal," reflects a translation debate. Other versions read "chief prince of Meshech and Tubal." Popular claims that Meshech means Moscow or that Rosh means Russia rest on similar sounds, not on biblical or historical evidence, and should not be taught as settled.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Among the nations after the flood (Genesis 10; 1 Chronicles 1). Meshech enters the Bible in the Table of Nations, the record of how Noah's descendants spread over the earth. Japheth's line is described as the peoples who spread to the coastlands and distant regions (Genesis 10:5). Meshech belongs to that wider world, far from the land where God would later plant Abraham's family. Even so, the list shows that every one of these peoples traces back to Noah and stands within God's view of the whole human family.

A byword for hostility (Psalm 120:5). By the time of the Psalms, Meshech had become shorthand for a harsh, foreign place. The psalmist cries, "Woe is me, that I dwell in Meshech, That I dwell among the tents of Kedar!" Kedar was a desert people to the south and east, and Meshech lay far to the north. Because the two were so widely separated, many read the verse as a poetic picture rather than a literal address: the psalmist names distant, harsh peoples to say that wherever he turns he is surrounded by people hostile to God's ways. The following verses explain the complaint. He has lived too long with those who hate peace. He is for peace, but when he speaks, they are for war (Psalm 120:6–7). Earlier in the psalm he prays for deliverance from lying lips and a deceitful tongue (Psalm 120:2). Psalm 120 opens the Songs of Ascents, the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem (Psalms 120–134). The journey to the house of the LORD begins in "Meshech."

Traders in people (Ezekiel 27:13). In Ezekiel's lament over the wealthy port of Tyre, Meshech appears among Tyre's business partners: "Javan, Tubal, and Meshech were your traders. They bartered human lives and vessels of bronze for your merchandise." The region was known for metalwork, and Ezekiel records that its commerce included human beings. Ezekiel does not condemn Meshech separately here, but he names the trade, and Scripture lets the detail stand.

Down to the Pit (Ezekiel 32:26). In a vision of Egypt's descent to the realm of the dead, Ezekiel sees the fallen powers already there: "There are Meshech and Tubal and all their multitudes, With all their graves around it, All of them uncircumcised, slain by the sword, Though they caused their terror in the land of the living." Peoples who once frightened others now lie among the slain. "Uncircumcised" marks them as outside the people of God, and "slain by the sword" shows that their violence ended in their own violent fall.

Gog's coalition (Ezekiel 38–39). Meshech's most prominent role comes in Ezekiel's prophecy against Gog. The LORD tells the prophet, "Son of man, set your face against Gog, of the land of Magog, the prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal, and prophesy against him" (Ezekiel 38:2). Then He says, "Behold, I am against you, O Gog, the prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal" (38:3). The same address returns at 39:1. Gog's army also includes Persia, Ethiopia, and Libya, along with Gomer and Togarmah from the far north (38:5–6). Several of these names come straight from Japheth's line in Genesis 10. In the latter years this vast force comes against a people regathered from the nations and living securely in their land (38:8–12). God says He will turn Gog around, put hooks into his jaws, and lead him out (38:4). The invasion ends in earthquake, confusion, plague, and fire from heaven (38:19–22). Afterward, Israel spends seven months burying the dead and seven years burning the weapons, and the birds are called to feast on the fallen (39:9–20). The prophecy closes with God hiding His face no longer and pouring out His Spirit on the house of Israel (39:29). Ezekiel's vision of the new temple follows immediately (chapters 40–48).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states several things directly. Meshech descends from Noah through Japheth. It traded human lives and bronze. It spread terror and was brought down to the grave. In Ezekiel 38–39, God declares Himself against Gog and his allies and destroys them. Ezekiel states the purpose more than once: God will magnify and sanctify Himself and make Himself known in the eyes of many nations, so that they will know that He is the LORD (Ezekiel 38:16, 23; 39:7). Meshech's defeat is not mainly about geography or military power. It concerns the honor of God's name.

Several implications follow closely from the text. Meshech's story moves from a name in a family list, to a merchant in human lives, to a terror laid in the grave, to a member of Gog's great assault on God's people. Across these passages the name comes to stand for distant, powerful, hostile peoples who do not know the LORD. The hooks in the jaw show that even such a force is not acting on its own. Gog believes he is choosing to plunder a defenseless land (38:10–12), yet God is the one leading him out, and He leads him to judgment.

The order of Ezekiel's book matters too. The judgment of Gog's coalition, including Meshech, comes between the promise of new life for Israel (chapters 36–37) and the vision of God's dwelling among His people, a city whose name is "The LORD Is There" (48:35). Read in this literary order, the defeat of these hostile powers stands before the vision of God's permanent presence, though Ezekiel's sequence is not a stated timetable; the promise of God's Spirit and dwelling already comes in chapters 36–37. Ezekiel 32 and 38–39 also share an irony that fits God's justice. A people who traded in bronze vessels and in human beings (27:13) fall by the sword, and the weapons of Gog's army become firewood (39:9–10). Ezekiel does not say Meshech traded weapons, so the link between their trade and their fall is a limited analogy, not a stated connection.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest canonical development comes in Revelation. When the thousand years are ended, Satan is released "and will go out to deceive the nations which are in the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, to gather them together to battle, whose number is as the sand of the sea" (Revelation 20:8). Meshech is not named there, but John is drawing on Ezekiel's prophecy, in which Meshech stands at Gog's side. As in Ezekiel, the enemy surrounds God's people, "the camp of the saints and the beloved city," and fire comes down from heaven (Revelation 20:9). John places this revolt after the thousand years, and in his vision the old northern coalition represents the unbelieving world's final revolt against Christ. How Ezekiel's vision and John's relate in time is debated, but both end the same way, by God's direct act.

A second connection is a strong resonance rather than an explicit citation. Revelation 18, the lament over Babylon's fall, echoes Ezekiel's lament over Tyre in many ways. Merchants weep and sailors stand far off as the city's trade collapses. Babylon's cargo list ends with "bodies and souls of men" (Revelation 18:13). Ezekiel had recorded that Meshech and its partners "bartered human lives" (Ezekiel 27:13). In both places Scripture puts the buying and selling of people at the bottom of a proud economic system, and in both places God brings that system down. Christ's kingdom moves the other way. He did not purchase people as property. He gave His own life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45).

There is also hope in the larger story. Meshech belongs to the line of Japheth, the far-off peoples of Genesis 10. The gospel reaches exactly such people. Paul tells Gentile believers that they were once strangers to God's promises and far off, and that in Christ Jesus they have been brought near by His blood (Ephesians 2:12–13). Scripture announces judgment on Meshech as a hostile power. It does not say that every person descended from those nations is beyond the reach of grace.

5. Spirit & Life

Psalm 120 gives believers honest words for a real experience. The psalmist does not pretend that the people around him are kinder than they are. He feels the strain of living among liars and people who want conflict, and he says so to God: "Woe is me, that I dwell in Meshech." Christians know this strain. Peter calls believers sojourners and pilgrims (1 Peter 2:11), and Jesus prayed for disciples who are in the world but not of it (John 17:14–16). The answer in the psalm is not to become like Meshech. The psalmist stays "for peace" when others want war, and his complaint becomes the first step of a pilgrimage. The next psalms lift their eyes to the hills and to the LORD who keeps His people (Psalm 121). The pain of dwelling in Meshech is meant to turn the heart toward God's house.

Ezekiel 38–39 also steadies God's people when the powers of the world seem huge and far beyond their control. Gog's army comes "like a cloud, to cover the land" (paraphrasing 38:16), yet God holds the hooks. Nothing in the coalition moves outside His rule, and its end is already written. God's people in that vision do not save themselves. They are living securely because He has gathered them, and He fights for them. The goal of the whole conflict is that the nations will know the LORD, and that Israel will know Him as the God who no longer hides His face and who pours out His Spirit (39:29).

Ezekiel 27:13 puts a hard question to every age. Meshech treated human lives as merchandise, and Revelation shows Babylon doing the same. God sees the people who are bought and sold, and He judges the systems that trade in them. Christ also "bought" a people (1 Corinthians 6:19–20; Revelation 5:9), but not as a slave trader exploits them; He gave His own blood in costly, freely given love. To belong to Him is to value people as He does, as those made in God's image.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5 — Meshech as a son of Japheth and the ancestor of a northern people.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:17 (with Genesis 10:23) — A separate Meshech in Shem's line, called Mash in Genesis, who should not be merged with Japheth's son.

  • Psalm 120:5 — "Woe is me, that I dwell in Meshech." The pilgrim's lament among people who hate peace, at the start of the Songs of Ascents.

  • Ezekiel 27:13 — Meshech trades "human lives and vessels of bronze" with Tyre.

  • Ezekiel 32:26 — Meshech and Tubal, once a terror, lie among the uncircumcised slain.

  • Ezekiel 38:2–3; 39:1 — God sets Himself against Gog, "the prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal."

  • Ezekiel 38:4, 16, 23; 39:7, 29 — God leads the invader out, judges him, makes His holy name known, and pours out His Spirit.

  • Revelation 20:8 — The "Gog and Magog" coalition drawn from Ezekiel in the final rebellion.

  • Revelation 18:13 — Babylon's trade in "bodies and souls of men," echoing Ezekiel's record of trade in human lives.
