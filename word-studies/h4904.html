BED, BEDROOM, COUCH - (mishkâb, Strong's H4904)

Summary

The Hebrew word mishkâb means a bed or a place for lying down. It names the couch where people sleep, the sickbed where they suffer, the private bedroom where they speak freely, and, by a natural extension, the act of lying together in sexual union. Poets and prophets also use it for the grave, where the dead are laid to rest. Because the bed is where people are least guarded, Scripture often uses this word to show what is really happening in a human heart.

The word's most important uses gather around four themes. First, God sets holy boundaries around the marriage bed, and Reuben lost his place as firstborn for violating his father's bed. Second, the bed reveals the heart: the faithful are told to "meditate within your heart on your bed, and be still" (Psalm 4:4), while the wicked "work out evil on their beds" (Micah 2:1). Third, the prophets describe Israel's idolatry as a defiled bed, which shows God grieving as a betrayed Husband. Fourth, the bed is a place of weakness where the Lord sustains the sick, and for the righteous even death becomes rest. Taken together, these passages show that nothing done in private is hidden from God, and that He offers His presence in our most defenseless hours.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mishkâb is a Hebrew noun built from the common verb shakab, "to lie down." It refers to wherever a person lies down, whether a bed, a couch, or a whole bedchamber. In an ancient home the bed was the most private space a family had. It was the place of sleep, sickness, marriage, and personal conversation. That privacy explains why the word appears in so many different kinds of passages: purity laws in Leviticus, stories of royal intrigue in 2 Samuel, the night prayers of the Psalms, and the indictments of the prophets.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A bed or couch, the place where someone lies to sleep, rest, or recover. Ishbosheth was "lying on his bed at noon" (2 Samuel 4:5), and Exodus 21:18 speaks of an injured man confined to his bed.

2. A bedroom or inner chamber, the most private room in a house. The frogs of Egypt came "into your bedroom, on your bed" (Exodus 8:3).

3. The act of lying with someone sexually. The NKJV often renders this sense as "intimately," as in women who had "known a man intimately" (Numbers 31:17).

4. The resting place of the dead, used poetically. In Isaiah 57:2 the righteous rest in their beds, and in Ezekiel 32:25 Elam's bed lies among the slain.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What the word conveys depends heavily on the setting. In the Law it marks where purity and impurity meet daily life. In historical narrative the bed is usually where a person is exposed, whether to an assassin or to temptation. Wisdom and poetry turn the word inward, so that the bed becomes the place where thoughts, prayers, and plots come to the surface. The prophets then take the marriage bed as a picture of Israel's relationship with God and describe idol worship as unfaithfulness in that bed.

Sense 3 calls for a brief explanation. In Numbers 31 and Judges 21, the Hebrew phrase says that a woman had known "the lying of a man," and the NKJV renders it "known a man intimately." Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 use the same word to describe a man lying with a man "as with a woman." In these passages the word refers to the act itself, which is why English translations sometimes render it simply as "with" or "lies."

4. Theological Significance

The passages using mishkâb show that God's holiness reaches into the most private room of the house. The Law regulated what made a bed unclean. The prophets exposed sins committed in bedrooms, and the psalmists brought their night thoughts before the Lord. No hour or place in life is outside His concern.

These passages also show how God relates to His people. When Isaiah and Ezekiel picture idolatry as a defiled bed, God speaks as a wounded Husband, which is far more personal than a ruler punishing broken rules. The same God who grieves over betrayal also comes near to the sick and promises rest to the upright. Holiness and tenderness are both present wherever this word appears.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Reuben and the boundary of the bed (Genesis 49:4; Leviticus 18:22; 20:13). The word first appears in Jacob's dying words to Reuben. Jacob tells his firstborn that he will not excel, because he went up to his father's bed and defiled it. This refers back to Genesis 35:22, where Reuben lay with Bilhah, his father's concubine. A single act in a private place cost Reuben the honor of the firstborn. Leviticus later puts this principle into law, using the same word to draw firm lines around sexual relations. From the beginning, Scripture treats the bed as a place where faithfulness is kept or broken, with consequences for a whole family line. Hebrews 13:4 carries the same conviction into the church: "Marriage is honorable among all, and the bed undefiled."

The bed as the revealer of the heart (Psalm 4:4; 36:4; Micah 2:1; Hosea 7:14; Song of Songs 3:1). At night, with no work or audience to distract, the mind goes where it truly wants to go. David counsels, "Meditate within your heart on your bed, and be still" (Psalm 4:4). The wicked man does the opposite: "He devises wickedness on his bed" (Psalm 36:4). Micah sharpens the picture: they "work out evil on their beds! At morning light they practice it" (Micah 2:1). The plan made lying down becomes the act carried out at sunrise. Hosea shows a quieter failure. Israel wept in distress, yet "they did not cry out to Me with their heart when they wailed upon their beds" (Hosea 7:14). Their tears came from hardship and never turned into prayer. In Song of Songs 3:1 the bride's bed is a place of longing: "By night on my bed I sought the one I love." The bed brings to light what a person loves, fears, or schemes.

Two beds in one chapter (Isaiah 57:2, 7–8; Ezekiel 23:17). Isaiah 57 opens with the righteous man who dies while no one takes it to heart. Isaiah says that he enters into peace and that the upright rest in their beds (57:2, paraphrased). A few verses later a very different bed appears: "On a lofty and high mountain you have set your bed; even there you went up to offer sacrifice" (57:7). God continues, "You have enlarged your bed and made a covenant with them; you have loved their bed" (57:8). The hilltop shrine has become a lovers' bed, and Israel has made room in it for other gods. Ezekiel 23:17 applies the same picture to Jerusalem's alliances: "the Babylonians came to her, into the bed of love, and they defiled her." When Judah trusted foreign powers and took up their gods, God called it adultery. His anger is the anger of a husband whose wife has given herself to others. The chapter sets the two beds side by side. One is the quiet rest given to the faithful dead, and the other is the crowded bed of the unfaithful living.

Sustained in weakness (Psalm 41:3; Job 33:15, 19). Psalm 41 promises the person who considers the poor: "The LORD will strengthen him on his bed of illness; You will sustain him on his sickbed." The word mishkâb appears in the second line. The sick person cannot rise to meet the Lord, so the Lord comes to the place where the sick person lies. Job 33 shows God at work in the same setting. He speaks "when deep sleep falls upon men, while slumbering on their beds" (33:15), and a man "is also chastened with pain on his bed" (33:19). Elihu explains that God does this to turn a person from pride and keep his soul from the Pit (33:17–18). Even the hours a person spends lying weak are part of God's purpose to rescue.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Leviticus 15 uses mishkâb repeatedly. A person with a bodily discharge made every bed he or she lay on unclean, and whoever touched that bed became unclean too. Leviticus 15:26 applies this to a woman whose bleeding continued beyond its normal time. Her impurity spread to her bedding and from her bedding to others, so that even her place of rest passed on her condition.

Mark 5 tells of exactly such a woman, one who "had a flow of blood for twelve years." Under the Law her touch would pass on uncleanness. She touched Jesus' garment anyway, and "immediately the fountain of her blood was dried up," while Jesus knew "that power had gone out of Him" (Mark 5:29–30). The flow went the other way. Her uncleanness did not pass to Him, and His healing passed to her. What a bed could only pass on, Christ reverses. In Mark 2 a paralyzed man is lowered on his bed, and Jesus tells him to take it up and walk, so the bed that once carried him is now carried by him.

The prophets' picture of a betrayed Husband points ahead to Christ the Bridegroom, who loves an unfaithful people and makes them pure (Ephesians 5:25–27). Isaiah's image of the grave as a bed also grows fuller in the New Testament. Paul calls believers who have died those who "sleep in Jesus" and will rise with Him (1 Thessalonians 4:13–14, paraphrased). Jesus Himself lay in a tomb and rose, and for this reason the rest Isaiah promised to the upright now has a resurrection morning in view.

7. Spirit & Life

The bed asks every believer a searching question: where does your mind go when the lights are out? Micah's schemers show that sin is often planned lying down before it is practiced in the morning. Psalm 4:4 offers the opposite habit, which is to bring anger and anxiety to God on your bed and be still. The Lord listens there as well. "Let the saints be joyful in glory; let them sing aloud on their beds" (Psalm 149:5). A believer's bed can become a place of praise.

Hosea 7:14 adds a needed warning. It is possible to cry out in distress at night without ever crying out to God. Tears are not yet prayer. The difference lies in whether the heart turns toward Him.

For anyone confined by illness, Psalm 41:3 is personal. You do not have to rise to find the Lord, because He comes to the sickbed and holds you up there. Job 33 adds that God can use those weak hours to humble us and bring us back to Him.

Psalm 132:3 uses a different Hebrew word for bed, but it supplies a fitting companion thought. David vowed not to "go up to the comfort of my bed" until he found a dwelling place for the Lord. Rest is God's gift, and David still counted it worth giving up for God's house. Finally, Isaiah 57:2 teaches believers to see the grave differently. The faithful who die enter peace and lie down to rest, and in Christ that rest ends in resurrection.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 8:3 — The frogs came "into your bedroom, on your bed." Pharaoh's most private room could not keep out God's judgment.

  • 2 Samuel 4:5–7 — Ishbosheth was killed "lying on his bed in his bedroom," a sign that a king's bed offers no real security.

  • 2 Samuel 11:2, 13 — David rose from his bed in the evening and saw Bathsheba, while Uriah slept on a bed among the king's servants and refused to go home. The two men's beds expose the difference in their loyalty.

  • 2 Kings 6:12 — Elisha told Israel's king the words the king of Syria spoke in his bedroom. No private word is hidden from God.

  • Ecclesiastes 10:20 — "Do not curse the rich, even in your bedroom." Words spoken in secret have a way of coming out.

  • 2 Chronicles 16:14 — Asa was laid in a bed filled with spices, an example of the word used for a burial couch.

  • Proverbs 7:17 — The adulteress perfumes her bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon. Myrrh and cinnamon were also ingredients in the holy anointing oil (Exodus 30:23), which suggests that her bed imitates something sacred while leading to death.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7901 (shakab), lexical relative: the verb "to lie down," from which mishkâb is formed (compare Genesis 35:22, where Reuben "lay with" Bilhah).

  • H4296 (mittah), distinct word: another Hebrew word for bed or couch, also used for a funeral bier. Some English "bed" passages, such as Amos 6:4, use this word instead of mishkâb.
