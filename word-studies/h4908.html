TABERNACLE, DWELLING - (mishkân, Strong's H4908)

Summary

The Hebrew word mishkân means a dwelling place, the place where someone lives. It can describe ordinary homes and tents, such as the "dwellings" of Israel that Balaam admired from a mountaintop (Numbers 24:5). Most often, though, it names the Tabernacle, the portable sanctuary Israel built in the wilderness so that the LORD could live in the middle of their camp. God said, "Let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8). When the work was finished, His glory filled the Tabernacle so completely that Moses could not go in.

The word carries a remarkable message. The holy God chose to live in a tent among His people's tents and to travel with them through the desert. His nearness was also dangerous for sinners, so the Tabernacle was surrounded by boundaries, priests and sacrifices. The psalmists longed for this dwelling place, and the prophets promised that God's dwelling would one day be with His people permanently. That promise reaches its fulfillment when the Word became flesh and "dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and it reaches its final form when God makes His home with His people in the new creation.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mishkân is a Hebrew noun related to the verb shakan, "to dwell, settle, or live somewhere." The connection appears directly in Exodus 25:8–9. God commands a sanctuary "that I may dwell among them," and the next verse calls the structure the "tabernacle," His dwelling.

The word appears mostly in Exodus and Numbers, where Israel builds the Tabernacle, sets it up, carries it and camps around it. After Israel entered the land, the word follows the Tabernacle's history at Shiloh and Gibeon until Solomon built the Temple (1 Chronicles 21:29; 2 Chronicles 1:5). In Job, the Psalms and the Prophets, it can mean the homes of the wicked, the homes of restored Israel, or God's own dwelling.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is "dwelling place" or "residence." Context shows whose dwelling is meant and what kind of dwelling it is.

1. A human home or tent. Balaam exclaims, "How lovely are your tents, O Jacob! Your dwellings, O Israel!" (Numbers 24:5). Job 18:21 speaks of "the dwellings of the wicked."

2. God's dwelling, the Tabernacle as a whole. This is the word's main use, as in "the pattern of the tabernacle" (Exodus 25:9).

3. The inner structure of the sanctuary in the building instructions. Here mishkân means the ten inner curtains of fine linen and their frames, as distinct from the goats'-hair "tent" spread over them (Exodus 26:1, 7; 40:19).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Exodus the word belongs to the language of building. It names the dwelling that is designed, assembled and finally set up "on the first day of the first month" (Exodus 40:2, 17). In Numbers the setting changes to the camp on the move. The Levites take the Tabernacle down, carry it and set it up again, and the tribes arrange their tents around it.

The word is often paired with "tent," a separate Hebrew word ('ohel). In Hebrew poetry, a writer often says the same thing twice in different words, and in such lines the two terms mean nearly the same thing: "I will bring back the captivity of Jacob's tents, And have mercy on his dwelling places" (Jeremiah 30:18). In Exodus 40:34–35 the two stand side by side. The cloud covers "the tabernacle of meeting," which uses the "tent" word, and the glory fills "the tabernacle," which uses mishkân.

One small grammatical feature makes a real difference. God repeatedly calls it "My tabernacle" (Leviticus 15:31; 26:11). The dwelling is His, so Israel's behavior around it is never a private matter.

4. Theological Significance

The Tabernacle shows that God wants to be near His people. The same word that describes Israel's family tents in Numbers 24:5 also describes the home of the LORD. Israel's God did not stay on the far side of the mountain. He told Moses to build Him a dwelling "according to all that I show you" (Exodus 25:9) and then lived in it at the center of the camp.

His nearness was also holy and dangerous. God warned that Israel must be separated from uncleanness "lest they die in their uncleanness when they defile My tabernacle that is among them" (Leviticus 15:31). The Levites camped in a ring around it "that there may be no wrath on the congregation of the children of Israel" (Numbers 1:53), and "the outsider who comes near shall be put to death" (Numbers 1:51). The closer God came, the more seriously His holiness had to be taken. In this way the Tabernacle held both truths at once: God wants to live with His people, and sinners cannot approach Him casually.

The Tabernacle was also a royal dwelling. Its inner curtains were woven with cherubim (Exodus 26:1). The last cherubim in the story had stood at the entrance to Eden, guarding the way to the tree of life (Genesis 3:24). Inside the Tabernacle, cherubim now surround the place where God meets His people, and Scripture describes the LORD as enthroned between the cherubim (1 Samuel 4:4). The Tabernacle was a partial return toward the presence lost in Eden, entered only through the way God provided. In the camp, the Tabernacle stood at the center of the tribes, who were arranged by their armies (Numbers 2). When the ark set out, Moses called on the LORD to rise up and scatter His enemies (Numbers 10:35). The dwelling place of God was the tent of a King who fought for His people as He led them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 40:34–38 and Numbers 9:15–23: Glory that fills and leads. The book of Exodus ends with the Tabernacle completed and the glory of the LORD filling it. Moses, who had spoken with God on the mountain, "was not able to enter the tabernacle of meeting, because the cloud rested above it, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exodus 40:35). The finished building did not give Israel control over God. God took possession of it. The same cloud then directed every move "in the sight of all the house of Israel, throughout all their journeys" (40:38). Numbers 9 shows what that looked like day by day: "At the command of the LORD the children of Israel would journey, and at the command of the LORD they would camp" (9:18). The cloud might stay "two days, a month, or a year" (9:22). Israel's calendar was set by the dwelling of God. Each time the cloud moved, the Levites took the Tabernacle apart, carried it and set it up again exactly as instructed (Numbers 10:17, 21). God lived among His people, and He also directed where they went.

Numbers 16:24–27 and Joshua 22:19: Which dwelling will you stand beside? During Korah's rebellion, Moses told the congregation to move away from the tents of Korah, Dathan and Abiram. The Hebrew uses mishkân for the rebels' dwelling. The people had to choose between two dwellings: the one where God's glory rested and the one where men had set themselves against God's appointed leaders. The ground opened under the rebels' dwelling. Generations later, when the eastern tribes built an altar that looked like a rival to the true one, Phinehas and the leaders urged them to cross over into the land where the LORD's tabernacle stood rather than rebel against Him (Joshua 22:19). Job draws the same line in poetry: "Surely such are the dwellings of the wicked, And this is the place of him who does not know God" (Job 18:21).

Psalms 26:8, 43:3, 84:1 and 132:5: A dwelling worth longing for. In the Psalms the Tabernacle becomes the focus of love. David says, "LORD, I have loved the habitation of Your house, And the place where Your glory dwells" (Psalm 26:8). What he loves is the glory that lives in the building. A troubled psalmist prays, "Oh, send out Your light and Your truth! Let them lead me," asking to be brought "to Your tabernacle" (43:3). The sons of Korah cry, "How lovely is Your tabernacle, O LORD of hosts!" (84:1). These psalmists belonged to the family line of the Korah whose rebellion ended in judgment, and now they sing about the dwelling their forefather opposed. Psalm 132 remembers David's restless vow: "Until I find a place for the LORD, A dwelling place for the Mighty One of Jacob" (132:5).

Leviticus 26:11 and Ezekiel 37:27: The promise that outlasted the tent. Among God's blessings for obedience in Leviticus 26, He promises to set His tabernacle among Israel and not reject them (26:11). Israel's rebellion later cost them Shiloh, then the Temple, then the land. Ezekiel spoke in exile, after the vision of dry bones coming to life and the promise of one shepherd from David's line (37:24). In that setting God renewed the same promise: "My tabernacle also shall be with them; indeed I will be their God, and they shall be My people" (Ezekiel 37:27). The building could be lost, but God's purpose to live with His people could not.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

When David wanted to build God a house, the LORD reminded him, "I have not dwelt in a house since the time that I brought the children of Israel up from Egypt, even to this day, but have moved about in a tent and in a tabernacle" (2 Samuel 7:6). Instead, God promised to build David a house through a son. That promise looks past Solomon's Temple to the King Ezekiel saw.

The New Testament identifies the earthly Tabernacle as a copy. Priests served "the copy and shadow of the heavenly things," which is why Moses was told to follow the pattern exactly (Hebrews 8:5). The Tabernacle "was symbolic for the present time" (Hebrews 9:9), and Christ has come as High Priest "with the greater and more perfect tabernacle not made with hands" (9:11). Stephen reminded his hearers that "our fathers had the tabernacle of witness in the wilderness" and that the Most High does not live in houses made with hands (Acts 7:44, 48).

John's Gospel announces the fulfillment in Jesus: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory" (John 1:14). The Greek word for "dwelt" comes from the word for a tent. In Jesus, the glory that once filled the Tabernacle lived in a human body. The tension of the wilderness, God near yet dangerous, is resolved in Him, because He is both the place where God dwells and the priest who brings sinners near. Scripture's final vision announces that the tabernacle of God is with men and that He will dwell with them (Revelation 21:3). This is the promise of Leviticus 26 and Ezekiel 37 brought to completion.

7. Spirit & Life

Israel's camp was arranged so that every tribe pitched its tents facing the dwelling of God. This was the shape of their whole life. A believer's life is meant to have the same center. Work, family and plans are arranged around the presence of God, and His presence is not treated as one more item on the list.

The cloud above the Tabernacle sometimes stayed a year and sometimes only a night. Israel's faithfulness meant moving when God moved and staying when He stayed. In seasons of change, the Levites' work offers an illustration: they took the dwelling down and set it up again at every new camp, and it was always set up according to the same pattern. A moving life still needs the same center rebuilt at every stop.

There is also a hidden beauty in the Tabernacle. Its linen curtains, woven with cherubim, were covered by goats' hair and skins, so from the outside it looked plain. This is an illustration rather than something the text states, but it fits the One in whom God's glory was seen only by those who came near and believed. Like the psalmists, believers may say, "How lovely is Your tabernacle," because through Christ they have been welcomed into the presence that once kept Moses outside.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 46:4 — The streams of God's river gladden the city of God, called the holy place of the tabernacle of the Most High. God's dwelling is the source of His people's security in upheaval.

  • Psalm 78:60 — God forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh because of Israel's idolatry. His dwelling among them was never a guarantee that He would stay regardless of their sin.

  • 1 Chronicles 21:29 — The Tabernacle Moses made stood at Gibeon, but David was afraid to go there because of the angel's sword. The dwelling of God still demanded reverence in the time of the kings.

  • Isaiah 22:16 — Shebna carves a tomb for himself in the rock, a "dwelling" for his own honor. He builds a monument to himself while God's people face judgment.

  • Psalm 49:11 — The rich imagine their dwelling places will last to all generations. The grave proves them wrong.

  • Isaiah 54:2 — Zion is told to stretch out the curtains of her dwellings. The restored people of God will grow beyond the size of their former tents.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7931 (shakan), lexical relative. This is the verb "to dwell" from which mishkân is formed. In Exodus 25:8, God commands a sanctuary "that I may dwell among them."

  • H0168 ('ohel), canonical pairing. "Tent" is often paired with mishkân (Numbers 24:5; Jeremiah 30:18) and appears with it in Exodus 40:34–35.

  • H4720 (miqdash), canonical pairing. "Sanctuary" is the term used in Exodus 25:8, beside "tabernacle" in 25:9.

  • G4633 (skēnē) and G4637 (skēnoō), thematic connection across languages. These are the Greek words for "tabernacle" and "to dwell." They appear in Hebrews 8:2, John 1:14 and Revelation 21:3, where the New Testament takes up the hope the Tabernacle expressed.
