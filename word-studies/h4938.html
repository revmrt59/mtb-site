STAFF, SUPPORT, STORE - (mishʻênâh, Strong's H4938)

Summary

The Hebrew word mishʻênâh means a support, something a person leans on. Most often it is a walking-stick or staff: the stick an injured man uses to walk outside again, the staff an old woman holds as she sits in a Jerusalem street, the staff of a shepherd. Once it means something you cannot hold in your hand. In Isaiah 3:1 it describes the bread and water a city leans on to stay alive.

Because a staff carries weight, Scripture uses this word to ask what we are really leaning on. David says of the Lord, "Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me" (Psalm 23:4). The prophets and even Israel's enemies describe Egypt as a staff made of broken reed that pierces the hand of anyone who leans on it. Between those two pictures lies a choice every believer faces. Every other support eventually gives way, and some leave wounds when they do. The Lord's staff holds.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mishʻênâh is a noun built from a Hebrew verb meaning to lean or to rest one's weight on something. The word names the object of that leaning. It appears twelve times. The settings vary widely. It shows up in a law about injury, a wilderness song, angelic and prophetic encounters, a siege speech, a psalm, and prophecies of judgment and restoration. In daily life a staff was ordinary equipment for travelers, shepherds, the elderly, and the injured. That ordinariness is what makes it useful as a picture of dependence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A walking-stick or staff, a physical support carried in the hand and leaned on, as with the recovering man in Exodus 21:19 or the elderly in Zechariah 8:4.

2. A support in the broader sense, whatever sustains life. In Isaiah 3:1 the NKJV renders it "store," and the verse names it as "the whole supply of bread and the whole supply of water."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses are literal staffs. Who holds the staff changes what it means. In the hand of the injured or the aged, it marks weakness that is being helped. Israel's nobles use their staves in Numbers 21:18, and there the staff belongs to leaders. The Angel of the LORD and Elisha also carry one, and in their hands the staff is linked with divine power or prophetic commission.

The word's figurative force comes out when Scripture describes something other than wood as a staff. Egypt is called a staff of reed (2 Kings 18:21; Isaiah 36:6; Ezekiel 29:6). Bread and water become Jerusalem's "store." In each case the question is the same: what will bear the weight of a life or a nation?

In Psalm 23:4 the word stands beside a different Hebrew word for "rod," shebet. Elsewhere that word often describes a club, scepter, or rod of correction. The pairing gives the shepherd two instruments. The rod guards and corrects. The staff supports and guides.

4. Theological Significance

This word teaches that people are made to lean. The Bible never treats dependence itself as weakness to be outgrown. The real question is whether the support can hold. God is the only support that does not break under the full weight of a human life, even in "the valley of the shadow of death."

The word also shows that God is the giver of every lesser support. Bread and water are His provision. Yet Isaiah 3:1 shows that the Lord Himself can take away Jerusalem's "stock and the store." The second word there is mishʻênâh, and the first is a closely related form from the same root. The surrounding chapter describes a city whose confidence had come loose from God, and He removes the very things they were leaning on. This is a severe mercy. When the gifts have replaced the Giver, losing the gifts exposes where the heart's weight had actually rested.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 23:4: the Shepherd's staff. David does not say the valley disappears. He says, "I will fear no evil; For You are with me." The rod and the staff are the shepherd's own instruments, so their comfort is the comfort of His presence. The rod means the sheep will be defended and corrected. The staff means the sheep will be guided and held up. David finds comfort in both, because a shepherd who only defended or only supported would not be enough for that valley.

The broken reed: 2 Kings 18:21; Isaiah 36:6; Ezekiel 29:6–7. When Assyria besieged Jerusalem, the Rabshakeh mocked Judah's hope in Egypt: "You are trusting in the staff of this broken reed, Egypt, on which if a man leans, it will go into his hand and pierce it." He was an enemy, but he was right. Years later God says the same thing through Ezekiel: Egypt had "been a staff of reed to the house of Israel." Ezekiel 29:7 shows what happened when Israel leaned: "You broke and tore all their shoulders; When they leaned on you, You broke and made all their backs quiver." A false support does more than fail. It splinters under pressure, and the splinters go into the one who trusted it. The deeper the lean, the deeper the wound.

2 Kings 4:29–31: the staff without the prophet. When the Shunammite woman's son died, Elisha sent Gehazi ahead with urgent instructions: "lay my staff on the face of the child." Gehazi obeyed, but the boy did not respond. The text reports no sound and no sign of hearing. The staff belonged to a genuine prophet and was sent with genuine authority, and it still could not give life. Elisha had to come himself, enter the room, pray, and stretch himself out over the boy (2 Kings 4:32–35). A real instrument of God's work could not stand in for His living, personal intervention.

Zechariah 8:4: the staff of old age. "Old men and old women shall again sit In the streets of Jerusalem, Each one with his staff in his hand Because of great age." Jerusalem had known siege, famine, sword, and exile, and in those disasters the old seldom survived. In this promise the streets hold people who have lived long enough to need a walking-stick and are safe enough to sit down. The staff here is a mark of peace. God's restoration is measured by grandparents resting in public, unafraid. Isaiah 65:20 carries the same hope: no more an old man "who has not fulfilled his days."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Psalm 23's Shepherd comes to the center of the gospel when Jesus calls Himself the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11). The staff that comforts in the valley of death belongs to One who has gone through death Himself.

Elisha's staff also speaks to the larger story. The staff could be carried by a servant and laid on the dead child, but life came only when the prophet himself came and stretched his body over the boy. That pattern fits the way God saved the world. He did not stop at sending instruments, messengers, and commands. In Christ He came in person, took on flesh, and entered death to bring life. This is a canonical resonance, not a stated fulfillment, and it shows the Bible's steady direction toward God's personal coming.

Zechariah's elderly in the streets look forward to the peace of God's kingdom. Hebrews 11:21 adds an echo from the patriarchs. It describes Jacob at the end of his life, worshiping as he leaned on the top of his staff. A different Hebrew word stands behind that scene, but the picture fits this word's whole witness: a pilgrim whose strength is gone, leaning and worshiping, held by God's promises.

7. Spirit & Life

This word asks one plain question: where is your weight resting? Most of us do not lean on Egypt, but we lean on savings, health, reputation, a relationship, or a plan. Ezekiel 29:7 is honest about how such supports fail. They do not quietly step aside. They tear the shoulder that relied on them. Some of the deepest wounds believers carry come from having leaned their full weight on something God never designed to hold it.

When the Lord removes a support, as He did in Isaiah 3:1, it can feel like judgment, and sometimes it is. It is also an invitation to shift the weight to the only support that will hold. The comfort of Psalm 23:4 is not the absence of the valley. It is the presence of the Shepherd whose rod guards and whose staff holds us up.

Elisha's staff carries a warning for anyone in ministry. Sermons, sacraments, programs, and credentials can all be real instruments of God and still be laid on a dead heart without effect. Life comes when the Lord Himself draws near, which is why prayer cannot be replaced by procedure. Zechariah gives the hope at the end. A life that has learned to lean on God can grow old in peace with a staff in hand, resting in the streets of His city.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 21:19. A man injured in a fight who "rises again and walks about outside with his staff" shows the most basic sense: a stick that helps someone recover.

  • Numbers 21:18. Israel sings of a well "Dug by the nation's nobles, By the lawgiver, with their staves." God had promised water (Numbers 21:16), and the leaders' staves take part in a provision they did not create.

  • Judges 6:21. The Angel of the LORD touched Gideon's offering with the end of His staff, "and fire rose out of the rock and consumed the meat and the unleavened bread," a sign that God accepted the offering.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Scripture itself interprets two of this word's symbols. Ezekiel 29:6 names Egypt "a staff of reed," a false alliance that breaks and wounds. Isaiah 3:1 calls bread and water the "store," the support of life itself. Established biblical imagery adds the pairing of rod and staff in Psalm 23:4, which pictures the Shepherd's protection and His sustaining guidance. The staff in the hands of the aged and the pilgrim (Zechariah 8:4; compare Hebrews 11:21) is a picture of dependent rest at the end of life. Some interpreters go further and read Elisha's staff as a figure of the law, which can show that a person is dead but cannot raise him, while the prophet's own coming points to the incarnation. That reading is a spiritual association, not the passage's stated meaning, though it fits the gospel's emphasis on God coming in person.
