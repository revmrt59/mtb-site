SWEET, SWEETNESS - (mâthôwq, Strong's H4966)

Summary

The Hebrew word mâthôwq means sweet. At its simplest it describes the taste of honey or ripe fruit. Scripture also uses it for anything a person finds deeply pleasant: the sleep of a tired worker, the light of the sun, kind words, and the judgments of the Lord. The word is rare, appearing only about a dozen times, mostly in Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song of Solomon, with a few striking uses in Judges, Isaiah and Ezekiel.

The word's richest uses concern appetite. David says the Lord's judgments are "sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb" (Psalm 19:10). Ezekiel eats a scroll filled with mourning and woe, and it tastes "like honey in sweetness" (Ezekiel 3:3). Isaiah warns against people "who put bitter for sweet, and sweet for bitter" (Isaiah 5:20), whose sense of taste has been turned upside down. Read together, these passages show that the human heart was made to delight in God. Sin damages that appetite, and God restores it through His Word. In Christ, believers learn to find His truth sweet, even when it is hard to hear.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Mâthôwq belongs to a land where honey, figs and fruit were among the few naturally sweet foods. To call something "sweet" was to compare it with the best thing a person could put in his mouth. That explains why honey appears beside the word so often (Judges 14:18; Psalm 19:10; Proverbs 24:13; Ezekiel 3:3).

The word appears mostly in poetry and wisdom writing, where the Bible reflects on desire, speech and the inner life. A closely related Hebrew verb, to be sweet, appears elsewhere. At Marah, for example, the bitter waters "were made sweet" (Exodus 15:25). That verb is a family relative and not this adjective, but it shows that the language of sweetness reaches back to Israel's earliest days in the wilderness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Sweet to the taste: the literal flavor of honey or fruit, as in Samson's riddle, "out of the strong came something sweet" (Judges 14:14), or the honeycomb "which is sweet to your taste" (Proverbs 24:13).

2. Pleasant or refreshing: an experience that satisfies the way good food does, such as rest after work or the sight of daylight (Ecclesiastes 5:12; 11:7).

3. Good and desirable: something judged worthwhile and right, set against what is bitter or harmful (Isaiah 5:20; Psalm 19:10).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The Bible usually measures sweetness by honey. Twice the word appears in the form "sweeter," a direct comparison: Samson's companions ask, "What is sweeter than honey?" (Judges 14:18), and David answers, in effect, that God's judgments are (Psalm 19:10). Twice the word stands as a noun, "sweetness," describing what something brings to the one who receives it (Proverbs 16:24; Ezekiel 3:3).

Sweetness is often set against bitterness, and the contrast keeps the word from meaning only "pleasant." Isaiah 5:20 places "bitter for sweet" beside "evil for good" and "darkness for light." In that setting sweetness becomes a moral category. The word is also sensitive to the one doing the tasting. Proverbs 27:7 observes that a hungry man finds "every bitter thing" sweet, so the judgment of sweetness depends on the condition of the person who tastes.

4. Theological Significance

God made human beings with appetites, and Scripture treats this as part of His good design. Honey is "good" (Proverbs 24:13). Light is sweet (Ecclesiastes 11:7). The Lord does not expect His people to despise pleasure. He made them to enjoy it, and above all to enjoy Him.

Scripture is also honest that sin has its own sweetness. Folly calls out, "Stolen water is sweet, And bread eaten in secret is pleasant" (Proverbs 9:17). That promise does not last. "Bread gained by deceit is sweet to a man, But afterward his mouth will be filled with gravel" (Proverbs 20:17). Job's friend pictures evil held "sweet in his mouth" until it "becomes cobra venom within him" (Job 20:12, 14). These passages use different Hebrew words, yet they describe the same pattern. The sweetness of sin belongs to the first taste. The sweetness God gives grows richer the more deeply it is received. One of the most searching questions Scripture asks is simply what a person finds sweet.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 19:10 and Ezekiel 3:3: the sweetness of God's Word. Psalm 19 moves from the heavens declaring God's glory to the law of the Lord "converting the soul" (19:7). David then says God's judgments are "More to be desired... than gold... Sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb." These judgments are the Lord's verdicts about what is right and wrong. David finds commandment and correction delicious, and he adds that "by them Your servant is warned" (19:11). The psalm treats warning itself as part of the sweetness.

Ezekiel pushes that truth to its limit. The scroll handed to him was written with lamentations, mourning and woe (Ezekiel 2:10), a message of judgment against his own people. God commands him, "fill your stomach with this scroll," and Ezekiel reports, "it was in my mouth like honey in sweetness" (3:3). The message was grievous, yet receiving it was sweet. The sweetness came from the Speaker. To take God's own words into oneself is communion with Him, whatever those words contain. A few verses later Ezekiel says he went away in bitterness and heat of spirit, with the Lord's hand strong upon him (3:14). The sweetness of the word in his mouth did not cancel the weight of the message he had to carry.

John's experience in Revelation shows the same two tastes. He eats the little book, and "it was as sweet as honey in my mouth. But when I had eaten it, my stomach became bitter" (Revelation 10:10). God's Word is sweet to the one who loves Him. Once it is digested, it brings grief over what it announces for a rebellious world. Jeremiah describes the same hunger: "Your words were found, and I ate them, And Your word was to me the joy and rejoicing of my heart" (Jeremiah 15:16).

Isaiah 5:20 with Proverbs 27:7: the inverted palate. Isaiah's song of the vineyard ends in a series of woes. One of them uses this word twice: "Woe to those... Who put bitter for sweet, and sweet for bitter!" The people Isaiah describes do more than make wrong choices. They have relabeled reality, so that what poisons them tastes good and what would heal them tastes repulsive.

Proverbs 27:7 offers a useful picture for this warning: "A satisfied soul loathes the honeycomb, But to a hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet." The proverb describes ordinary hunger, and it also sheds light on Isaiah's woe. A soul that is starving will swallow almost anything and call it good. A people who have turned from the Lord do not stop being hungry, so they begin calling bitter things sweet. Isaiah speaks to a whole nation and Proverbs to a single appetite, but the proverb's picture of hunger helps us apply Isaiah's warning.

Judges 14:14, 18: sweetness out of the strong. On his way to Timnah, Samson tore apart a young lion. Later he found bees and honey in its carcass. His riddle, "Out of the eater came something to eat, And out of the strong came something sweet," turns that discovery into a puzzle no one could solve. When the Philistines finally answer, "What is sweeter than honey? And what is stronger than a lion?" (14:18), they only have the answer because they pressured Samson's wife. The story is full of human weakness, yet it preserves a striking picture: food from the eater, sweetness from what had come to kill.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Samson's riddle reads like a parable of the gospel, though Judges never makes that connection. Revelation calls Jesus "the Lion of the tribe of Judah" who "has prevailed" (Revelation 5:5). He conquered through His death, and from that death has come life for all who believe. Out of the cross, the place of greatest violence in history, God brought the sweetest thing the world has known. The likeness is a resonance and not a stated fulfillment, but it fits the way God works throughout Scripture.

The bitter waters at Marah give a similar picture. Israel complained, "What shall we drink?" and "the LORD showed him a tree. When he cast it into the waters, the waters were made sweet" (Exodus 15:24–25). The New Testament never calls the cross the tree of Marah. Yet Christians have long seen in it the way God answers human bitterness, by providing a remedy His people could not find for themselves.

The Song of Solomon offers the most personal picture. The bride says of her beloved, "I sat down in his shade with great delight, And his fruit was sweet to my taste" (Song of Songs 2:3). The verse describes love between a man and a woman. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, it also describes what believers find in Christ: shade from the heat and food that satisfies. The judgments David found sweeter than honey are ultimately the words of the One who became flesh. Paul extends the imagery to fragrance, saying believers are "to God the fragrance of Christ" (2 Corinthians 2:15). That is a different word for a different sense, but it carries the same idea: those who have tasted Christ's sweetness begin to carry it.

7. Spirit & Life

The warning in Isaiah 5:20, read with Proverbs 27:7, is personal. Many believers know the experience of finding sin attractive and prayer dull. Scripture does not merely scold that condition. The proverb's picture invites us to ask where we are seeking satisfaction. The remedy is to be fed. A soul filled with the Lord can turn away from even good honey, and far more easily from stolen water. The psalmist's appetite for God's judgments was the result of a converted soul (Psalm 19:7). God heals appetites as His Word is taken in, and the believer who keeps eating it finds the tastes changing.

Ezekiel and John also free believers from a shallow view of Bible reading. God's Word is sweet even when it rebukes, warns or announces judgment, because God Himself is speaking. A mature believer learns to love the hard chapters along with the comforting ones. The same believer also feels the bitterness John felt when he understood what the message meant for others. Delight in God and grief over the lost belong together.

Sweetness also passes from person to person. "Pleasant words are like a honeycomb, Sweetness to the soul and health to the bones" (Proverbs 16:24). Kind, wise speech reaches the inner person and the body alike. James asks, "Does a spring send forth fresh water and bitter from the same opening?" (James 3:11). A tongue that has tasted God's goodness should give that sweetness to others.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 24:13 — "My son, eat honey because it is good, And the honeycomb which is sweet to your taste." The next verse compares wisdom to that honey, so the ordinary pleasure of food becomes a picture of what wisdom gives the soul.

  • Ecclesiastes 5:12 — "The sleep of a laboring man is sweet, Whether he eats little or much." Rest is God's gift to honest work, and the rich man's abundance cannot buy it.

  • Ecclesiastes 11:7 — "Truly the light is sweet, And it is pleasant for the eyes to behold the sun." Even a sober book calls simply being alive under the sun a sweet gift.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G1099 (glykys, sweet or fresh). This is a Greek word, so it is a related idea and not a family relative of the Hebrew word. It appears where John's little book is "sweet as honey" (Revelation 10:9–10) and for "fresh" water in James 3:11.

  • H5207 (nichoach, soothing or restful). This is a thematic connection and not a lexical relative. It describes the "soothing aroma" of sacrifice (Genesis 8:21), which older English Bibles often called a "sweet savour." It concerns smell, while mâthôwq concerns taste.
