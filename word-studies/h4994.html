PLEASE - (nâʼ, Strong's H4994)

Summary

This small Hebrew word stands behind many of the Old Testament's "please"s, "I pray"s, and persuasive "now"s. It turns a bare command or wish into an appeal. Servants use it before masters, brothers use it with brothers, and worshipers use it before God. Most surprising of all, God uses it with His own people: "Come now, and let us reason together" (Isaiah 1:18). Once you can hear the word, familiar passages sound different. A request becomes earnest, a prayer becomes humble pleading, and a prophetic summons becomes the voice of a Lord who urges sinners to turn before He judges them.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

This word does not stand alone like a cry of "Alas!" Instead, it attaches to a request, command, or wish and changes its tone. A speaker who uses it is asking rather than simply ordering. Usually the speaker is leaning toward the hearer with respect, urgency, or both.

The NKJV translates it in several ways: "please," "I pray," "I beg," "I urge," "Oh," and very often "now." When this word comes out as "now," it usually does not mark time. It works like the English "Come now" or "Listen now," adding a persuasive push to the request.

The speaker and the occasion determine its force:

  • From a servant to a ruler, it shows deference.

  • From Abraham to the servant who managed his household, it is courtesy from someone who held authority: "Please, put your hand under my thigh" (Genesis 24:2).

  • From a desperate person to God, it is supplication.

  • From an enemy, it can become a sneer.

The word supplies the tone of appeal. The setting tells us whether that appeal is humble, warm, urgent, or mocking.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Courteous and peacemaking requests. Much of the family life in Genesis is carried on with this word. When quarreling herdsmen threatened to divide the family, Abram said to Lot, "Please let there be no strife between you and me" (Genesis 13:8). Abram was the elder, and he was the one who had received God's promises. Yet he pleaded with Lot and then let him choose the land first.

Years later, Joseph held complete power over the brothers who had sold him. He used this word to ask them to come near to him (Genesis 45:4). After their father died, the brothers used the same word as they begged Joseph to forgive their wrong (Genesis 50:17). One small word carries both the invitation and the plea for mercy.

Pleading with God. Facing Esau, Jacob prayed, "Deliver me, I pray, from the hand of my brother" (Genesis 32:11). After the golden calf, Moses asked God to forgive the people. He even offered to be blotted out of God's book in their place (Exodus 32:32). Then he asked, "Please, show me Your glory" (Exodus 33:18).

Samson's last prayer uses the word twice as he asks God to remember him and strengthen him once more (Judges 16:28). Nehemiah uses it repeatedly as he asks God to hear, to remember His word, and to grant success (Nehemiah 1:6, 8, 11). In each case the person asks for something he has no right to demand.

God's appeals and summons. The word appears on God's own lips and in His prophets' messages.

  • In Isaiah 1:18 the LORD says, "Come now, and let us reason together," and then promises that scarlet sins shall be as white as snow.

  • Through Zechariah He called His people to turn from their evil ways (Zechariah 1:4).

  • In Jeremiah 44:4 God recalls His prophets pleading, in effect, "Oh, do not do this abominable thing that I hate!"

  • In Malachi He challenged a people who were holding back their tithes: "try Me now in this" (Malachi 3:10).

  • Even in Genesis 18:21, God announces, "I will go down now and see," as He resolves to examine Sodom before He judges it.

In worship, Psalm 124:1 uses the word to call the congregation to respond: let Israel now say that the LORD was on their side.

Irony and taunt. The same courteous word can be turned into a dare. The Assyrian spokesman mocked Jerusalem's defenders, "urging" them to make a wager with his king (2 Kings 18:23; Isaiah 36:8). Isaiah told proud Babylon to stand now with her enchantments and see whether her astrologers could save her (Isaiah 47:12–13). In these passages the polite form makes the mockery sharper.

3. Biblical Connections & Distinctions

The English words "please" and "I pray" do not always translate this Hebrew word.

In Genesis 44:18, Judah says, "O my lord, please let your servant speak a word in my lord's hearing." The "please" is this word. The "O my lord," however, reflects a different Hebrew expression, usually identified as Strong's H0994. Judah uses that expression as a respectful opening, asking a superior for permission to speak. Aaron's "Oh, my lord! Please do not lay this sin on us" (Numbers 12:11) pairs the two expressions in the same way.

Likewise, the opening "I pray" in Nehemiah 1:5 and the "O Lord" in Daniel 9:4 are not uses of this word. Those prayers begin with a related and more intense cry of entreaty, commonly listed as H577. All of these expressions help a humble speaker plead, so their purposes overlap. This word, however, moves easily between ordinary conversation and prayer.

The pattern of God appealing before He judges continues into the New Testament, although Greek uses different words. Jesus calls, "Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). This is a thematic connection, not another use of the same word. Still, the Lord who said "Come now" through Isaiah speaks with the same urgency in person.

A second connection is an analogy. Moses offered to be blotted out for Israel (Exodus 32:32). When the plague struck, David pleaded for God's hand to fall on him and his father's house rather than on the people, whom he called "these sheep" (2 Samuel 24:17). Both shepherds asked to stand between God's judgment and the flock. Neither could actually bear that judgment for them. Jesus, the Good Shepherd, did.

4. Spirit & Life

God has every right to command, and He often does. Yet Isaiah 1 opens like a courtroom case against Judah's bloodstained hands and empty sacrifices. In the middle of that charge, God says, "Come now." The Judge offers cleansing to the people He is accusing. Jeremiah 44 shows the same heart at a later point. God reminds His people that He sent prophets again and again with the same plea, and only after they refused does judgment fall. When God's judgment comes, it comes after He has pleaded with His people.

The word also exposes the heart of the one who uses it. After disobeying God, Saul said "please" as he asked Samuel to pardon his sin. Then, in the same scene, he said "please" again to ask Samuel to honor him before the elders (1 Samuel 15:25, 30). His second request shows what he cared about most: his standing before people.

David, after his own sin, used the same word three times. He asked God to take away his iniquity. He chose to fall into the LORD's hand because God's mercies are great. Finally, he asked that the punishment fall on himself (2 Samuel 24:10, 14, 17). Both kings used the same courteous Hebrew word, but they had very different hearts. Saying "please" to God does not prove repentance. True repentance appeals to God's mercy, not to protect our reputation.

5. Reading Guidance

When the NKJV puts "now" inside a request, such as "Come now" or "try Me now," ask whether it marks time or makes an appeal. Then ask who is speaking to whom. A servant before a king, a sinner before God, God before His people, and a mocking enemy all give the same word a very different voice.
