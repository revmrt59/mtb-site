LOVELY, ADORNS, BEAUTIFUL - (nâʼâh, Strong's H4998)

Summary

The Hebrew word nâʼâh means "to be lovely," "to be beautiful," or "to be fitting." It describes beauty that suits its subject and delights the one who sees it. The word appears only three times in the Old Testament, and each use comes at a meaningful moment. In the Song of Solomon a lover tells his beloved that her cheeks are "lovely with ornaments." In Psalm 93 the psalmist declares that "Holiness adorns Your house, O LORD, forever." In Isaiah 52 the prophet cries, "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news."

Taken together, these three verses show what Scripture finds beautiful. The word begins with the delight of a lover looking at his bride. It then describes the holiness that fits God's own dwelling, and finally the messenger who runs to announce that God has saved His people. Two of the three uses are tied directly to the announcement that God reigns. Paul later takes up Isaiah's words to describe everyone who preaches the gospel of Christ. In God's eyes, holiness and the good news of His saving rule are among the loveliest things in the world.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâʼâh is a Hebrew verb that appears only in poetry: once in the Psalms, once in the Song of Solomon, and once in Isaiah. It never appears in the Law or the historical narratives. This fits the word's character, because poets use it to praise what is pleasing and fitting. The NKJV translates it three different ways, "lovely," "adorns," and "beautiful," and each rendering suits its setting. Because the word is so rare, each occurrence carries real weight, and the three can be read together without forcing them into a pattern.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one core meaning: to be lovely, beautiful, or fitting. It describes something that is pleasing to look at and well suited to the person or place it belongs to. The NKJV's three renderings express this single sense in different settings:

1. Lovely, attractive in appearance. In Song of Solomon 1:10 the word describes a young woman's cheeks set off by ornaments.

2. Fitting, becoming, adorning. In Psalm 93:5 holiness is the beauty that suits God's house, so the NKJV renders the verb "adorns."

In Isaiah 52:7 the word carries the first sense, "beautiful," but the context gives that beauty an unexpected focus, as Section 5 explains.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the word changes in each verse, and that change carries the meaning forward. In the Song of Solomon, the lovely thing is a person, seen through the eyes of someone who loves her. In Psalm 93, the subject is a quality, holiness, that belongs to the LORD's house. In Isaiah 52, the beautiful thing is a messenger's feet, which are beautiful because of the news they carry.

All three verses connect beauty with something worn or carried. The bride wears ornaments of gold, God's house is adorned with holiness, and the messenger's feet bring the news of salvation. In each case the word points to more than surface appearance. It describes beauty that fits who someone is and what they are doing.

4. Theological Significance

Psalm 93 and Isaiah 52 tie this word to the reign of God. Psalm 93 opens, "The LORD reigns, He is clothed with majesty" (v. 1), and closes with holiness adorning His house. Isaiah's messenger reaches his climax with the words "Your God reigns!" The beauty that nâʼâh describes in these passages flows from the LORD being King. When God rules, His dwelling becomes beautiful with holiness, and the news of His rule is the loveliest message a suffering people could hear.

These verses also show that holiness is beautiful. Many people think of holiness only as restriction, a line that keeps sinners out. Psalm 93:5 describes it as the beauty that fits God's house. Psalm 29:2 points the same direction when it calls God's people to "worship the LORD in the beauty of holiness." In this vision, God's purity is something His people can delight in as well as reverence.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:10 — the delight of love. "Your cheeks are lovely with ornaments, Your neck with chains of gold." The man has just called the young woman "my love" (v. 9). He praises her beauty, and the next verse promises more: "We will make you ornaments of gold With studs of silver" (v. 11). This is the plainest use of nâʼâh: real, physical beauty seen by someone who cherishes her. The verse also shows that beauty in Scripture is often relational. The man notices her loveliness because he loves her, and he wants to add to it. That ordinary human delight gives the word its warmth when the Psalms and Isaiah use it for holier things.

Psalm 93:5 — holiness fitting God's house. Psalm 93 celebrates the LORD as King. Against His steady throne the floods rise and roar: "The floods have lifted up their voice; The floods lift up their waves" (v. 3). The psalmist answers, "The LORD on high is mightier Than the noise of many waters" (v. 4). Then the psalm ends quietly with two things that last: "Your testimonies are very sure; Holiness adorns Your house, O LORD, forever." The world's chaos is loud and passing. God's word and God's holiness stand firm. Placing those two side by side matters. God's house is beautiful because it is ruled by what He has said and set apart by who He is. Holiness is the ornament that fits His dwelling, in the same way gold suits the bride in the Song.

Isaiah 52:7 — beautiful feet. Isaiah 52 begins with Zion as a captive in the dust. God tells her, "Shake yourself from the dust, arise... Loose yourself from the bonds of your neck" (v. 2). He also calls her to "Put on your beautiful garments, O Jerusalem" (v. 1). Then a runner appears on the mountains, carrying good news to the ruined city. Isaiah does not praise the messenger's face or his voice. He praises his feet: "How beautiful upon the mountains Are the feet of him who brings good news, Who proclaims peace, Who brings glad tidings of good things, Who proclaims salvation, Who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'"

Feet that have crossed mountain roads are not attractive in themselves. They are beautiful because of what their arrival means. The exile is over, the captives are redeemed "without money" (v. 3), and God is acting so that His people "shall know My name" (v. 6). Here nâʼâh moves from beauty that is seen to beauty that comes through action. The loveliest thing on the horizon is the person bringing word that God has saved His people.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Paul quotes Isaiah 52:7 when he explains how people come to faith in Christ. People must call on the Lord, but they cannot call without believing, believe without hearing, or hear without someone preaching. "And how shall they preach unless they are sent? As it is written: 'How beautiful are the feet of those who preach the gospel of peace, Who bring glad tidings of good things!'" (Romans 10:15). Isaiah's lone runner to Zion becomes the whole company of gospel messengers. The "good news" of Jerusalem's release becomes the message of Christ's death and resurrection.

Jesus Himself is the first and greatest of these messengers. He came preaching the gospel of the kingdom of God (Mark 1:14–15), and Paul says He came and preached peace to those far off and to those near (Ephesians 2:17). The message "Your God reigns!" reaches its fullness in Christ. He is the messenger, and He is also the King whose reign is announced. Ephesians 6:15 then pictures believers ready for this work, "having shod your feet with the preparation of the gospel of peace." The feet Isaiah called beautiful now belong to the church.

The bridal language of the Song also fits this larger story. Isaiah 52 calls Zion to put on beautiful garments, and the New Testament describes the church as the bride of Christ. This is a resonance rather than a stated fulfillment of Song 1:10, but it is a fitting one. The God who adorns His house with holiness is preparing His people to be lovely in His sight.

7. Spirit & Life

These three verses challenge what we usually admire. The world tends to find beauty in appearance, wealth, and polish. Scripture uses its word for "lovely" for a bride seen through the eyes of love, for God's holiness, and for the tired feet of a messenger. Each verse invites us to see as God sees.

Believers are now God's temple, the place where His Spirit dwells (1 Corinthians 3:16). If holiness is what adorns God's house, then holiness is the beauty that fits a Christian life. Peter writes, "as He who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct" (1 Peter 1:15). Psalm 93 shows the kind of holiness Peter means. It stands next to God's sure testimonies and rests on His word, and it holds steady while the floods roar. Holiness of this kind is God's own beauty being formed in His people.

Isaiah 52:7 also offers great dignity to ordinary witness. The runner on the mountains was not famous. He was beautiful because he carried true news about God. When a believer crosses a street, a room, or an ocean to say that Jesus reigns and offers peace, God calls that person's feet beautiful. Most Christians never think of their witness this way, yet Paul applies the verse to every sent preacher of the gospel.

The Song of Solomon keeps all of this warm. Holiness and witness flow from being loved. The man in the Song sees beauty in the young woman and adds gold to it. In the same way, the Lord delights in His people and adorns them Himself.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All three occurrences of nâʼâh — Song of Solomon 1:10, Psalm 93:5, and Isaiah 52:7 — are developed in Section 5. Two nearby verses use different Hebrew words but strengthen the same themes:

  • Isaiah 52:1 — "Put on your beautiful garments, O Jerusalem, the holy city!" Zion is called to dress for her restoration just before the messenger arrives.

  • Psalm 29:2 — "Worship the LORD in the beauty of holiness." This verse joins holiness and beauty, as Psalm 93:5 does.
