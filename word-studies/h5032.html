NEBAIOTH - (Nᵉbâyôwth, Strong's H5032)

Summary

Nebaioth was the firstborn son of Ishmael and a grandson of Abraham through Hagar. His name also became the name of the Arabian tribe that descended from him, a pastoral people of the eastern deserts known for their flocks. The meaning of the name is uncertain. It has often been linked to ideas such as "heights," "prominences," or "fruitfulness," but none of these is settled, and Scripture itself offers no explanation of the name. Nebaioth appears only five times. He heads the list of Ishmael's twelve sons (Genesis 25:13; 1 Chronicles 1:29). He is named as the brother of the woman Esau married when he tried to win his father's approval (Genesis 28:9; 36:3). Finally, Isaiah names his tribe in the great vision of Zion's coming glory (Isaiah 60:7).

The few verses tell a story worth noticing. Nebaioth's father had been sent away into the wilderness with bread and a skin of water (Genesis 21:14), and the family line that grew from him lived outside the promise God made through Isaac. Yet when Isaiah describes the nations streaming to the light of the Lord, he says, "The rams of Nebaioth shall minister to you; They shall ascend with acceptance on My altar." The wilderness people come back as worshipers, and God receives what they bring. That picture comes to its fullness in Christ, through whom those "who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (Ephesians 2:13). It also anticipates the New Jerusalem, where "they shall bring the glory and the honor of the nations into it" (Revelation 21:26).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Nebaioth refers to one individual and to the people descended from him. In Genesis and 1 Chronicles, he is a person, Ishmael's firstborn, the eldest of twelve brothers: Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam, Mishma, Dumah, Massa, Hadar, Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah (Genesis 25:13–15). His sister married Esau, so Nebaioth was also Esau's brother-in-law (Genesis 28:9). By Isaiah's day the name stands for a tribe, set beside Kedar as a people of flocks and rams (Isaiah 60:7). The individual and the tribe are part of the same family history. The tribe carries its founder's name, much as Israel and Edom do.

The name's meaning is uncertain. Proposed connections to "heights," "prominences," or "fruitfulness" appear in reference works, but the derivation is debated, and the Bible makes no wordplay on it. No spiritual lesson should be built on any of these suggestions. English readers will also notice two spellings. In the NKJV, Genesis 25:13, Genesis 28:9, Genesis 36:3, and 1 Chronicles 1:29 read "Nebajoth," while Isaiah 60:7 reads "Nebaioth." The difference is only in the English. All five occurrences belong to the same Hebrew entry.

The tribe lived in the Syro-Arabian desert, east and southeast of Israel, within the broad region Genesis assigns to Ishmael's descendants, "from Havilah as far as Shur, which is east of Egypt" (Genesis 25:18). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia notes that Assyrian records name an Arabian people, the Nabaiati, together with Kedar, which would fit the biblical pairing of Nebaioth and Kedar. Josephus and Jerome identified Nebaioth with the later Nabataeans, the builders of Petra, but the two names are spelled with different Hebrew consonants (taw and teth), so that identification remains debated. The biblical picture does not depend on settling either question.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

A promise kept to Ishmael. Before Ishmael had any sons, God told Abraham, "As for Ishmael, I have heard you," and promised to multiply him exceedingly: "He shall beget twelve princes, and I will make him a great nation" (Genesis 17:20, paraphrasing the surrounding wording). In the next breath God said His covenant would be established with Isaac (17:21). When Hagar and Ishmael were later sent away, God heard the boy's voice in the wilderness and renewed the promise to make him a great nation (21:17–18). Genesis 25:12–16 records how that promise was kept. Twelve princes are listed "according to their nations," and Nebaioth stands first. He was the first visible proof that God had done exactly what He said for the son who was not the heir.

The placement of this list is telling. It comes right after Isaac and Ishmael bury Abraham together (25:9) and just before "the genealogy of Isaac" begins (25:19). Genesis regularly gives the line outside the promise a brief, respectful record before turning to the chosen line. Ishmael's family is neither forgotten nor made the center of the story.

Esau's marriage. Nebaioth's next appearance is through his sister. Esau saw that "the daughters of Canaan did not please his father Isaac" (Genesis 28:8, paraphrased). So "Esau went to Ishmael and took Mahalath the daughter of Ishmael, Abraham's son, the sister of Nebajoth, to be his wife in addition to the wives he had" (28:9). The text describes her by her relationship to her famous grandfather, her father, and her eldest brother. Genesis 36:3 lists Esau's wife as "Basemath, Ishmael's daughter, sister of Nebajoth." Genesis 36 gives other names for Esau's wives that differ from earlier chapters, and this is generally understood as the same woman known by another name.

Why name Nebaioth at all? Naming her brother may identify her as a full sister of the firstborn, or it may reflect Nebaioth's standing as the leading son of the clan. Some go further and suggest Ishmael had died or was no longer leading the family. The text says Esau "went to Ishmael," however, so that point should not be pressed. What is clear is that Nebaioth was prominent enough to serve as a family landmark.

The genealogical record. Centuries later, after the exile, the Chronicler opened Israel's history with Adam. Before narrowing to Israel, he traced the nations, including "The firstborn of Ishmael was Nebajoth; then Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam" (1 Chronicles 1:29). Even a book written to reassure returning Judeans of their identity keeps Ishmael's sons on the record.

The prophetic ingathering. Nebaioth then drops out of Israel's story until Isaiah 60. There Zion is told, "Arise, shine; for your light has come" (60:1, paraphrased), and the nations come to that light. The wealth of the Gentiles arrives on camels from Midian, Ephah, and Sheba (60:5–6). Then: "All the flocks of Kedar shall be gathered together to you, The rams of Nebaioth shall minister to you; They shall ascend with acceptance on My altar, And I will glorify the house of My glory" (60:7). These two Ishmaelite peoples, desert tribes known for their herds, bring their best animals to the Lord's altar. A related name with its own Strong's entry, Kedar, appears in a similar hope elsewhere, where Isaiah calls the villages Kedar inhabits to sing praise to the Lord (Isaiah 42:11).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God's promise of a chosen line went through Isaac and not Ishmael (Genesis 17:21; 21:12). It states just as plainly that God blessed Ishmael, heard him, was with him (21:20), and fulfilled His word by giving him twelve princes, beginning with Nebaioth. Being outside the line of promise did not put Ishmael's family outside God's attention or care.

Esau's marriage invites a careful observation. Genesis presents his choice as a response to his parents' displeasure, but it does not say God approved of it or that it changed anything. Esau had already despised his birthright (Genesis 25:34), and Hebrews later describes him as one who "found no place for repentance" though he sought the blessing with tears (Hebrews 12:17, paraphrased). Esau added an Ishmaelite wife to the Canaanite wives he already had, so the marriage did not remove the original problem, and Genesis does not describe his private motives or the state of his heart in this choice. As a pastoral comparison, not a revealed verdict, the scene can be read as a man trying to improve his standing through family connections. Isaiah 60 shows Nebaioth's people drawn to God in a very different way, by the Lord's light and His own initiative.

The most striking development is the word "acceptance." Isaiah does not merely say that Nebaioth's rams will be brought to Jerusalem as tribute. He says they will go up on God's altar and be accepted. Isaiah has already promised that foreigners who join themselves to the Lord will have their offerings accepted on His altar, because His house will be called a house of prayer for all nations (Isaiah 56:6–7, paraphrased). Isaiah 60:7 gives that hope names and faces. The people descended from the son who was sent away are welcomed into worship at the center.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never names Nebaioth, but Revelation clearly draws on Isaiah 60 to describe the New Jerusalem. Isaiah speaks of the Lord as Zion's everlasting light and of gates that stay open to receive the wealth of the nations (Isaiah 60:11, 19). John echoes this: "And the nations of those who are saved shall walk in its light, and the kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into it. Its gates shall not be shut at all by day (there shall be no night there). And they shall bring the glory and the honor of the nations into it" (Revelation 21:24–26). Because the New Testament explicitly develops the vision Nebaioth belongs to, its hope is not left as a vague theme.

Christ is the reason such worship can be accepted. Isaiah used the language of his own day, rams on an altar. The New Testament teaches that animal sacrifices have given way to the one offering of Christ (Hebrews 10:10–14), and that the city John saw has no temple, "for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple" (Revelation 21:22, paraphrased). Paul describes his gospel work among the Gentiles in priestly terms, so that "the offering of the Gentiles might be acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit" (Romans 15:16, paraphrased). That is very close to what Isaiah saw: outsiders made into an acceptable offering to God. In Christ, "you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ. For He Himself is our peace, who has made both one, and has broken down the middle wall of separation" (Ephesians 2:13–14).

5. Spirit & Life

Ishmael's name recalls that the Lord had heard Hagar's affliction (Genesis 16:11). The story of his firstborn shows that God's hearing does not end in the wilderness. A family sent away with a little bread and water is counted in Scripture's records, remembered in the genealogies, and finally pictured at God's altar. People who feel they come from the wrong side of the story, outside the circle of blessing, should take that seriously. God keeps His word even to those who are not at the center of the narrative, and He has always intended to bring the far-off near.

The contrast between Esau and Isaiah's vision also speaks plainly. As a pastoral analogy, Esau's marriage looks like an attempt to improve his standing by making the right connection. Isaiah shows people coming because God's light has drawn them, offering their best, and being received because God says so. Acceptance with God is not secured by strategy or family ties. It is given through the sacrifice He provides. Those who belong to Christ now offer themselves and their praise as sacrifices acceptable to God through Him (Romans 12:1; 1 Peter 2:5). Whatever "rams" a believer brings in obedience, whether time, gifts, or work, are welcome at His altar for Christ's sake and not on their own merit (compare Romans 12:2).

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:20; 21:17–18 – God's promise to bless Ishmael and make him a great nation with twelve princes, the promise Nebaioth's birth begins to fulfill.

  • Genesis 25:12–18 – Nebaioth listed as Ishmael's firstborn, along with the region where Ishmael's descendants lived.

  • Genesis 28:6–9 – Esau marries "Mahalath... the sister of Nebajoth" in an effort to please Isaac.

  • Genesis 36:3 – The same marriage recorded under the name Basemath (on the common harmonization that these are the same woman).

  • 1 Chronicles 1:29 – Nebaioth preserved in the post-exilic genealogy of the nations.

  • Isaiah 56:6–7 – Foreigners' offerings promised acceptance at God's altar, the background for Isaiah 60:7.

  • Isaiah 60:1–11 – The nations come to Zion's light, and the rams of Nebaioth are accepted on the Lord's altar.

  • Revelation 21:22–26 – The New Jerusalem receives the glory and honor of the nations, drawing directly on Isaiah 60.

  • Ephesians 2:13–14; Romans 15:16 – In Christ the far-off are brought near, and the Gentiles become an acceptable offering.
