THOUSAND, CLAN, DIVISION - (ʼeleph, Strong's H0505)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼeleph means "thousand." Throughout the Old Testament it counts soldiers, sheep, silver and sacrifices. It also names a large family group within a tribe, which the NKJV translates "clan" or "division." Gideon uses this second sense when he protests that "my clan is the weakest in Manasseh" (Judges 6:15). Micah uses it when he calls Bethlehem "little among the thousands of Judah" (Micah 5:2), and then announces that Israel's eternal Ruler will come from that small town.

Because a thousand stood for a very large number in everyday life, Scripture often sets it beside God to show what human counting cannot measure. God judges sin "to the third and fourth generations" but shows mercy "to thousands" (Exodus 20:5–6). One day in His courts is better than a thousand anywhere else (Psalm 84:10). One man chases a thousand when the LORD fights for His people (Joshua 23:10). A thousand years are like yesterday to Him (Psalm 90:4). Israel used this word to count its strength, and Scripture uses the same word to show that God's mercy, worth and power are beyond counting.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The word appears most often in Numbers and Chronicles, the books that record census totals, army musters, temple gifts and sacrifices. Its second sense grew out of Israel's wilderness organization. Jethro advised Moses to appoint "rulers of thousands" so that the work of judging the people could be shared (Exodus 18:21, 25). The large family units of each tribe also supplied soldiers when Israel went to war, so a "thousand" could be a family group and a military company at the same time.

Some scholars suggest that in the wilderness census lists of Numbers, ʼeleph means "unit" or "clan" rather than a literal thousand, which would produce smaller totals. The question is still debated. The spiritual uses of the word discussed in this study do not depend on how it is settled.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A thousand. The number 1,000, often combined with other numbers to form large totals, such as "forty-six thousand five hundred" in the census of Reuben (Numbers 1:21). In poetry it often works as a round number for vast abundance, as in "the cattle on a thousand hills" (Psalm 50:10).

2. A clan or division. A major family group within a tribe. At Saul's selection as king, Samuel told the people to "present yourselves before the LORD by your tribes and by your clans" (1 Samuel 10:19). The NKJV translates the same word as "divisions" when it refers to the leaders of these groups (Numbers 10:4; Joshua 22:14, 21, 30).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses are plain record-keeping. Scripture counts the exiles who returned with Zerubbabel (Ezra 2), the gold given for the temple and the animals offered at its feasts. These figures remind readers that God's people were real people in real history, and that their giving was remembered.

The word also names leadership. Samuel warns that a king would "appoint captains over his thousands" (1 Samuel 8:12), and David consulted "with the captains of thousands and hundreds" before bringing up the ark (1 Chronicles 13:1).

In poetry and prophecy, a thousand is usually set against one. The comparison may favor the one, as in Psalm 84:10, or it may show how rare a thing is, as when the Preacher found one upright man among a thousand (Ecclesiastes 7:28).

The prophets use "thousand" as a picture of fullness that judgment cuts down. "The city that goes out by a thousand Shall have a hundred left" (Amos 5:3). Isaiah describes the most valuable land in Judah, "wherever there could be a thousand vines Worth a thousand shekels of silver," turning to "briers and thorns" (Isaiah 7:23). A full community or a prosperous vineyard gives no protection once God's judgment falls.

4. Theological Significance

When Scripture measures God against a thousand, it shows that God is the owner of everything and needs nothing from anyone. "For every beast of the forest is Mine, And the cattle on a thousand hills" (Psalm 50:10). In that psalm, God tells Israel He will take no bull from their houses as though He were hungry (Psalm 50:9, 12). The God who owns every hill cannot be enriched by a gift.

That truth gives meaning to the great offerings of Israel's history. Solomon "offered a thousand burnt offerings" at Gibeon (1 Kings 3:4; 2 Chronicles 1:6), and that same night the LORD appeared to him in a dream. When the temple was being prepared, Israel's leaders gave "five thousand talents and ten thousand darics of gold" (1 Chronicles 29:7). The next day they offered "a thousand bulls, a thousand rams, a thousand lambs" (1 Chronicles 29:21). David's prayer between those two verses explains why they gave so freely: everything they offered had first come from God's hand (1 Chronicles 29:14). Generosity on that scale was the response of people who knew who owned it all.

Micah adds the needed counterweight. He asks whether the LORD would be pleased with "thousands of rams" (Micah 6:7), and answers that God requires justice, mercy and a humble walk with Him (Micah 6:8). A thousand offerings honor God only when they come from such a heart. Without it, they cannot buy His favor.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Mercy to thousands (Exodus 20:5–6; Deuteronomy 7:9). The Ten Commandments place two numbers side by side. God visits iniquity "upon the children to the third and fourth generations of those who hate Me," "but showing mercy to thousands, to those who love Me and keep My commandments" (Exodus 20:5–6; repeated in Deuteronomy 5:10). Deuteronomy spells out what the "thousands" means: God "keeps covenant and mercy for a thousand generations with those who love Him" (Deuteronomy 7:9). Three or four generations is roughly the span of one household, the great-grandchildren a person might live to see. A thousand generations stretches beyond all recorded history. When the LORD proclaimed His name to Moses, He repeated the pairing and placed mercy first (Exodus 34:6–7). God's judgment is real, and it has limits. His mercy far outlasts it. This mercy is also personal: it rests on "those who love Me." God's jealousy in verse 5 is the jealousy of a love that will not share His people with idols.

One chases a thousand (Joshua 23:10; Deuteronomy 32:30; Isaiah 30:17). In his farewell, Joshua promised, "One man of you shall chase a thousand, for the LORD your God is He who fights for you." Moses had already described the reverse. If Israel was routed, it could only be because "their Rock had sold them" (Deuteronomy 32:30). Isaiah saw that warning come true when Judah trusted Egypt's horses instead of the LORD: "One thousand shall flee at the threat of one" (Isaiah 30:17).

Israel's history shows the promise in action. God reduced Gideon's army until twenty-two thousand had gone home, so that Israel could not claim it had saved itself (Judges 7:2–3). After the Spirit of the LORD came upon Samson, he picked up "a fresh jawbone of a donkey" and killed "a thousand men with it" (Judges 15:14–15). A donkey's jawbone was no real weapon. The outcome of a battle depended on whether the LORD was present, not on how many soldiers stood on each side.

The least of the clans (Judges 6:15; Micah 5:2). In the clan sense, the word marks the smallness God keeps choosing. Gideon objected, "Indeed my clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house" (Judges 6:15). God answered with the promise of His presence (Judges 6:16) and did not argue that Gideon was stronger than he thought. Micah then addresses a town that would have seemed easy to overlook: "But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, Though you are little among the thousands of Judah, Yet out of you shall come forth to Me The One to be Ruler in Israel, Whose goings forth are from of old, From everlasting" (Micah 5:2). Micah sets Bethlehem's smallness right beside the Ruler's eternal origin. The King whose existence has no beginning would enter history through one of Judah's least impressive clans.

Better than a thousand (Psalm 84:10; 119:72). The psalmists use the word to name the most a person could want and then set it aside. "For a day in Your courts is better than a thousand. I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God." Standing at the threshold of God's house is better than living comfortably anywhere else. Psalm 119 measures God's Word the same way: "The law of Your mouth is better to me Than thousands of coins of gold and silver" (Psalm 119:72).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Gospel states the fulfillment of Micah's prophecy directly. When Herod asked where the Christ would be born, the chief priests and scribes quoted Micah and pointed to Bethlehem (Matthew 2:4–6). The pattern seen in Gideon's clan and Bethlehem's smallness continues in the gospel. Paul writes that "God has chosen the weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty" (1 Corinthians 1:27). The Son of God came from the least of the thousands of Judah, so the credit for salvation belongs to God alone.

The word's use for time also leads toward Christ. Moses wrote, "For a thousand years in Your sight Are like yesterday when it is past" (Psalm 90:4). Peter took up those words while believers wondered why Christ had not yet returned: "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Peter 3:8). The delay, Peter explains, is God's patience, not slowness (2 Peter 3:9). Revelation then uses the Greek word for thousand when it describes the saints who "lived and reigned with Christ for a thousand years" (Revelation 20:4). Believers differ on whether that period is literal or symbolic. Either way, the last great "thousand" in Scripture belongs to the reign of Christ with His people.

7. Spirit & Life

People count things all the time: money, enemies, years left. This word shows the true size of what we are counting. Psalm 50:10 removes the idea that we can enrich God, and 1 Chronicles 29 removes the fear that giving to Him leaves us poorer, because what we give was His already.

Exodus 20:6 speaks directly to anyone who fears that a family's sin has settled his or her future. Judgment reaches a few generations. Mercy reaches thousands. A believer who loves God stands in the larger of those two numbers, and that mercy is meant to run on to his or her children.

Gideon's objection is common among believers who feel too small for God's call. God did not answer it by flattering Gideon. He answered with His presence, and that same answer brought Christ out of Bethlehem. Weakness does not disqualify anyone God has chosen. It is often where God chooses to work.

Psalm 84 also gives a test. If one day near God is worth more than a thousand days elsewhere, then the time we give to His presence and His Word is never wasted.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 18:21 — Moses appoints "rulers of thousands." God provided capable leaders so that one tired man would not carry the whole nation alone.

  • 1 Kings 19:18 — When Elijah believed he was the only faithful one left, God told him He had kept seven thousand in Israel who had not bowed to Baal. Paul later cites this to show that in his own day too "there is a remnant according to the election of grace" (Romans 11:4–5).

  • Psalm 91:7 — The psalmist pictures a thousand falling at the believer's side while the one who shelters in God is kept safe.

  • Psalm 105:8 — God remembers His word "for a thousand generations" (also 1 Chronicles 16:15). His promises last as long as His mercy.

  • Isaiah 60:22 — God promises that the little one will become a thousand. Isaiah's picture of Zion's future glory looks back to God's long habit of starting small.

  • Daniel 8:14 — "For two thousand three hundred days; then the sanctuary shall be cleansed." Even a long period of distress has a limit, and God has set its number.
