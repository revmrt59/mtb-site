NOBLE, WILLING, GENEROUS, PRINCE - (nâdîyb, Strong's H5081)

Summary

The Hebrew word nâdîyb describes a person who is noble. The Old Testament uses that idea in two connected ways. Sometimes it refers to character: a heart that is willing and generous and gives freely without being forced. More often it refers to rank, the "princes" and "nobles" who sat at the top of society. The NKJV translates it as "willing," "generous," "noble," "princes," and "nobles," depending on which of these two sides is in view.

Scripture holds these two meanings side by side and lets them question each other. Israel's tabernacle and temple were built from the gifts of people with "a willing heart." The psalms warn against trusting princes. God reverses human status by lifting the poor from the dust to sit among princes. A broken David asks God to uphold him by His "generous Spirit." Isaiah looks ahead to a righteous kingdom where no fool will be called noble and the truly generous will stand. Across these passages, true nobility is a heart made willing by God, not a title. In Jesus Christ, the King who freely gave Himself, the title and the character finally belong to the same person.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâdîyb is a Hebrew word that can work as a noun (a noble, a prince) or as a description (willing, generous). It is related to the verb nadab (H5068), which means to volunteer or to offer something freely. The word appears in three main settings: the giving that built Israel's places of worship (Exodus, Chronicles), wisdom and poetry that weigh the worth of powerful people (Job, Psalms, Proverbs), and Isaiah's prophecies about righteous leadership. The word's connection to nadab helps explain why "willing" and "noble" can sit together in one word. The meaning in any given verse still comes from its context.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Willing, generous in heart. This sense describes a person who gives or serves freely, without compulsion. Example: "Whoever is of a willing heart, let him bring it as an offering to the LORD" (Exodus 35:5).

2. Noble, prince, person of rank. This sense describes someone of high social or political standing. Example: "It is better to trust in the LORD Than to put confidence in princes" (Psalm 118:9).

A few passages, especially Isaiah 32, deliberately draw on both senses at once.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the word describes the heart, it belongs to worship. People bring gold, skill, and sacrifices because they want to (Exodus 35:22; 1 Chronicles 28:21, "every willing craftsman"; 2 Chronicles 29:31). When it names a class of people, the tone shifts. Princes are courted ("Many entreat the favor of the nobility," Proverbs 19:6), and wisdom rules through them (Proverbs 8:16). Yet the poets and wisdom writers keep them at a distance. Job's friend Elihu reminds Job that God does not tremble before nobles (Job 34:18–19). Babylon's "gates of the nobles" become the target of judgment (Isaiah 13:2). The word also carries warmth in the Song of Solomon, where the bride is called a "prince's daughter" (Song 7:1). The subject and the setting decide whether the word is praising a heart or simply naming a rank.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God who wants hearts offered freely, not forced. When He asked Israel for the gifts that would build His tabernacle, He called for willing hearts: "From everyone who gives it willingly with his heart you shall take My offering" (Exodus 25:2, using the related verb). The tabernacle was the place where God would dwell among His people, and His invitation welcomed their freely offered love. The same pattern returns at the temple under David and at Hezekiah's rededication. God makes His home among people who welcome Him.

The second truth is the reliability of God set against the unreliability of rank. "Do not put your trust in princes, Nor in a son of man, in whom there is no help" (Psalm 146:3). The next verse explains why: the prince dies, and his plans perish with him. God is not impressed by human status. "He pours contempt on princes, And disarms the mighty" (Job 12:21), and He "causes them to wander in the wilderness where there is no way" (Psalm 107:40). The powerful can lose their way as easily as anyone. Trust placed in them rests on someone who is as frail as the person trusting.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Samuel 2:8 and Psalm 113:8 — God makes new nobles. Hannah had been barren and mocked. Her prayer celebrates a God who "raises the poor from the dust And lifts the beggar from the ash heap, To set them among princes And make them inherit the throne of glory." The ash heap was where outcasts sat. Psalm 113 takes up her words and adds a telling detail: "That He may seat him with princes— With the princes of His people." The nobility God grants is not a borrowed place in a pagan court. It is a place among His own people. God gives honor as a gift, and He gives it to people who had nothing to recommend them.

Psalm 51:12 — Upheld by a generous Spirit. David prays this psalm after his sin with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah (see the psalm's heading). Israel's king had used his royal power to take what was not his. Now he prays, "Restore to me the joy of Your salvation, And uphold me by Your generous Spirit." The Hebrew can be read as God's own willing, generous Spirit, as the NKJV's capital letter indicates, or as a willing spirit that God creates in David. Verse 11 has just pleaded, "Do not take Your Holy Spirit from me," and the two readings meet there. The generosity David needs comes from God, and it produces willingness in him.

David is not asking to be propped up for a while so that he can carry on by himself. He has learned that his own heart failed under the weight of power. The only nobility that will hold is one God supplies and keeps supplying. The willing heart that once built the tabernacle cannot be produced on command, not even by a king. It must be given and sustained by God. This is why the psalm moves next to teaching sinners God's ways (v. 13) and to a "broken and a contrite heart" (v. 17). Restored willingness flows outward.

Isaiah 32:1–8 — When names match hearts. Isaiah announces a king who will reign in righteousness (32:1). In that kingdom, he says, "The foolish person will no longer be called generous, Nor the miser said to be bountiful" (32:5). Fallen societies flatter people with power, calling the wealthy fool "noble" simply because of his position. Isaiah promises an end to that false labeling. Verse 8 uses the word three times: the generous person plans generous things, and by generosity he stands. Nobility will no longer be a matter of birth or office. It will be a settled way of life that plans good for others, and in that sense the person will stand firm.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

From beginning to end, the Old Testament shows a split between the title "prince" and the character "willing." Israel's earthly rulers failed, David among them, and the psalms concluded that no prince could save. Jesus closes that gap. He is the righteous King whom Isaiah's vision anticipates, and He is willing to the core. He said that no one takes His life from Him; He lays it down of Himself (John 10:18, paraphrased). Paul describes His generosity this way: though He was rich, for our sakes He became poor, so that through His poverty we might become rich (2 Corinthians 8:9, paraphrased). The prince who could rightly have taken everything gave everything instead.

The reversal Hannah sang about reaches its full scope in the gospel. Mary's song in Luke 1 echoes Hannah's, and in Christ the poor in spirit are lifted from the ash heap to share an inheritance with Him. Psalm 47:9 extends the vision to the nations: "The princes of the people have gathered together, The people of the God of Abraham." The rulers of the world, often pictured in the psalms as rebels, gather as worshipers. God promised Abraham that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3), and the psalm pictures that promise reaching even the powerful. The church, drawn from every nation to worship Abraham's God through Christ, is the beginning of that gathering.

7. Spirit & Life

The tabernacle and Psalm 51 belong together. In Exodus, God's dwelling was built from willing hearts. In Psalm 51, a sinner asks God to supply the willing heart he no longer has. This means believers do not have to manufacture devotion before they come to God. When our willingness has collapsed through sin, weariness, or the slow wear of self-interest, David's prayer is still open to us: uphold me by Your generous Spirit. God is the source of the very generosity He desires.

This also sets us free from courting human favor. Proverbs observes that many seek the favor of the nobility, and Psalm 146 calls that a dead end. A believer who has been seated among the princes of God's people does not need to scramble for a place at someone else's table. Jesus applied the same wisdom when He told guests to take the lower seat and wait to be invited higher (Luke 14:8–10). That counsel echoes Proverbs 25:7, where it is better to be told "Come up here" than to be put down in the presence of a prince.

For anyone who leads, whether a parent, pastor, or employer, Isaiah 32:8 is the measure. A noble person in God's kingdom is someone who plans generous things for others, whatever title they hold.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 21:18 — In Israel's song about the well, the princes and nobles dig with their staffs. Here leaders labor to provide water for the people.

  • Proverbs 8:16 — Wisdom says that by her nobles rule. Rightful authority depends on God's wisdom, not on rank alone.

  • Proverbs 17:7 — Lying lips are even less fitting for a prince than fine speech is for a fool. Rank raises the expectation of integrity.

  • Psalm 83:11 — The psalmist asks God to make Israel's enemies' nobles like Oreb and Zeeb. Hostile rulers fall as Midian's leaders fell.

  • Song of Solomon 6:12 — The bride finds herself among the chariots of her noble people, a picture of being honored by love.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H5068 (nadab) — A lexical relative: the verb meaning to volunteer or give freely. Exodus 25:2 uses it for those who give "willingly with his heart," which prepares for the "willing heart" of Exodus 35:5.
