DRIVE, LEAD, GUIDE - (nâhag, Strong's H5090)

Summary

The Hebrew word nâhag is the verb a herdsman uses for moving animals along. It means to drive, lead, or guide, whether the thing being moved is a flock, a team pulling a chariot, a cart, or a line of captives. Jacob drives his livestock toward Canaan. Moses leads Jethro's flock to Horeb. Jehu is recognized from far off because "he drives furiously." Raiders carry off David's family at Ziklag. The word does not decide in advance whether the leading is gentle or harsh. The one doing the driving, and his purpose, settle that.

That is why the word matters so much when God holds the reins. The same verb describes the Lord driving rebellious Israel among the nations and the Shepherd of Israel leading Joseph like a flock. Isaiah promises that "He who has mercy on them will lead them" to springs of water. The psalmist says, "He will be our guide even to death." Isaiah even pictures a coming kingdom where "a little child shall lead" the wolf and the lion. Across these passages, nâhag shows a God who moves His people with purpose. Even His discipline is aimed at bringing them home, and His leading ends at living water.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâhag comes from the everyday world of herding, travel, and war. In Israel's pastoral economy, people constantly drove animals from pasture to pasture. The same action of steering something from behind or ahead applied naturally to chariots and carts, and grimly to prisoners marched off after a battle. The verb appears in narrative (Genesis, Exodus, Samuel, Kings), in law (Deuteronomy), in poetry and wisdom (Psalms, Job, Ecclesiastes, Song of Solomon), and in prophecy (Isaiah, Lamentations, Nahum). Because Israel knew this action so well, the picture was ready to hand when psalmists and prophets wanted to describe how God moves His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, nâhag means to make something or someone move forward under direction. Context decides whether that direction feels like force or care.

1. To drive or lead animals, people, or a vehicle. Moses "led the flock to the back of the desert" (Exodus 3:1), and Jehu "drives furiously" (2 Kings 9:20).

2. To carry off captives or plunder. The Amalekites "carried them away" from Ziklag (1 Samuel 30:2), and Assyria will "lead away the Egyptians as prisoners" (Isaiah 20:4).

3. To guide or direct, including directing oneself. God "guided them in the wilderness like a flock" (Psalm 78:52). The Preacher speaks of "guiding my heart with wisdom" (Ecclesiastes 2:3).

4. To moan or lament. Many lexicons treat this as a separate word spelled the same way. It probably appears in Nahum 2:7, where NKJV reads "lead."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When human beings are the drivers, the word often carries haste, force, or loss. Laban accuses Jacob of carrying away his daughters "like captives taken with the sword" (Genesis 31:26). Egyptian chariots lurch along after God takes off their wheels, "so that they drove them with difficulty" (Exodus 14:25). Jehu's reckless speed identifies him before anyone can see his face. Job lists among the crimes of the wicked that they "drive away the donkey of the fatherless" (Job 24:3).

When God is the subject, the word reaches beyond what any herdsman can do. He drives the wind itself: "the LORD brought an east wind on the land" to carry the locusts (Exodus 10:13), and "by His power He brought in the south wind" with the quail (Psalm 78:26). He drives nations and He leads His flock.

Nahum 2:7 needs a brief note. NKJV says Nineveh's maidservants "shall lead her as with the voice of doves, beating their breasts." The doves' cooing and the breast-beating point to grief, so most interpreters understand the maidens as moaning while their city is led away captive. The two senses sit together in that scene. A queen is driven off, and her servants sigh behind her.

4. Theological Significance

Because the verb is neutral in itself, its uses with God as subject reveal His character. He is Lord over the forces people cannot steer. The wind that ruins Egypt and the wind that feeds Israel in the wilderness are both driven by His hand.

He is also the Judge who drives. Moses warns that if Israel turns to idols, "the LORD will scatter you among the peoples," among "the nations where the LORD will drive you" (Deuteronomy 4:27; compare 28:37). Lamentations gives the sentence a human voice: "He has led me and made me walk in darkness and not in light" (Lamentations 3:2). Yet the warning in Deuteronomy 4 continues straight on. From that place of exile Israel would seek the Lord and find Him, because He is merciful and would not forget His promises to their fathers (4:29–31; see also 30:1–3). Lamentations 3 likewise turns, within the same chapter, to mercies that are new every morning (3:22–23). The God who drives His people into exile intends to lead them back, so His severity serves His mercy.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 3:1; Psalm 78:52; Psalm 80:1 — the Shepherd of Israel. Moses "led the flock to the back of the desert, and came to Horeb, the mountain of God." At that mountain God told him that he and the people would serve God there (Exodus 3:12). The ground Moses crossed with Jethro's sheep became the ground he would cross with Israel. Psalm 78 tells the result from God's side: "He made His own people go forth like sheep, and guided them in the wilderness like a flock." Psalm 80:1 builds a prayer on it: "O Shepherd of Israel, You who lead Joseph like a flock." Moses was the shepherd people could see. The psalms name the Shepherd who was actually leading.

Isaiah 49:10; Psalm 48:14 — mercy all the way to the end. Isaiah promises that the returning people "shall neither hunger nor thirst," for "He who has mercy on them will lead them, even by the springs of water He will guide them." The leading comes from mercy, and it ends at water, shade, and rest. Psalm 48 closes its celebration of Zion's security by moving from the city to God Himself: "For this is God, our God forever and ever; He will be our guide even to death." Some scholars read the final phrase as a musical note. Taken as NKJV renders it, the promise is plain. God's guidance does not stop short of the last step a person takes.

Isaiah 11:6 — predators led by a child. Under the Branch from Jesse, "the wolf also shall dwell with the lamb... and a little child shall lead them." Herdsmen drove cattle and sheep. Nobody drove lions. Isaiah uses the herding verb to show a creation so changed by the Messiah's righteous rule that the fiercest animals go where the weakest human leads them.

2 Samuel 6:3; 1 Chronicles 13:7 — driving the ark. David's men "set the ark of God on a new cart," and "Uzzah and Ahio... drove the new cart." The Philistines had sent the ark home on a cart (1 Samuel 6:7–8), but God had assigned it to the Levites, who were to carry it on their shoulders. When the oxen stumbled, Uzzah took hold of the ark and died. David later acknowledged that they had not sought God about the proper order (1 Chronicles 15:13–15). The cart was handled as if God's holy presence were freight to be moved by the most convenient method. The second, successful attempt bore the ark the way God had said.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Shepherd who led Joseph like a flock comes in person in Jesus: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). Moses learned the wilderness road with sheep before he led a nation along it. Christ goes further, laying down His life for the flock He leads.

Isaiah 49:10 reappears almost word for word at the end of the Bible's story. Revelation 7 describes people who will not hunger or thirst, "for the Lamb who is in the midst of the throne will shepherd them and lead them to living fountains of waters" (7:17). The merciful Leader of Isaiah is the Lamb, and the springs are now living fountains. Isaiah 11 belongs to the same hope. Paul says creation was "subjected to futility... in hope" and "will be delivered from the bondage of corruption" (Romans 8:20–21), and Isaiah's child leading the lion shows what that deliverance looks like.

The elements obey Him too. The Lord who drove the east wind over Egypt stood in a boat and "rebuked the wind... and there was a great calm" (Mark 4:39). The captive-driving of war is also turned around. Isaiah sees Zion's gates open day and night so that the wealth of the nations may come in, "and their kings in procession" (Isaiah 60:11). The same verb that described prisoners marched away now describes kings brought into the city of God.

7. Spirit & Life

Nâhag raises a searching question: what is driving you? Jehu's fury, Egypt's chariots, and the raiders at Ziklag show human driving at its worst. It is fast, forceful, and careless of whoever gets run over. Much of life runs that way, pushed by fear, ambition, and appetite. Nahum's moaning maidens show where that kind of driving ends. They are breathless with grief. Paul hears the same groan in a fallen creation and in believers who wait for redemption (Romans 8:22–23).

Isaiah 63:14 shows a different kind of leading: "As a beast goes down into the valley, and the Spirit of the LORD causes him to rest, so You lead Your people, to make Yourself a glorious name." God's leading brings His people to rest. When His guidance feels like pressure without peace, it is fair to ask whether something else has taken the reins.

Uzzah's cart warns against bringing that driven, practical spirit into worship. Convenience is not reverence. God's presence is to be carried on His terms. For our own hearts, Ecclesiastes 2:3 offers a smaller picture of guiding the heart with wisdom. Even Solomon needed reins.

Psalm 48:14 is the deepest comfort. The God who leads His people through the wilderness, through discipline, and through darkness will still be guiding at the end, "even to death," and the Lamb will lead beyond it to living fountains.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 31:18 — Jacob "carried away all his livestock" toward Canaan, which is the word in its plainest herding sense.

  • 1 Samuel 30:20, 22 — After recovering his family, David's men drive the recovered flocks before them, and some begrudge sharing them. The driving that had stolen everything is turned back on the raiders.

  • 2 Kings 4:24 — The Shunammite tells her servant, "Drive, and go forward," hurrying toward Elisha to seek help for her dead son. This is urgent driving in the service of faith.

  • Song of Solomon 8:2 — The bride longs to "lead" her beloved into her mother's house, an unusual use where leading expresses love.

  • 2 Chronicles 25:11 — Amaziah "strengthened himself, and leading his people" went out to battle, a use of the word for a king's command.
