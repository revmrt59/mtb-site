GUIDE, LEAD - (nâhal, Strong's H5095)

Summary

The Hebrew verb nâhal means to lead or guide, and it usually describes a leader who takes responsibility for the well-being of those who follow. It appears only ten times in the Old Testament. NKJV renders it "lead," "guide," "guided," and "leads," and in two places it reads "fed" and "ride." Jacob uses it of moving his weary flocks and young children at a pace they could bear. The narrative uses it of Joseph carrying Egypt through famine with bread. It describes Israelite soldiers setting feeble captives on donkeys. David uses it when he says the Lord "leads me beside the still waters." In this word, guidance includes care, provision, and rest.

The word's greatest significance comes in Isaiah, where the sovereign Lord "gently lead[s] those who are with young" and guides His people "by the springs of water." The God whose arm rules the nations sets His pace by the weakest in His flock. The same shepherding is completed in the Lamb who leads His people "to living fountains of waters" (Revelation 7:17). Where this guidance is missing, as in ruined Jerusalem with "no one to guide her," the result is desolation.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâhal comes from the world of herding. Hebrew has a more common verb for leading someone in a direction, nachah (H5148). Nâhal stays closer to a shepherd's practical work: bringing animals to water, letting them rest, and keeping the weak alive along the way. Its ten uses run from Genesis through Isaiah. They appear in family narrative, famine, war, worship songs, and prophecy. The word moves easily from literal flocks to the Lord's care for His people because Israel knew firsthand what a good shepherd did.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, nâhal means to lead with care, guiding others so they are sustained and brought safely to rest. Context determines how much of that care is in view.

1. To lead or guide gently, with attention to the strength of those being led. The flocks in Genesis 33:14 and the ewes in Isaiah 40:11 are examples.

2. To lead to refreshment, bringing others to water and rest. Psalm 23:2 and Isaiah 49:10 use the word this way.

3. To sustain or provide for, carrying people through hardship. Joseph "fed them with bread" (Genesis 47:17), and the Lord "guided them on every side" after the Assyrian siege (2 Chronicles 32:22).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the narratives, the subject of nâhal is a human leader, and the people being led are vulnerable. They include livestock, children, starving families, and captives. The verb measures the leader by how well those dependent on him fare. In the poetry and prophecy, the Lord becomes the subject, and the same practical care becomes a portrait of His character.

The English renderings show the range. "Fed" in Genesis 47:17 and "ride" in 2 Chronicles 28:15 can look out of place, but they fit the word. Guiding a starving people meant feeding them. Guiding exhausted captives meant carrying them. In 2 Chronicles 32:22 the exact sense is debated, and some translations prefer "gave them rest" or "provided for them." Either way, the phrase describes the Lord's care on every side once the danger had passed.

4. Theological Significance

Nâhal shows that God's guidance carries out the purpose of His redemption. After the Red Sea, Moses sings that the Lord has guided the people He redeemed, in His strength, to His holy habitation (Exodus 15:13). Rescue from Egypt was the beginning of the journey. God's shepherding carries His people the rest of the way, into His own dwelling place. David leans on that same reality in Psalm 31:3: "For You are my rock and my fortress; Therefore, for Your name's sake, Lead me and guide me." David bases his confidence on God's name. The Lord has committed His reputation to finishing what He begins.

The word also shows God sustaining life when every human resource is gone. Joseph keeps Egypt alive through famine. The Lord delivers Hezekiah's Jerusalem "from the hand of Sennacherib" and then guides them "on every side" (2 Chronicles 32:22). Isaiah 51:18 shows the reverse: "There is no one to guide her Among all the sons she has brought forth." Jerusalem had raised many sons, but none of them could do what only the Lord could do. Without His sustaining care, the city lies desolate.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 33:14 — Jacob's pace. After meeting Esau, Jacob declines to travel with his brother's band of men. "I will lead on slowly at a pace which the livestock that go before me, and the children, are able to endure." The verse before explains why. One day of hard driving would kill the nursing flocks. Jacob's leadership is governed by the limits of the weakest animals and the youngest children. This is literal herding wisdom, and it supplies the picture Isaiah will later use for the Lord.

Isaiah 40:11 — The Sovereign Lord as Shepherd. Isaiah has just announced that "the Lord GOD shall come with a strong hand, And His arm shall rule for Him" (v. 10). The next verse turns that ruling arm to a different use: "He will gather the lambs with His arm, And carry them in His bosom, And gently lead those who are with young." The God who measures the waters in the hollow of His hand (v. 12) slows His pace for nursing ewes. His strength shows itself in tenderness toward the weak. When Isaiah used this word, Israel had a shepherd like Jacob to picture.

2 Chronicles 28:15 — Mercy for the captives. Israel had taken prisoners from Judah, and a prophet rebuked them for it. Then the men named for the task clothed the naked, gave them sandals, food, and drink, anointed them, "and they let all the feeble ones ride on donkeys" back to Jericho. Ancient victors usually drove captives on foot. Here the feeble are carried home. God required His people to lead the weak the way He leads.

Psalm 23:2 and Isaiah 49:10 — Where the Shepherd leads. "He leads me beside the still waters." The Shepherd's leading has a destination, which is water and rest. Isaiah widens this into a promise for the returning exiles: "For He who has mercy on them will lead them, Even by the springs of water He will guide them." Mercy is what moves the Shepherd, and refreshment is where He brings them. In that promise, hunger, thirst, and scorching sun no longer strike them.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The shepherd pattern in nâhal reaches its fullness in Jesus. He says, "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The One who sets His pace by the weak goes so far as to lay down His life for them. He calls the weary and heavily burdened to come to Him and promises rest, with a yoke that is easy and light (Matthew 11:28–30, paraphrased). The religious leaders loaded heavy burdens on people. Jesus leads at a pace His people can bear.

The destination named in Psalm 23 and Isaiah 49 is reached in Revelation 7:17: "the Lamb who is in the midst of the throne will shepherd them and lead them to living fountains of waters. And God will wipe away every tear from their eyes." John directly echoes Isaiah's springs of water, and he names the Shepherd as the Lamb who was slain.

Joseph also offers a fitting picture, though Scripture never calls him a type in this passage. Rejected by his brothers and raised to power, he keeps a starving world alive with bread. Christ, the Bread of Life, sustains His people in the same way when they have nothing left to give.

7. Spirit & Life

Many people think of guidance as directions given from a distance, after which they are left to find their own way. Nâhal corrects that idea. In Scripture, guidance can mean bread placed in empty hands, a donkey for a captive who cannot walk, or a city protected on every side. The Lord does more than point out the road. He sees that His people arrive.

This matters most for the weary. Isaiah 40:11 tells the believer who feels slow, burdened, or spent that the Lord has already set His pace by people in that condition. He does not ask the nursing ewe to keep up with the strong rams. A heavy season does not mean God has given up on you. He leads more gently in such times.

Isaiah 51:18 also gives a sober warning. Jerusalem's own sons could not guide her. Talent, heritage, and human strength cannot do the Shepherd's work. The prayer of Psalm 31:3, "for Your name's sake, Lead me and guide me," rests on the only guide who can bring us home.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 47:17 — Joseph "fed them with bread." This verse shows the word's sense of sustaining people through crisis.

  • Exodus 15:13 — The first use of the word for God's own action. The Lord guides His redeemed people to His holy habitation.

  • 2 Chronicles 32:22 — After the deliverance from Assyria, the Lord "guided them on every side," an image of complete care and security.

  • Psalm 31:3 — David's prayer for guidance, grounded in God's name and His strength as rock and fortress.

10. Other Relevant Insights

In Psalm 23:2 and Isaiah 40:11, nâhal appears inside open shepherd metaphors. The Lord is the Shepherd, and His people are His flock. His gentle leading is a picture of His character.

The waters of rest (Psalm 23:2) and springs of water (Isaiah 49:10) belong to a wider biblical image of God bringing His people to the source of life. That image culminates in the Lamb's "living fountains of waters" (Revelation 7:17). The mercy shown to the feeble captives in 2 Chronicles 28:15 stands in contrast to the forced marches of conquering empires. In God's way, strength serves the weak.

Joseph sustaining the famished world with bread (Genesis 47:17) is a fitting picture of Christ sustaining His people. The leaderless city in Isaiah 51:18 anticipates Jesus' lament that Jerusalem would not be gathered to Him and its house would be left desolate (Matthew 23:37–38). These are suggestive connections and should not be pressed as the meaning of the word. Genesis 33:14 and 2 Chronicles 28:15 remain literal history. They illustrate a pattern that Scripture later applies to God, but they are not allegories.
