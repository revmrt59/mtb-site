FLOW, STREAMS, FLOODS - (nâzal, Strong's H5140)

Summary

The Hebrew verb nâzal means "to flow." It covers almost every kind of moving water: dew settling on grass, rain dropping from clouds, a mountain stream running cold from the snows of Lebanon, and a torrent splitting out of desert rock. The NKJV translates it with words like "distill," "drop," "flow," "gush," "streams," and "floods." Often the word works as a noun, so that "flowing things" simply become "streams."

Scripture uses this ordinary word for water to describe some of God's greatest gifts. Moses prays that his teaching will distill like dew (Deuteronomy 32:2). Through Isaiah, God promises "floods on the dry ground" and then names what those floods mean: "I will pour My Spirit on your descendants" (Isaiah 44:3). He calls the skies to "pour down righteousness" (Isaiah 45:8). He makes streams run from a rock for a rebellious people in the wilderness, and Paul later says, "that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4). The same word also carries a warning. Jeremiah asks why anyone would abandon "the cold flowing waters" for strange ones (Jeremiah 18:14), and he describes a people whose eyelids "gush with water" in mourning. Life flows from God, and a people who turn from Him find that grief flows in its place.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâzal appears sixteen times in the Old Testament, entirely in poetry and prophecy: Balaam's oracle in Numbers, the songs of Moses and Deborah, Job, the Psalms, Proverbs, the Song of Solomon, Isaiah, and Jeremiah. This is the language of Israel's worship and of the prophets' preaching, not of legal codes or genealogies.

The setting helps explain why the word carries so much weight. Israel lived in a dry land where survival depended on rain in its season, dew on summer nights, and springs fed by distant snowmelt. A people who watched the sky for rain and treasured a spring that ran year-round heard "flowing water" as a description of life itself.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is the movement of a liquid: to flow, trickle, run, or pour. The word describes the action of flowing more than any particular substance, which is why it can describe spices flowing out on the wind. Its range runs from very gentle to overwhelming:

1. Gentle flowing or dropping, as dew distills or clouds drop rain (Deuteronomy 32:2; Job 36:28).

2. Steady running water, or streams, especially fresh, moving water as opposed to water stored in a container (Proverbs 5:15; Jeremiah 18:14).

3. Abundant or forceful flowing, or floods, water poured out in volume or gushing with force (Exodus 15:8; Isaiah 44:3; Isaiah 48:21).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears most often in recollections of God's mighty acts. Psalm 78 and Isaiah 48 look back to the water from the rock. Exodus 15:8 and Judges 5:5 celebrate God's power over sea and mountain. In these passages the flowing is something God causes, and it shows His hand at work.

In the prophets, flowing water becomes a picture of what God gives from above: His Spirit, His righteousness, His teaching. In the wisdom books, the word describes the refreshment of faithful married love. Proverbs 5:15 urges a husband, "Drink water from your own cistern, And running water from your own well," and the following verses make plain that this means delighting in "the wife of your youth" rather than straying to another woman (Proverbs 5:18–20).

One grammatical pattern is worth noting. Several times the word appears in a form meaning "flowing ones," which English naturally renders "streams" (Psalm 78:16; Song of Solomon 4:15). The effect is that the stream is named by what it does. In these passages a stream is water that keeps moving.

4. Theological Significance

These passages first show God's command over His creation. In the Song of Moses, the sea's "floods stood upright like a heap" at the blast of God's nostrils (Exodus 15:8). Water that by nature flows stood still like a wall. Deborah's song turns the picture around: "The mountains gushed before the LORD" (Judges 5:5). What is solid flowed at His presence. (Some translators read Judges 5:5 as "quaked," but the NKJV keeps the image of flowing.) Psalm 147:18 shows the same lordship in the ordinary turn of the seasons: "He sends out His word and melts them; He causes His wind to blow, and the waters flow." Every spring thaw answers to God's word. Creation does not run on its own. It moves when He speaks.

The second truth concerns God's speech. Moses opens his final song with a prayer: "Let my teaching drop as the rain, My speech distill as the dew, As raindrops on the tender herb" (Deuteronomy 32:2). Dew comes quietly, overnight, and in a dry climate it keeps tender plants alive between rains. Moses is asking that God's truth will soak into Israel the same way, so that the people are kept alive by it and not just informed. Isaiah 55:10–11 develops the same comparison: as rain waters the earth and makes it bud, God's word "shall not return to Me void."

A pattern runs through many of these uses. The flow begins above and moves down to human need: dew, clouds, skies, and a rock struck in the desert. People are pictured as thirsty ground waiting for what only God can send.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 44:3 — Floods of the Spirit on dry ground. Here nâzal reaches its fullest meaning. "For I will pour water on him who is thirsty, And floods on the dry ground; I will pour My Spirit on your descendants, And My blessing on your offspring." Hebrew poetry often says a thing twice, the second line explaining the first, and that is what happens here. The second half of the verse tells us what the "floods" are: God's own Spirit. The context sharpens the promise. Israel had been called "Jacob My servant" and "Jeshurun" (44:2), a people whose history was full of failure and who were facing exile. Yet God does not offer them a little damp relief. He promises floods, and He promises them to the next generation, the "descendants" and "offspring." The verses that follow picture those children springing up "like willows by the watercourses" (44:4). Where God's Spirit is poured out, a barren people becomes a growing people.

Isaiah 45:8 — Righteousness from the skies. "Rain down, you heavens, from above, And let the skies pour down righteousness; Let the earth open, let them bring forth salvation." The verse follows a clear order. The skies pour, and then the earth opens and brings forth. Righteousness here does not grow up out of the ground on its own. It falls first from above, and only then does salvation spring up. The verse ends by naming the source: "I, the LORD, have created it." Salvation is something God makes and sends. People are the soil that receives it.

Psalm 78:16 and Isaiah 48:21 — Streams from the rock. Psalm 78 recalls that God "brought streams out of the rock, And caused waters to run down like rivers." The very next lines say, "But they sinned even more against Him By rebelling against the Most High in the wilderness. And they tested God in their heart" (78:17–18). God gave the water to people who did not deserve it, and they answered with more complaint. Centuries later, Isaiah uses the same memory to comfort exiles about to leave Babylon: "He caused the waters to flow from the rock for them; He also split the rock, and the waters gushed out" (48:21). The God who supplied water to rebels in the desert would supply His people again on the road home.

Jeremiah 18:14 with 9:18 — Forsaking the flowing waters. Jeremiah asks a question that seems absurd: "Will a man leave the snow water of Lebanon... Will the cold flowing waters be forsaken for strange waters?" No one in a dry land would walk away from a spring that never fails. Yet that is exactly what Judah did. Jeremiah had already given God's verdict: "They have forsaken Me, the fountain of living waters, And hewn themselves cisterns—broken cisterns that can hold no water" (2:13). Elsewhere the same word describes what follows that choice. Jeremiah calls for mourning women to come quickly, "That our eyes may run with tears, And our eyelids gush with water" (9:18). People who would not drink from the living stream end up weeping. The word that once described God's gifts now describes their tears.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The flowing waters of nâzal point forward to Jesus Christ. Paul looks back at the wilderness and says Israel "drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them, and that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4). The streams of Psalm 78:16 were more than an emergency water supply. They showed ahead of time how God would give life through His Son, who was struck so that others could drink.

Isaiah's promise of floods of the Spirit finds its stated fulfillment in John 7. At the feast, Jesus "stood and cried out, saying, 'If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink'" and promised that "out of his heart will flow rivers of living water." John explains: "But this He spoke concerning the Spirit" (John 7:37–39). The thirsty one and the dry ground of Isaiah 44:3 are now anyone who comes to Christ. Joel's promise, "I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh" (Joel 2:28), widens the flood beyond Jacob's descendants.

Isaiah 45:8 also resonates with the gospel, even though the New Testament does not quote it directly. The righteousness that must come down from heaven arrived in a Person. Jesus came down from heaven to give the world a righteousness it could never produce, and Paul calls this "the righteousness of God" revealed apart from the law (Romans 3:21–22).

7. Spirit & Life

These passages describe the human condition with uncomfortable honesty. In them we are "dry ground," thirsty people, earth that cannot grow righteousness on its own, and wanderers in a desert of hard rock. None of these passages asks the dry ground to water itself. Life with God begins when we accept that what we most need has to be given.

The passages also show that God's way of giving is generous. He promises floods on dry ground and rivers from a rock, and He gives them to people who are complaining at the time (Psalm 78:17). No one has to earn the right to come thirsty to Christ.

Moses' prayer in Deuteronomy 32:2 also teaches patience in receiving God's Word. Dew does its work quietly, night after night. Much of what keeps a believer alive comes the same way, through Scripture taken in steadily over time.

Jeremiah's question stands as a warning. Most people who leave the spring do not set out to drink poison. They simply turn to "strange waters" that promise more and hold nothing. Faithfulness in marriage, worship, and daily trust means returning to the source that has never run dry.

Song of Solomon 4:16 adds one more image. The bride invites the north and south winds to blow on her garden "That its spices may flow out," and then asks her beloved to come and enjoy its fruit. Scripture does not say directly that this wind is the Spirit, but the picture fits what Isaiah and John teach: what God pours in is meant to flow back out as fragrance that delights Him and blesses others.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Numbers 24:7 — In Balaam's blessing, Israel is pictured pouring water from its buckets, an image of overflowing abundance from a people he was hired to curse.

Job 36:28 — Elihu describes rain "Which the clouds drop down And pour abundantly on man," one of the ordinary wonders that show God's greatness.

Song of Solomon 4:15 — The bride is "A well of living waters, And streams from Lebanon," fresh, pure, and given to her husband alone.

Psalm 78:44 — In the plagues on Egypt, God "Turned their rivers into blood, And their streams, that they could not drink." The God who can make water flow for His people can also take it away from His enemies.
