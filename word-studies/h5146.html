NOAH - (Nôach, Strong's H5146)

Summary

Noah, son of Lamech, stands at the hinge between the world before the Flood and the world after it. He was the tenth generation from Adam through Seth, the father of Shem, Ham, and Japheth, and the man through whom God preserved human life when He judged a world "filled with violence" (Genesis 6:11). His name, Nôach, is generally understood to mean "rest." Genesis explains it with a related-sounding word: at his birth Lamech said, "This one will comfort us concerning our work and the toil of our hands, because of the ground which the LORD has cursed" (Genesis 5:29). That wordplay of rest and comfort runs through his story. It surfaces again when God smells the "soothing" aroma of Noah's offering and promises never again to curse the ground for man's sake (Genesis 8:21).

Noah's story begins with grace: "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD" (Genesis 6:8). He walked with God, built the ark exactly as God commanded, came through the waters, built the first altar named in Scripture, and received God's first named covenant, sealed by the rainbow. He also got drunk in his vineyard, a sober reminder that the Flood washed the earth but did not change the human heart. Later Scripture keeps returning to him. Isaiah grounds God's oath of peace in "the waters of Noah." Ezekiel names him as a model of righteousness. Jesus warns that "as the days of Noah were, so also will the coming of the Son of Man be" (Matthew 24:37). Peter sees in the ark a picture of salvation through Christ's resurrection. In Noah we see the God who judges sin, rescues by grace, and keeps His word to the whole earth.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Noah is a single, clearly identified individual. He was the son of Lamech and the grandson of Methuselah, and he stands tenth in the line from Adam through Seth (Genesis 5:28–32; 1 Chronicles 1:1–4). His father is the Sethite Lamech of Genesis 5. He should not be confused with the violent Lamech of Cain's line in Genesis 4:19–24. Noah's three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth (Genesis 6:10), became the ancestors of the nations listed in Genesis 10. Noah was six hundred years old when the Flood came (Genesis 7:6). He lived 350 years after it and died at 950 (Genesis 9:28–29). The New Testament refers to him by the Greek form Nōe (G3575), including his place in Luke's genealogy of Jesus (Luke 3:36).

One distinction matters here. English Bibles also give the name "Noah" to one of the five daughters of Zelophehad (Numbers 26:33; 27:1; 36:11; Joshua 17:3). Her name is spelled differently in Hebrew, comes from a different root, and is indexed separately (H5270). She has no connection to the Noah of the Flood.

The Hebrew name נֹחַ (Nôach) is generally connected with the verb nuach, "to rest," so its probable meaning is "rest." Genesis 5:29 explains the name through wordplay rather than strict derivation. Lamech uses a similar-sounding verb, naham, "to comfort": "This one will comfort us." The two ideas sit close together. Lamech's hope was relief from the hard labor that had come with the curse on the ground (Genesis 3:17–19). The theme of rest returns in Genesis 8:21, where the "soothing aroma" of Noah's sacrifice uses a word built on the same root as his name. Genesis itself makes these connections, so they can be traced with confidence. Noah's name should still not be treated as a code that explains his whole life. It voiced a father's longing, and the narrative shows how God answered that longing in His own way.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

A World Filled with Violence

Noah was born near the end of the world before the Flood. Genesis describes that world in stark terms: human wickedness was great, every intent of the heart was "only evil continually" (Genesis 6:5), and "the earth was filled with violence" (Genesis 6:11). The puzzling account of the "sons of God" and the Nephilim (Genesis 6:1–4) belongs to this setting. Its meaning is debated, but its function in the story is clear. Human life had moved far beyond its God-given boundaries. "All flesh had corrupted their way on the earth" (Genesis 6:12), and the LORD grieved that He had made man (Genesis 6:6).

Grace, Righteousness, and the Command to Build

Then the narrative turns on one word: "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD" (Genesis 6:8). This is the first time Scripture speaks of anyone finding grace. The order matters. Only after this does Genesis describe Noah's character: "Noah was a just man, perfect in his generations. Noah walked with God" (Genesis 6:9). The phrase "walked with God" was last used of Enoch (Genesis 5:22, 24), his great-grandfather. Noah lived in close fellowship with God while the world around him rotted.

God told Noah plainly what was coming: "The end of all flesh has come before Me" (Genesis 6:13). He gave him precise instructions: "Make yourself an ark of gopherwood; make rooms in the ark, and cover it inside and outside with pitch" (Genesis 6:14). The instructions continued with the dimensions, the door, and the animals. Here God also used the word "covenant" for the first time in the Bible, promising to establish it with Noah (Genesis 6:18). Genesis 6:22 summarizes Noah's response: he did everything just as God commanded him. Genesis 7:5 repeats the point.

What Noah does not do in these chapters is worth noticing. Through the warning, the building, the Flood, and the exit from the ark, Genesis records no words from Noah at all. God speaks; Noah acts. His first recorded words do not come until after the vineyard incident (Genesis 9:25). Scripture does not explain the silence. Still, the effect is striking: the man is known by his obedience. The New Testament adds that he was also "a preacher of righteousness" (2 Peter 2:5). Genesis does not record that preaching, and Peter does not tell us what Noah said or how long he said it.

The Flood and God's Remembering

Noah entered the ark with his wife, his sons, and their wives, along with the animals: pairs of every kind, and seven of each clean kind (Genesis 7:1–9, 13–15). Then "the LORD shut him in" (Genesis 7:16). The fountains of the deep burst open and the windows of heaven were opened (Genesis 7:11). Everything with the breath of life on dry land died, "only Noah and those who were with him in the ark remained alive" (Genesis 7:23).

The story turns again with a single sentence: "Then God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the animals that were with him in the ark. And God made a wind to pass over the earth, and the waters subsided" (Genesis 8:1). In Scripture, God's remembering is never a recovered memory. It means God acting on His commitment, and here His remembering includes the animals too. The wind over the waters recalls the Spirit hovering over the deep in Genesis 1:2, as though creation were beginning again. The ark came to rest on the mountains of Ararat (Genesis 8:4). Noah sent out a raven and then a dove, until the dove returned with a freshly plucked olive leaf (Genesis 8:6–12). Even when the ground was dry, Noah waited for God's word before leaving the ark (Genesis 8:15–18).

The Altar and the Promise

Noah's first recorded act on the renewed earth was worship. He built an altar to the LORD, the first altar Scripture names, and offered burnt offerings of every clean animal and bird (Genesis 8:20). God smelled the soothing aroma and resolved never again to curse the ground for man's sake or to destroy every living thing, "although the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21, paraphrased in part). That reason is remarkable. The diagnosis is nearly the same as before the Flood (Genesis 6:5). God's promise does not rest on improved human nature. It rests on His own mercy. Genesis links God's resolve with the soothing aroma of Noah's offering; reading that offering as the sacrificial basis of the promise is a canonical inference that later Scripture develops (see Section 4). He then pledged the steady rhythm of seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, as long as the earth remains (Genesis 8:22).

The Covenant with Noah

God blessed Noah and his sons and renewed the creation mandate, telling them to be fruitful, multiply, and fill the earth (Genesis 9:1, 7). These words echo Genesis 1:28: "Be fruitful and multiply; fill the earth and subdue it." This time the blessing came with new provisions and boundaries. Animals would now fear humans. Meat was given for food, but not with its lifeblood (Genesis 9:2–4). Human life was to be protected under the principle that whoever sheds man's blood will have his blood shed, "for in the image of God He made man" (Genesis 9:5–6).

God then established His covenant with Noah, with his descendants, and with every living creature, promising that never again would a flood destroy the earth (Genesis 9:8–11). He set His bow in the clouds as the sign. When it appears, God says, He will remember His covenant (Genesis 9:12–17). The promise was universal, and it rested entirely on God. Noah was not asked to keep his side to keep it in force.

The Vineyard and the Sons

The narrative does not end on the mountaintop. Noah became a farmer and planted a vineyard. He drank its wine, became drunk, and lay uncovered in his tent (Genesis 9:20–21). Ham saw his father's nakedness and told his brothers. Shem and Japheth walked in backward and covered him without looking (Genesis 9:22–23). When Noah awoke, he spoke for the first time in the story: "Cursed be Canaan; A servant of servants He shall be to his brethren" (Genesis 9:25). He blessed the LORD, the God of Shem, and prayed that God would enlarge Japheth (Genesis 9:26–27). The curse falls specifically on Canaan, not on Ham's descendants as a whole and not on any race. The passage was tragically misused in later centuries to defend racial slavery. That use has no basis in the text, which looks ahead to Israel's later conflict with the Canaanites.

Legacy

Genesis 10 traces the nations "according to their generations" from Noah's sons. It closes by noting that from them "the nations were divided on the earth after the flood" (Genesis 10:1, 32). The Chronicler later places Noah in the line from Adam that leads to Israel (1 Chronicles 1:4). Every people on earth, according to Scripture, shares Noah as a common father.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God judged the corrupt world and saved Noah and his household (Genesis 6:13; 7:23; 2 Peter 2:5). From that center, several lines of God's purpose can be traced.

First, Noah's story establishes that salvation begins with God's favor. Genesis says Noah found grace before it says he was just. Hebrews confirms the point from the other side: Noah "became heir of the righteousness which is according to faith" (Hebrews 11:7). His righteousness was real, but it grew from God's grace and was received by faith.

Second, God's covenant with Noah provides the stage on which the rest of redemption unfolds. The promises of Genesis 8:22 and 9:11 guarantee that the world will continue, with its seasons, harvests, and days, until God's purposes are complete. Abraham, Israel, David, and finally Christ all appear on an earth that God promised to preserve. This is a reasonable inference from how the canon is arranged, and it fits the universal scope of the promise "with every living creature" (Genesis 9:10).

Third, the story presents Noah as a new beginning for humanity, set against the pattern of Adam. The waters cover the earth as in Genesis 1:2, and a wind passes over them. Dry land appears. The blessing of Genesis 1:28 is spoken again. Then the pattern of failure repeats as well: a man in a planted place, the fruit of what he grew, nakedness and shame, and a curse spoken afterward. Genesis does not call Noah a "second Adam." The parallels are strong enough, though, to support a clear conclusion. A cleansed world cannot produce a cleansed heart. God Himself says as much in Genesis 8:21.

Fourth, Noah stands at the head of the biblical pattern of the remnant. When corruption filled the whole earth, God judged the whole but preserved a few, "eight souls" (1 Peter 3:20), to carry His purposes forward. The same shape recurs throughout Scripture, from Lot to the faithful in Israel's exile.

Later prophets drew directly on Noah. Through Isaiah, God tells exiled Israel, "For this is like the waters of Noah to Me; For as I have sworn That the waters of Noah would no longer cover the earth, So have I sworn That I would not be angry with you, nor rebuke you" (Isaiah 54:9). The oath sworn to Noah, visible in every rainbow, becomes the proof that God's promised peace to His people will hold. Ezekiel uses Noah differently. He names Noah with Daniel and Job as men of exceptional righteousness, yet insists that even they "would deliver neither son nor daughter; they would deliver only themselves by their righteousness" (Ezekiel 14:20; see 14:14). Noah's household was saved alongside him, but Ezekiel makes clear that righteousness cannot be transferred to a rebellious nation. Each person must turn to God.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament speaks of Noah directly, and these passages carry his significance into the gospel.

Jesus and the days of Noah. Jesus Himself makes Noah's generation a pattern for His return: "But as the days of Noah were, so also will the coming of the Son of Man be. For as in the days before the flood, they were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, until the day that Noah entered the ark, and did not know until the flood came and took them all away, so also will the coming of the Son of Man be" (Matthew 24:37–39; compare Luke 17:26–27, where Jesus pairs Noah's days with Lot's). Jesus does not stress the violence of that age. He stresses its ordinariness. Meals, weddings, and daily routines went on with no thought of God's warning, right up to the day the door was shut. Jesus applies this explicitly to His own coming, which will be just as sudden and just as decisive.

The ark and salvation. Peter writes that "the Divine longsuffering waited in the days of Noah, while the ark was being prepared, in which a few, that is, eight souls, were saved through water. There is also an antitype which now saves us—baptism (not the removal of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God), through the resurrection of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 3:20–21). Here the New Testament itself identifies a pattern. The waters that brought judgment were the same waters Noah passed through to a new world. Baptism corresponds to that passage, and its saving power lies in the risen Christ, not in the water. Peter's word "longsuffering" also matters. The time it took to build the ark was a season of God's patience toward a world under judgment.

The preacher and the coming judgment. Second Peter 2:5 says God "did not spare the ancient world, but saved Noah, one of eight people, a preacher of righteousness, bringing in the flood on the world of the ungodly." Peter later sets the Flood beside the final judgment, when the present heavens and earth are reserved for fire (2 Peter 3:5–7). God's past rescue of Noah becomes assurance that "the Lord knows how to deliver the godly out of temptations" (2 Peter 2:9).

Faith that acts. Hebrews 11:7 summarizes Noah's life in one sentence: "By faith Noah, being divinely warned of things not yet seen, moved with godly fear, prepared an ark for the saving of his household, by which he condemned the world and became heir of the righteousness which is according to faith." Noah believed God about something he could not yet see, and that belief put a hammer in his hand.

Rest through sacrifice. A further connection is canonical rather than explicitly stated, but it is well grounded. Lamech longed for rest from the curse. In Genesis his longing finds a partial answer when God accepted the "soothing aroma" of Noah's sacrifice and promised never again to curse the ground in that way, though the narrative does not call this the fulfillment of Lamech's words. The New Testament describes Christ's self-offering in similar language, as "a sweet-smelling aroma" to God (Ephesians 5:2), and Jesus invites the weary and burdened to come to Him for rest (Matthew 11:28). Noah was not Christ, and his name was not a prophecy of the Messiah. Yet the movement of his story, from a cursed ground to an accepted sacrifice to a promise of mercy, points toward the One whose sacrifice brings rest that Noah's offering could only foreshadow. Luke's genealogy places Noah in Jesus' own ancestry (Luke 3:36), so the line God preserved through the waters is the line through which the Savior came.

5. Spirit & Life

Noah's story opens with grace and should be read that way. "Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD" comes before any word about his goodness. No one stands before God by being the best person in a bad generation. God's favor comes first, and a life of walking with Him grows out of it. That is good news for anyone who feels surrounded by a culture that has lost its way. God can keep a person faithful in the middle of it, as He did Noah.

Noah's obedience was quiet and costly. Genesis gives us no speeches from him in those chapters, only a man doing what God said. Hebrews says he was moved by "godly fear" about things "not yet seen." Faith often looks exactly like that: taking God's warning seriously before the evidence arrives, and building accordingly while others go on eating, drinking, and marrying. Jesus' words about the days of Noah press the same question on every reader. The danger in Noah's day was not only open violence. It was ordinary life lived as though God had never spoken.

The altar after the Flood shows what God's people should do with deliverance. Noah's first act on dry ground was worship, and God answered that offering with a promise for the whole earth. God's patience with the world rests on His mercy, which Genesis places right after an accepted offering and which later Scripture ties to sacrifice. It does not rest on human improvement, because God Himself said the human heart was still inclined to evil (Genesis 8:21). Every harvest and every sunrise is evidence that God is keeping His word (Genesis 8:22), and every rainbow is a sign He set in the sky for Himself to remember (Genesis 9:16).

The vineyard scene keeps us honest. Even a man who walked with God, survived judgment, and received God's covenant could fall into shame. Noah's failure does not cancel God's promise. It shows why we need a better Deliverer than Noah. Ezekiel's warning adds that no one can rest on another person's righteousness. Peter directly ties the Flood deliverance to baptism and to salvation through Christ's resurrection (1 Peter 3:20–21). Building on that, and as this study's own pastoral analogy rather than Peter's explicit statement, each of us must find shelter in Christ as Noah found it in the ark God provided.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 5:28–32 — Noah's birth, Lamech's explanation of his name, and his place in the line from Adam.

  • Genesis 6:5–13 — The corruption and violence of the world, and the grace and righteousness of Noah.

  • Genesis 6:14–22; 7:1–24 — The ark, God's first mention of covenant, Noah's full obedience, and the Flood.

  • Genesis 8:1–22 — God remembers Noah, the waters recede, the first altar, and the promise of the seasons.

  • Genesis 9:1–17 — The renewed blessing, the sanctity of human life in God's image, and the covenant sealed by the rainbow.

  • Genesis 9:18–29 — The vineyard, Noah's shame, the curse on Canaan, the blessings on Shem and Japheth, and Noah's death.

  • Genesis 10:1, 32; 1 Chronicles 1:4 — All nations traced from Noah's sons; Noah in Israel's genealogical record.

  • Isaiah 54:9–10 — God's oath to Noah as the foundation for His promise of peace to His people.

  • Ezekiel 14:14, 20 — Noah as a model of righteousness that cannot be transferred to others.

  • Matthew 24:37–39; Luke 17:26–27 — Jesus uses Noah's days as a warning about the sudden coming of the Son of Man.

  • Luke 3:36 — Noah in the genealogy of Jesus.

  • Hebrews 11:7 — Noah's faith, godly fear, and inheritance of righteousness by faith.

  • 1 Peter 3:20–21 — God's patience in Noah's day and the ark as a pattern of salvation through Christ's resurrection.

  • 2 Peter 2:5; 3:5–7 — Noah as a preacher of righteousness, and the Flood as a pattern of the final judgment.
