NAHOR - (Nâchôwr, Strong's H5152)

Summary

Nahor is the name of two men in Abraham's family, and the reader has to keep them apart. The first Nahor was Abraham's grandfather, the son of Serug and the father of Terah, listed in the line from Shem to Abram (Genesis 11:22–25; 1 Chronicles 1:26). Luke's genealogy of Jesus later passes through this man (Luke 3:34). The second Nahor was Abraham's brother, a son of Terah who married his niece Milcah and became, through his son Bethuel, the grandfather of both Rebekah and Laban. The meaning of the name is not certain. It is generally thought to mean "snorting" or "breathing hard," from a root used of the heavy breathing of an animal. It may also simply be an old Semitic personal name with no message attached, so the meaning should not be read into either man's life.

The more developed story belongs to Abraham's brother. He and Abram grew up in the same household, under the same father, among people who, as Joshua later said, "served other gods" (Joshua 24:2). God called Abram out, and Nahor's family stayed in Mesopotamia. Yet God did not forget that branch of the family. He used Nahor's household to provide Rebekah for Isaac and Rachel and Leah for Jacob. Then, in Genesis 31, Laban swore by "the God of Nahor" while Jacob swore by "the Fear of his father Isaac," and the two families parted, setting a boundary neither would cross to harm the other. It is the last encounter Genesis narrates between them. Nahor's story makes God's call of Abraham stand out sharply. The same family soil produced a man who went and a man who stayed, and the difference was God's grace answered by faith.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H5152 covers two separate men, and their biographies must not be blended.

Nahor, Abraham's grandfather. Serug fathered him at thirty, and Nahor fathered Terah at twenty-nine (Genesis 11:22, 24). Genesis says that "after he begot Terah, Nahor lived one hundred and nineteen years, and begot sons and daughters" (Genesis 11:25), which gives him the shortest recorded life in the list from Shem to Abram. First Chronicles 1:26 repeats his place in the line, and Luke 3:34 names him among the ancestors of Jesus.

Nahor, Abraham's brother. He was a son of Terah: "Terah begot Abram, Nahor, and Haran" (Genesis 11:27). After Haran died in Ur, Nahor married Haran's daughter Milcah (Genesis 11:28–29). Through their son Bethuel he became the grandfather of Rebekah and Laban (Genesis 22:20–23; 24:15). Terah seems to have named this son after his own father, so the two men are grandfather and grandson.

Two related expressions also belong to the brother. "The city of Nahor" in Mesopotamia (Genesis 24:10) is most naturally read as the town where Nahor's family lived. Genesis does not say whether it was named after him or which town it was, though later chapters place Laban in Haran (Genesis 27:43; 29:4–5). "The God of Nahor" (Genesis 31:53) is Laban's phrase for the deity of that branch of the family.

The meaning of the name. Nahor is generally thought to mean "snorting" or "breathing hard," taken from a root that describes the heavy snorting of a horse or similar animal. That is a probable derivation, not a settled one. It may simply be an ancient personal name. Scripture makes no wordplay on it and never connects it with either man's character.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The grandfather in the line of Shem. Nahor I appears only in genealogy. Genesis 11 traces the line from Shem to Abram generation by generation, showing the line through which God would act after Babel. Nahor's short entry tells us his age at Terah's birth, how long he lived afterward, and that he had other children. Nothing more is recorded about his life or faith.

The brother who stayed. Nahor II grew up in Ur of the Chaldeans with Abram and Haran. When Terah set out for Canaan, Genesis 11:31 lists Abram, Sarai, and Lot as the ones he took, and it does not mention Nahor. The family later settled in Haran. By Abraham's old age, Nahor's household is firmly established in Mesopotamia, which suggests he followed later or settled in the region separately. The text does not explain how. What is clear is that Abram left at God's command (Genesis 12:1–4) and Nahor's line remained in the land beyond the River.

News after Mount Moriah. Genesis 22:20–24 is set right after Abraham's offering of Isaac. Word reaches Abraham that Milcah has borne children to his brother Nahor: eight sons by Milcah, including Bethuel, and four more by his concubine Reumah, twelve sons in all. The list closes with a single granddaughter: "Bethuel begot Rebekah" (Genesis 22:23). God has just renewed His promise of descendants to Abraham (Genesis 22:16–18), and the next thing the narrator records is the birth of the woman who will marry Isaac. The promise is already being prepared for in a household hundreds of miles away.

The city of Nahor and the well. Abraham refused to let Isaac marry a Canaanite and sent his servant back to his kindred (Genesis 24:3–4). The servant "went to Mesopotamia, to the city of Nahor" (Genesis 24:10). At the well he prayed, "O LORD God of my master Abraham, please give me success this day" (Genesis 24:12), and "before he had finished speaking," Rebekah came out, "born to Bethuel, son of Milcah, the wife of Nahor, Abraham's brother" (Genesis 24:15). Rebekah identifies herself by the same line (Genesis 24:24), and the servant repeats it when he tells the story (Genesis 24:47). Laban and Bethuel confess that "the thing comes from the LORD" (Genesis 24:50), and Rebekah agrees to go (Genesis 24:58).

Jacob among Nahor's descendants. A generation later Jacob fled to the same family. At a well near Haran he asked the shepherds whether they knew "Laban the son of Nahor" (Genesis 29:5). Laban was actually Nahor's grandson, but Hebrew often uses "son" for a descendant, and the phrase identifies the clan. Jacob served Laban twenty years and married Leah and Rachel. The household mixed some knowledge of the LORD (Genesis 24:31, 50) with divination (Genesis 30:27) and household gods, which Rachel stole when they left (Genesis 31:19, 30).

The last narrated parting. When Laban caught up with Jacob, the two set up a heap of stones as a boundary and witness (Genesis 31:44–52). Laban swore, "The God of Abraham, the God of Nahor, and the God of their father judge between us." Genesis then adds, "And Jacob swore by the Fear of his father Isaac" (Genesis 31:53). The covenant forbade either side to cross the boundary to do harm (31:52); it did not forbid all future contact. Laban went home, and Nahor's line drops out of the story of Israel.

Joshua's backward look. Centuries later, Joshua reminded Israel at Shechem: "Your fathers, including Terah, the father of Abraham and the father of Nahor, dwelt on the other side of the River in old times; and they served other gods" (Joshua 24:2). That single sentence explains the family Abraham came from and the family Nahor's line never fully left.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Joshua 24:2 states plainly that the family of Terah, from which both Abraham and Nahor came, served other gods. Abraham did not begin as a seeker who found the true God on his own. The LORD says, "I took your father Abraham from the other side of the River" (Joshua 24:3). Nahor stands beside Abraham as the brother God did not call out in that way. The contrast puts the beginning of Israel where Scripture puts it, in God's initiative.

Nahor's household also served God's purpose, though it stood outside the promise given to Abraham. The narrative itself shows how. Abraham would not let Isaac take a Canaanite wife, and Isaac and Rebekah later grieved over Esau's Hittite wives and sent Jacob to Laban for the same reason (Genesis 26:34–35; 27:46–28:2). Nahor's family supplied three of Israel's matriarchs: Rebekah, Leah, and Rachel. It would be an inference, though a reasonable one, to say that God used this related family as a bridge while Abraham's descendants were too few to marry among themselves. Genesis does not state that as a principle, but the pattern is plain in the story.

Genesis 31:53 shows how far the two branches had drifted. Laban names the God of Abraham and the God of Nahor side by side, then adds "the God of their father," Terah, whom Joshua says served other gods. The Hebrew verb "judge" in this verse is commonly noted as plural, which fits a speaker thinking of more than one deity. Laban had used the name of the LORD before (Genesis 24:50), yet his household kept idols. Jacob answered by swearing only by "the Fear of his father Isaac." The text does not say Jacob rejected Laban's oath in so many words. Still, his choice of a different name is deliberate. He swears by the God who had revealed Himself to Isaac, not by the mixed heritage of Terah's house.

The women who came from Nahor's household had to leave it. Rebekah said, "I will go" (Genesis 24:58). Rachel and Leah told Jacob their father had treated them as strangers and urged him to do what God had said (Genesis 31:14–16). Even so, Rachel carried the household gods with her, and only later did Jacob command his household to put away foreign gods (Genesis 35:2–4). Leaving Nahor's house was a real departure, but not one completed all at once.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Luke traces the ancestry of Jesus back through "the son of Abraham, the son of Terah, the son of Nahor" (Luke 3:34). That is Nahor the grandfather, named in Greek under a separately indexed Strong's entry (G3493), and Luke's reference confirms the line given in Genesis 11 and 1 Chronicles 1. Luke carries the genealogy all the way to Adam (Luke 3:38). He shows Jesus as the promised Son bound to the whole human story, including the generations "on the other side of the River" before God called Abram. The Messiah's family tree runs straight through a household Scripture describes as serving other gods. God's promise of a redeemer was carried by His faithfulness, not by the merit of the ancestors.

The brother's story connects to the larger biblical theme of God calling people out. Paul describes the God of Abraham as the One "who justifies the ungodly" (Romans 4:5), and Joshua 24:2 shows how literally that applied to Abraham's background. Hebrews observes that if the patriarchs had kept thinking of the country they left, "they would have had opportunity to return," but they desired a better, heavenly country (Hebrews 11:15–16). Nahor's family is that country with a face on it. It is the home the patriarchs could have gone back to, and the family did return to it for wives: Abraham sent his servant (while forbidding Isaac himself to go back, Genesis 24:5–8), and Jacob went personally but returned to Canaan at God's command (Genesis 31:3). Neither made it a permanent home. This is a canonical theme that Scripture develops, not a claim that Nahor is a designed type.

5. Spirit & Life

Nahor's story makes grace concrete. Abram and Nahor shared a father, a city, and a religious upbringing. One became the father of all who believe, and the other remained in the land of his fathers' gods. Scripture never says Abraham was the better man to start with. It says God took him (Joshua 24:3). Anyone who has come to know Christ out of a family or culture that did not know Him can see their own story here. The difference began with God's call.

God's kindness also reached beyond the chosen line. He prospered Nahor with twelve sons, answered a servant's prayer at the well of Nahor's city, and brought Rebekah out "before he had finished speaking" (Genesis 24:15). The LORD was at work in a household that did not fully belong to Him, and He used it to keep His promise to Abraham. Believers often worry about family members who still live in the old country. Nahor's story does not promise every relative will follow, but it shows that God's attention does not stop at the edge of the family of faith.

There is also a sober word. Laban knew the name of the LORD and could say "the thing comes from the LORD," yet he kept his idols and swore by a mixed list of gods. Jacob could not stay in that house and remain who God was making him. Joshua drew the same line when he pointed Israel back to the gods their fathers served beyond the River and called them to put those gods away and serve the LORD (Joshua 24:14–15). Our heritage may include truth and error together. Faithfulness means choosing the God who has revealed Himself and leaving the rest behind, sometimes slowly, as Jacob's household did, but truly.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 11:22–25 — Nahor the grandfather, son of Serug and father of Terah.

  • Genesis 11:26–29 — Nahor the brother, son of Terah, and his marriage to Milcah, Haran's daughter.

  • Genesis 22:20–24 — Nahor's twelve sons and the birth of Rebekah, placed right after the renewed promise to Abraham.

  • Genesis 24:10–15 — The servant's journey to "the city of Nahor" and God's answer at the well.

  • Genesis 24:24, 47 — Rebekah identified through Bethuel, Milcah, and Nahor.

  • Genesis 29:5 — Laban named as "the son of Nahor," meaning his descendant.

  • Genesis 31:19, 53 — Laban's household gods and his oath by "the God of Nahor," set against Jacob's oath by "the Fear of his father Isaac."

  • Joshua 24:2–3, 14–15 — Terah's family "served other gods," God took Abraham out, and Israel is called to put those gods away.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:26 — Nahor the grandfather in the line from Shem to Abram.

  • Luke 3:34 (Greek, G3493, separately indexed) — Nahor the grandfather in the genealogy of Jesus.
