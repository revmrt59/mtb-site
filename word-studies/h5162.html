COMFORT, RELENT, REGRET - (nâcham, Strong's H5162)

Summary

Nâcham is a Hebrew verb that carries two meanings English readers might not expect to share one word: "to comfort" and "to relent." Friends use it when they come to sit with the grieving, as Job's friends did. Prophets use it when God turns back from a disaster He has threatened, as He did for Nineveh. Isaiah uses it when God Himself speaks tender words to an exiled people and promises to rebuild their ruins. Both meanings describe a heart that is moved. One person is moved toward another's sorrow, or someone's settled course turns in response to what has happened.

Applied to God, the word shows that the Lord of Scripture responds to people. He grieved over the violence before the flood. He turned from judgment when Moses interceded and when Nineveh repented. He comforts His people the way a mother comforts her child. Scripture also says plainly, "God is not a man, that He should lie, Nor a son of man, that He should repent" (Numbers 23:19). His character and His sworn word never waver, and His responses always come out of that unchanging character. Throughout the Old Testament the word also marks a deep human ache: people who have "no comforter." That longing is finally answered in Jesus Christ and in the Helper He promised would "abide with you forever."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâcham appears 108 times, from Genesis to Zechariah. It shows up in mourning households (Genesis 37:35), in royal diplomacy, where David sends servants to comfort a grieving king (2 Samuel 10:2), and in prophetic warnings and prayers. Its heaviest concentrations are in Isaiah, Jeremiah, and the Psalms. Hebrew separates the word's main meanings largely by verb form. One form describes comforting someone else. Other forms describe being comforted, being sorry, or relenting. Some lexicons connect the root with sighing or deep breathing. That background is uncertain, and the word's meaning is best learned from how Scripture actually uses it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To comfort or console another person: to strengthen someone in grief or fear by presence and kind words. Joseph "comforted" his frightened brothers and "spoke kindly to them" (Genesis 50:21).

2. To be comforted: to have grief eased or settled. Isaac "was comforted" after his mother's death once Rebekah became his wife (Genesis 24:67).

3. To relent, regret, or be sorry: to turn from a course already set or to feel grief over it. God "relented" from the harm He had threatened (Exodus 32:14), and in Genesis 6:6 the Lord "was sorry" that He had made man.

4. To find relief or satisfaction, especially in judgment. In Isaiah 1:24 the Lord says, "I will rid Myself of My adversaries," and in Ezekiel 5:13 He declares, "I will be avenged."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Who acts, and toward whom, makes the difference. When people comfort people, the word belongs to ordinary life: visits after a death, words spoken across a family rift, a stranger's kindness to Ruth (Ruth 2:13). When God comforts, the word moves into prayer and promise (Psalm 71:21; 86:17; 119:76). When God relents, it appears inside warnings and intercession. When people are the ones who "repent" in this sense, they are grieving over their own conduct (Jeremiah 8:6; 31:19; Job 42:6).

Two constructions affect interpretation. The first is refusal: Jacob "refused to be comforted" over Joseph (Genesis 37:35), and Rachel likewise refuses comfort for her children (Jeremiah 31:15). The second is absence. The recurring phrases "no comforter" or "none to comfort" turn the word into a description of desolation. The negative "will not relent" also matters. It marks a decision God declares settled (Jeremiah 4:28; Ezekiel 24:14).

4. Theological Significance

Nâcham shows a God who is personally moved by the world He made. His grief in Genesis 6:6 comes before the flood. Before anything else, the judgment that follows is the sorrow of a Creator over violence and corruption. The same responsiveness makes Him the Comforter of the broken and the One who turns from disaster when people turn to Him. Comforting the grieving and relenting from judgment flow from one compassionate heart.

The word also guards God's holiness. In Isaiah 1:24 and Ezekiel 5:13, God's anger against persistent rebellion comes to rest when justice is done: "Thus shall My anger be spent." Peace with God never ignores evil. There is relief in heaven when evil is finally dealt with, and that relief belongs to the same God who longs to comfort.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15: God who relents and does not relent. Balaam is forced to declare, "God is not a man, that He should lie, Nor a son of man, that He should repent." Yet 1 Samuel 15 places both statements in one chapter. The Lord regrets making Saul king (15:11, 35), and Samuel tells Saul that "the Strength of Israel will not lie nor relent" (15:29). Samuel says this as Saul pleads to keep his throne, so the point is that God's verdict on Saul's rebellion will not be reversed. God grieves the outcome, but He will not be bargained out of His judgment.

Jeremiah 18:8–10 gives the principle behind both statements. If a nation God has warned turns from its evil, He will relent of the disaster. If a nation He has blessed turns to evil, He will relent of the good. God's response changes because His character does not. A God who is always just and always merciful must answer rebellion and repentance differently. Human regret often comes from shortsightedness or failure. God's relenting is the steady faithfulness of His holiness toward people whose conduct has changed.

Jeremiah 26, Joel 2, and Jonah 3: an invitation to return. God sends Jeremiah to the temple court with this hope: "Perhaps everyone will listen and turn from his evil way, that I may relent concerning the calamity." The relenting is what God wants. Joel builds his call on God's character: "Return to the LORD your God, For He is gracious and merciful... And He relents from doing harm." Joel's following "Who knows" (2:14) and the Ninevite king's "Who can tell" (Jonah 3:9) are not doubts about God's kindness. They show humility in people who have no claim on mercy. Nineveh found the answer: "God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God relented." Jonah's complaint (4:2) admits that he had known all along this is what God is like.

Isaiah 40–66: God becomes the Comforter. Isaiah 40:1 opens the book's message to exiles with a doubled command to comfort God's people. The comfort that follows is action. The Lord will comfort Zion by turning her wilderness into something like Eden (51:3). The earth is told to sing because He has comforted His people (49:13; 52:9). He presses His own identity on frightened exiles in 51:12, declaring that He is the One who comforts them. In 66:13 He compares Himself to a mother comforting her child. Comfort in Isaiah means God restores His people, brings them home, and holds them close.

Lamentations 1: none to comfort. Jerusalem's lament keeps returning to one line: she has no one to comfort her (1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21). The city's suffering is worse because it is lonely. Ecclesiastes 4:1 sees the same thing under the sun: "The tears of the oppressed, But they have no comforter." The repeated cry shows how much people need God Himself to come.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah's promised comfort became the hope Israel waited for. Luke describes Simeon as waiting for "the Consolation of Israel" (Luke 2:25) and finding it in the infant Jesus. Isaiah 61:2 describes the Anointed One sent "to comfort all who mourn," and Jesus echoes it when He says that those who mourn will be comforted (Matthew 5:4).

The cry of the abandoned also reaches Christ. In Psalm 69:20, a psalm the New Testament applies to Jesus' sufferings, the sufferer says, "I looked for someone to take pity, but there was none; And for comforters, but I found none." Jesus took that loneliness on Himself, with disciples asleep in Gethsemane and scattered at His arrest. He then promised that His people would not be left that way: "He will give you another Helper, that He may abide with you forever" (John 14:16). Lamentations had grieved that no comforter came. Christ promises one who stays.

The "not relent" side of the word points to Christ as well. In Psalm 110:4 the Lord swears that He will not relent concerning a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. Hebrews 7:21 applies that oath to Jesus. The God who relents toward repentant sinners has sworn, without any possibility of reversal, to give them a permanent High Priest. His mercy rests on a decision He will never take back.

7. Spirit & Life

Job's friends agreed "to come and mourn with him, and to comfort him" (Job 2:11), and they did well when they sat silently beside him. Once they began explaining his suffering as guilt, Job called them "Miserable comforters." Psalm 23:4 describes the opposite: "For You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me." The Shepherd comforts by being near and by protecting. Jacob refused comfort over Joseph because no words could replace a son. People in grief usually need someone present and faithful more than they need an explanation. The God who walks into the valley teaches His people how to comfort.

The relenting passages also make prayer and repentance urgent. Moses pleaded and the Lord relented (Exodus 32:14). Amos cried out twice, and twice "the LORD relented" (Amos 7:3, 6). God brings His servants into His counsel and gives their prayers a real place in what He does. For anyone who fears it is too late, Joel and Jonah give a firm answer. God's willingness to relent comes from His gracious and merciful character, so turning back to Him is never wasted.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 5:29 — Lamech names Noah in hope that the child will bring comfort from the toil of the cursed ground. A chapter later the same word describes God's sorrow over the earth (6:6).

  • Genesis 50:21 — Joseph comforts the brothers who sold him, showing comfort extended across real guilt.

  • Job 42:6 — Job, having seen the Lord, repents "in dust and ashes." Here the word means grief over his own words.

  • Jeremiah 31:15 — Rachel refuses to be comforted for her children. Matthew 2:18 recalls her weeping at Herod's slaughter in Bethlehem.

  • Psalm 119:52 — The psalmist remembers God's ancient judgments and is comforted. Scripture itself becomes a source of comfort.

  • Ezekiel 14:22–23 — Survivors of Jerusalem's fall bring an odd comfort to the exiles: they show that God's judgment was not without cause.
