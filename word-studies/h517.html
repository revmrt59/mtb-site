MOTHER - (ʼêm, Strong's H0517)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼêm is the Old Testament's ordinary word for "mother," the woman who bears, nurses, teaches, and raises a child. Scripture also stretches the word. It can name a grandmother or the first mother of the human race. It can describe a woman like Deborah, who rose to protect God's people, and it can describe a city, or the nation of Israel itself, as the mother of the people who belong to her.

Through this word God reveals a great deal about Himself. He commands children to honor their mother as fully as their father. He describes His own comfort as the comfort a mother gives a hurting child. He holds kings' mothers responsible for the direction of their sons' reigns, and He pictures unfaithful Jerusalem as a mother whose sins wound her children. In Psalm 22 the suffering One traces His trust in God back to His mother's breasts, a psalm Jesus took on His lips at the cross. Paul later calls the heavenly Jerusalem "the mother of us all." Yet the Bible also teaches that even this closest of human bonds must give way when it competes with loyalty to the Lord.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼêm is a basic family word, common to the Semitic languages and used from Genesis through Zechariah. It appears in family stories about Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, and Moses' mother, and in the laws of Exodus, Leviticus, and Deuteronomy. It is also prominent in the royal records of Kings and Chronicles, in the teaching of Proverbs and the poetry of Psalms and the Song of Solomon, and in the prophets' laments and accusations.

In Israel, inheritance and tribal standing ran through the "father's house." Alongside it, Scripture also speaks of the "mother's house." Rebekah "ran and told her mother's household" about Abraham's servant (Genesis 24:28). Naomi urged her widowed daughters-in-law, "Go, return each to her mother's house" (Ruth 1:8). The phrase points to the circle of women's care, belonging, and intimacy within the larger family.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A woman who has borne a child, a mother in the plain sense. Moses' sister brought "the child's mother" to nurse him when Pharaoh's daughter found him (Exodus 2:8).

2. A female ancestor, especially a grandmother. Maachah is called the "grandmother" of King Asa (1 Kings 15:10, 13). Eve is named the mother of all living (Genesis 3:20).

3. A woman, or a city, that sustains and protects a community. Deborah arose as a mother in Israel (Judges 5:7), and the city of Abel is called "a mother in Israel" (2 Samuel 20:19).

4. A city or nation pictured as the mother of its citizens. Hosea tells Israel's children, "Bring charges against your mother" (Hosea 2:2).

One rare idiom shows how flexible the word is. In Ezekiel 21:21 the Hebrew speaks of the "mother of the road," the main junction from which smaller roads branch. The NKJV translates it "the parting of the road."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Genesis, mothers stand at the center of the promise's family line. Rebekah steers Jacob toward the blessing and then sends him to her brother's house to escape Esau (Genesis 27:13–14; 28:2, 5). The mother's decisions, faithful and flawed, move the story of God's purposes forward.

The phrase "son of your mother" carries special weight. In a household with several wives, it marked a full brother, the nearest kin a person had. When Deuteronomy 13:6 warns against being enticed to idolatry by "your brother, the son of your mother," it names the tightest bond it can find.

Kings and Chronicles use the word more than fifty times, mostly in the regular formula introducing a king of Judah: "His mother's name was…" That repeated detail is more than record-keeping, because the narrative shows how much a king's mother could influence him. Ahaziah walked in Ahab's ways because his mother, Athaliah, counseled him to do wickedly (2 Chronicles 22:3). After her son's death she tried to destroy the entire royal line in order to seize the throne (2 Kings 11:1). Asa had to remove his grandmother Maachah from her position because she had made an obscene idol (1 Kings 15:13). By contrast, Josiah's mother Jedidah is named at the start of the most thorough reform in Judah's history (2 Kings 22:1). Proverbs 31:1 even preserves the teaching that King Lemuel's mother gave him.

Wisdom literature treats the mother as a teacher. The bride in the Song recalls her mother as "she who used to instruct me" and longs to bring her beloved into "the chamber of her who conceived me" (Song of Songs 8:2; 3:4). The mother's house is pictured as a place of safety, origins, and closeness.

4. Theological Significance

God's law guards the mother's place in human life. Striking or cursing a mother brought the same penalty as striking or cursing a father (Exodus 21:15, 17). The law's tenderness extends even to animals: a young goat was not to be boiled in its mother's milk (Exodus 23:19), and a person who found a nest could take the young but had to "let the mother go" (Deuteronomy 22:6–7). These laws do not say why. The pattern suggests, though, that God did not want His people to treat the source of life with careless cruelty.

Because the mother bond is so strong, Scripture uses it to test where a person's final loyalty lies. Deuteronomy 13:6 says that even "the son of your mother" must not be followed into idolatry. Moses blessed Levi for putting God's word above father and mother (Deuteronomy 33:9). Zechariah foresees a purified people in which a false prophet's "father and mother who begot him" will refuse to protect his lies (Zechariah 13:3). Jesus carries the same claim into discipleship. Anyone who "does not hate his father and mother… cannot be My disciple" (Luke 14:26). In that verse "hate" is a strong way of describing what must come second when loyalties collide.

When God's people abandon Him, Scripture shows motherhood itself turned upside down. In ruined Jerusalem, starving children cry to their mothers for grain and wine and die in their mothers' arms (Lamentations 2:12). Elsewhere, using other Hebrew words for women, Scripture records mothers eating their own children during sieges (2 Kings 6:28–29; Lamentations 2:20). Athaliah turned a royal mother's influence into murder. When the very source of life brings death, a society has reached the bottom of judgment.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Honor equal to the father (Leviticus 19:3; Exodus 20:12; Proverbs 1:8). The fifth commandment joins mother and father in one command with one promise of long life in the land (Exodus 20:12; Paul calls it "the first commandment with promise," Ephesians 6:2). Leviticus 19:3 names the mother first: "Every one of you shall revere his mother and his father, and keep My Sabbaths." The word "revere" is the language of fear and awe, and the command to keep the Sabbaths follows immediately, so honoring one's mother sits beside honoring God's holy day. Proverbs then makes the mother a teacher of wisdom. The son is told not to forsake his mother's law, a word that means "teaching" (Proverbs 1:8; 6:20). God gives the mother's instruction real authority in forming a child.

A mother in Israel (Judges 5:7; 2 Samuel 20:19). In Deborah's song, village life in Israel had ceased, and roads were abandoned, until she arose as a mother in Israel (Judges 5:6–7). The title describes a woman who roused a frightened people and stood between them and their oppressors. Later, when Joab besieged the city of Abel, a wise woman called out, "You seek to destroy a city and a mother in Israel. Why would you swallow up the inheritance of the LORD?" (2 Samuel 20:19). Her appeal saved the city. In both passages, "mother" describes someone who keeps God's people alive.

God's comfort (Isaiah 66:13; Psalm 131:2; Psalm 27:10). Isaiah pictures restored Jerusalem nursing her children, carrying them on her side and bouncing them on her knees (Isaiah 66:11–12). Then the Lord speaks for Himself: "As one whom his mother comforts, So I will comfort you; And you shall be comforted in Jerusalem." God remains Father and Lord, yet He chooses a mother's comfort to describe the way He will hold a people shattered by exile. Psalm 131:2 shows the result in one heart: "Like a weaned child with his mother; Like a weaned child is my soul within me." A weaned child no longer cries for the breast; he simply rests near his mother. In the same way, the psalmist has learned to be content in God's presence and is no longer demanding that God give him what he wants. When human love fails, God's care remains: "When my father and my mother forsake me, Then the LORD will take care of me" (Psalm 27:10).

The mother put away (Hosea 2:2–5; Isaiah 50:1). In these prophets the mother is the nation, and the children are her citizens. Hosea's children must "bring charges against your mother," because "their mother has played the harlot" by chasing lovers she thought gave her bread, water, wool, and oil (Hosea 2:2, 5). The children share in her shame (2:4). Isaiah answers the exiles' complaint with a question: "Where is the certificate of your mother's divorce, Whom I have put away?" (Isaiah 50:1). God produces no such document, because He was never unfaithful: "For your iniquities you have sold yourselves." Hosea's chapter goes on to God's pledge to allure her back and betroth her to Himself forever (Hosea 2:14, 19). The children suffer for their mother's unfaithfulness, and they are also restored when God brings her back.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story starts in the shadow of the curse. After God promised that the woman's Seed would crush the serpent (Genesis 3:15), and after He announced pain in childbearing (3:16), Adam named his wife Eve, because she was the mother of all living (3:20). Death had just been pronounced, yet Adam named her for life. Every later mother in the promised line carries that hope forward.

Psalm 22 brings the word closest to Christ. The sufferer who cries, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?", the cry Jesus spoke from the cross (Matthew 27:46), anchors his trust in his earliest days: "You made Me trust while on My mother's breasts. I was cast upon You from birth. From My mother's womb You have been My God" (Psalm 22:9–10). The Servant in Isaiah says the same. The Lord called Him from the womb and named Him from His mother's body (Isaiah 49:1). The New Testament shows these words fully lived out. The angel told Mary, "you will conceive in your womb and bring forth a Son, and shall call His name JESUS" (Luke 1:31). The Son of God entered the world as a nursing infant, dependent on a mother. The trust He learned at Mary's breast held firm at Golgotha. Even there, Jesus honored His mother by placing her in John's care (John 19:26–27).

Paul then lifts the mother-city image heavenward. Earthly Jerusalem, tied to Sinai and Hagar, is in bondage. "The Jerusalem above," he writes, is free, and she is the mother of us all (Galatians 4:25–26). The mother whom Hosea and Isaiah saw put away finds her answer in a city whose children are born free through God's promise.

7. Spirit & Life

The comfort promised in Isaiah 66 comes to us through the Son who learned trust on a mother's breast. He knows from the inside what it is to be small and dependent on another's care. Psalm 131 describes the result for the believer: a soul weaned from grasping, resting with God because God is there, not because He has given what was demanded.

Solomon's judgment shows what true mothering looks like. Two women claimed one child. The false mother agreed to have the child cut in two. The real mother would rather lose him than see him die, and Solomon gave the child to her because of that sacrificial love (1 Kings 3:26–27). Love that gives up its own rights so that the beloved may live points beyond that courtroom to the heart of God's grace.

Scripture also holds two truths together. God commands honor for mothers with the full weight of the Ten Commandments, and Jesus kept that command while He was dying. Yet Jesus also said that whoever does His Father's will is His brother, sister, and mother (Matthew 12:50). We honor our mothers deeply and worship God alone. That order does not lessen our love for our mothers; it keeps that love from becoming an idol.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:24 – A man leaves his father and mother to be joined to his wife; the closest bond of birth gives way to the new bond of marriage.

  • Psalm 51:5 – David traces his sin back to his conception: in sin his mother conceived him.

  • Psalm 139:13 – God formed the psalmist and covered him in his mother's womb, so a person's life is known to God before birth.

  • Proverbs 23:22, 25 – The son is told not to despise his mother when she is old, and to let her who bore him rejoice; honor continues into old age.

  • Jeremiah 20:14, 17 – In his anguish Jeremiah wishes his mother's womb had been his grave, an honest lament that Scripture preserves.

  • Micah 7:6 – A daughter rises against her mother; family breakdown is a sign of a corrupt age, and Jesus applies the verse to the division His coming brings (Matthew 10:35–36).
