NAHATH - (Nachath, Strong's H5184)

Summary

Nahath is the name of three different men in the Old Testament, and they should not be confused with one another. The first is a grandson of Esau who became a chief of Edom (Genesis 36:13, 17; 1 Chronicles 1:37). The second is a Kohathite Levite in the ancestry of the prophet Samuel (1 Chronicles 6:26), who appears to be the same man called Toah in 1 Chronicles 6:34 and Tohu in 1 Samuel 1:1. The third is a Levite overseer in King Hezekiah's day who helped manage the flood of offerings that poured into the temple after the nation returned to the LORD (2 Chronicles 31:13). The Hebrew name probably means "rest" or "quietness." Some connect it instead to a root meaning "to descend," so the exact derivation is not certain.

None of the three men speaks or acts in a recorded scene, yet each stands at a meaningful point in the biblical story. The Edomite chief belongs to God's kept promise that Esau's descendants would become a people in Mount Seir. The Levite ancestor is a quiet link in the family line that produced Samuel and, later, Heman the singer. Hezekiah's overseer shows that when God's people give generously in worship, faithful people must care for what is given. Together they show God working through ordinary names on a list: keeping His word to Esau, preserving a Levite family through dark generations, and honoring the plain faithfulness of men who kept the storerooms of His house.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Three separate individuals share the name Nahath, and Scripture keeps them clearly apart by family, nation, and era.

Nahath the Edomite chief was a son of Reuel and grandson of Esau through his wife Basemath (Genesis 36:3–4, 13). He is listed among the chiefs of Edom (Genesis 36:17) and again among Reuel's sons in 1 Chronicles 1:37.

Nahath the ancestor of Samuel was a Kohathite Levite named in 1 Chronicles 6:26. When the genealogies are compared, his position matches that of Toah in 1 Chronicles 6:34 and Tohu in 1 Samuel 1:1. Toah (H8430) and Tohu (H8459) are indexed separately, so they are not counted as occurrences of Nahath. Even so, the parallel lists line up closely enough that most readers take them as the same man, though no narrative says so outright. The surrounding names show the same pattern: Zophai, Zuph, and Eliab, Eliel, Elihu.

Nahath the overseer served in Jerusalem under Hezekiah as one of ten Levite assistants to Cononiah and Shimei (2 Chronicles 31:12–13). Scripture does not give his family line.

The name Nachath (נַחַת) is most likely connected to the Hebrew idea of rest or quietness, from the root associated with resting. A less certain proposal links it to a root meaning "to go down" or "descend." "Rest" is the probable meaning, not a settled one. Scripture never comments on the name or makes wordplay with it, so it should not be read as a description of any of these men's lives.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Edomite chief (Genesis 36:13, 17; 1 Chronicles 1:37). Genesis 36 records the descendants of Esau as they settled in Mount Seir. Nahath was the first-listed of Reuel's four sons, with Zerah, Shammah, and Mizzah. All four became chiefs, leaders of clans within Edom. The chapter adds that Edom had kings "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). Edom had organized rule before Israel had kings, though Genesis does not date Nahath's chiefdom to any particular point in Israel's history.

The ancestor of Samuel (1 Chronicles 6:26; compare 6:34; 1 Samuel 1:1). The Chronicler traces the descendants of Kohath, son of Levi, through Korah (1 Chronicles 6:22). Nahath appears in that line a few generations before Elkanah, Samuel's father. The family lived in the hill country of Ephraim at Ramathaim Zophim, so 1 Samuel 1:1 calls Elkanah an Ephraimite, describing where the family lived. The Kohathites had been given cities within Ephraim (1 Chronicles 6:66–67). The later list in 1 Chronicles 6:33–38 runs the same line in reverse, beginning with "Heman the singer," Samuel's grandson, whom David appointed over the music of the LORD's house. Nahath most likely lived during the period of the judges, though the shortened genealogy does not allow a precise date.

Hezekiah's overseer (2 Chronicles 31:13). After Hezekiah cleansed the temple and restored the Passover (2 Chronicles 29–30), he commanded the people to provide for the priests and Levites. Their response overflowed. From the third month to the seventh, offerings piled up in heaps (2 Chronicles 31:5–7). When Hezekiah asked about the heaps, Azariah the chief priest explained that the people had given so much that there was plenty left over, because the LORD had blessed His people (31:10, paraphrased). Hezekiah ordered storerooms prepared, and "they faithfully brought in the offerings, the tithes, and the dedicated things" (31:12). Nahath was among the men who served "under the hand of Cononiah and Shimei his brother, at the commandment of Hezekiah the king and Azariah the ruler of the house of God" (31:13). Others then distributed the gifts fairly to the priestly divisions, "great and small" (31:14–15, paraphrased).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God gave Mount Seir to Esau as a possession (Deuteronomy 2:5), and Genesis 36 records the fulfillment. Nahath the chief belongs to that fulfillment. God kept His word even to the brother outside the chosen line. The chapter's record of chiefs and kings before Israel had any king also stands in contrast with Israel's long wait. One can draw a careful observation from this, though the text does not draw it: the line that organized first was not the line through which God's greatest purposes would come.

The second Nahath matters because of where his line led. Scripture states that this Kohathite family produced Samuel (1 Chronicles 6:27–28, 33–34). It is also striking that the line runs through Korah, whose rebellion brought judgment in the wilderness, yet "the children of Korah did not die" (Numbers 26:11, paraphrased). Out of that family God later raised a prophet who anointed David and a singer who led Israel's worship. Nahath is one of the links in that chain. Scripture tells us nothing of his personal faith, so the point concerns God's preserving purpose, not Nahath's own character.

The third Nahath shows how Chronicles values faithful service. The Chronicler took care to name ten ordinary overseers. That choice signals that the stewardship of offered things was itself service to God. The chapter closes by saying that Hezekiah did what was good, right, and true before the LORD, and that he prospered (31:20–21).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No New Testament passage mentions any Nahath, and none of the three is presented as a type of Christ. There is, however, a fair thematic resonance in the third Nahath's role. When the early church faced a dispute over the daily distribution of food, it appointed trustworthy men so that the ministry of the word would not be neglected (Acts 6:1–6). Paul also names "helps, administrations" among the gifts God has appointed in the church (1 Corinthians 12:28). Hezekiah's overseers offer an Old Testament picture of the same concern. When God's people give generously, faithful hands must make sure the gifts serve their purpose.

5. Spirit & Life

Hezekiah's revival began at the altar but did not end there. Joyful giving produced heaps of grain, oil, and honey, and someone had to build storerooms, keep the records, and distribute the gifts fairly. Scripture records Nahath's name in that work. God notices the person who counts the tithes as surely as the one who leads the singing, and integrity in practical matters is part of honoring Him.

The Levite Nahath offers a different encouragement. He probably lived during the troubled days of the judges, and Scripture records nothing he did. Yet, as a canonical reflection rather than a record of Nahath's own acts, we can see that God was keeping that family, a family descended from rebellious Korah, for the day Hannah would pray at Shiloh and Samuel would be born. God's purposes often run quietly through generations who never see the outcome.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:13, 17 – Nahath, son of Reuel, grandson of Esau, a chief of Edom.

  • Genesis 36:31; Deuteronomy 2:5 – Edom's early kings and God's gift of Mount Seir to Esau.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:37 – The Chronicler's repetition of Reuel's sons.

  • 1 Chronicles 6:22–28, 33–38 – The Kohathite line through Korah to Samuel and Heman, including Nahath (6:26) and Toah (6:34).

  • 1 Samuel 1:1 – Tohu in the ancestry of Elkanah the Ephraimite.

  • Numbers 26:11 – The sons of Korah preserved.

  • 2 Chronicles 31:5–15 – The overflowing offerings under Hezekiah and Nahath's role as an overseer.

  • Acts 6:1–6; 1 Corinthians 12:28 – New Testament concern for faithful administration.
