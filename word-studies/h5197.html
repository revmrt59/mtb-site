DRIP, DROP, PREACH - (nâṭaph, Strong's H5197)

Summary

The Hebrew word nâṭaph means to drip, drop, or fall in drops. Scripture uses it for rain falling from the heavens, honey and myrrh on the lips, and new wine running down the mountains. Hebrew also uses the word for speech. Words can fall on hearers the way rain falls on ground, so nâṭaph can mean to preach or prophesy. Job's counsel "settled on them as dew" (Job 29:22), and God told Ezekiel to "preach against the holy places" (Ezekiel 21:2).

The word's most searching uses come from the prophets. Israel told God's messengers to stop dripping their words on them, as if the prophets were a leak that would not quit (Micah 2:6; Amos 7:16). The same people would gladly welcome a preacher who dripped promises of wine and strong drink (Micah 2:11). Yet the same prophets end with mountains dripping sweet wine when God restores His people (Amos 9:13; Joel 3:18). The word raises a question every listener must answer: what do we do with what God lets fall on us from above?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâṭaph is a Hebrew verb used mostly in poetry and prophecy. Hebrew writers often pictured speech as weather, with teaching falling on people like rain or dew. Moses prayed, "Let my teaching drop as the rain, My speech distill as the dew" (Deuteronomy 32:2), though he used different Hebrew words. That shared picture explains how a verb for dripping water could also come to mean preaching.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to fall in drops, or to let something fall in drops. Context decides whether the subject is weather, a sweet liquid, or speech.

1. To drip or drop as a liquid. The heavens drop rain (Psalm 68:8), lips drip honey (Proverbs 5:3), and mountains drip wine (Amos 9:13).

2. To let words fall gently on hearers. Job says his speech "settled on them as dew" (Job 29:22).

3. To preach or prophesy, that is, to let a message fall on a people. The sense can be neutral, as in God's command to Ezekiel (Ezekiel 20:46). On the lips of scornful hearers it becomes dismissive, which the NKJV renders "prattle" or "spout" (Micah 2:6; Amos 7:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears in four main settings. In old victory poems, the heavens pour rain when the LORD comes near (Judges 5:4; Psalm 68:8). In wisdom and love poetry, it describes sweetness on the lips, both the false sweetness of the immoral woman and the true sweetness of the bride and groom (Proverbs 5:3; Song of Solomon 4:11; 5:13). In Job and the prophets, it describes words falling on listeners. In Joel and Amos, it describes a restored land so fruitful that its hills seem to run with wine.

Tone matters most in the speech uses. The verb itself is ordinary. Ezekiel receives it as a divine command, but Israel's leaders use it as a sneer. The same word can carry respect or contempt, depending on who is speaking and what they think of the message.

4. Theological Significance

When God draws near, His creation responds. Psalm 68:8 says, "The earth shook; The heavens also dropped rain at the presence of God; Sinai itself was moved at the presence of God, the God of Israel." The Song of Deborah describes the same scene in two lines: "the heavens poured, The clouds also poured water" (Judges 5:4). Nothing in creation stays still when the Creator comes near. The sky gives up what it holds.

Using the same verb for rain and for preaching teaches something about God's word. True prophecy is not one person's opinion offered to another. It comes down from above, as rain does. Isaiah draws the same comparison: "as the rain comes down... So shall My word be that goes forth from My mouth; It shall not return to Me void" (Isaiah 55:10–11). God sends His word where He chooses, and it accomplishes what He intends. The listener's part is to receive it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Amos 7:16 and Micah 2:6, 11: "Do not prattle." The word's sharpest use exposes the human heart. At Bethel, the priest Amaziah ordered Amos home to Judah, and God answered through Amos: "You say, 'Do not prophesy against Israel, And do not spout against the house of Isaac'" (Amos 7:16). In Micah the same contempt comes from the people: "'Do not prattle,' you say to those who prophesy" (Micah 2:6). The verb appears three times in Micah 2:6, like the very drip the people want to stop. They treat God's messengers as a steady drip they want to stop.

Micah then shows how inconsistent their complaint is: "If a man should walk in a false spirit And speak a lie, saying, 'I will prophesy to you of wine and drink,' Even he would be the prattler of this people" (Micah 2:11). The people do not object to preaching. They object to preaching that convicts them. A liar who drips promises of drink would be welcome. Micah uses the same verb for the true prophets they silence and for the false prophet they adopt. Their objection was never to the act of preaching. They objected to what God was saying. Paul warns of the same desire in the church, when people "will not endure sound doctrine" and gather teachers to suit their own desires (2 Timothy 4:3, paraphrased).

Ezekiel 20:46 and 21:2: preaching under orders. God tells Ezekiel, "set your face toward the south; preach against the south" (Ezekiel 20:46), and then, "set your face toward Jerusalem, preach against the holy places" (Ezekiel 21:2). The surrounding verses speak of a fire in the forest and the LORD's drawn sword. Ezekiel did not choose his target. God aimed the message, and the prophet let it fall. Even so, his hearers brushed it aside, saying he only spoke in parables (Ezekiel 20:49). Ezekiel's own audience shows the same dismissal Amos and Micah faced.

Job 29:22–23: wisdom that settles. Job remembers when his counsel was welcomed: "my speech settled on them as dew." The next verse shows how people received it: "They waited for me as for the rain, And they opened their mouth wide as for the spring rain." Dew falls quietly and soaks in. Job's hearers did not wait for him to finish so they could argue. They opened themselves to his words, the way dry ground takes in water. This is the opposite of Micah's audience, which saw the same kind of speech as a nuisance.

Proverbs 5:3 and the Song of Solomon: two kinds of sweetness. "The lips of an immoral woman drip honey, And her mouth is smoother than oil" (Proverbs 5:3). The bridegroom tells his bride, "Your lips, O my spouse, Drip as the honeycomb" (Song of Solomon 4:11), and she says of him, "His lips are lilies, Dripping liquid myrrh" (5:13). Both drip honey. Temptation does not present itself as bitter. It imitates the real sweetness God gives within married love, and Proverbs goes on to show where that false sweetness leads. The sweetness is the same on the surface, so discernment has to look at the source and where the path ends.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Amos uses the word twice, and the two uses frame the book's story. In chapter 7, Israel tells the prophet to stop dripping words of judgment. In chapter 9, God promises that "The mountains shall drip with sweet wine, And all the hills shall flow with it" (Amos 9:13). Joel ends the same way: "the mountains shall drip with new wine... A fountain shall flow from the house of the LORD" (Joel 3:18). The people refused the word that dripped down in warning, but God's last word is a land overflowing with blessing. In Amos the harvest comes so fast that "the plowman shall overtake the reaper," which reverses the hard ground of Genesis 3.

These pictures point to Christ. He is the Word who came down from heaven (John 1:14; 6:38). Many people heard Him the way Israel heard Micah. When His teaching convicted them, they said, "This is a hard saying," and walked away (John 6:60, 66). Joel's fountain from the house of the LORD connects with Ezekiel's river flowing from the temple (Ezekiel 47) and with Jesus' promise of living water, which John identifies as the Spirit (John 7:37–39). The vision is completed in the river of life flowing from God's throne (Revelation 22:1). The New Testament does not directly link the wine at Cana (John 2) to the dripping mountains, but the sign fits them well. When Jesus begins His ministry by providing abundant good wine, He gives an early taste of the plenty the prophets promised.

7. Spirit & Life

This word asks each of us how we respond to what God sends. The same God who once made the heavens drop rain still lets His word fall on His people. He does it through Scripture, through faithful preaching, and through the Spirit's quiet conviction. That conviction often comes as a drip rather than a flood. A truth returns again and again until we deal with it. It is tempting to call it prattle, a leak in our comfort we would like to fix. Micah shows that the problem with that attitude lies with the hearer, not the message.

The Bible shows two ways to receive God's word. Micah's audience wanted the word stopped. Job's hearers "opened their mouth wide as for the spring rain." The difference is not how smart or educated a listener is. It is hunger. Those who know they are dry welcome the rain. Proverbs adds a warning: what sounds sweet is not always good. A message that only flatters us should make us suspicious, while a word that wounds and heals may be the honeycomb itself. Amos's own book holds out hope for those who listen. The God who sends a word of conviction is the same God who promises mountains running with wine.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 5:4 — Deborah's song uses the word twice, for the heavens and the clouds pouring water as the LORD marches out from Seir. The doubling stresses how completely the sky gave way before Him.

  • Song of Solomon 5:5 — The bride rises to open the door for her beloved, and her hands and fingers drip with myrrh on the handles of the lock. The scene shows longing and readiness, and the fragrance lingers after he has gone.

  • Psalm 68:8 — The heavens drop rain at God's presence as Sinai shakes before Him. Creation responds when the God of Israel draws near.
