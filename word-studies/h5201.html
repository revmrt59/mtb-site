KEEP, BEAR A GRUDGE, RESERVE - (nâṭar, Strong's H5201)

Summary

The Hebrew verb nâṭar means to keep, to guard, or to maintain. Scripture uses it in two ways. In the Song of Solomon it describes the ordinary work of keeping and tending a vineyard. Everywhere else it describes keeping something inside: holding onto anger or a grudge and refusing to let it go. In that second sense, "keeping" is something a person does. A grudge does not simply linger in the heart. It is held.

That second sense makes this small word, found only nine times, the vocabulary for one of the Bible's clearest pictures of God's heart. God forbids His people to "bear any grudge" against one another (Leviticus 19:18). He declares that He Himself will not "keep His anger forever" toward His people (Psalm 103:9) and calls the unfaithful to return on that basis (Jeremiah 3:12). He also warns that He "reserves wrath for His enemies" (Nahum 1:2). God has every right to keep His anger, yet He chooses mercy toward those who return to Him. He then calls His people to treat one another the same way.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâṭar is a Hebrew verb used across the Law, the Psalms, the Song of Solomon, and the Prophets. It belongs to the everyday world of guarding and maintaining. A vineyard needed someone to watch over it, protect it from damage, and bring in its fruit. That practical background helps explain the word's other use. To keep anger is to guard it carefully, as a keeper guards a vineyard.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To keep or tend property, especially a vineyard: guarding it, caring for it, and looking after its fruit (Song of Solomon 1:6; 8:11–12).

2. To keep anger or resentment: holding onto wrath or a grudge against someone instead of releasing it (Leviticus 19:18; Psalm 103:9; Jeremiah 3:5, 12; Nahum 1:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The emotional sense often appears without a stated object. In passages such as Leviticus 19:18 and Jeremiah 3:5, the Hebrew has no separate word for "grudge" or "anger." The verb alone carries the idea, and English translations supply the missing word. In these settings, simply "keeping" against someone already means holding resentment.

What changes the meaning is who does the keeping and toward whom. When people keep a grudge against their own people, Scripture forbids it. When God declines to keep His anger toward His people, Scripture celebrates it as mercy. When God keeps wrath for His enemies, the word announces His justice.

One pairing deserves notice. In both Leviticus 19:18 and Nahum 1:2, nâṭar stands beside "vengeance." Israel is told, "You shall not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge." Nahum says the LORD "will take vengeance on His adversaries, And He reserves wrath for His enemies." The same two actions that God forbids His people, He claims for Himself.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God whose anger is real and fully justified, yet never uncontrolled. He is not a God who cannot let go, and He is not a God who never minds. When the LORD revealed His name to Moses, He described Himself as merciful and gracious, slow to anger, forgiving iniquity, yet by no means clearing the guilty (Exodus 34:6–7, paraphrased). The uses of nâṭar follow both halves of that self-description. He does not keep His anger forever toward His people, and He does keep wrath for those who stand against Him to the end. Micah marvels at the same thing: God does not retain His anger forever, because He delights in mercy (Micah 7:18, paraphrased).

When God releases anger, then, He is not ignoring sin. The God who could rightly keep His wrath chooses to lay it down. Mercy in Scripture always comes from the one who had the right to judge.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 19:18 — the forbidden grudge. This command sits in a chapter that opens with God's call, "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (Leviticus 19:2). Verse 18 ends with "I am the LORD," so the command rests on God's own character. Keeping a grudge is set directly against the positive command beside it: "you shall love your neighbor as yourself." A person cannot guard an offense against a neighbor and love that neighbor at the same time. Something kept that carefully slowly divides God's people from one another.

Psalm 103:9 — the anger God does not keep. David writes, "He will not always strive with us, Nor will He keep His anger forever." The surrounding psalm explains why. God has not dealt with us according to our sins, and He has removed our transgressions as far as the east is from the west (Psalm 103:10–12, paraphrased). Verse 9 gives the reason for that removal. God does not hold onto the offense. The verse does not say He was never angry. His anger is real, and He will not keep it.

Jeremiah 3:5 and 3:12 — presumption and return. The word appears twice in this chapter with opposite effects. In verse 5, Judah talks as if it knows God's mercy: "Will He remain angry forever? Will He keep it to the end?" God answers immediately: "Behold, you have spoken and done evil things, As you were able." The people quoted a true thing about God and used it as permission to go on sinning. Seven verses later, God makes the promise in earnest: "Return, backsliding Israel... For I am merciful... I will not remain angry forever." The words are almost the same, but the second promise comes inside a summons. The mercy Judah presumed upon is offered only to those who come back. Paul faces the same distortion: "Shall we continue in sin that grace may abound? Certainly not!" (Romans 6:1–2).

Nahum 1:2 — wrath kept for enemies. "The LORD will take vengeance on His adversaries, And He reserves wrath for His enemies." Nahum spoke against Nineveh, a city that had already been spared once in Jonah's day and then returned to cruelty. God's patience is not indifference. Wrath that He does not keep toward the repentant, He does keep toward those who remain His adversaries. This sober verse is what gives Psalm 103:9 its weight. The mercy is precious because the wrath is real.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus named Leviticus 19:18, the very verse that forbids the grudge, as the second great commandment: love your neighbor as yourself (Matthew 22:39, paraphrased). He made love of neighbor central to the life of His kingdom, and He also made forgiveness of others part of the prayer He taught His disciples (Matthew 6:12).

The larger story also answers a question the Old Testament raises. How can God both reserve wrath and refuse to keep His anger toward His people? Paul warns the unrepentant that they are "treasuring up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath" (Romans 2:5), language close to Nahum's picture of wrath being stored. Paul also says that believers are justified by Christ's blood and saved from wrath through Him (Romans 5:9, paraphrased). The New Testament does not quote Psalm 103:9 as fulfilled at the cross. Still, the gospel shows how God can lay down His anger without abandoning justice. That justice was satisfied in His Son, so the promise "I will not remain angry forever" rests on a finished work.

7. Spirit & Life

Because God judges justly, His people can leave vengeance in His hands. Leviticus 19 still calls them to judge their neighbor in righteousness (19:15), but verse 18 forbids both vengeance and the grudge, and Nahum 1:2 shows God taking both into His own hands. Settling personal scores is not our job. Moses records God's words that vengeance belongs to Him (Deuteronomy 32:35, paraphrased). A believer who lets go of a grudge has not given up on justice. He has handed the case to the only Judge who never errs. Paul's counsel fits this word closely: "do not let the sun go down on your wrath, nor give place to the devil" (Ephesians 4:26–27). Anger that is kept overnight, guarded and tended, gives the enemy room to work.

Forgiving others also means sharing God's own way of treating people. The God who could keep His anger forever toward us chose not to. Asking us to release a grudge is asking us to treat others the way He has treated us. Jeremiah 3 adds a caution for our own hearts. The comfort that God does not stay angry belongs to people who are returning to Him. It gives no protection to people who are using His mercy as cover.

The vineyard use offers a searching analogy. In Song of Solomon 1:6 the young woman says, "They made me the keeper of the vineyards, But my own vineyard I have not kept." In the poem, her "own vineyard" most naturally refers to herself, darkened and worn by forced labor in the sun. The same verb appears twice. She kept what others assigned her and neglected what was hers. The verse is not written as a lesson on stewardship, but the picture applies to the believer. It is possible to keep everyone else's expectations, ministries, and demands while leaving one's own walk with God untended. It is also possible to guard a grudge with great care while neglecting one's own heart.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Song of Solomon 8:11 — Solomon's vineyard at Baal Hamon is leased to "keepers," each expected to bring a large payment for its fruit. Here the word describes ordinary, accountable stewardship of another's property.

Song of Solomon 8:12 — The bride speaks of her own vineyard as before her and grants a share to those who "tend" its fruit. The theme of keeping one's own vineyard, raised in 1:6, returns near the end of the book with her own vineyard now in view.

The remaining occurrences, Leviticus 19:18, Psalm 103:9, Jeremiah 3:5 and 3:12, Nahum 1:2, and Song of Solomon 1:6, are discussed above.
