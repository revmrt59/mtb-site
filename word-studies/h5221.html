STRIKE, SMITE, KILL, DEFEAT - (nâkâh, Strong's H5221)

Summary

The Hebrew verb nâkâh means to strike: to land a blow, whether with a hand, a rod, a sword, or an army. Depending on the setting, it can describe a beating that wounds, a blow that kills, or a military attack that defeats a whole city. It is one of the most common action words in the Old Testament. It fills the battle accounts of Joshua, Samuel, and Kings. It also governs the laws about violence in Exodus and Numbers, and it describes God's own hand in judgment, as early as the Flood (Genesis 8:21) and most memorably when He struck Egypt so that His people could go free.

The word gains its greatest weight in the passages that turn God's blow in an unexpected direction. Isaiah describes the Servant of the Lord as "stricken, Smitten by God," and Zechariah hears the Lord command, "Strike the Shepherd." Jesus applied Zechariah's words to Himself on the night He was arrested. Between the plagues of Egypt and the cross runs a long story of God striking oppressors, disciplining His own people, and even striking David's heart with conviction. At the end of that story, the blow His justice required falls on His own Son.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâkâh is a Hebrew verb describing physical impact. It appears throughout the Old Testament, with its heaviest use in the historical books: Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings, and Chronicles. These books record Israel's battles, assassinations, and royal conflicts. The word also has a firm place in the Law, where it describes assault and homicide. In the Prophets it becomes a primary way of speaking about God's judgment and discipline.

The NKJV translates it with a wide range of English words, including "struck," "killed," "defeated," "attacked," "beat," "wounded," and even "clapped" (2 Kings 11:12). That variety reflects context, since the Hebrew verb itself stays the same. The word describes what happened physically. The surrounding story tells the reader whether the blow was murder, justice, accident, victory, or judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, nâkâh means to strike or hit. Its main senses are:

1. To strike a blow that wounds or punishes without necessarily killing. An Egyptian was "beating a Hebrew" (Exodus 2:11). Balaam struck his donkey (Numbers 22:23). Proverbs speaks of a parent who will "beat him with a rod" in correction (Proverbs 23:13).

2. To strike fatally, to kill. Examples include David's blow against Goliath (1 Samuel 17:50) and the homicide laws of Numbers 35.

3. To attack or defeat in battle, as when Abram attacked the kings by night (Genesis 14:15) or Joshua struck city after city (Joshua 10:28–39).

4. To strike an object, or to afflict through natural forces: striking the river or a rock with a rod (Exodus 7:17; 17:6), or the sun beating down on a person (Jonah 4:8).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Whether a blow wounds or kills depends on who strikes, what is struck, and why. When an army is the subject, the word usually means military defeat. When an individual strikes another person, the question of intent becomes central. When God is the subject, the word describes plagues, judgment, or chastening.

One figurative use deserves particular attention. Twice the subject of nâkâh is David's own heart. In 1 Samuel 24:5, "David's heart troubled him." In 2 Samuel 24:10, "David's heart condemned him." In both verses the Hebrew says his heart struck him. Section 7 explores what that reveals.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture establishes early that God's striking serves His justice and His purpose to set His people free. Before the plagues began, the Lord promised, "I will stretch out My hand and strike Egypt with all My wonders which I will do in its midst; and after that he will let you go" (Exodus 3:20). The blow and the release are joined in the same sentence. The first plague carried its purpose openly: "By this you shall know that I am the LORD" (Exodus 7:17). The last plague confronted Egypt's gods as well as its people: "against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment" (Exodus 12:12). Israel remembered this in worship for centuries, singing, "To Him who struck Egypt in their firstborn, For His mercy endures forever" (Psalm 136:10). The same act was judgment for the oppressor and mercy for the oppressed.

Because the word is morally neutral, God's law looks behind the blow to the heart. Numbers 35 uses nâkâh repeatedly as it sets up the cities of refuge. Someone who strikes another with an iron implement "so that he dies," or strikes him in enmity so that he dies, is a murderer (Numbers 35:16, 21). Someone who kills a person accidentally, without hatred, may flee to a city of refuge and be protected (Numbers 35:11, 15; Deuteronomy 19:4–6). The physical result may be the same in both cases. God's justice weighs intention, instrument, and circumstances, and it makes room for mercy where there was no malice.

When God strikes His own people, the prophets present it as discipline meant to bring them back. Isaiah pictures Israel as a body bruised from head to foot and asks why they should be struck any longer when they only rebel more (Isaiah 1:5). He grieves that the people do not turn to the One who strikes them (Isaiah 9:13). Jeremiah says the Lord struck them but they did not grieve, and that they refused correction and made their faces harder than rock (Jeremiah 5:3; compare 2:30). The tragedy in these passages lies less in the blows than in the numbness that will not feel them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 53:4 — The Servant who was smitten. "Surely He has borne our griefs And carried our sorrows; Yet we esteemed Him stricken, Smitten by God, and afflicted." Throughout the Old Testament, nâkâh describes the blow that falls on rebels and oppressors. So when onlookers saw the Servant suffering, they drew the obvious conclusion: God must be striking Him for His own sin. The passage corrects them immediately. The next verse explains that He was wounded for our transgressions and bruised for our iniquities. Their observation that He was smitten was true. Their explanation of why was wrong. He was struck in the place of others, and Peter draws on this chapter when he writes of Christ, "by whose stripes you were healed" (1 Peter 2:24).

Zechariah 13:7 — "Strike the Shepherd." In Isaiah, people misread the Servant's suffering. In Zechariah, the Lord Himself gives the command: "Awake, O sword, against My Shepherd, Against the Man who is My Companion... Strike the Shepherd, And the sheep will be scattered." The One struck is God's own Companion, close to Him and dear to Him. On the night of His arrest, Jesus quoted this verse and applied it to Himself: "I will strike the Shepherd, And the sheep of the flock will be scattered" (Matthew 26:31). The disciples' flight from Gethsemane fulfilled the scattering. The cross fulfilled the blow.

Exodus 17 and Numbers 20 — The rock that was struck. At Horeb, God told Moses to take "your rod with which you struck the river" and go (Exodus 17:5). The rod that had brought judgment on Egypt's Nile was now lifted over a rock, while God said, "I will stand before you there on the rock... you shall strike the rock, and water will come out of it, that the people may drink" (Exodus 17:6). A grumbling people deserved the rod. The rock received the blow instead, and the people received water. Years later at Kadesh, God told Moses to speak to the rock (Numbers 20:8). Moses instead "struck the rock twice with his rod" (Numbers 20:11). Water still came, but God held Moses accountable for failing to honor Him before the people (Numbers 20:12). Paul writes that "that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4), and Jesus later cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink" (John 7:37). Exodus does not label the first striking as a picture of the cross. Read alongside Paul and John, though, the pattern stands out clearly. As a further devotional analogy, not something Numbers 20 itself explains, many have seen in the striking at Horeb and the command to speak at Kadesh a picture of Christ, struck once for all (compare Hebrews 9:28), to whom His people now come for life.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word traces an arc across the Old Testament. In Egypt, God strikes the oppressor and the captives go free. In Isaiah and Zechariah, God strikes His innocent Servant, His Shepherd, and captives again go free. Both blows accomplish deliverance, but the second reveals what mercy costs. Other occurrences anticipate the same suffering. The Servant says He gave His back to those who struck Him (Isaiah 50:6), and Micah describes the judge of Israel struck on the cheek with a rod (Micah 5:1), a humiliation in a siege setting that many read as resonating with Christ's sufferings, though the New Testament does not quote it.

In Christian book order, the Old Testament's closing verse uses this word. God promises to send Elijah to turn the hearts of fathers and children, or else He will come and strike the earth with a curse (Malachi 4:6). The prophetic books end with that threat unresolved. The New Testament answers it. The Shepherd was struck, the curse fell on Him, and the Bible's final chapter promises that there shall be no more curse (Revelation 22:3).

7. Spirit & Life

David's story shows that God's striking can be a kindness. In the cave, David spared Saul's life but cut off a corner of his robe, and afterward his heart struck him (1 Samuel 24:5). The offense was small by most standards, yet his conscience would not let it pass, and he restrained his men from harming the Lord's anointed. Years later, after numbering the people out of pride, his heart struck him again. This time he went straight to God: "I have sinned greatly... take away the iniquity of Your servant" (2 Samuel 24:10).

Set these verses beside the people in Jeremiah who were struck and felt nothing (Jeremiah 5:3), and the contrast becomes sobering. A heart that can still be wounded by its own sin is a gift. It stops a person early and sends them back to God, as David's prayer for a clean heart (Psalm 51:10) shows. The danger lies in hardness that no longer feels the blow.

There is also comfort here for the believer. The blow that sin deserved has already fallen on Christ. God's correction of His children is meant to restore them. Hosea's call carries the same hope: the One who has struck us is the One who will bind us up (Hosea 6:1).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 121:6 — The psalmist promises that the sun will not strike God's people by day, nor the moon by night. The Lord guards His people against the hostile forces of a fallen world as well as against human enemies.

  • Jonah 4:8 — "The sun beat on Jonah's head, so that he grew faint." God used a natural blow to expose the anger in His prophet's heart.

  • 2 Kings 13:18–19 — Joash struck the ground only three times with his arrows, and Elisha was angry. His few strikes, commonly read as half-hearted, limited the victory God was ready to give.

  • Proverbs 23:13 — "If you beat him with a rod, he will not die." Wise correction is presented as an act of care for a child's future.

  • Hosea 6:1 — Israel says that the Lord has stricken and will bind up. The hand that disciplines is also the hand that heals.
