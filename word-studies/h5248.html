NIMROD - (Nimrôwd, Strong's H5248)

Summary

Nimrod was a descendant of Noah through Ham and Cush. Genesis singles him out as the one who "began to be a mighty one on the earth" (Genesis 10:8). The name is traditionally connected with the Hebrew root marad, "to rebel," and is often explained as "rebel" or "let us rebel." That derivation is a long-standing traditional reading rather than a certain etymology, and Scripture itself never comments on the name's meaning. What Scripture does make clear is his role. He was a renowned "mighty hunter before the LORD." His kingdom began at Babel and other cities in the land of Shinar, and from there he went on to Assyria and built Nineveh and its neighboring cities (Genesis 10:9–12). In the first generations after the Flood, Nimrod is the first person Scripture describes as ruling a kingdom.

Nimrod is named only four times, but his shadow is long. Babylon and Assyria, the two empires that later devastated Israel and Judah, both trace their beginnings to his kingdom. Centuries later Micah could call Assyria "the land of Nimrod" (Micah 5:6). He did so in the same prophecy that promises a Ruler from little Bethlehem whose "goings forth are from of old, From everlasting" (Micah 5:2), and Micah declares, "this One shall be peace" (Micah 5:5). The first empire-builder of the Bible stands at one end of a long story about human power. At the other end, God answers that power with His own King.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Nimrod is a single individual: son (or descendant) of Cush, grandson of Ham, and great-grandson of Noah (Genesis 10:6–8; 1 Chronicles 1:8–10). The Hebrew word for "begot" can describe a more distant descent, so Nimrod may be a later descendant of Cush rather than his immediate son. The text does not settle that question. Genesis does treat him differently from his relatives. Cush's other sons are listed in Genesis 10:7, but Nimrod receives a separate sentence and a short narrative of his own. He belongs to the period after the Flood, during the spread of Noah's descendants into nations.

The name Nimrod is traditionally linked to the Hebrew verb marad, "to rebel," and on that basis is rendered "rebel" or "let us rebel." Readers should hold this meaning loosely. It is a traditional and widely repeated derivation, not one the Bible states or explains, and the actual origin of the name is uncertain. Some historians have tried to connect Nimrod with a Mesopotamian god or with a known king such as Sargon of Akkad. None of these identifications can be confirmed, and the biblical text presents him simply as a man in Noah's line. The name's possible meaning should not be allowed to carry the interpretation. Nimrod's character has to be read from what Genesis and Micah actually say about him.

All four occurrences of the name are accounted for: Genesis 10:8, 10:9, 1 Chronicles 1:10, and Micah 5:6. In the last, the name stands for a region, "the land of Nimrod," set in parallel with "the land of Assyria." Babel/Babylon, Assyria, Nineveh, and Shinar are separately indexed names. They are discussed here because they are tied to Nimrod's story, not as further occurrences of his name.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The first mighty one (Genesis 10:8; 1 Chronicles 1:10). The Table of Nations in Genesis 10 moves mostly by names and descent. At Nimrod the rhythm changes, and the text slows down to say, "he began to be a mighty one on the earth." The word for "mighty one" is gibbor, used elsewhere for warriors and heroes, and it is not negative in itself. Here it marks something new in the narrative. Nimrod is the first post-Flood figure Genesis describes this way. That he rose above the families and clans around him is a reasonable inference from the kingdom notice that follows, though the text does not describe all earlier post-Flood government. The Chronicler repeats the sentence word for word, so even in Israel's condensed genealogy of the nations, Nimrod's rise is the one detail worth keeping.

The mighty hunter (Genesis 10:9). "He was a mighty hunter before the LORD; therefore it is said, 'Like Nimrod the mighty hunter before the LORD.'" His reputation became a saying. People evidently compared a man of exceptional prowess to Nimrod. Whether "hunter" refers only to tracking game or also to the conquering of people is not stated. Hunting skill and royal power were closely linked in the ancient world, and the next verse moves straight to his kingdom, so the two ideas sit side by side in the text.

The kingdom and its cities (Genesis 10:10–12). "And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar." This is the first use of the word "kingdom" in the Bible. Nimrod's legacy is a set of cities under one rule, not just a family line. In the NKJV reading, he then went north (some translations instead make Asshur the subject who went out and built Nineveh): "From that land he went to Assyria and built Nineveh, Rehoboth Ir, Calah, and Resen between Nineveh and Calah." Babel became Babylon. Nineveh became the capital of Assyria. The two powers that would later carry Israel and Judah into exile both stand in this one short paragraph.

Babel (Genesis 11). Genesis 11 does not name Nimrod. Even so, the Tower of Babel is built in "the land of Shinar" at Babel (Genesis 11:2, 9), the place where his kingdom began. There people say, in effect, "let us build a city and a tower and make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered" (Genesis 11:4, paraphrased). God had told Noah's family to fill the earth (Genesis 9:1). God came down, confused their language, and scattered them. The Table of Nations in chapter 10 and the Babel account in chapter 11 overlap in time rather than following one another strictly. That makes it reasonable to read Babel as an expression of the kind of kingdom Nimrod began, though Scripture does not say he personally led the tower project.

The land of Nimrod (Micah 5:5–6). In the eighth century B.C., Assyria was the terror of the region. Micah promised that when "the Assyrian comes into our land," God's people would raise up "seven shepherds and eight princely men" against him. "They shall waste with the sword the land of Assyria, And the land of Nimrod at its entrances; Thus He shall deliver us from the Assyrian." By calling Assyria "the land of Nimrod," Micah reaches all the way back to Genesis. The empire threatening Judah was the latest form of the kingdom Nimrod started.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states several things plainly: Nimrod is the first post-Flood figure said to begin "to be a mighty one," he ruled a kingdom, its beginning was in Shinar at Babel, Nineveh and other Assyrian cities were tied to him, and Micah later used his name for Assyria. Other conclusions are drawn from the context. The most important, an inference rather than a statement, is that Nimrod marks a shift toward rule by a powerful king over several cities. Genesis does not condemn Nimrod in so many words, but it places his kingdom right beside Babel, where God judged human pride. The connection is hard to miss.

The phrase "before the LORD" carries the central tension. It can simply mean "in the sight of the LORD," as when Abraham "stood before the LORD" (Genesis 18:22). Read that way, the saying may mean Nimrod's might was great even by God's measure. Jewish and Christian interpreters have long read it more darkly, as might displayed in God's face, in defiance of Him. Hebrew usage does not require the hostile reading. That reading draws its strength from the setting at Babel and from the later history of the cities he founded. The safest statement is this: Nimrod's strength was exercised under God's eye, and what grew from it eventually set itself against God.

The detail that one man stands at the root of both Babylon and Assyria matters. Israel experienced these as two separate waves of judgment, the northern kingdom falling to Assyria and Judah later falling to Babylon. Genesis shows that they shared a common origin. The prophets saw both empires as instruments in God's hand, Assyria as "the rod of My anger" (Isaiah 10:5) and Babylon's king as God's servant (Jeremiah 25:9), and also as proud powers God would judge (Isaiah 10:12; Jeremiah 25:12). God was sovereign over Nimrod's kingdom from its first city to its last.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest link is Micah's own. In Micah 5:2 God promises a Ruler from Bethlehem Ephrathah, "little among the thousands of Judah," whose origins are "from everlasting." Matthew 2:5–6 explicitly applies this prophecy to Jesus' birth in Bethlehem. A few verses later in the same chapter, the land of Nimrod appears as the enemy from which "He shall deliver us." Micah sets the first great empire-builder against the coming Shepherd-King. Nimrod's kingdom began with powerful cities. The Messiah comes from a small town, stands and shepherds His flock "in the strength of the LORD" (Micah 5:4, paraphrased), and "this One shall be peace." His strength is received from God, not seized from others. In Micah the delivered people "waste with the sword" the land of Nimrod, but the deliverance itself is credited to Him.

A wider pattern develops across the canon. Babel in Shinar becomes Babylon, the city that destroyed Jerusalem. In Daniel, Babylon's king sets up a golden image and demands worship (Daniel 3). Revelation 17–18 then uses "Babylon the Great" as the name for the proud, seductive world system that God brings down before the New Jerusalem descends. Nimrod is not named in the New Testament, and Revelation does not mention him. Still, the line from his Babel to Revelation's Babylon is a real canonical development and goes beyond a loose association. The human city that seeks a name for itself in Genesis 11 finally falls, and the city "having the glory of God" takes its place (Revelation 21:10–11). Isaiah's title for the coming Child, "Mighty God" (Isaiah 9:6), also sets the true Mighty One against every "mighty one on the earth." That contrast is a thematic resonance and should not be read as a type Scripture formally identifies.

5. Spirit & Life

Nimrod's story is told in just a few verses, yet it lays out a pattern every generation meets. Human strength that gathers power, builds cities, and makes a name for itself can look unstoppable. Genesis reports Nimrod's achievements honestly and does not mock his ability. It simply keeps the story moving, and the story moves to Babel, where the Lord "came down" (Genesis 11:5). God is never threatened by the strongest kingdom on earth. He watches it, limits it, uses it, and in the end judges it.

Micah gave this truth to a frightened people. Judah faced Assyria, the land of Nimrod, with no army able to match it. God did not answer with a bigger empire. He answered with a promise of a Ruler from Bethlehem who would be their peace. That is still how God answers proud power. The gospel does not invite us to become the next Nimrod, mighty in our own name. It calls us to belong to the Shepherd whose kingdom comes through humility and whose strength is the strength of the LORD. Christians who feel small before powerful systems can take heart. The empires of Nimrod's line have all fallen, and the Child of Bethlehem still reigns.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:8 – Nimrod's descent from Cush and his description as one who "began to be a mighty one on the earth."

  • Genesis 10:9 – The "mighty hunter before the LORD" and the proverbial saying about him.

  • Genesis 10:10–12 – The first "kingdom" in Scripture: Babel and the cities of Shinar, then Nineveh and the Assyrian cities.

  • Genesis 11:1–9 – The Tower of Babel in Shinar, the setting that shapes how Nimrod's kingdom is understood (Nimrod not named).

  • 1 Chronicles 1:10 – Verbatim repetition of Genesis 10:8, preserving his place in Israel's genealogy of the nations.

  • Micah 5:2, 5–6 – The Ruler from Bethlehem who "shall be peace," and deliverance from Assyria, "the land of Nimrod."

  • Matthew 2:5–6 – The New Testament application of Micah 5:2 to Jesus' birth.

  • Revelation 17–18 – The fall of "Babylon the Great," the final form of the proud city whose beginnings Genesis ties to Nimrod's kingdom.
