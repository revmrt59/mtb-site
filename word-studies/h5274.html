LOCK, BOLT, ENCLOSE, SANDAL - (nâʻal, Strong's H5274)

Summary

The Hebrew verb nâʻal means to fasten something securely. In some passages it describes sliding a bar across a door to lock it. In others it describes tying sandals onto someone's feet. Both are acts of fastening, and the word appears only eight times in the Old Testament. Its few uses are vivid. Ehud locks a king's doors behind him. Amnon has a door bolted against his wounded sister Tamar. The bridegroom in the Song of Solomon calls his bride "a garden enclosed." Captives and an abandoned child are given sandals.

The word gains its spiritual weight from what is fastened and why. In Amnon's hands a bolted door becomes a wall of cruel rejection. In the Song it pictures a love kept whole and reserved for one beloved. When the word describes sandals, it speaks of mercy that lifts the humiliated to dignity, as God did for Jerusalem in Ezekiel 16. These pictures point toward Christ. He is the Door who does not bolt out those who come to Him, the Bridegroom who keeps His people for Himself, and the One who, like the father of the prodigal, puts sandals on the feet of returning sons.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâʻal is a Hebrew verb for securing something, either with a bar or with a cord. Ancient doors were often secured by sliding a wooden bar into place. Sandals were held on with leather straps tied around the foot. One verb covers both actions, so the same word that locks a room can also describe dressing someone's feet.

The word appears in historical narrative (Judges, 2 Samuel, 2 Chronicles), love poetry (the Song of Solomon), and prophecy (Ezekiel). Footwear carried social meaning in Israel's world. Going barefoot often marked grief, poverty, or captivity. David climbed the Mount of Olives barefoot and weeping as he fled Absalom (2 Samuel 15:30). To be given sandals meant being treated as a free and honored person.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to fasten securely. Scripture uses it in two distinct senses:

1. To lock, bolt, or shut up a door or an enclosed space so that no one can pass through. Ehud "locked" the doors of the upper room (Judges 3:23), and the bride is "a garden enclosed… a spring shut up" (Song of Solomon 4:12).

2. To fasten sandals on someone's feet, that is, to provide them with footwear. The rescuers of Judah's captives "gave them sandals" (2 Chronicles 28:15).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word tells us only that something was secured. The meaning of the act comes from the person doing the fastening and from what is being shut in or shut out. In Judges 3 a lock hides what has happened in a room. In 2 Samuel 13 a bolt keeps a person out. In Song of Solomon 4:12 the language of enclosure describes something precious that is protected. In the two sandal passages the fastening is done for someone, and it is always an act of kindness toward people who had nothing.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these uses show that a boundary takes its moral character from the heart of the one who sets it. People bar doors to escape, to conceal, and to discard someone they have used. Yet Scripture also uses the same word to honor a bride whose love is guarded for her beloved. A lock can serve cruelty or faithfulness.

The sandal passages show that God's grace goes beyond rescue. In Ezekiel 16 God saves the abandoned child and then also clothes her and gives her sandals. In 2 Chronicles 28 repentance moves the northern kingdom of Israel to do more than release its prisoners. The soldiers give up the captives, and men chosen by name then dress them, give them sandals, feed them, and anoint them. God's mercy, and the mercy He produces in His people, restores what humiliation had stripped away.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Ehud and Amnon: doors locked by human hands (Judges 3:23–24; 2 Samuel 13:17–18). After killing Moab's king Eglon, Ehud "shut the doors of the upper room behind him and locked them" (Judges 3:23). Ehud was the deliverer God had raised up for oppressed Israel, and the lock gave him time to escape. Eglon's servants found the doors locked and assumed their master was "attending to his needs in the cool chamber" (Judges 3:24). Their delay worked in Israel's favor. In this passage the lock hides a death and buys time.

Amnon's bolted door is far darker. After forcing himself on his half-sister Tamar, Amnon turned his lust into hatred. He ordered, "Put this woman out, away from me, and bolt the door behind her" (2 Samuel 13:17). He does not even use her name. The narrator then pauses to describe what Tamar is wearing: "a robe of many colors, for the king's virgin daughters wore such apparel. And his servant put her out and bolted the door behind her" (13:18). The robe marked her as an honored virgin daughter of the king, and the bolt shut that honor out in the street. Tamar tore the robe and went away in grief. Sin does not stop at the act itself. It goes on to push out and isolate the person it has wronged, and the bolt on Amnon's door shows that cruelty in physical form.

The enclosed garden (Song of Solomon 4:12). The bridegroom sings, "A garden enclosed Is my sister, my spouse, A spring shut up, A fountain sealed." Nâʻal appears twice in the verse, in "enclosed" and "shut up." Gardens and springs were valuable in a dry land. A walled garden was guarded, and a sealed spring was kept clean for its owner. The bride's love is not open to anyone who passes by. It is kept whole for her beloved, and he praises her for it.

A few lines later the garden opens. The bride invites her beloved to come into his garden, and he replies that he has come (Song of Solomon 4:16–5:1). She was never closed off from him. She was enclosed for him. The contrast with Tamar comes from this same verb. Tamar, a virgin daughter, was violated and then shut out. The bride's purity is honored, and her enclosure is a protected place of joy that her beloved enters with delight.

Sandals for captives and a foundling (2 Chronicles 28:15; Ezekiel 16:10). When Israel's army carried off men, women, and children of Judah as captives, the prophet Oded warned the victors that they were adding to their guilt before the LORD, and leaders in Samaria responded (2 Chronicles 28:9–14). The armed men left the captives and the spoil before the leaders and the assembly (2 Chronicles 28:14). Then "the men who were designated by name rose up and took the captives." They clothed and dressed them, gave them sandals, food, and drink, and anointed them. They "let all the feeble ones ride on donkeys" and brought them "to their brethren at Jericho" (2 Chronicles 28:15). Repentance showed itself in specific, practical care.

In Ezekiel 16 God describes Jerusalem as a newborn girl thrown into an open field, unwashed and unwanted. He passed by, told her to live, and later bound her to Himself in a pledge of marriage (16:4–8). Then He dressed her like a queen: "I clothed you in embroidered cloth and gave you sandals of badger skin" (16:10). The child no one wanted now walks in fine leather. The rest of the chapter grieves over how Jerusalem used these gifts in unfaithfulness. That makes the sandals even more striking, because they were pure gift, given before she had proved anything.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The doors of nâʻal lead toward the One who said, "I am the door. If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved, and will go in and out and find pasture" (John 10:9). Amnon bolted a door against someone he had wronged. Christ opens a door for those who have wronged Him. Scripture does speak of doors God Himself shuts. He shut Noah in for safety (Genesis 7:16), and the wedding door closes on those who were not ready (Matthew 25:10). Yet the story ends in a city whose "gates shall not be shut at all by day" (Revelation 21:25).

The enclosed garden finds its fullest echo in Christ's love for His church. He "gave Himself for her, that He might sanctify and cleanse her… that she should be holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:25–27). The Song of Solomon is first a song of married love, and Paul's teaching makes a reading of it toward Christ and His church fitting. The Bridegroom sets His people apart for Himself.

The sandals point to the gospel's welcome of sinners. When the prodigal comes home, his father orders "sandals on his feet" (Luke 15:22), the mark of a son rather than a hired servant. The scene in 2 Chronicles 28 also has a resonance that is easy to miss. Men of Samaria clothe and anoint Judahite captives, set the feeble on donkeys, and bring them to Jericho. Jesus' parable of the Good Samaritan follows a remarkably similar pattern, with oil, a ride on an animal, and the road to Jericho (Luke 10:30–35). Jesus may well have been recalling this forgotten act of mercy, though the parable does not say so.

7. Spirit & Life

The two doors in this word set a searching question before every believer. What is your heart locked against? Our sinful nature locks out people we have hurt, people who inconvenience us, and people whose presence reminds us of our guilt. Grace works the opposite way. It opens the bolted doors we have set against others, and it guards the heart's garden from rival loves so that it can be kept for Christ. Holiness in this picture is a walled garden whose gate opens wide to the Beloved. It is not a cold, closed room. A believer who guards his or her heart is protecting what belongs to the Lord. "Let my beloved come to his garden" is the prayer of such a heart.

The sandals speak to anyone who still lives like a captive. God did not rescue the foundling and leave her barefoot, and the father did not let his son stand in the doorway as a servant. The Lord equips the people He saves for the life ahead of them. Israel ate the Passover with "sandals on your feet" (Exodus 12:11), ready to leave Egypt. The believer is told to stand "having shod your feet with the preparation of the gospel of peace" (Ephesians 6:15). The God who fastens sandals on His children means for them to walk out of captivity and carry His peace to others.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This word occurs in only seven verses, and each one has been discussed above. Two of them repay a second look:

  • Judges 3:24 — The servants found "the doors of the upper room were locked," and their wrong guess about the closed room gave God's deliverer time to escape.

  • 2 Samuel 13:18 — The narrator repeats that the servant "bolted the door behind her." The repetition makes the reader stay with Tamar outside the door, cast out and still wearing the robe of a king's daughter.
