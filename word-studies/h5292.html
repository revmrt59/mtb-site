NAARAH - (Naʻărâh, Strong's H5292)

Summary

Naarah is the name of two unrelated subjects in the Old Testament: a town on the eastern border of Ephraim's territory (Joshua 16:7) and a woman of the tribe of Judah, one of the two wives of Ashhur, "the father of Tekoa" (1 Chronicles 4:5–6). The name matches the ordinary Hebrew word naʻărâh, meaning "girl" or "young woman." Scripture never explains why a woman or a town carried this name, and nothing in the text builds a message on its meaning.

Each Naarah occupies only a line or two of Scripture, but both lines matter. The town marks a point on the border God gave the descendants of Joseph. That boundary ran down through the Jordan Valley toward Jericho, the first city Israel took in the land. Chronicles names Naaran, probably the same town under a slightly different spelling, on the east side of Ephraim's possessions (1 Chronicles 7:28). The woman Naarah bore four sons whose families became part of Judah's clan structure in the region of Tekoa, the town that later gave Israel a wise woman, fighting men, wall builders, and the prophet Amos. Neither Naarah is a type of Christ. Both show a God who keeps His land promises down to the last landmark and who keeps the names of mothers on record in the tribe from which the Messiah came.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H5292 covers two separate referents that should not be confused.

Naarah the town stood on the boundary of Ephraim's allotment, between Ataroth and Jericho (Joshua 16:7). The Hebrew attaches a directional ending ("toward Naarah") to the name in this verse, which is why some older English versions, such as the KJV, render it "Naarath." A town called Naaran in 1 Chronicles 7:28 is indexed separately (H5295). It is very likely the same place under a later spelling, since it also marks Ephraim's eastern edge. The match rests on location and the similar name, not on an explicit statement in the text.

Naarah the woman belonged to Judah. She was a wife of Ashhur, whom Chronicles elsewhere names as a son of Hezron and the "father of Tekoa" (1 Chronicles 2:24; 4:5). In these genealogies "father" of a town usually means its founder or leading ancestor. Naarah's husband therefore stood near the head of the community that settled Tekoa, in the hills south of Bethlehem.

The name Naarah (נַעֲרָה) is identical in form to the common noun naʻărâh, "girl, young woman, maiden." It is the feminine counterpart of naʻar, "boy, youth." For the woman, the name is a natural personal name. For the town, the reason behind the name is unknown. Scripture makes no wordplay on it in either case.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The border town. Joshua 16 records the inheritance of Joseph's sons, which was assigned by lot (Joshua 16:1). Describing Ephraim's eastern line, the text says the border "went down from Janohah to Ataroth and Naarah, reached to Jericho, and came out at the Jordan" (Joshua 16:7). Naarah thus sat in the descent toward the Jordan Valley near Jericho, where Israel had crossed the river and seen the walls fall (Joshua 3–6). The Chronicler's record of the possessions and dwelling places of Ephraim's descendants includes "Bethel and its towns: to the east Naaran, to the west Gezer" (1 Chronicles 7:28). That places Naaran, most likely the same town, on the same eastern side of Ephraim's territory.

The woman of Judah. The Chronicler's record is brief: "And Ashhur the father of Tekoa had two wives, Helah and Naarah. Naarah bore him Ahuzzam, Hepher, Temeni, and Haahashtari. These were the sons of Naarah" (1 Chronicles 4:5–6). Helah's sons follow separately (4:7). This care keeps each mother's line distinct within the household. Temeni probably means "southerner" or "Temanite," which may point to settlement in Judah's southern reaches. Haahashtari is an unusual name whose meaning and origin remain uncertain.

Tekoa, the town Ashhur's family headed, appears often in later Scripture:

  • Joab brought a wise woman from Tekoa to plead for Absalom's return (2 Samuel 14:2).

  • One of David's mighty men, Ira, was a Tekoite (2 Samuel 23:26).

  • Rehoboam fortified Tekoa for Judah's defense (2 Chronicles 11:6).

  • Amos was "among the sheepbreeders of Tekoa" (Amos 1:1).

  • After the exile, men of Tekoa repaired sections of Jerusalem's wall, although their nobles held back (Nehemiah 3:5, 27).

Scripture does not say whether any of these people descended from Naarah rather than Helah. It does show that her household helped root the community from which they came.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The text itself states that Ephraim's border passed Naarah, and that the lot assigned that land (Joshua 16:1, 7). From this it is fair to conclude that God's promise of land to Abraham's descendants was fulfilled concretely, town by town, rather than only in general terms.

For the woman, the text states her marriage and her sons. The implication follows naturally. Chronicles was compiled for a people rebuilding after exile, and it traced Judah's families carefully to show that God had preserved the tribe and its clans. Naarah's name in that record means that a mother's place in Judah's history was worth keeping.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament makes no reference to either Naarah, and neither should be treated as a type of Christ. The connections are genealogical and geographical only. Naarah married into the house of Hezron, the Hezron listed in Matthew's genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:3). Her own sons, however, belong to a side branch, not the direct line to David. The region near her namesake town, around Jericho and the Jordan, later saw John's baptizing and Jesus' passing through Jericho (Mark 10:46; Luke 19:1). These are settings in the larger story, not symbolic links.

5. Spirit & Life

The border list in Joshua 16 reads like a surveyor's notes, and that is part of its message. God did not promise Israel a vague spiritual idea. He promised a land, and He marked it out to the point of naming a small town on the road down to Jericho. The God who kept that promise in such detail can be trusted with promises that are still unfolding.

Naarah the woman left no recorded words or deeds, only four sons. Chronicles still pauses to say, "These were the sons of Naarah." Judah's history was carried forward through women like her, in households most people never heard of. God's record keeps them, and that should steady anyone whose faithfulness is quiet and unseen.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Joshua 16:1, 7: Naarah as a landmark on Ephraim's eastern border, within the land assigned by lot.

  • 1 Chronicles 7:28: Naaran (H5295), probably the same town, also on Ephraim's eastern side.

  • 1 Chronicles 2:24: Ashhur, son of Hezron and father of Tekoa.

  • 1 Chronicles 4:5–7: Naarah the wife of Ashhur and her four sons, distinguished from Helah's line.

  • 2 Samuel 14:2; 2 Chronicles 11:6; Amos 1:1; Nehemiah 3:5, 27: Tekoa's later role in Israel's story.
