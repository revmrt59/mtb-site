SOUL, LIFE, PERSON - (nephesh, Strong's H5315)

Summary

The Hebrew word nephesh describes a living, breathing creature: the whole person as alive, needy, and vulnerable before God. English Bibles translate it "soul," "life," "person," "creature," "heart," "desire," "appetite," and often just "I" or "myself." When God breathed into the dust in Genesis 2:7, "man became a living being," a living nephesh. Hebrew speaks of a person's hunger, longing, fear, love, and very life with this one word, which is why "my soul" in the Psalms so often means "my whole self."

Some of the Bible's weightiest teaching rests on this word. In Leviticus 17:11 God says that the life (nephesh) is in the blood and that He has given it on the altar to make atonement for souls (nephesh). This is the foundation of sacrifice. Israel is commanded to love the LORD with all the nephesh. The psalmist's nephesh thirsts for the living God. Isaiah's Suffering Servant pours out His nephesh to death for others. Through Jeremiah, God promises to satisfy the weary nephesh. Read across Scripture, the word traces the human story: a life given by God, forfeited by sin, ransomed by a substitute life, and finally satisfied in Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nephesh is a common Hebrew noun found throughout the Old Testament. It appears most often in the Psalms, followed by Jeremiah, Leviticus, and Proverbs. The concentrations match the word's range. Leviticus and Numbers use it for persons who sin, bring offerings, or become unclean. Narratives use it for lives at risk in war and flight, as when Saul hunts David. Poetry uses it for the inner life that longs, grieves, and praises.

Many readers hear "soul" as an invisible part of a person that lives inside the body and is separate from it. Hebrew nephesh usually points elsewhere. It names the living person as a whole, especially as someone who breathes, hungers, desires, and can die. Animals are nephesh too. Genesis 1:20, 21, and 24 call the creatures of sea and land "living creatures." The word places human beings among God's dependent creatures. What sets humanity apart in Genesis 2:7 is God's personal act of forming and breathing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning of nephesh is a living being, or the life that animates it. Context brings out several distinct senses:

1. Living being, person. A creature or human individual, often used in counting people. Seventy persons of Jacob's household went down to Egypt (Genesis 46:27).

2. Life. The vital life that can be threatened, sought, spared, or lost. Saul seeks David's life (1 Samuel 23:15), and the life of the flesh is in the blood (Leviticus 17:11).

3. Appetite, desire, inner longing. The craving self, hungry or satisfied. A satisfied nephesh turns down even honey, while to a hungry one bitter food tastes sweet (Proverbs 27:7).

4. Self (reflexive). "My soul" can simply mean "I" or "myself" (Genesis 12:13; Numbers 30:4).

5. Dead body. In purity laws, the word can refer to a corpse, the remains of one who had been a living nephesh (Numbers 19:11; Leviticus 21:1).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting changes the emphasis. Law speaks of "the person who" sins or touches something unclean, which makes each individual personally answerable. Narrative uses nephesh when lives hang in the balance: Elijah flees for his life (1 Kings 19:3), and Rahab asks Israel's spies to spare her family's lives (Joshua 2:13). Poetry turns inward. There the nephesh is cast down, waits in silence, faints, and blesses the LORD.

The reflexive use matters for reading. When a psalmist says "my soul," he is usually speaking of himself as a whole, with his body, emotions, and will all included, and not of one compartment of himself. Psalm 63:1 sets "my soul thirsts" beside "my flesh longs," and the two lines describe one longing person.

Scripture can also speak of the nephesh departing at death, as when Rachel's soul was departing (Genesis 35:18), and of a child's nephesh returning (1 Kings 17:22). The word mainly describes life as embodied and lived, but these passages show that Scripture does not reduce a person to biology. Other passages, especially in the New Testament, speak more fully of life beyond death.

4. Theological Significance

Nephesh first teaches that life is a gift. Man becomes a living being only when God breathes into him, so no nephesh is self-sustaining. That same dependence explains why the word so often carries hunger and thirst. A creature that receives its life from outside itself must keep receiving.

Because life belongs to God, He guards it. After the flood, God forbids eating flesh with its life, its blood, and declares that He will require the lifeblood of anyone who sheds human blood (Genesis 9:4–5). The law's "life for life" (Exodus 21:23) and its refusal to accept a ransom for a murderer's life (Numbers 35:31) treat a nephesh as something no payment can equal. Only God can give a life, and only God can appoint what will stand in its place.

God even speaks of His own nephesh, in human terms, to reveal what moves Him. When Israel cried out under oppression, His soul could no longer endure their misery (Judges 10:16). The same God says His soul hates Judah's festivals when they are emptied by injustice (Isaiah 1:14), and He asks whether He should not avenge Himself on such a nation (Jeremiah 5:9, 29). God is not indifferent. He is moved to rescue the suffering, and He recoils at worship that hides cruelty.

The word also defines what God asks of us. Israel was to love the LORD with all the heart, all the nephesh, and all the strength (Deuteronomy 6:5). Since nephesh covers a person's life, desires, and appetites, the command reaches every part of that person. Deuteronomy 30:6 adds grace to command: God Himself will circumcise the heart so that His people love Him with all their heart and soul, and live.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 17:11 — life given for life. God explains why Israel must not eat blood: "For the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement for your souls; for it is the blood that makes atonement for the soul." Nephesh appears three times in one verse, and the repetition carries the logic. The life of the animal is in its blood. That blood, poured out at the altar, makes atonement for the nephesh of the worshiper. One life stands in for another. Notice also the phrase "I have given it to you." The sinner does not invent the remedy. God supplies it. Every time an Israelite drained the blood from meat (Deuteronomy 12:23), he was reminded that life belongs to God and that his own forfeited life could be covered only by a life God provided.

Psalms 42 and 63 — the thirsting soul. The appetite sense of nephesh becomes the language of prayer. "As the deer pants for the water brooks, so pants my soul for You, O God" (Psalm 42:1). David, in the wilderness of Judah, writes: "My soul thirsts for You; My flesh longs for You In a dry and thirsty land Where there is no water" (Psalm 63:1). The psalmists do not borrow hunger as a loose metaphor. They recognize that the same craving self that needs water also needs God. Psalm 42:2 names what it wants: "My soul thirsts for God, for the living God." The thirst is not a defect to cure but a need to bring to God, and it leads somewhere: "My soul follows close behind You; Your right hand upholds me" (Psalm 63:8).

1 Samuel 18:1–3 — souls knit together. The narrator says Jonathan's soul was knit to David's soul and that he loved him as his own soul, and the two made a covenant. Judah uses similar language of Jacob, whose life, he says, is bound up in Benjamin's life (Genesis 44:30). In both scenes one person's nephesh is so joined to another's that losing the other would be like losing oneself. Jonathan proves it by risking his life and his claim to the throne for David's sake.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament uses nephesh to trace a clear line of movement. God breathes the breath of life into Adam, and Adam becomes a living nephesh. Sin makes that life forfeit. The altar of Leviticus 17 provides a substitute life. Then Isaiah 53 does something no animal sacrifice could do. Three times in quick succession the word is used of the Servant Himself: the LORD makes His soul an offering for sin (53:10); He sees the fruit of His soul's anguish and is satisfied, and by it justifies many (53:11); He pours out His soul to death and bears the sin of many (53:12). Leviticus describes animal blood poured out at the altar. Isaiah describes a righteous Person whose own nephesh becomes the offering.

Jesus understood His mission in these terms: "the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). "The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). Hebrews summarizes the principle of the old altar: "without shedding of blood there is no remission" (Hebrews 9:22). Christ fulfills that principle in His own death. The Servant also lives again. Psalm 16:10, where the speaker trusts God not to leave his soul in Sheol, is applied by the apostles to Jesus' resurrection (Acts 2:27).

The story ends with satisfied souls. Through Jeremiah, God promises that His people's souls will be like a well-watered garden and that He will satiate the weary soul and replenish every sorrowful one (Jeremiah 31:12, 25). Jesus stands at the feast and cries, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink" (John 7:37). He also promises rest for the souls of those who come to Him (Matthew 11:29). The thirst of Psalm 42 finds its answer in a Person.

7. Spirit & Life

Because nephesh is the whole self, God will not settle for a part of us. Nobody can love the LORD "with all the soul" while keeping their appetites, money, habits, or friendships in a separate category. The Shema's claim reaches the dinner table and the calendar as surely as the prayer closet.

Our hunger is not the enemy. The psalmists show what to do with a craving self: aim it at God. Much of what we call sin is the nephesh trying to feed God-given appetites at wells that hold no water. The cure is not to stop wanting but to bring that wanting to the living God, as David did in a dry land. Psalm 119:81 shows the honest shape of that life: "My soul faints for Your salvation, But I hope in Your word." Faintness and hope sit in the same verse.

The cross means that the deepest claim on your life has already been met by another life. The One who gave Adam life is the One who poured out His own life for sinners. That is why the believer can pray Psalm 23:3 with confidence: "He restores my soul." The same God who made the soul and ransomed it keeps restoring it.

Finally, Jonathan's knit soul shows that a life given to God is not lived alone. Godly love binds one life to another's good, at real cost.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 23:9 — Israel must not oppress a stranger, because they know the stranger's heart (nephesh), having been strangers in Egypt. Their own remembered suffering becomes a reason for compassion.

  • Numbers 19:11 — Whoever touches the dead body of any person is unclean seven days. Death defiles because it is the undoing of the life God gave.

  • 1 Kings 17:21–22 — Elijah prays that the widow's son's soul may come back to him, and the LORD hears. Life returns only by God's word.

  • Psalm 131:2 — David has calmed and quieted his soul like a weaned child with its mother. The restless craving self learns contentment in God.

  • Ezekiel 18:4 — All souls belong to God, and the soul who sins will die. Each person stands personally accountable before the God who owns every life.
