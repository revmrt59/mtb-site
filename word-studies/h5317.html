HONEY, HONEYCOMB - (nôpheth, Strong's H5317)

Summary

Nôpheth is a Hebrew word for honey as it flows from the comb, the freshest and richest sweetness an Israelite could taste. It appears only five times in the Old Testament, and every time it appears in poetry. Its rarity matters. Whether it means real honey that a person eats or serves as a figure of speech, the Bible uses it to teach: what is sweeter than the best honey, what only looks like honey, and who can actually enjoy it.

Those five uses form a small map of the human appetite. David says God's judgments are "sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb" (Psalm 19:10). Proverbs warns that the immoral woman's lips "drip honey" (Proverbs 5:3), tells a son to eat the honeycomb as a picture of taking in wisdom (Proverbs 24:13–14), and observes that a full soul "loathes the honeycomb" (Proverbs 27:7). The Song of Solomon uses the same dripping image for a bride's words to her husband (4:11). Taken together, these verses teach that God made us to desire sweetness. He made His own Word the sweetest thing of all, and He gives it to those who are hungry for it.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The ordinary Hebrew word for honey is debash (H1706). It appears more than fifty times and can mean bee honey or a sweet syrup made from fruit. Nôpheth is narrower. It describes the honey that drips straight from the comb before anyone has pressed or processed it. The NKJV usually translates it "honeycomb."

All five occurrences are in Psalms, Proverbs, and the Song of Solomon. The word never appears in the Law, the historical books, or the Prophets. The writers who use it are poets and teachers of wisdom, and they reach for it when ordinary "honey" is not strong enough for what they want to say.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Nôpheth means honey flowing from the comb, or the honeycomb itself as the source of that honey. In two verses it refers to actual honey that a person eats (Proverbs 24:13; 27:7). In the other three, the word compares something else to honey, such as God's judgments or a person's speech. In those verses the comparison comes from the setting. The word itself still means honey.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses nôpheth in three ways. First, it serves as the highest standard of comparison. Psalm 19:10 places it beside debash ("honey and the honeycomb") and says God's judgments surpass both.

Second, it describes speech. In Proverbs 5:3 and Song of Solomon 4:11 the image is lips that drip honey. The two verses use almost the same picture, and they describe opposite kinds of relationship.

Third, it serves as real food that becomes a lesson. Proverbs 24:13 commands a son to eat it. Proverbs 27:7 notices that a person who is already full does not want it. In both verses, honey from the table teaches something about the soul.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these verses show that Scripture does not treat desire as the enemy. God made people with an appetite for sweetness, and the Bible uses the richest sweetness people knew to describe Him and His Word. David never presents God's law as dry medicine that has to be forced down. He calls it more desirable than fine gold and sweeter than the comb.

The same word also shows how desire can go wrong. Whatever is truly sweet can be imitated, and the imitation can taste the same for a while. A third truth sits quietly in Proverbs 27:7. The condition of the person tasting matters as much as the honey. God's best gifts are wasted on someone who believes he needs nothing.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 19:10: better than gold, sweeter than honey. David first lists what God has said and done for His people: the law is "perfect, converting the soul," the testimony "sure, making wise the simple," the statutes "right, rejoicing the heart," and the judgments "true and righteous altogether" (19:7–9). He then measures them against two things. The first is gold, and specifically "much fine gold," which is the most valuable thing a person can own. The second is honey and the honeycomb, the most pleasing thing a person can taste. Gold has value outside a person. It can protect and it can buy, but nobody can eat it. Honey satisfies from the inside. God's Word surpasses both. It is worth more than the best a person can own and it satisfies more deeply than the best a person can enjoy. David also notes that this sweetness carries warning and "great reward" (19:11). The commands that delight him also protect him.

Proverbs 5:3 and Song of Solomon 4:11: one image, two worlds. "The lips of an immoral woman drip honey, and her mouth is smoother than oil" (Proverbs 5:3). Temptation does not introduce itself as bitter. It borrows the very sweetness Psalm 19 gives to God's Word. Honey that drips from the comb also seems to come freely, with no effort, and that is part of why seduction works: it seems natural and easy. Then Solomon describes where it ends: "bitter as wormwood, sharp as a two-edged sword. Her feet go down to death" (5:4–5).

The Song of Solomon uses the same picture with no warning attached: "Your lips, O my spouse, drip as the honeycomb; honey and milk are under your tongue" (4:11). Here the sweetness belongs to faithful married love, and nothing bitter waits at the end. "Honey and milk" also recalls the familiar description of the Promised Land as a land flowing with milk and honey (Exodus 3:8). The husband finds in his bride something like that land of rest and delight. Set side by side, these two verses show that sweetness is never the problem. The source decides whether the honey brings life or death.

Proverbs 24:13–14: eat, and have a future. "My son, eat honey because it is good, and the honeycomb which is sweet to your taste; so shall the knowledge of wisdom be to your soul." The father makes the comparison himself. Wisdom is meant to be eaten and enjoyed, not admired from a distance. The promise that follows is the reverse of Proverbs 5: "there is a prospect, and your hope will not be cut off." The immoral woman's honey is sweet at first and bitter later. Wisdom is sweet now and still good at the end. The same chapter opens with "Through wisdom a house is built... by knowledge the rooms are filled" (24:3–4). The chapter never calls the honeycomb a picture of that house. Still, the comb's ordered chambers make a fitting image for wisdom that is sweet and also gives a life its structure.

Proverbs 27:7: the hunger that tastes. "A satisfied soul loathes the honeycomb, but to a hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet." The finest honey cannot help someone who is already full. Hunger, meanwhile, finds sweetness even in bitter food. On the surface this is a plain proverb about the stomach. In a book about receiving wisdom, it also describes how the heart receives truth.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Several passages about the Messiah's speech connect naturally to these images of sweet lips and a sweet Word. Psalm 45:2 says of the royal Bridegroom, "Grace is poured upon Your lips." When Jesus spoke in Nazareth, the people "marveled at the gracious words which proceeded out of His mouth" (Luke 4:22). The temple officers sent to arrest Him came back saying, "No man ever spoke like this Man!" (John 7:46). Jesus Himself said, "The words that I speak to you are spirit, and they are life" (John 6:63).

These verses never mention honey. Even so, the line from Psalm 19 runs through them. David found God's written instruction sweeter than the comb. Jesus, the Word who "became flesh" (John 1:14), speaks the gracious, life-giving words described above, and He offers them to people who have tasted the false honey of Proverbs 5 and found it bitter.

The Song of Solomon adds a quieter connection. Scripture calls the church Christ's bride (Ephesians 5:25–32). Read alongside that teaching, the husband's delight in the honey on his bride's lips suggests how Christ receives the prayers and praise of His people. He does not merely put up with them. He delights in them.

7. Spirit & Life

These five verses show that the Christian life is largely a contest over appetite. James describes how temptation works: each person "is drawn away by his own desires and enticed," and desire leads to sin, which "brings forth death" (James 1:14–15). Proverbs 5 shows that the bait usually tastes like honey. Willpower alone does not win that fight. A person resists false honey best when he has learned to love the true. Psalm 19 is not written by someone gritting his teeth through God's commands. David has tasted something better, and that taste guards him.

Proverbs 27:7 also deserves a careful look. If God's Word seems flat to us, the problem may not be the Word. A soul that is stuffed with distraction and self-sufficiency is like the one the proverb describes: "A satisfied soul loathes the honeycomb." Laodicea said, "I am rich... and have need of nothing" and did not know it was poor and blind (Revelation 3:17). Mary sang that God "has filled the hungry with good things, and the rich He has sent away empty" (Luke 1:53). Jesus promised that those who "hunger and thirst for righteousness" will be filled (Matthew 5:6). Hunger is a gift from God. For a hungry believer, even hard things such as correction, conviction, or a difficult command can taste sweet, because he knows they come from the One who gives life.

Our own lips belong in this picture too. The same mouth can drip honey that flatters and destroys, or honey that blesses, like the bride's.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Nôpheth occurs only five times, in Psalm 19:10, Proverbs 5:3, 24:13, 27:7, and Song of Solomon 4:11. All five have been discussed above. Readers who want to explore the theme further can turn to the related passages in Section 9.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1706 (debash), honey. This is the common word for honey, and it is paired directly with nôpheth in Psalm 19:10 ("honey and the honeycomb") and Proverbs 24:13. It also appears in the phrase "honey and milk" in Song of Solomon 4:11. It is a separate word, not a form of nôpheth.

  • Thematic parallels. Psalm 119:103 ("How sweet are Your words to my taste, sweeter than honey to my mouth!") and Proverbs 16:24 ("Pleasant words are like a honeycomb") carry the same imagery of sweet words. Both use different Hebrew words for honey and honeycomb.
