FLOWER - (nitstsân, Strong's H5339)

Summary

The Hebrew word nitstsân means flower or blossom. It appears only once in the Old Testament, in Song of Solomon 2:12: "The flowers appear on the earth." In the surrounding verses the beloved calls his bride to come away with him (Song of Solomon 2:10, 13), and the flowers are part of his reason: winter has ended. The blossoms are the first sign the poem names, and it sets them side by side with singing, the turtledove's voice, green figs, and fragrant vines. Together these signs announce that spring has come.

Because the word appears only once, its spiritual weight comes from where it stands. In that setting the flowers show that winter is truly over, and they give the reason for the beloved's call: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away." Elsewhere in Scripture, flowers often picture how quickly human life fades. Here they announce life returning. Christians have long heard in this song the voice of Christ calling His people. Read that way, these flowers point to the God who ends seasons of barrenness, raises the dead, and gives new life in order to draw His people near to Himself.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nitstsân is a Hebrew noun found only in Song of Solomon 2:12. Strong's traces it to a small family of words for blossoms and links that family to a verb meaning to gleam or sparkle. The connection is suggestive, since bright blossoms against a bare landscape do catch the eye. Still, a word's meaning comes from how it is used, and this word's one use simply means flowers.

The setting is one of the best-known love poems in Scripture. In Song of Solomon 2:8–13 the bride hears her beloved coming "leaping upon the mountains" (Song of Solomon 2:8). He stands at her window and calls her outside, because the land has turned from winter to spring. In Israel the winter rains ended in early spring. Then the hills burst into color, and the fig and the vine began to show the harvest to come.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Nitstsân means a flower or blossom, the visible bloom of a growing plant. It has one sense. The NKJV translates it "flowers," and nothing in the context suggests a technical or specialized meaning. What gives the word its color is the season it announces, and Song of Solomon 2:11–13 supplies that season.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The poem uses the flowers as a marker of time. They appear "on the earth," which suggests they are open and plain to see. They head the poem's list of spring signs: the time of singing, the turtledove's voice "heard in our land" (Song of Solomon 2:12), the fig tree's green figs, and the fragrant vines with their tender grapes (Song of Solomon 2:13). The flowers are named first, but the poem presents all of these signs together as one picture of a land waking up from winter.

Scripture uses a different Hebrew word for flower (tsiyts) in its best-known warnings about human frailty. Isaiah writes, "All flesh is grass, And all its loveliness is like the flower of the field" (Isaiah 40:6). The psalmist says that man flourishes "as a flower of the field," and then "the wind passes over it, and it is gone" (Psalm 103:15–16). The flowers of Song of Solomon 2:12 carry none of that sadness. The single word is too slender to build a contrast between two vocabularies. Still, the setting itself makes the difference plain: Isaiah's flowers wither under the breath of the LORD, while the Song's flowers open because winter has lost its grip.

4. Theological Significance

Read in the light of the rest of Scripture, the passage points to a God of seasons who ends what He has allowed to begin. "For lo, the winter is past, The rain is over and gone" (Song of Solomon 2:11). The flowers did not end the winter. They appeared because it had ended. Applied to spiritual renewal, the picture shows blossoming as a response to a change God brings. The ground does not argue its way into spring. It answers the warmth when it comes.

The passage also suggests that renewal has a direction. The flowers appear together with singing and with the first green figs, and these early signs of life point ahead toward joy and a coming harvest. The God who brings the blossom intends the harvest. Isaiah 27:6 states that direction plainly when Israel is promised that it "shall blossom and bud, And fill the face of the world with fruit."

Finally, there is beauty here. From the beginning God made trees "pleasant to the sight" as well as "good for food" (Genesis 2:9). He did not create a merely useful world. The spring landscape is lovely, and the beloved points out its loveliness to the bride. God's restoring work makes things beautiful again, and He delights in that beauty.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:10–13: the flowers as the reason for the call. The poem is framed by a repeated invitation. It opens, "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away" (Song of Solomon 2:10), and the same words close it (Song of Solomon 2:13). Between these two calls the beloved names the evidence: the winter is past, the flowers appear, the dove is singing, and the figs and vines are coming on. The flowers belong to his argument. He points to them because they show that the season for staying indoors has ended. The new life in the land is his reason for asking her to come out and be with him.

This detail is the heart of the word's one use. The spring has not arrived just to be admired through a window. Renewal comes with a summons attached, and the beloved names the change in the season so that the bride will act on it. The flowers say, "Now." Whatever readers decide about how to interpret the Song, the poem's logic is clear: the signs of new life are given to draw the beloved one closer.

Song of Solomon 2:11–12: proof that winter is over. Verse 11 announces the end of winter and verse 12 shows the evidence. Rain and cold can stop for a few days without winter truly ending. Flowers settle the question. They are what hidden life looks like once it can finally show itself. The roots were alive all winter under the bare ground, and the blossoms reveal what had been kept safe there.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song's spring belongs within a larger hope that runs through the prophets. Isaiah sees a day when "the desert shall rejoice and blossom as the rose; It shall blossom abundantly and rejoice, Even with joy and singing" (Isaiah 35:1–2). The picture is close to Song of Solomon 2, where blossoms and singing belong to the same spring. Isaiah adds the goal of it all: "They shall see the glory of the LORD" (Isaiah 35:2). These passages use other Hebrew words, so they are not further uses of nitstsân. They are related pictures, and they show that one couple's springtime echoes what God intends for His whole creation. The thorns and barrenness that followed Adam's sin will give way to a world in bloom.

The church has long read the Song as a picture of Christ and His bride. In that reading, His voice is the one saying, "Rise up, my love." The New Testament does not quote this passage, but the resonance is strong. Christ ended the deepest winter of all. "Christ is risen from the dead, and has become the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). In Israel's calendar the firstfruits sheaf was waved before the LORD in spring, early in the harvest season (Leviticus 23:10–11). The Song's flowers also appear in spring, as an early sign of the harvest to come. The link through timing is an analogy rather than a stated fulfillment. Even so, it is a fitting one. The risen Christ is the first bloom of the new creation, and His resurrection promises the full harvest "afterward those who are Christ's at His coming" (1 Corinthians 15:23). Paul's words in Romans 6:5 belong to the same pattern: those united with Christ in His death will be united with Him in His resurrection.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers know what spiritual winter feels like: prayer seems to bring no answer, Scripture feels dry, and nothing seems to grow. Song of Solomon 2 does not deny that such seasons happen, but it refuses to let winter have the last word. Life can be hidden without being dead. When the blossoms come, they show what God was keeping safe all along.

The first signs of renewal are often small. Prayer feels like conversation again, a verse lands with fresh weight, or old love for Christ stirs again. These signs are flowers, not the full fruit. The figs in Song of Solomon 2:13 are still green. The early signs are still worth noticing, because they can show where God is leading.

The most searching part of the passage is what the flowers ask of us. The beloved points to the spring so that the bride will get up and come. When God renews a heart, the right response goes beyond enjoying the relief. We should go out to meet Him. A believer cannot force flowers to open in January. The believer's task is to listen for the Beloved's voice when He says the season has changed, and then to rise.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Nitstsân occurs only in Song of Solomon 2:12, which is discussed above. The following verses use other words (Hebrew in Hosea and Isaiah, Greek in 1 Peter) but carry related blossoming imagery:

  • Hosea 14:5 — "He shall grow like the lily." After Israel's long unfaithfulness, God promises to be "like the dew to Israel." Restoration blooms from His presence.

  • Isaiah 27:6 — "Israel shall blossom and bud, And fill the face of the world with fruit." Here blossoming leads to fruitfulness that reaches the whole world.

  • 1 Peter 1:24 — "The grass withers, And its flower falls away." Writing in Greek, Peter quotes Isaiah 40 to set fading human glory against the enduring word of God.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative (as traced by Strong's): H5340, a verb meaning to gleam or sparkle, which Strong's links to this word's family of blossom terms.

  • Canonical contrast: H6731 (tsiyts), the common word for flower in Isaiah 40:6–8 and Psalm 103:15. It pictures the frailty and swift fading of human life.
