LIFT, BEAR, CARRY, FORGIVE - (nâsâʼ, Strong's H5375)

Summary

The Hebrew verb nâsâʼ is one of the busiest words in the Old Testament. Its basic meaning is simple: to lift something up, to carry it, or to bear its weight. Brothers load grain onto donkeys with it, Levites carry the Ark of God with it, and a young soldier earns the title "armorbearer" from it. The same everyday verb also describes lifting the eyes in hope, lifting the voice in weeping, and lifting the face toward someone in favor.

The word matters most where sin is the load. A person who sins "bears" his guilt, and the guilt stays on him. When God forgives, He lifts that guilt and carries it away. Isaiah then shows a Servant who bears the sin of many, and the New Testament finds the fulfillment of that picture in Jesus. Around that center gather other truths. God carries His people as a father carries a son. His holy presence must be carried His way. His name must be borne honestly. Only He has the right to lift anyone up. Underneath them all lies one gospel question: who will carry the weight?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nâsâʼ is a Hebrew verb found in nearly every book of the Old Testament. It belongs to ordinary life. Jacob's sons loaded their donkeys with grain (Genesis 42:26). Joseph sent carts to carry his father's household (Genesis 45:19, 27), and families carried their belongings on the road. Because lifting and carrying are such basic human actions, Hebrew used this concrete verb to express inner realities: guilt that weighs on a person, mercy that removes it, favor that looks someone in the face, and pride that raises itself too high.

The English translations show how wide the word's reach is. The NKJV renders it with words as different as "bear," "lift," "carry," "take," "forgive," "pardon," "exalt," "accept," and "show partiality." Context decides which English word fits, and each one still carries the picture of lifting or bearing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, nâsâʼ means to lift up or to carry a weight. Its principal senses are:

1. To lift or raise: eyes, hands, voice, head, or a banner. "I will lift up my eyes to the hills" (Psalm 121:1); "He will lift up a banner to the nations from afar" (Isaiah 5:26).

2. To carry or bear a load from one place to another, such as the Ark and the tabernacle furnishings carried by the Levites (Numbers 4:15).

3. To bear guilt or its consequences, or to carry guilt away, which is forgiveness. The sinner "bears guilt" (Leviticus 5:1), while the LORD is "forgiving" iniquity (Exodus 34:7).

4. To lift the face. When the LORD lifts up His own face, or countenance, toward His people, He looks on them with favor (Numbers 6:26). When someone lifts the face of another person, it means accepting or favoring that person, and in a court of law it means showing partiality (Deuteronomy 10:17).

Less common uses include taking a wife (Ruth 1:4) and taking a census, literally lifting the head of the people (Numbers 1:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of nâsâʼ often depends on who does the carrying and what is being carried. Sin carried by the sinner is guilt and judgment. Sin carried by God is forgiveness. Sin carried by another on the sinner's behalf is substitution. This single verb holds the whole movement of the Bible's teaching on sin and grace.

Several idioms recur. To lift the voice and weep expresses strong emotion. Jacob did it when he first met Rachel (Genesis 29:11), and Naomi and her daughters-in-law did it at the pain of parting (Ruth 1:9). To "take up" a discourse introduces a weighty speech, as when Balaam "took up his oracle and said" (Numbers 23:7) and when "Job continued his discourse" (Job 27:1). The phrasing suggests a message that is carried and delivered, not tossed off. Lifting the eyes in Genesis often marks a moment of discovery. Abraham lifted his eyes and saw the three visitors (Genesis 18:2), and later the ram caught in the thicket (Genesis 22:13).

The same verb can turn dark. Korah accused Moses and Aaron of exalting themselves (Numbers 16:3), and Psalm 24:4 says that the one who may stand in God's holy place is the person "who has not lifted up his soul to an idol." The soul can be lifted toward the wrong object. Lifting the eyes can also go wrong. "Lot lifted his eyes and saw all the plain of Jordan, that it was well watered everywhere" (Genesis 13:10), and he chose the road toward Sodom.

The title "armorbearer" (1 Samuel 14:7; 31:4–6) is a form of the verb that means the one carrying. It describes a man's role by the load he carries for his master.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture uses nâsâʼ to show that God Himself is the great Carrier. He told Israel that He "bore" them on eagles' wings (Exodus 19:4). Moses reminded the people that God carried them through the wilderness as a man carries his son (Deuteronomy 1:31). Isaiah sharpens the point by contrast. The idols of Babylon must be lifted onto animals and carried, and they cannot carry anyone (Isaiah 46:7; Jeremiah 10:5). The LORD, however, has carried His people from birth and promises to carry them into old age (Isaiah 46:3–4). Isaiah 63:9 adds that in His love He carried them "all the days of old." A false god is a burden to the people who serve it. The living God bears His people Himself.

Moses felt how heavy that load is. He cried that he could not "bear" the people alone (Numbers 11:12, 14), and God answered by taking of the Spirit that was on Moses and putting it on seventy elders, so that they would "bear the burden of the people" with him (Numbers 11:17). What crushed the best of human leaders is something God carries without strain.

The word also shows God's holiness. He forgives by lifting away iniquity (Micah 7:18), yet He never lifts the face in corrupt favoritism. Deuteronomy 10:17 praises Him as the God "who shows no partiality nor takes a bribe." Because He is the Most High, Isaiah warns that everything proud and "lifted up" will be brought low (Isaiah 2:12–14), while the LORD declares that He Himself will be exalted (Isaiah 33:10).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 5:1; 16:22 and Isaiah 53:4, 12 — Who bears the sin? Under the Law, a person who stayed silent when called to testify would "bear guilt" (Leviticus 5:1). Sin did not evaporate. It stayed on the one who committed it. The priests were appointed to bear the iniquity connected with the holy things (Exodus 28:38; Leviticus 10:17), and on the Day of Atonement the live goat bore all Israel's iniquities on itself into an uninhabited land (Leviticus 16:22). The picture is vivid: guilt laid on another and carried far away. Isaiah moves from the animal to a Person. The Servant has borne our griefs (Isaiah 53:4) and bore the sin of many (Isaiah 53:12). The weight that the Law said the sinner must carry is carried instead by Someone else. That is the heart of substitution.

Exodus 34:7; Numbers 14; Psalm 32 — Forgiveness as lifting away. When the LORD proclaimed His name to Moses, He described Himself as "forgiving" iniquity, transgression, and sin. In the same breath He said He would by no means clear the guilty (Exodus 34:7). Moses later pleaded those very words for rebellious Israel (Numbers 14:18–19), and God pardoned. Even so, that generation still had to bear the consequences of their unfaithfulness in the wilderness (Numbers 14:33–34). Forgiveness lifts the guilt, though it does not always remove every earthly consequence. David knew both the weight and the relief. Before he confessed, God's hand was heavy on him (Psalm 32:3–4). Then he could sing, "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, Whose sin is covered" (Psalm 32:1). In Psalm 32:1, "forgiven" translates nâsâʼ: the transgression has been lifted off. Another psalm confesses, "Like a heavy burden they are too heavy for me" (Psalm 38:4). The psalmists treat sin as a real load.

Numbers 6:26 and Deuteronomy 10:17 — Lifting the face. The priestly blessing asks that the LORD lift up His countenance on His people and give them peace (Numbers 6:26). God turning His face toward someone is the highest favor a person can receive. Yet a closely related expression, lifting another person's face, is forbidden in court. Judges must not "be partial to the poor" (Leviticus 19:15), which in Hebrew is literally to lift the face of the poor. The same verse forbids them to "honor the person of the mighty," using a different verb, so neither the weak nor the powerful may buy the judge's favor. Job warns his friends that God "will surely rebuke you If you secretly show partiality" (Job 13:10). Both uses can be true of God at once because His favor is not a bribe. He turns His face toward His people in grace, never at the expense of justice.

Exodus 20:7 and Numbers 4:15 — Bearing His name and His presence. The command not to "take" the LORD's name in vain uses nâsâʼ (Exodus 20:7). The same verb describes Aaron bearing the names of Israel's tribes on his shoulders and over his heart (Exodus 28:12, 29). Israel carried God's name before the nations. To carry it emptily, claiming to be His while living as if He did not matter, is a misuse of that name, as surely as a careless oath is. God's presence also had to be carried His way. The Kohathites were to carry the holy things only after the priests had covered them (Numbers 4:15), and they carried them on their shoulders (Numbers 7:9). The Ark itself was carried by its poles (Exodus 25:14). When David moved the Ark on a cart, Uzzah died (2 Samuel 6). David then confessed that no one may carry the Ark of God except the Levites (1 Chronicles 15:2), and they bore it on their shoulders by its poles, as Moses had commanded (1 Chronicles 15:15). Nearness to God is a gift, and it is received on God's terms.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah connects the bearing of sin to the lifting up of glory. In Isaiah 6:1 the prophet sees the Lord "high and lifted up." In Isaiah 52:13 the same word describes the Servant, who will be exalted and extolled and very high. In Isaiah 57:15 it describes the High and Lofty One who dwells with the contrite. John's Gospel joins these passages. It quotes Isaiah 53 and Isaiah 6 together and explains that Isaiah saw Christ's glory (John 12:38–41). The One enthroned in Isaiah 6 is the Servant who stoops to bear sin in Isaiah 53 and is then lifted to the highest place.

The New Testament states the fulfillment openly, though it uses Greek words rather than nâsâʼ. John the Baptist calls Jesus the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29). Peter echoes Isaiah when he writes that Christ bore our sins in His own body on the tree (1 Peter 2:24). Hebrews says He was offered once to bear the sins of many (Hebrews 9:28). Matthew sees Isaiah 53:4 at work when Jesus heals the sick (Matthew 8:17). The scapegoat carried sin into the wilderness. Christ carried it to the cross and away from His people.

Genesis 40 offers an illustration rather than a prophecy. Joseph told the butler, "Pharaoh will lift up your head and restore you to your place" (Genesis 40:13). He told the baker that Pharaoh would lift his head off (Genesis 40:19). The same phrase leads to restoration for one man and execution for the other. Everyone will one day stand before the King. At the cross Jesus took the place of judgment so that those who belong to Him receive the butler's outcome instead of the baker's.

The pattern reaches the nations as well. God who lifts a banner to summon armies in judgment (Isaiah 5:26; 18:3) also lifts a banner to gather the outcasts of Israel and the dispersed of Judah (Isaiah 11:12). In the gospel, that gathering signal is raised for the world.

7. Spirit & Life

The first gift of this word is relief. Sin is a weight that has to go somewhere. Either we carry it ourselves, as Cain feared when he said his punishment was more than he could bear (Genesis 4:13), or it is lifted from us by the One who carried it. Confession, as Psalm 32 shows, is the moment we stop holding the load and let God take it.

The word also teaches us where to look. Lot lifted his eyes by his own appetite and chose the well-watered plain near Sodom (Genesis 13:10). Only a few verses later, God told Abram to lift his eyes and see the land He was giving him (Genesis 13:14). The two men performed the same physical act with very different hearts. Abraham lifted his eyes on Mount Moriah and found God's provision already waiting (Genesis 22:13). Psalm 121 turns that habit into a prayer: "I will lift up my eyes to the hills— From whence comes my help?" Jesus Himself "lifted up His eyes to heaven" before going to the cross (John 17:1). Lifting the eyes in this way is how dependent people pray.

Ezekiel adds that God's Spirit lifts His servants. "Then the Spirit lifted me up, and I heard behind me a great thunderous voice" (Ezekiel 3:12). The prophet did not raise himself to a higher view. He was carried there (Ezekiel 3:14; 8:3; 11:1, 24; 43:5). The proud lift themselves and are brought low. The humble are lifted by God and given to see His glory. Genuine spiritual insight comes the same way.

Finally, the word gives a picture of faithful service. Jonathan's armorbearer answered, "Do all that is in your heart. Go then; here I am with you, according to your heart" (1 Samuel 14:7). David's service to Saul began with that role (1 Samuel 16:21). Scripture does not call the armorbearer a symbol of discipleship, but the comparison fits. Believers carry the name of Christ, stay close to Him, and share His path. Anyone who bears His word also carries a weight, as the prophets who "took up" their message knew. That message is to be carried faithfully and delivered whole.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 50:17 — Joseph's brothers ask him to "forgive" their trespass. Human forgiveness also means lifting a wrong off the person who did it.

  • Exodus 32:32 — Moses pleads with God to "forgive" Israel's sin. Otherwise, he asks to be blotted out himself. He is an intercessor willing to stand under the people's load.

  • Ezekiel 4:4–6 — Ezekiel lies on his side to "bear" Israel's and Judah's iniquity for an appointed number of days. It is a living sign of the weight of national sin.

  • Psalm 24:7, 9 — The gates are summoned to lift up their heads so the King of glory may come in.

  • Isaiah 40:11 — The Shepherd will "carry" the lambs in His bosom. God's strength bends low to bear the weakest.

  • Psalm 63:4; 134:2 — Lifting the hands in prayer and blessing expresses worship with the whole body.
