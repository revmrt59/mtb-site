KISSES - (nᵉshîyqâh, Strong's H5390)

Summary

The Hebrew noun nᵉshîyqâh means kiss. It appears only twice in the Old Testament, both times in the plural, "kisses." Those two uses stand at opposite ends of human relationships. In Proverbs 27:6 the "kisses of an enemy are deceitful." Warmth is used as a disguise. In Song of Solomon 1:2 the bride cries, "Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth," longing for the nearness of the one she loves. The same small word names both the counterfeit and the real.

Read together, these two verses put a choice before every believer. The world offers affection that flatters and approves while it does harm. The Lord offers a love the bride calls "better than wine," and that love sometimes arrives as a faithful wound. Scripture carries this contrast all the way to Jesus. He received the traitor's kiss from Judas, and He welcomes sinners home the way the father in His parable kissed his returning son. A heart that has truly tasted His love has the strongest protection against the enemy's kisses.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Nᵉshîyqâh is a noun built from the common Hebrew verb nashaq, to kiss. That verb appears throughout the Old Testament's narratives, where people kiss in greeting, farewell, reconciliation, and homage. The noun is far rarer. It appears only in poetry and wisdom literature: once in Proverbs and once in the Song of Solomon.

In the ancient world, a kiss carried more weight than a casual greeting does today. It signaled peace, family belonging, loyalty, or submission. Both uses of the noun depend on that weight. A kiss promised closeness and good will, so a false kiss was a real betrayal and a longed-for kiss was a real hope.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Nᵉshîyqâh means a kiss, the act of pressing the lips to another person as a sign of affection, welcome, or loyalty. Both occurrences are plural, "kisses." The plural suggests repeated or abundant expressions of affection rather than a single formal greeting.

The word has one basic sense. Its two contexts do not create two dictionary meanings. They show the same act offered from two opposite hearts.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Proverbs 27:6 the kisses belong to an "enemy." The proverb is built as a contrast in two lines. "Faithful are the wounds of a friend" stands opposite "the kisses of an enemy are deceitful." Wounds normally hurt and kisses normally please, but the proverb reverses what we expect. The friend's painful honesty can be trusted. The enemy's pleasant gesture cannot. Some translations render the final word as "profuse" rather than "deceitful." That reading makes a related point: an enemy can be generous with kisses because they cost him nothing.

In Song of Solomon 1:2 the kisses belong to the beloved and are eagerly desired. The phrase "the kisses of his mouth" stresses personal, face-to-face nearness. The verse also shifts abruptly from speaking about the beloved ("let him kiss me") to speaking to him ("your love is better than wine"). Longing turns into direct address, as though the bride moves from wishing for his presence to speaking with him face to face.

4. Theological Significance

These two verses show that the outward sign of love can be separated from love itself. A kiss can be faked. For that reason Scripture judges affection by the heart and faithfulness of the giver rather than by how warm it feels. Proverbs teaches that truth spoken in love, even when it wounds, is more faithful than flattery. Hebrews applies the same principle to God: "For whom the LORD loves He chastens" (Hebrews 12:6). God's correction is evidence that He loves us. It does not mean He has withdrawn His love.

The Song shows the other side. God made the human heart for real communion, and He does not regard holy desire as something to be embarrassed about. The wider biblical use of the kiss adds a sober point here. A kiss can also express allegiance and worship. The Lord preserved in Israel "every mouth that has not kissed" Baal (1 Kings 19:18). Job refused even the small gesture of a kiss blown to the sun and moon (Job 31:26–27). The kiss of devotion is meant for one Lord alone.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 27:6 — the kiss used as a weapon. The enemy's kisses are dangerous because they look like love. The highest sign of trust is used to lower someone's guard. Israel's history shows this vividly, even though the stories use the verb rather than this noun. Joab greeted Amasa with "Are you in health, my brother?" and took his beard "to kiss him." Then "Amasa did not notice the sword that was in Joab's hand" (2 Samuel 20:9–10). Joab's show of greeting was cover for murder, and the narrative does not even say the kiss was given. Absalom's kisses were real, and they worked more slowly. Whenever anyone came to bow before him, he put out his hand, took hold of the person, and kissed him, "so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel" (2 Samuel 15:5–6). Joab's pretended greeting killed one man, while Absalom's flattering kisses won over a nation and turned it against its king. Proverbs warns that a false show of affection can do both kinds of damage. The friend's wound is called "faithful" because it serves our good even when it hurts.

Song of Solomon 1:2 — the kiss longed for. After the title, the Song opens with the bride's cry of desire. The bride does not ask for gifts or messages. She wants "the kisses of his mouth," which means the beloved himself, near enough to touch. Her reason is that his love is "better than wine." Wine stands for celebration and the best earthly pleasures, and she ranks his love above them all. The Song is first a celebration of love between a bride and her king. Christians have long heard in it the love between God and His people, a reading supported by Paul's statement that marriage points to "Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). On that reading, the verse gives words to a soul that wants more than to know about the Lord. It wants His presence.

The two verses together — two offers of kisses. The word's entire biblical use is these two verses, and between them they set out a choice. One set of kisses comes from an enemy and is deceitful. The other comes from the Beloved and is better than wine. James puts the choice in plain terms: "friendship with the world is enmity with God" (James 4:4). The world's approval often comes as the enemy's kiss: it accepts our sin and asks nothing of us. The two verses also explain each other. Only someone who is sure of the Beloved's love can accept His wounds as faithful. Only someone who has tasted His love as better than wine will lose the taste for the enemy's flattery.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The two sides of this word meet in Jesus Christ. In Gethsemane, Judas "drew near to Jesus to kiss Him," and Jesus answered, "Judas, are you betraying the Son of Man with a kiss?" (Luke 22:47–48). Jesus' question exposes the false kiss for what it is. He received the enemy's kisses in their worst form, and He went on to the cross anyway.

Because He did, the kiss of welcome is open to sinners. In Jesus' parable, the father sees his son "a great way off," runs to him, falls on his neck, and kisses him (Luke 15:20), before the son can finish his confession. That kiss is the father's answer to everything the son had done. Earlier stories prepared for this picture. Esau kissed Jacob, the brother who had wronged him (Genesis 33:4), and Joseph kissed the brothers who had sold him (Genesis 45:15). The bride's longing in the Song finds its fullest answer in this welcome.

Psalm 2:12 shows that this love is also royal: "Kiss the Son, lest He be angry." The One who welcomes us with a kiss is also the King, and He receives our kiss of homage. Within His people, the command to "greet one another with a holy kiss" (Romans 16:16), which Paul repeats in different words in 1 Thessalonians 5:26, turns the sign back toward honesty. Among those reconciled to God, a kiss is meant to mean what it says.

7. Spirit & Life

The enemy's kisses rarely look dangerous. They come as easy approval, as voices that call our sin acceptable, and as friendships that never tell us the truth. Proverbs 7:13 shows the same allure in the woman who seizes the simple young man and kisses him on the way to ruin. Rules and warnings do not give the strongest protection against these kisses. The strongest protection is a stronger love. When the Lord's love is tasted again as "better than wine," the world's kisses lose much of their pull. Peter describes believers who love a Christ they have not seen and "rejoice with joy inexpressible and full of glory" (1 Peter 1:8). Joy like that leaves little appetite for flattery.

This changes how we hear the Lord's correction. When He wounds through Scripture, through the Spirit's conviction, or through a faithful brother or sister, those wounds come from the same mouth the bride longs to kiss. We can trust them because we know His heart.

There is also a fitting devotional picture in "the kisses of his mouth." Kisses come from the mouth, and so do God's words. Jesus "breathed on them, and said to them, 'Receive the Holy Spirit'" (John 20:22). The Song does not teach this connection directly. Still, a believer who longs for the Lord's nearness will find that nearness through His spoken Word and His Spirit.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Nᵉshîyqâh occurs only in Proverbs 27:6 and Song of Solomon 1:2, and both verses are developed above. The following passages use related words for kissing and help fill out the picture:

  • Ruth 1:9 — Naomi kissed her daughters-in-law in farewell, and "they lifted up their voices and wept." Here a kiss expresses sorrowful love at parting.

  • 1 Kings 19:18 — God preserved "every mouth that has not kissed" Baal. In this verse a kiss marks worship, and faithfulness means keeping that kiss from false gods.

  • Hosea 13:2 — Hosea condemns people who kiss calf idols. The prophet treats this misplaced devotion as both absurd and tragic.

  • 1 Corinthians 16:20 — "Greet one another with a holy kiss." Among God's people, the kiss becomes a sign of genuine fellowship.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H5401 (nashaq), to kiss. This is the verb from which nᵉshîyqâh comes. It is used in Genesis 33:4, 2 Samuel 20:9, and Psalm 2:12.

  • Thematic connections: The kisses of Judas (Luke 22:47–48) and of the father in Jesus' parable (Luke 15:20) use different Greek words. They are not the same word, but they continue the same contrast between the false kiss and the true one.
