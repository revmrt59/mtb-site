SEBA - (Çᵉbâʼ, Strong's H5434)

Summary

Seba is first a man and then a people. Genesis names him as the first-listed son of Cush, the son of Ham, and his descendants became a nation remembered in Scripture as distant, wealthy, and impressive. The meaning of the name Seba is unknown. It is a foreign proper name carried over into Hebrew, and no reliable derivation has been established. Seba must not be confused with Sheba, a different name belonging to different descendants. The two sound alike and are paired in Psalm 72, but Scripture keeps them distinct. Ancient tradition located Seba in Africa, south of Egypt along the Nile. This is a reasonable placement, though the Bible itself does not give its borders.

The Bible mentions Seba only four times. Two of those references are genealogical, and the other two carry real theological weight. In Isaiah 43:3 God tells exiled Israel, "I gave Egypt for your ransom, Ethiopia and Seba in your place." He names faraway, prosperous kingdoms to show how highly He values His people. In Psalm 72:10, "the kings of Sheba and Seba will offer gifts" to the righteous King, and the nations at the edges of the known world come with honor to the one God has enthroned. Together these verses trace a remarkable movement. A nation once named as the price of Israel's release becomes a people bringing gifts to the King. That hope reaches its fullest expression in Jesus Christ, to whom the nations are now being gathered.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

Seba (Hebrew סְבָא) refers to two related realities:

  • The individual: Seba was a son of Cush and a grandson of Ham. He heads the list of Cush's sons in Genesis 10:7 and 1 Chronicles 1:9.

  • The nation: Seba also names the people descended from him. When Psalm 72 and Isaiah 43 mention Seba, they mean this people and its land.

The name's meaning is unknown. Seba appears to be a foreign proper name brought into Hebrew, and no secure Hebrew root explains it. Scripture offers no wordplay on the name. Its significance comes entirely from how the biblical writers use it, not from etymology.

Seba and Sheba. Seba must be kept separate from Sheba (שְׁבָא, Strong's H7614). In Hebrew the two names begin with different letters. In the Bible they belong to different people:

  • Seba is a son of Cush (Genesis 10:7).

  • One Sheba is Seba's nephew, the son of his brother Raamah (Genesis 10:7).

  • Another Sheba descends from Shem through Joktan (Genesis 10:28).

The Sheba familiar from the queen who visited Solomon is generally associated with southern Arabia. Seba is associated with Africa.

Location. Seba's location is a matter of tradition rather than explicit biblical statement. The Bible links Seba with Cush (often translated "Ethiopia") and Egypt, and that grouping points to the Nile regions south of Egypt. The first-century Jewish historian Josephus identified Seba with Meroë, a royal city of the ancient Nubian kingdom in what is now Sudan. That is one traditional proposal. The Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon instead prefers a location on the western shore of the Red Sea, so the exact site remains uncertain, though both proposals place Seba in Africa to the south.

A related name. A related, separately indexed term, the "Sabeans" (סְבָאִי, Strong's H5436), appears in Isaiah 45:14 and is generally understood as the people of Seba. The "Sabeans" who raid Job's herds in Job 1:15 are a different matter. That verse is not among the attested occurrences of H5434 and is usually connected with Sheba, so it should not be used to describe Seba.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

In the Table of Nations. Seba enters Scripture in Genesis 10:7, the Table of Nations, which traces how the families descended from Noah's sons spread across the earth after the flood. Chronicles repeats the line in 1 Chronicles 1:9, as part of the long genealogy that opens Israel's story. Nothing is recorded of Seba's life. His importance is that he stands at the head of a people. Genesis has already placed the whole human family under God's eye, and that includes nations Israel would know only by reputation.

In Isaiah 43. Seba next appears in Isaiah 43, addressed to Israel as a people scattered and humbled by exile:

"For I am the LORD your God, The Holy One of Israel, your Savior; I gave Egypt for your ransom, Ethiopia and Seba in your place." (Isaiah 43:3)

The surrounding verses press the point. God has redeemed and called His people by name (Isaiah 43:1). They are precious in His sight, and He will give people in exchange for their lives (Isaiah 43:4). Many interpreters connect this with historical events, in which the great powers God used to free Israel received the lands of the south. That connection is a reasonable reading of history, but the text's own emphasis is plain without it. God names three famous, faraway kingdoms and says, in effect, that He would let all of them go before He would let Israel go.

In Psalm 72. Psalm 72 is a royal psalm, headed "of Solomon," praying for the reign of a righteous king. The king's rule reaches from sea to sea, and distant rulers come with honor:

"The kings of Tarshish and of the isles Will bring presents; The kings of Sheba and Seba Will offer gifts." (Psalm 72:10)

Tarshish is commonly placed in the far west across the sea, while Sheba and Seba lie to the far south. The pairing of the two similar names makes for good poetry, and it also covers the ground on both sides of the Red Sea. The point is that the king's honor reaches the edges of the known world.

In Isaiah 45 (related name). The related term in Isaiah 45:14 adds a vivid detail. "The Sabeans, men of stature," come over to God's people along with the labor of Egypt and the merchandise of Cush. They bow and confess, "Surely God is in you, and there is no other; There is no other God." The scene is one of submission, since they "come over in chains." Even so, it ends in confession of the one true God.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

What Scripture states. Scripture states several things directly:

  • Seba descends from Ham through Cush (Genesis 10:7).

  • God names Seba with Egypt and Cush as Israel's ransom (Isaiah 43:3).

  • The kings of Seba will bring gifts to the righteous king (Psalm 72:10).

None of these passages accuses Seba of the gross wickedness charged against Babylon or Edom. Seba functions instead as a picture of human greatness at a distance: rich, remote, and impressive. It is exactly the kind of nation that might seem beyond the reach of Israel's God.

What follows from these texts. Two things can be drawn out carefully. First, in Isaiah 43 the size of the ransom shows the depth of God's love. God measures Israel's worth by naming nations the world admired. Israel's value did not come from its strength, since it was a defeated people at the time. It came from God's choice and His love: "you were precious in My sight" (Isaiah 43:4).

Second, Psalm 72 and Isaiah 45 show that God's purposes for the nations do not end with their being traded or subdued. They are brought to honor the King and to confess that there is no other God. Seba thus marks part of a larger biblical pattern. The nations that stand outside Israel's story at the start, as with Seba in Genesis 10, are drawn in at the end.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

Psalm 72. Psalm 72 prays for a king whose justice, endless reign, and worldwide blessing go beyond anything Solomon achieved. It says "all nations shall call Him blessed" (Psalm 72:17), echoing God's promise to Abraham. This is why the church has long read the psalm as pointing to the Messiah. The New Testament does not quote Psalm 72:10 directly. Still, the gospel is announced as good news for all nations, so the hope that distant kings will honor God's King finds its fulfillment in Jesus. When the magi bring gifts to the child in Bethlehem (Matthew 2:11), Matthew does not name Seba. The scene nonetheless shows the kind of honor from distant lands that Psalm 72 anticipates.

The ransom. Isaiah 43:3 opens a line of thought that the rest of Scripture carries much further. In Isaiah, God gives other nations as a ransom for His people. In the gospel, the ransom is far more costly. Jesus says the Son of Man came "to give His life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45), and Paul marvels that God "did not spare His own Son" (Romans 8:32). The comparison is a canonical development rather than an explicit fulfillment, since no New Testament writer cites Isaiah 43:3. But the direction is clear. The God who once named Egypt, Cush, and Seba as the price of Israel's freedom finally pays with His own Son.

The Ethiopian official. Acts 8:26–39 tells of an official in the court of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians. Candace was a royal title associated with the Nubian kingdom of Meroë. He travels to Jerusalem to worship, hears the gospel from Philip, and is baptized. If Josephus is right that Seba is Meroë, this man comes from the very region Psalm 72 names. Luke does not mention Seba or cite Psalm 72, so this is best understood as a striking thematic connection rather than a stated fulfillment. Even so, it vividly shows the nations of the far south beginning to come to Israel's God through Christ. Revelation completes the picture, as the kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into the holy city (Revelation 21:24–26).

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

God's love sets our worth. Isaiah 43:3 was spoken to people who felt forgotten, defeated, and small. God answers them by naming the price He is willing to pay. Our worth before God does not rest on our strength or standing, any more than Israel's did in exile. It rests on His love. For those who belong to Christ, the price was not distant kingdoms but the life of God's own Son. Shame and fear lose their grip in the face of that kind of love.

Human greatness must bow. Seba was the kind of nation people admire: wealthy, remote, and imposing, its people remembered as "men of stature." Scripture never mocks that greatness, but it does place it under God. The kings of Seba come with gifts. The Sabeans confess, "There is no other God." Wealth, achievement, and influence are best used when they are laid before the King.

No one is too far away. Seba lay far from Israel. Yet God named it in His promises long before an Ethiopian official read Isaiah on a desert road. Whatever distance a person feels from God, whether in geography, culture, or history, the gospel reaches that far. The fitting response is to come as the nations come in Psalm 72: to honor Jesus as King and to offer Him what we have.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 10:7 — Identifies Seba as a son of Cush, first in the list. The same verse names Sheba as a son of Raamah, which establishes the distinction between the two names.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:9 — Repeats Seba's place in the genealogy of the nations.

  • Isaiah 43:3–4 — God names Egypt, Ethiopia, and Seba as Israel's ransom. This shows the depth of His love for His people.

  • Psalm 72:10, 17 — The kings of Sheba and Seba bring gifts to the righteous King, whose blessing reaches all nations.

  • Isaiah 45:14 — The related term "Sabeans, men of stature" (H5436). It describes Seba's people coming over and confessing that there is no other God.

  • Mark 10:45; Romans 8:32 — The ransom theme reaches its height in the self-giving of Christ.

  • Acts 8:26–39 — The Ethiopian official from Candace's court comes to faith, a thematic echo of the nations of the far south coming to God.
