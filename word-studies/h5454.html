SABTAH - (Çabtâʼ, Strong's H5454)

Summary

Sabtah was a son of Cush, a grandson of Ham, and a great-grandson of Noah. His name appears only twice in the Bible, both times in family lists: the Table of Nations in Genesis 10:7 and the matching genealogy in 1 Chronicles 1:9, where the NKJV spells it "Sabta." The meaning of the name is uncertain. It has no clear Hebrew root and probably comes from a non-Hebrew language, perhaps South Arabian. Like many names in Genesis 10, Sabtah refers both to a man and to the people descended from him. One long-standing proposal places that people in southern Arabia, though the Bible itself does not say where they settled.

Scripture records nothing Sabtah said or did. His place in the record still matters. Genesis lists him among the nations of the earth just before God calls Abram and promises that all the families of the earth will be blessed through him (Genesis 12:3). Sabtah belongs to that wide world God set out to bless. His brother Seba and a Sheba (one of more than one line by that name, here his nephew through Raamah) later appear in prophetic pictures of distant peoples bringing gifts to Israel's King (Psalm 72:10; Isaiah 60:6). The Bible never mentions Sabtah again, but his family line stands inside the reach of God's promise.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Sabtah is one individual. Genesis 10:7 names him third among the sons of Cush: "The sons of Cush were Seba, Havilah, Sabtah, Raamah, and Sabtechah; and the sons of Raamah were Sheba and Dedan." First Chronicles 1:9 repeats the list with slightly different spellings: "Sabta, Raama, and Sabtecha." No other person in Scripture bears this name. He should not be confused with his brother Sabtechah, whose similar name is indexed separately as H5455. Nimrod, also called a son of Cush, is mentioned separately in Genesis 10:8.

The meaning of the name is unknown. It has no convincing Hebrew derivation and most likely preserves a name from South Arabia, Cush, or the trade languages of that region. Some older reference works suggest it means "striking," but that proposal is speculative and should not be treated as established. Scripture offers no wordplay on the name.

Genesis 10 often uses a forefather's name for the people who came from him, so Sabtah also stands for a tribe or region. One long-standing proposal connects it with Sabota, an ancient South Arabian town on the frankincense trade routes. Others suggest a site near the Persian Gulf or the African coast of the Red Sea. The Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon treats all these conjectures as uncertain, and none is stated in the Bible.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Sabtah has no story of his own. His entire biblical presence lies in two genealogies. Genesis 10 traces how Noah's descendants spread across the earth after the Flood. Cush's sons appear to represent peoples living around the southern Red Sea and Arabia, and Sabtah's line was likely one of them. First Chronicles, written for Israel after the exile, opens its history with Adam and then traces the nations before narrowing to Israel. By keeping Sabtah's name, the Chronicler reminded a small, recovering people that their story belonged within the history of the whole human race.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The text plainly shows that God's record of humanity includes peoples far from Israel. Genesis names Sabtah and the other nations in chapter 10 and then turns to Abram in chapter 12. That order prepares the reader for God's promise to bless "all the families of the earth." Seen against that promise, Sabtah's name suggests that the nations listed are not background filler. They are the very families the promise has in view. Paul later affirms that God "has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26). Sabtah's unrecorded move into distant lands happened under that same sovereign care.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No later passage mentions Sabtah, and he is not a type of Christ. His close relatives do reappear, however. "The kings of Sheba and Seba will offer gifts," says Psalm 72:10, describing the reign of God's King. Isaiah 60:6 adds that "all those from Sheba shall come; they shall bring gold and incense, and they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD." Psalm 68:31, where the Hebrew "Ethiopia" is Cush, declares that "Ethiopia will quickly stretch out her hands to God." Earlier, when the wise men presented "gold, frankincense, and myrrh" to the young Child (Matthew 2:11), the scene echoed these prophecies of gifts from distant lands. Acts 8:27 later shows that hope at work when an official of the Ethiopian queen comes to Jerusalem to worship and soon hears the gospel of Jesus. Scripture does not connect either scene to Sabtah directly, but his family line belongs to the world those prophecies describe.

5. Spirit & Life

It is easy to skip a name like Sabtah. God did not skip it. He preserved it twice, for a man whose deeds are unknown to us and whose homeland we cannot locate with certainty. The Bible does not treat humanity as a faceless crowd. It names fathers and peoples, and that care reaches far beyond Israel. When Jesus sends His disciples to make disciples of all nations (Matthew 28:19), He sends them to peoples like the descendants of Sabtah, whose names first appeared in Genesis 10. No family is too remote for the gospel, because none was ever too remote for God to know.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:7 names Sabtah as a son of Cush in the Table of Nations.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:9 repeats the genealogy, spelling the name "Sabta," for post-exilic Israel.

  • Genesis 12:3 gives God's promise to bless all the families of the earth through Abram.

  • Acts 17:26 affirms God's sovereignty over the origins and boundaries of every nation.

  • Psalm 72:10; Isaiah 60:6 picture Seba and Sheba, Sabtah's brother and nephew, bringing gifts to the LORD's King.

  • Psalm 68:31; Acts 8:27 show Cush reaching toward God and an Ethiopian worshiper meeting the gospel.
