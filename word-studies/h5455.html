SABTECA - (Çabtᵉkâʼ, Strong's H5455)

Summary

Sabteca was the fifth-listed son of Cush, the grandson of Ham, and a great-grandson of Noah. His name appears only twice in Scripture, both times in the list of Cush's sons (Genesis 10:7; 1 Chronicles 1:9). The NKJV spells it "Sabtechah" in Genesis and "Sabtecha" in Chronicles. The meaning of the name is unknown. Scholars have compared it with the name of a later Cushite ruler of Egypt, Shabataka, but that comparison is a historical proposal. It does not tell us what the name means. Like most names in the Table of Nations, Sabteca stands for both an ancestor and the people who came from him. Where those descendants settled cannot be fixed with certainty.

Scripture records nothing Sabteca did. His importance comes from where God's Word places him. He belongs to the families who spread across the earth after the Flood, and his name appears in the same breath as his brother Nimrod, the first kingdom founder described here (Genesis 10:8). The Chronicler later copied his name again for a people returning from exile. Sabteca's household of Cush, which the NKJV often calls Ethiopia, later appears in prophetic promises that distant peoples will worship the Lord (Psalm 68:31; Zephaniah 3:10). God's blessing through Abraham was always aimed at families like his (Genesis 12:3).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Sabteca is one individual, the last-listed of the five sons in Cush's first list (Nimrod is named separately): "The sons of Cush were Seba, Havilah, Sabtah, Raamah, and Sabtechah; and the sons of Raamah were Sheba and Dedan" (Genesis 10:7). That makes him a brother of Seba, Havilah, Sabtah, and Raamah, an uncle of Sheba and Dedan, and a brother of Nimrod, whom Genesis lists separately (Genesis 10:8). His brother Sabtah (H5454) has a similar name and his own Strong's entry, so the two should not be confused.

The meaning of the name is unknown. The Hamite peoples of Genesis 10 spread into regions where Egyptian, Cushitic, and Semitic languages were spoken, so possible roots have been sought in several directions without a settled result. Michael Astour proposed (1965) that the name resembles Shabataka (Shebitku), a ruler of Egypt's Cushite Twenty-Fifth Dynasty. The resemblance is suggestive, but it does not show that a name lasted through the generations, and it is not a definition. It also does not make the pharaoh and the son of Cush the same person. Scripture offers no wordplay on the name.

Cush's descendants are generally linked with East Africa and southern or western Arabia. Because Sheba and Dedan are commonly placed in Arabia, Sabteca's people are often placed there as well. The Bible does not name a territory for Sabteca, and no undisputed location has been identified.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Sabteca has no narrative. He appears in Genesis 10, the record of how Noah's sons became "the families of the sons of Noah" spread out "on the earth after the flood" (see Genesis 10:32). Genesis lists him and his brothers in ordinary fashion. Then the text stops to describe Nimrod, who "began to be a mighty one on the earth" (Genesis 10:8) and whose kingdom began in Babel and the land of Shinar (Genesis 10:10). Centuries later, the Chronicler repeated the list word for word apart from the spelling (1 Chronicles 1:9). In doing so he placed Israel's story, after the exile, within the history of all humanity from Adam onward.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states only Sabteca's parentage and his place among Cush's sons. Several points follow from that context. His name testifies that the families of the earth grew and spread after the Flood under God's hand, in keeping with His command to fill the earth (Genesis 9:1). His bare listing stands beside Nimrod's rise to power. Genesis does not comment on Sabteca's life, but the contrast between a name simply listed and the first kingdom notice in the chapter is set right there in the text. The chapter's placement just before God's call to Abraham also matters. When God promised that "all the families of the earth" would be blessed in Abraham (Genesis 12:3), Sabteca's people were among those families.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No later passage names Sabteca. His father's house, however, does reappear in the prophets. "Ethiopia will quickly stretch out her hands to God" (Psalm 68:31). The Lord also says, "From beyond the rivers of Ethiopia My worshipers, the daughter of My dispersed ones, shall bring My offering" (Zephaniah 3:10). Psalm 68 names Ethiopia (Cush) stretching out her hands to God, and Zephaniah speaks of worshipers coming from beyond its rivers. These are prophetic hopes concerning the region and people of Cush broadly; they do not name Sabteca's line or promise the conversion of every Cushite family.

The New Testament shows that hope moving toward fulfillment. An Ethiopian official heard the good news of Jesus from Philip and was baptized (Acts 8:26–39). Through Christ, the gospel goes to every nation (Matthew 28:19). In Acts 2, people of many languages hear God's works in their own tongue. That event echoes the scattering of nations and languages in Genesis 10–11, though the link is thematic rather than stated outright.

5. Spirit & Life

The lesson here is a modest one. God recorded the name of a man about whom He said nothing else, and He kept that name in Scripture twice. A man's worth in God's sight did not depend on building cities, as Nimrod did. The nations that seem farthest from Israel's story were never outside God's knowledge or His plans. The Lord who numbered Cush's sons is the same Lord who promised that worshipers would come "from beyond the rivers of Ethiopia."

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:6–8 – Sabteca's lineage through Ham and Cush, and the move to Nimrod.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:9 – The Chronicler's repetition of the list for Israel after the exile.

  • Genesis 12:3 – God's promise of blessing to all the families of the earth.

  • Psalm 68:31; Zephaniah 3:10 – Prophetic promises of worship from Cush/Ethiopia.

  • Acts 8:26–39 – An Ethiopian receives the gospel.
