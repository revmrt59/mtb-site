ROUNDED, ROUNDNESS - (çahar, Strong's H5469)

Summary

The Hebrew word çahar means roundness. It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 7:2, where the NKJV renders it "rounded": "Your navel is a rounded goblet; It lacks no blended beverage." The word paints a picture of a smooth, curved, well-formed bowl, the kind of vessel that holds wine at a celebration. In the Song, that picture becomes a husband's way of praising the beauty of his bride.

A single word about the curve of a cup might seem too small to study, yet çahar says something important. God placed in Scripture a love poem that looks closely and gladly at the human body and describes it with the language of craftsmanship, feasting, and abundance. The "rounded goblet" is also a full one. It "lacks no blended beverage." Delight in marriage is pictured as a banquet that never runs short. The word honors the body God made and the joy He intends a husband and wife to find in each other.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Çahar is a Hebrew noun built on a root associated with roundness. Because the word occurs only once, everything we know about its use comes from that single line of poetry. Strong's dictionary connects it to a related word, saharon, which refers to round or crescent-shaped ornaments. Gideon took "the crescent ornaments that were on their camels' necks" after defeating Zebah and Zalmunna (Judges 8:21). That connection supports the basic idea of a curved, rounded shape.

The setting is Song of Solomon 7:1–9, a poem of descriptive praise. The speaker moves through the bride's appearance from her feet in sandals (7:1) to her head and hair (7:5), comparing each feature to something beautiful or valuable: jewels, fawns, an ivory tower, Mount Carmel, a palm tree. The "rounded goblet" belongs to this sequence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Çahar means roundness: the quality of being curved and smoothly shaped. In Song of Solomon 7:2 it describes a goblet or bowl, so the NKJV translates the phrase as "a rounded goblet." The word itself is visual. It tells us what the vessel looks like rather than what it means. Since the word appears only here, there is no wider range of senses to sort out. One definition is enough.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The single use of çahar sits inside a comparison. The bride's navel, or perhaps more broadly her midsection, is likened to a rounded drinking bowl. The exact part of the body intended is debated, but the point of the image is clear: graceful curves and pleasing form.

The comparison does not stop at shape. The next clause adds, "It lacks no blended beverage." Mixed or spiced wine was the drink of a festive table. So the word "rounded" describes a vessel that is both beautiful and full. The following line moves to a harvest image: "Your waist is a heap of wheat set about with lilies." Wine and wheat, the basic provisions of a good harvest, come together here as pictures of plenty and pleasure.

4. Theological Significance

The God who made the human body is not embarrassed by it. Song of Solomon 7 describes a woman's body with open admiration, and the Holy Spirit included that description in Scripture. The poem calls the curves of her thighs "the work of the hands of a skillful workman" (7:1). Human beauty is presented as craftsmanship, something made with care. Behind every skillful workman stands the Creator, who formed the man and the woman and called His work very good (Genesis 1:31).

This guards against a long-standing error. At various times in church history, some have treated the body as an obstacle to holiness and physical love as something to be tolerated at best. A Bible that contains Song of Solomon 7:2 will not allow that conclusion. Scripture honors the body and celebrates the delight of husband and wife within the faithful bond God established. Paul later warned against teachers who forbid marriage, insisting that what God created is good and is to be received with thanksgiving (1 Timothy 4:1–5).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 7:1–9: praise that lingers. The rounded goblet is one stroke in a full portrait. The speaker does not offer a quick compliment. He takes his time, noticing her feet, her thighs, her waist, her neck, her eyes, her hair, and he finds something worth praising in each. The section reaches its peak in verse 6: "How fair and how pleasant you are, O love, with your delights!" Just before that exclamation, at the close of verse 5, the poem has already made a striking admission: "A king is held captive by your tresses." The one who holds power is captivated by the one he loves. This is love that looks closely, speaks openly, and is not ashamed to be overwhelmed.

The full cup: beauty joined to abundance. The word çahar describes the goblet's shape, but the verse immediately describes its contents: "It lacks no blended beverage." A rounded bowl is made to hold something, and this one is full. The image fits a pattern found elsewhere in the Song. In 5:1 the bridegroom says, "I have drunk my wine with my milk," and the invitation follows: "Drink, yes, drink deeply, O beloved ones!" The chapter 7 poem ends with the same picture. The bridegroom compares the roof of her mouth to wine: "like the best wine," wine that "goes down smoothly for my beloved" (7:9). It is an intimate image of the sweetness he finds in her. Throughout the Song, married love is described as a feast where the wine does not run out.

Proverbs 5:15–19: satisfaction at home. Proverbs uses different words but the same kind of imagery. The father tells his son, "Drink water from your own cistern, and running water from your own well" (5:15). Then he adds: "Rejoice with the wife of your youth... Let her breasts satisfy you at all times; and always be enraptured with her love" (5:18–19). Proverbs gives the command, and the Song gives the picture of that command being joyfully obeyed. The cup a man is told to drink from is his own wife, and the Song pictures married love as a cup that is full. The two passages together teach that faithfulness and delight belong together. The man who stays at his own well is not settling for less. He is seeking his joy where God told him to find it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The full cup is a recurring biblical picture of God's generous provision. David, writing of the Lord as his Shepherd, says, "You prepare a table before me... My cup runs over" (Psalm 23:5). The Song's rounded goblet that "lacks no blended beverage" belongs to the same family of images: blessing that fills the vessel to the brim.

Scripture carries the themes of marriage and wine forward to Jesus. His first sign was at a wedding in Cana, where He turned water into wine so the celebration would not run dry, and the best wine was served last (John 2:1–11). Paul teaches that the union of husband and wife points to Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:31–32). The story of Scripture ends with a wedding feast, the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:7–9).

Many Christians through history have read the whole Song as a picture of Christ and His people. The Song's first meaning is the love of a man and a woman, and the New Testament never labels it as an allegory. Still, the connection is real. The same God who made married love a feast of delight also describes His relationship with His people as a marriage. Human marriage at its best gives a faint taste of the joy Christ has toward His bride and the abundance He will pour out at His table.

7. Spirit & Life

The rounded goblet teaches a husband and wife to see each other as God's handiwork. The bridegroom in Song of Solomon 7 does not look at his bride with indifference or treat her as a convenience. He studies her, names what he sees, and tells her. Many marriages grow quiet in this area. The praise that once came easily stops, and familiarity dulls attention. The Song shows another way: love that keeps looking and keeps speaking. Saying aloud what you find beautiful in your spouse is part of what Scripture models, not a romantic extra.

This verse also gives honest help to people who have been taught to feel uneasy about the body. Some believers carry quiet shame about physical desire even within marriage, as though it were less spiritual than prayer or service. God wrote Song of Solomon 7:2 into His Word. Physical love, enjoyed faithfully within marriage, is part of His good design. It is something to thank Him for.

The fullness of the cup speaks to contentment. The world says satisfaction is found somewhere else, in someone new. Proverbs 5 and Song of Solomon 7 answer by pointing a husband and wife back to each other. Proverbs calls a man to "rejoice with the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18), and the Song pictures married love as a cup that "lacks no blended beverage." These passages celebrate that delight and invite couples to pursue it faithfully. They do not promise that hardship will never come or that every desire will be met, and many faithful marriages pass through sickness, grief or long dry stretches. Even then, the call remains the same: turn toward your spouse rather than away, and keep looking for the good God has given you in each other. Behind that call stands the God who prepares a table even "in the presence of my enemies" and fills cups until they overflow (Psalm 23:5).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Çahar occurs only in Song of Solomon 7:2, which has been discussed above. Readers who want to trace its setting further should read the full poem in Song of Solomon 7:1–9 alongside the related wine and banquet imagery of Song of Solomon 5:1.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7720 (saharon), lexical relative: Round or crescent-shaped ornaments, linked in Strong's to the same root as çahar. Gideon took "the crescent ornaments that were on their camels' necks" (Judges 8:21).

  • Canonical pairing, the full cup: This is a thematic connection, not a lexical one. Psalm 23:5 ("My cup runs over") and Proverbs 5:15–19 use related images of drinking and satisfaction that illuminate the full goblet of Song of Solomon 7:2.
