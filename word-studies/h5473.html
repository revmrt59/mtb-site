SET ABOUT, FENCED - (çûwg, Strong's H5473)

Summary

This Hebrew verb means to fence in, hem in, or set something about with a border. It appears only once in the Bible, in the bridegroom's praise of his bride in Song of Solomon 7:2: "Your waist is a heap of wheat Set about with lilies." Because the word has only this one setting, its meaning is learned from the picture it helps paint. A heap of wheat is harvest, food, and life. Lilies are beauty and fragrance. The verb joins them, so the bride is pictured as a source of life surrounded and guarded by beauty.

The surprising detail is what the fence is made of. Israel's fences and hedges were usually rough stone or sharp thorns, built to keep out thieves and animals. Here the boundary is a ring of flowers. A ring of lilies could stop no intruder. It frames what it surrounds and depends on the honor of the one who comes near. Against the thorns of the curse in Genesis 3 and the thorn hedge of God's discipline in Hosea 2, the Song shows love protected by beauty rather than by hostility. This points ahead to Christ, who bore the curse, and to His people, whose holiness is guarded by love for Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The word is Hebrew and occurs once, in the Song of Solomon. Its spelling matches another Hebrew verb (H5472) that means to turn back or backslide. Hebrew dictionaries treat the two as separate words that happen to look alike. The verb in Song 7:2 has nothing to do with turning away.

The Song is wedding poetry, and in chapter 7 the bridegroom describes his bride from her feet upward (7:1–9), naming her beauty in pictures drawn from Israel's fields, vineyards, and gardens. His images come from farm life. At harvest, threshed grain was piled on the threshing floor. A heap of wheat there meant the year's labor had succeeded and there would be bread. Field fences in Israel were normally made of stone walls or thorny hedges. Micah calls even the best men of his day "like a brier" and "sharper than a thorn hedge" (Micah 7:4).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb means to enclose with a border, to fence or hem something in. In Song 7:2 the NKJV renders it "set about," describing a heap of wheat encircled by lilies. The word speaks of a boundary that surrounds and marks off something valuable. What kind of boundary it is depends on the material, and here the poem supplies flowers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

With a single occurrence, there is no range of uses to compare. The word serves one poetic picture. It does not describe anyone building a fence. It describes a state: the heap of wheat is already encircled. The lilies are part of what the bridegroom admires.

Comparing passages that use other words helps. Isaiah's vineyard had a "hedge" and a "wall" that God would remove so the vineyard would be "burned" and "trampled down" (Isaiah 5:5). Hosea's hedge was of "thorns" (Hosea 2:6). Those boundaries are defensive or restraining. Song 7:2 uses a different verb and a different material, and the effect is adornment rather than defense.

4. Theological Significance

The Song celebrates the love of husband and wife as God's good gift. Paul explains that marriage was designed from the beginning to picture "Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32). Both levels are in view: the honored intimacy of marriage and the love between Christ and His people.

The picture in Song 7:2 shows that God values boundaries around love. Elsewhere the bride is "A garden enclosed... A spring shut up, A fountain sealed" (Song 4:12). Her life and love are kept for her husband alone. The lily fence adds something the enclosed garden does not say outright. The boundary that guards her is beautiful in itself. God's limits around intimacy are not grudging restrictions laid over a good thing. They are part of the good thing, and they make it lovelier to see.

A boundary of lilies also depends on honor. Stone and thorns stop intruders by force, while flowers protect only because the one who approaches respects what they mark. This is how faithful love works. Purity is kept by faithfulness, and the boundary is visible, so the bride is framed rather than hidden.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 7:2 — wheat fenced with lilies. The verse sets two images side by side. "Your navel is a rounded goblet; It lacks no blended beverage" speaks of fullness and delight. "Your waist is a heap of wheat Set about with lilies" speaks of plenty that has been gathered in. Wheat is the most basic of foods, the grain of daily bread. Lilies feed no one, but they are beautiful and fragrant. The verb brings the useful and the beautiful together, with the beauty forming the border around the life. The bride is fruitful and lovely at once, and neither quality cancels the other.

Genesis 3:18 and Hosea 2:6 — the boundaries of thorns. After the fall, God told Adam that the ground would bring forth "thorns and thistles." Thorns entered the biblical story as a mark of the curse. Later, when Israel chased other lovers, God said through Hosea, "I will hedge up your way with thorns, And wall her in, So that she cannot find her paths." That thorn hedge was an act of mercy, because God was blocking His wife's road to ruin. Hosea's chapter does not end there. God goes on to speak tenderly to her in the wilderness and promises to betroth her to Himself forever in righteousness, faithfulness, and mercy (Hosea 2:14–20). The thorns of discipline lead toward a renewed marriage.

The Song shows the other end of that road. Within the Song itself, the bridegroom calls his bride a lily among thorns (Song 2:2, paraphrased). In 7:2 the thorns are gone from the picture entirely. The bride's life is fenced by lilies, the same flowers among which her beloved "feeds his flock" (Song 2:16). The boundaries of a wayward bride are thorns that hurt and restrain. The boundaries of a loved and faithful bride are flowers that adorn.

Exodus 19:12 — the bounds at Sinai. God told Moses, "You shall set bounds for the people all around," warning that "Whoever touches the mountain shall surely be put to death." Those bounds were holy and necessary, because sinful people could not approach the holy God without perishing. The Bible itself traces a movement from that mountain to another. Hebrews contrasts the fire and terror of Sinai with Mount Zion, where believers come to "Jesus the Mediator of the new covenant" (Hebrews 12:18–24, paraphrased). The lily fence of the Song is a fitting picture of that change. This is a canonical connection drawn by comparison, since the Song does not mention Sinai. The boundary does not vanish, but its character changes from warning to welcome.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The thorns that began with Adam's sin come to rest on Christ's own head. The soldiers twisted a crown of thorns and pressed it on Jesus (Matthew 27:29). The Gospels do not draw the link to Genesis 3, but it is hard to miss. Paul states the truth beneath it plainly: Christ redeemed us from the curse by becoming a curse for us (Galatians 3:13). The thorns of the curse fell on the Bridegroom so that His bride could be surrounded by lilies.

The Song's bride, then, pictures the church as Christ makes her: nourishing like a heap of wheat and lovely like a border of lilies. Paul describes Christ giving Himself for the church to present her to Himself without spot or wrinkle (Ephesians 5:25–27). Her holiness is His gift, and her beauty is what He loves. The heap of wheat may also call to mind Israel's wheat harvest, which was marked by "the Feast of Weeks, of the firstfruits of wheat harvest" (Exodus 34:22), the feast later known as Pentecost, when the Spirit came and the church's first great harvest was gathered (Acts 2). The Song does not say this. It is an echo, but a fruitful one: the bride's fruitfulness belongs first to God as an offering of thanks.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers think of holiness as a wall of thorns, made of rules that sting, suspicion of everyone outside, and a hard edge that keeps people at a distance. Song 7:2 offers a better picture. The bride's purity is real and guarded, but its guard is beauty. When Christ's people live in faithful love toward Him, their boundaries become something others can see and even admire, the way a ring of lilies draws the eye to what it surrounds.

This applies directly to marriage. God's limits around sexual intimacy are not a cage around desire. In the Song, the enclosed garden and the lily border belong to the same celebration as the bridegroom's delight. Faithfulness protects the joy, and the joy makes faithfulness glad rather than grim.

The pairing of wheat and lilies also corrects two opposite failures. Some Christians are busy and useful but have lost the fragrance of devotion to Christ. Others guard a delicate inward piety that nourishes no one. The bride is both a heap of wheat and a garden of lilies. Christ's people are meant to feed others with the bread of His Word and His kindness while keeping their lives fragrant with love for Him.

Finally, Hosea shows that God sometimes hedges a wandering heart with thorns. When that happens, the thorns are His mercy, but they are not His goal. He disciplines in order to speak tenderly and bring His people back to the place where lilies, not thorns, mark the boundary of their lives.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This word occurs only in Song of Solomon 7:2, which has been discussed above. The following passages use different Hebrew words but carry related images that help in reading it:

  • Song of Solomon 4:12 — "A garden enclosed Is my sister, my spouse." The bride's love is kept for her husband alone, which is the enclosure the lily fence also expresses.

  • Song of Solomon 2:16 — "He feeds his flock among the lilies." Lilies mark the place where the beloved is present, so the lily border around the bride belongs to him.

  • Isaiah 5:5 — God threatens, "I will take away its hedge." A protecting boundary is God's gift, and its removal is judgment.

  • Micah 7:4 — "Sharper than a thorn hedge." The prophet's image confirms that thorny hedges were the ordinary boundary, which makes the Song's ring of lilies all the more striking.
