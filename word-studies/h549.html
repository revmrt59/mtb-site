AMANA - (ʼĂmânâh, Strong's H0549)

Summary

Amana is a northern height in the mountains beyond Israel's border. Scripture connects the name with two related features: a peak in the Anti-Lebanon range, named in Song of Solomon 4:8 beside Senir and Hermon, and the river of Damascus, which flows down from those mountains. The river is named in 2 Kings 5:12 in a marginal reading that Hebrew scribes preserved. The name probably comes from the Hebrew root meaning firm, established, or constant. That meaning fits a mountain source that fed a river flowing all year, unlike the many streambeds in Israel that dried up in summer.

In both places Amana stands outside Israel as a picture of natural splendor. Naaman the Syrian pointed proudly to the rivers of Damascus as "better than all the waters of Israel" and nearly walked away from his healing because God told him to wash in the Jordan instead. In the Song, the bridegroom calls his bride away from the high, wild country of Amana, the haunt of lions and leopards, to come with him. Set side by side, these two passages illustrate a theme the Bible returns to often: God's way comes through humble trust in His word rather than through the impressive things the world admires. Jesus Himself pointed to Naaman's cleansing as an example of God's mercy reaching beyond Israel.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

Amana is a geographical name tied to the Anti-Lebanon mountains, the range that runs northeast of Israel, east of Lebanon and above Damascus. Song of Solomon 4:8 uses it for a mountain peak, "the top of Amana," and lists it with Senir and Hermon in the same northern highlands. The wording "top of Amana" points clearly to a summit rather than to a river or a wider region.

The second appearance needs a short explanation. In 2 Kings 5:12 the written Hebrew text names the first river of Damascus "Abanah," and the NKJV follows that spelling. Hebrew scribes, however, preserved a marginal reading, "Amanah," for the same river. Strong's indexes this reading under H0549 and the written form "Abanah" separately under H0071. Both spellings name the same river, which flows from the Anti-Lebanon heights through Damascus. This river is widely identified with the modern Barada. The marginal reading may reflect the river's link to Mount Amana, its mountain source. That link is reasonable, but the text does not state it.

The name probably comes from the Hebrew root ʼaman, which carries the idea of being firm, established, steady, or faithful. The same root stands behind the familiar word "amen," a firm "so be it." Applied to a mountain or a river, the name would suggest something steady and lasting. For a river, that would mean a stream that keeps running all year. The derivation is likely but not stated in Scripture, so constant or firm should be held as the probable meaning rather than a certain one. Neither passage makes a play on words with the name.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

The rivers of Damascus (2 Kings 5:9–14). In Elisha's day Damascus was the capital of Aram (Syria), Israel's frequent rival to the north. Naaman, the commander of Aram's army, came to Israel with leprosy, hoping for healing. He arrived in style: "Then Naaman went with his horses and chariot, and he stood at the door of Elisha's house" (2 Kings 5:9). Elisha did not even come out. He sent a messenger with a plain instruction: "Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and your flesh shall be restored to you, and you shall be clean" (5:10).

Naaman was furious. He had pictured a ceremony in which the prophet would "stand and call on the name of the LORD his God, and wave his hand over the place, and heal the leprosy" (5:11). Then he compared the rivers: "Are not the Abanah and the Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel? Could I not wash in them and be clean?" (5:12). To Naaman, the comparison seemed obvious. Damascus sat in a well-watered oasis fed from the mountains, and beside the rivers of home the Jordan looked unimpressive to him. "So he turned and went away in a rage."

His servants reasoned with him: "My father, if the prophet had told you to do something great, would you not have done it? How much more then, when he says to you, 'Wash, and be clean'?" (5:13). Naaman humbled himself, "went down and dipped seven times in the Jordan, according to the saying of the man of God; and his flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child, and he was clean" (5:14). The verb "went down" fits the geography, and it can also picture the change in Naaman himself.

The heights of Amana (Song of Solomon 4:8). In the middle of a love song, the bridegroom calls: "Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse, With me from Lebanon. Look from the top of Amana, From the top of Senir and Hermon, From the lions' dens, From the mountains of the leopards." Amana appears here among the high, remote places the poet names. The views are grand, but the land is wild, and lions and leopards live there. The bridegroom's call is to come away with him from that distant and dangerous place.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states plainly that Naaman judged the rivers of Damascus better than Israel's waters and that he was healed only when he obeyed God's word through Elisha. The point of the story is that God chose to heal Naaman through the river Naaman despised, in Israel, through His own prophet, and by a command that required humility. The healing came from the Lord who spoke, not from the quality of the water. Naaman's own conclusion confirms this: after his cleansing he confessed that there is no God in all the earth except in Israel (2 Kings 5:15).

The story's wider lesson is a careful implication drawn from the text, though a strong one. God often passes over what people consider excellent so that faith will rest on His word. Naaman nearly lost his healing because his country's rivers looked better. What kept him away from cleansing was his pride, not his illness.

The Song of Solomon passage is love poetry, and its first meaning is the bridegroom's longing for his bride to leave the far-off heights and be with him. Amana's role is poetic. It marks the remote, beautiful, and dangerous edge of the land, and it sets off the closeness the bridegroom desires.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

Jesus referred directly to Naaman's story. In His hometown synagogue He reminded His hearers that there were many lepers in Israel in Elisha's time, yet none was cleansed except Naaman the Syrian (Luke 4:27). The crowd's anger at these examples shows how pointed they were (Luke 4:28). God's mercy had gone to a foreign commander, and it came only when that commander humbled himself. Jesus does not mention the rivers of Damascus, but the account He cites turns on Naaman's choice between those rivers and the Jordan. The Gospel calls people in the same way. Cleansing comes on God's terms, through a word that can look too simple. Paul speaks of God choosing the foolish and weak things of the world to put the wise and mighty to shame, so that no one may boast before Him (1 Corinthians 1:27–29).

The call from Amana belongs to a different kind of connection. The New Testament describes the church as a bride betrothed to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:25–27). Christians have long read the Song's bridegroom as a picture of Christ's love for His people, and this verse fits that reading well. He calls His bride away from high and dangerous places to come with Him. This is a devotional resonance rather than an interpretation the New Testament gives to Song 4:8 itself, but it rests on the Bible's own bridal language.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

To Naaman, the rivers of Damascus looked far better. His mistake was to judge God's promise by the appearance of the water. Many people stand where he stood, holding on to what is impressive, familiar, and reliable in ordinary life and offended that God's way of cleansing seems so plain. The rivers of Damascus could water a city, but they could not take away his leprosy. Only God's word, received in humble obedience, made Naaman's flesh "like the flesh of a little child." If the name Amana does carry the sense of constant, then the irony runs deep. Naaman preferred the steady waters of home, when the One truly faithful was the God of Israel. That wordplay is an observation from the name, not something the text itself points out, but it fits the story's lesson closely.

Song 4:8 offers a gentle counterpart. The heights of Amana are majestic, yet lions and leopards make their homes there. The bridegroom does not scold his bride for standing on the mountaintop. He calls her to come with him. Read as a picture of Christ's love for His people, the Lord's call away from the world's high places is a call of love, drawing His people from dangers they may not see into His company and care.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • 2 Kings 5:12: The appearance of the river name (marginal reading "Amanah," written as "Abanah" in the NKJV) in Naaman's proud comparison of Damascus's rivers with Israel's waters.

  • 2 Kings 5:9–14: The full setting, showing Naaman's expectations, his anger, his servants' counsel, and his cleansing through humble obedience.

  • Song of Solomon 4:8: Mount Amana as a remote, wild northern height from which the bridegroom calls his bride.

  • Luke 4:27: Jesus cites Naaman's cleansing as evidence of God's mercy reaching beyond Israel.

  • 1 Corinthians 1:27–29: God's pattern of choosing humble means so that no one boasts before Him.

  • 2 Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:25–27: The New Testament's bridal language for Christ and His church, which supports a devotional reading of Song 4:8.
