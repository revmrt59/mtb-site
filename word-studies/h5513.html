SINITE - (Çîynîy, Strong's H5513)

Summary

"Sinite" is the name of an ancient people descended from Canaan, the son of Ham. The word appears only twice in the Bible, both times in the same short line of a family record: "the Hivite, the Arkite, and the Sinite" (Genesis 10:17; 1 Chronicles 1:15). Scripture tells us nothing about what the Sinites did, whom they fought, or where they worshiped. We know only that they came from Canaan's line and were listed among the clans along the northern edge of Canaanite territory, near the Arkites and Arvadites.

The name still matters because of where God placed it. Genesis 10 records the nations that spread across the earth after the Flood. It names Canaan's descendants just before God calls Abram and promises him "this land." Centuries later, the Chronicler copied the same line for Israel's returning exiles. In both places the Sinite shows that the God of Israel knows every people by name and history. The Lord who promised Abram that "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" had already written down who those families were.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Çîynîy is a Hebrew gentilic, a word that names a people by their ancestor or place of origin, much as "Texan" or "Scot" does in English. The NKJV renders it "Sinite" in both of its occurrences. Each is found in a genealogy. Genesis 10, often called the Table of Nations, traces the descendants of Noah's sons after the Flood. First Chronicles 1 repeats that material as the opening of Israel's own family history.

The Sinites are grouped with the Arkites, Arvadites, Zemarites, and Hamathites, peoples associated with the coast and hinterland of what is now Lebanon and western Syria. One proposal connects the name with an ancient town in that region called Siannu. That identification is reasonable but unproven, and Scripture itself gives only the name and the family line. The Sinites do not appear again by this name in Israel's conquest or settlement records. If their home lay in the far north, that may help explain their absence, though Scripture does not give the reason, and Joshua 11 does describe a northern campaign reaching as far as Sidon and the valley of Lebanon.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one meaning: a member of the Sinite people, a clan descended from Canaan. It carries no secondary senses, no figurative use, and no change of meaning between its two occurrences. Its importance comes from its setting in Scripture rather than from anything in the word itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Both occurrences sit in identical wording within parallel lists. One pattern in those lists helps a reader follow them. Genesis 10:15 begins with persons: "Canaan begot Sidon his firstborn, and Heth." Then the list changes form and names peoples: "the Jebusite, the Amorite, and the Girgashite," continuing through "the Hivite, the Arkite, and the Sinite" (Genesis 10:16–17). "The Sinite" is singular with the article, but it stands for a whole clan. The genealogy is moving from individual sons to the nations that grew from them. Verse 18 states this directly: "Afterward the families of the Canaanites were dispersed."

4. Theological Significance

A genealogy can look like the dullest page in the Bible, but Genesis 10 makes a theological claim through its form. God is not only the God of one family. Every branch of Noah's descendants is accounted for, including small clans on the northern coast that never return to the story. The Sinite is listed because God knows where each people came from and where it settled.

Paul states plainly the truth that this chapter shows through its record. Speaking in Athens, a city whose civic tradition claimed its people had sprung from their own soil, he said that God "has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26). Genesis 10 records those boundaries. It even sketches the Canaanite border "from Sidon as you go toward Gerar, as far as Gaza" (Genesis 10:19). The Sinites' small place on that map belongs to a world God governs.

The list also stands in the shadow of Genesis 9. Just before it, Noah pronounced, "Cursed be Canaan; A servant of servants He shall be to his brethren" (Genesis 9:25). The Sinites descend from that line. Scripture traces both judgment and history through particular families, and it does not let us forget where a people came from.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:15–19 and 12:1–7: naming the land's people before promising the land. The order of Genesis is deliberate. Chapter 10 names Canaan's clans and outlines their territory. Chapter 11 recounts Babel and then narrows through Shem to Terah's family. Then in chapter 12 the Lord tells Abram to go "to a land that I will show you" (12:1). When Abram arrives, the narrator adds, "And the Canaanites were then in the land" (12:6). The reader is not surprised, because Genesis 10 has already introduced them. Only then does the Lord say, "To your descendants I will give this land" (12:7).

This order matters. God's promise was not given for empty or imaginary territory. It concerned real ground, held by real peoples whose names had already been written down. Some of those clans, like the Sinites in the far north, lay outside the core lists of nations Israel would later face (compare Genesis 15:18–21 and Deuteronomy 7:1). Genesis 10 gives the whole family of Canaan, and the promise then marks out the land God intended. He knew exactly what He was promising and to whom it then belonged, and He made the promise anyway.

The same chapter holds a wider hope. Genesis 10 speaks of "the families of the Canaanites" (10:18), and two chapters later God tells Abram, "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (12:3). The nations listed in chapter 10 are the families of the earth. The Sinite therefore stands both among the peoples whose land is promised to Abram and among the families the promise reaches.

1 Chronicles 1:15: the same line for a people starting over. The Chronicler wrote for Jews who had returned from exile to a small, struggling province surrounded by powerful empires. He opened his history with the whole human race, from Adam through the nations of Genesis 10, before narrowing to Israel. Repeating "the Hivite, the Arkite, and the Sinite" word for word told the returnees that their story began within God's dealings with all peoples. They were not a forgotten remnant of a minor nation. They belonged to the purpose God had been pursuing across the whole map of the world.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The promise that "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" in Abram (Genesis 12:3) finds its fulfillment in Jesus Christ, the Seed through whom blessing comes to the nations (Galatians 3:8, 16). The Table of Nations lists the peoples who need that blessing, and the Sinites are among them.

This connection works as a canonical pattern rather than a direct reference, but it can be traced in the Gospels. Jesus ministered in the region of Tyre and Sidon, the old heartland of Canaan's northern descendants. There a woman Matthew calls "a woman of Canaan" begged mercy for her daughter and received it (Matthew 15:21–28). Crowds from Tyre and Sidon came to hear Him (Mark 3:8). As a canonical resonance rather than a direct fulfillment of Noah's words, the line that began under Noah's curse in Genesis 9 was meeting the Son of Abraham, who bore the curse of the law for others (Galatians 3:13). In Revelation the redeemed are gathered "out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation" (Revelation 5:9). The world of Genesis 10 is not erased in that gathering. It is redeemed.

7. Spirit & Life

The Sinites have two verses and no story. Scripture records no hero from them, and no oracle names them explicitly. Yet God saw fit to name them twice in His Word. That should steady anyone who feels like a name in a list. The Lord who recorded a minor northern clan, and the boundaries of the land where it lived, knows the history of every family, including yours. Paul's point in Acts 17 was that God set those times and boundaries "so that they should seek the Lord" (17:27).

The structure of Genesis also teaches something about how God keeps His word. He named the land's inhabitants before He promised the land. He did not make promises in ignorance of the obstacles, and He did not hide those obstacles from Abram. When God commits Himself to something in your life, He already knows the names of everything standing in the way. Abram's response was to build an altar to the Lord "who had appeared to him" (Genesis 12:7), while the Canaanites were still in the land.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 10:17 and 1 Chronicles 1:15 are the only two occurrences of this word, and both have been discussed above. The surrounding verses that give them meaning are Genesis 9:25–27, Noah's words over Canaan, and Genesis 10:18–19, which records the dispersion and borders of the Canaanite families.

10. Other Relevant Insights

"Sinite" sounds like several other biblical names, but they are separate words: the Wilderness of Sin (H5512), Mount Sinai (H5514), and the land of Sinim (H5515), from which Isaiah foresees people coming: "these from the land of Sinim" (Isaiah 49:12). The Sinites of Canaan's line have no stated connection to the giving of the Law at Sinai or to Isaiah's prophecy. Building a sermon on such a link would rest on English spelling rather than on Scripture.
