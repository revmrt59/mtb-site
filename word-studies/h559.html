SAY, SPEAK, ANSWER, COMMAND - (ʼâmar, Strong's H0559)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʼâmar is the Old Testament's ordinary word for saying something. Most often it means simply "said," "say," or "saying," and it carries the conversations of Scripture. Patriarchs bargain, kings give orders, widows plead, and prophets argue with this word. Depending on the setting it can also mean answer, tell, command, promise, or even think, because Hebrew describes thinking as saying in one's heart. The word has no special religious force of its own. Its weight comes from who is speaking and what is said.

When God is the speaker, the word gains great significance. "Thus says the LORD" opens message after message in the prophets. In those messages God does more than pass along information. What He says governs nations, shakes heaven and earth, and keeps His people's future secure. The same word records God's command in Eden and the serpent's first question, "Has God indeed said...?" (Genesis 3:1). Human beings also say things, both aloud and in their hearts: confessions of faith, cries from the depths, and the proud boasts God brings down. Scripture looks ahead to a day when God's people will finally say what is true: "Behold, this is our God" (Isaiah 25:9).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb ʼâmar occurs 5,308 times in 4,338 verses of the Hebrew Bible. That makes it one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible. It occurs in every Old Testament book, and it is especially frequent in narrative books such as Genesis, Samuel, and Kings, and in the prophets Jeremiah and Ezekiel. In narrative it usually introduces a direct quotation ("And he said..."), so it signals that a voice is about to be heard. Another common Hebrew verb, dabar, also means to speak. The two words often overlap, and readers should not press a sharp contrast between them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to say or express something in words. Context brings out several distinct senses:

1. To say, speak, or answer in ordinary conversation. This is by far the most common use, as in Moses' reply at the bush, "Here I am" (Exodus 3:4).

2. To command or give an order. Here the word expresses authority, as when David gave a "commandment" to burn the Philistine idols (1 Chronicles 14:12).

3. To think or resolve inwardly, usually in the phrase say in the heart or say to oneself: "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 14:1).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In narrative, ʼâmar turns description into relationship. The story moves forward as people speak to one another and to God.

In prophecy, the word appears in the formula "Thus says the LORD." This formula marks the prophet's message as God's own speech rather than the prophet's opinion.

The prophets also use the word to quote the people back to themselves. Malachi shows the pattern sharply. God says, "I have loved you," and the people answer, "Yet you say, 'In what way have You loved us?'" (Malachi 1:2). Malachi repeats the same phrase later in the book (2:14; 3:13–14). By quoting them, God shows that He has heard their evasions.

Finally, Scripture sometimes gives speech to things that do not literally talk. In Job, "The deep says, 'It is not in me'; And the sea says, 'It is not with me'" (Job 28:14). God also asks Job whether he can send lightnings that "say to you, 'Here we are!'" (Job 38:35). These are poetic images and do not claim that nature is conscious. The deep confesses that it cannot supply wisdom, and the lightning reports for duty. In both pictures, all creation answers to its Maker.

4. Theological Significance

The God of Israel speaks. The idols of the nations stay silent, but the LORD creates, commands, calls, reveals Himself, and judges by what He says. Genesis opens with God saying, "Let there be light," and light appears. That pattern runs through the prophets. When God announces, "I will shake heaven and earth, the sea and dry land" (Haggai 2:6), or tells the exiles that people "shall again use this speech in the land of Judah... 'The LORD bless you, O home of justice, and mountain of holiness!'" (Jeremiah 31:23), the announcement already contains the outcome. Isaiah explains the reason. Like rain that waters the earth before it returns, God's word "shall not return to Me void, But it shall accomplish what I please" (Isaiah 55:11). Human promises often fail. God's saying does not.

God's speech also reveals who He is. When Moses asks to see God's glory, God answers in words: "I will make all My goodness pass before you, and I will proclaim the name of the LORD before you" (Exodus 33:19). Moses sees only God's back, but he hears God's name and character spoken over him: merciful, gracious, longsuffering (Exodus 34:6–7). Earlier, God had bound Himself to Abraham with an oath: "By Myself I have sworn, says the LORD" (Genesis 22:16). Because no one is greater than God, He swears by Himself (Hebrews 6:13). Through such speech the infinite God draws near to finite people.

For that reason, human speech is never trivial. What people say, aloud or in the heart, shows whether they agree with God's reality or resist it. The prince of Tyre was judged because "your heart is lifted up, And you say, 'I am a god'" (Ezekiel 28:2). The fool's private sentence in Psalm 14:1 is a statement God takes seriously. Inner speech is often where a person's true allegiance is spoken.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 2:16 and 3:1 — The garden command and the serpent's counter-word. "And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, 'Of every tree of the garden you may freely eat.'" God's garden command begins with generosity. The single forbidden tree marks a protective limit within a wide freedom. Humanity was made to live within what God has said. One chapter later the serpent uses the same verb to undercut that word: "Has God indeed said, 'You shall not eat of every tree of the garden'?" (Genesis 3:1). The question turns God's every tree you may eat into not every tree, so that a generous Father is made to sound stingy. The serpent then contradicts God outright (3:4). The Fall began as a contest between two sayings. Temptation still often involves a question about whether God really said what He said, and whether He meant it kindly.

Isaiah 6:8 and Exodus 3:4 — Calling as conversation. "I heard the voice of the Lord, saying: 'Whom shall I send, And who will go for Us?' Then I said, 'Here am I! Send me.'" The same verb carries both voices. God's call comes as a question, and Isaiah answers freely. Moses' calling follows the same pattern. God "said, 'Moses, Moses!' And he said, 'Here I am'" (Exodus 3:4). In both scenes, God's commission begins when He speaks and a servant answers. Both men answer only after God has spoken first. Isaiah speaks as a man whose lips have just been cleansed (6:7), so his answer is itself the fruit of grace.

Psalm 16:2 and Jonah 2:4 — Saying that anchors the soul. David preaches to himself: "O my soul, you have said to the LORD, 'You are my Lord, My goodness is nothing apart from You.'" He reminds his own soul of a confession it has already made, and that confession steadies him. Jonah, inside the fish, records two statements in one breath: "Then I said, 'I have been cast out of Your sight; Yet I will look again toward Your holy temple.'" He speaks honest despair first and chosen hope second. His words turn his face back toward God before his circumstances change at all.

Malachi 2:14 and Ezekiel 33:27 — God answers self-justification. When God refuses Judah's offerings, the people protest, "Yet you say, 'For what reason?'" God answers plainly. He "has been witness Between you and the wife of your youth, With whom you have dealt treacherously." In Ezekiel, the survivors in the ruins were claiming the land as Abraham's heirs while still living in sin (Ezekiel 33:24–26). God tells the prophet, "Say thus to them" (Ezekiel 33:27), and His word overturns their confident claim. In both books, God hears the arguments people use to excuse themselves and answers them with the truth.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The biblical story can be traced through who is saying what. God speaks first in creation and in Eden. The serpent answers with "Has God indeed said?" From then on, human hearts say foolish and proud things, from "There is no God" (Psalm 14:1) to "I am a god" (Ezekiel 28:2). In Revelation the same boast is heard from Babylon, which "says in her heart, 'I sit as queen'" (Revelation 18:7).

Jesus enters this conflict of words as the faithful Son. In the wilderness the tempter questions God's word again. Jesus answers each temptation with "It is written," and He holds to what God has said at the very point where Adam let go of it (Matthew 4:1–11). The New Testament goes further still. God, who spoke through the prophets "Thus says the LORD," has now spoken to us in His Son (Hebrews 1:1–2). The Word who was with God and was God became flesh (John 1:1, 14). The God whose saying created light now speaks in a Person.

The prophets also anticipated a final, redeemed speech: "And in that day you will say: 'O LORD, I will praise You; Though You were angry with me, Your anger is turned away, and You comfort me'" (Isaiah 12:1). They foresaw a people who will say, "Behold, this is our God; We have waited for Him, and He will save us" (Isaiah 25:9). The gospel brings that day near now: "if you confess with your mouth the Lord Jesus and believe in your heart that God has raised Him from the dead, you will be saved" (Romans 10:9). History closes when every tongue confesses that Jesus Christ is Lord (Philippians 2:11).

7. Spirit & Life

Because God's saying cannot fail, a believer can rest the full weight of life on His promises. "Thus says the LORD" is the firmest ground a person can stand on, firmer than circumstances, feelings, or the confident claims of others. Temptation often comes first as a question about God's goodness: Did He really say that? Does He really have your good in mind? The answer Eden lacked is to remember exactly what God said, including the generous "freely eat" that comes before every "do not."

The heart is always saying something. Scripture does not let us treat our private sentences as harmless, because God hears what the fool says in his heart and what Tyre's prince says in his pride. The psalmist shows a better way to talk inwardly. He tells his own soul, "You are my Lord." Jonah shows that faith can speak in the same breath as despair: "I have been cast out... Yet I will look again." The Spirit teaches believers to say to God what is true, and He replaces the heart's quiet rebellion with the cry, "Abba, Father" (Galatians 4:6). Prayer, confession, and praise are practice for the day when all God's people will say together, "This is the LORD; We have waited for Him."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ezekiel 16:6 — God pictures Israel as an abandoned newborn struggling in her blood. He says to her, "Live!" His word alone gives her life.

  • Lamentations 3:37 — The poet asks who can speak and see it come to pass unless the Lord has commanded it. This is a sober reminder that only God's word is certain to stand.

  • Jeremiah 8:11 — False prophets kept saying "Peace, peace!" when there was no peace. Human saying cannot create the reality it announces.

  • Job 37:6 — God says to the snow, "Fall on the earth," and creation obeys His word.

  • 1 Kings 17:24 — After her son is raised, the widow of Zarephath says she now knows that Elijah is a man of God and that the LORD's word in his mouth is truth.

  • Psalm 87:5 — Of Zion it will be said that this one and that one were born in her. God's people will be counted as citizens of Zion by His own declaration.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1696 / H1697 (dabar, to speak / word) — These are distinct words, not relatives of ʼâmar. Dabar is the other major Hebrew word group for speech. It appears in Isaiah 55:11 ("So shall My word be"), the passage that explains why God's "Thus says the LORD" never returns empty.
