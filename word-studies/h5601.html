SAPPHIRE - (çappîyr, Strong's H5601)

Summary

The Hebrew word çappîyr names a precious deep-blue stone, translated "sapphire" in the NKJV. Many scholars believe the ancient stone was lapis lazuli: an opaque, rich blue stone often flecked with gold, rather than the clear gem sold as sapphire today. Its color matters. Scripture's most important uses of the stone appear where heaven meets the presence of God. At Sinai, the elders of Israel saw beneath God's feet something "as it were a paved work of sapphire stone, and it was like the very heavens in its clarity" (Exodus 24:10). In Ezekiel's visions, God's throne appears "like a sapphire stone" (Ezekiel 1:26).

From the visions of God's throne, the image moves outward. A sapphire was set in the high priest's breastplate and engraved with the name of one of Israel's tribes. Job says that even sapphires cannot buy wisdom. Poets use the stone to describe beauty that is cherished and beauty that has been lost. Finally, Isaiah hears God promise to lay the foundations of His storm-battered people with sapphires (Isaiah 54:11), and the same image reappears in the foundations of the new Jerusalem (Revelation 21:19). The stone that pictured the pavement beneath God's feet becomes part of the promise of His people's home. That movement traces the hope of the whole Bible, a hope fulfilled in Christ. He reigns from the throne, carries His people before God as their High Priest, and is preparing a city for them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Çappîyr is a Hebrew noun found in Exodus, Job, the Song of Solomon, Isaiah, Lamentations, and Ezekiel. The English word "sapphire" probably descends from this word family by way of Greek and Latin. The ancient stone, however, was most likely lapis lazuli. It was mined in distant mountains and traded across the ancient world as the finest blue stone available. Ancient descriptions mention its golden flecks, so a polished piece looked like a night sky scattered with stars. Translations keep the traditional word "sapphire." Knowing which stone the Bible means helps readers see what Moses and Ezekiel were describing.

The word appears in five kinds of settings: the craftsmanship of the priest's garments, visions of God, wisdom poetry about mining, love poetry, and prophetic lament and promise.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Çappîyr means a precious blue gemstone. Sometimes Scripture speaks of the actual stone: a jewel set in gold, a treasure dug from the earth, or an ornament on a king. At other times the stone serves as a comparison. A writer uses it to describe how something looked or how much it was worth. One definition covers every occurrence. The richness of the word comes from where Scripture places the stone.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the stone itself is in view, the setting is either sacred craftsmanship (Exodus 28:18; 39:11) or earthly wealth (Job 28:6, 16; Ezekiel 28:13).

When the stone is a comparison, the language of the visions is noticeably careful. Exodus says "as it were a paved work of sapphire stone." Ezekiel speaks of "the likeness of a throne, in appearance like a sapphire stone" (Ezekiel 1:26). He also describes "something like a sapphire stone" (Ezekiel 10:1). These writers never claim that God's throne is made of quarried rock. They give the reader's eye something to hold while admitting that what they saw was greater than any earthly material.

In poetry, the stone lends its color and value to people. The beloved's body is "carved ivory inlaid with sapphires" (Song of Solomon 5:14). Jerusalem's Nazirites were "like sapphire in their appearance" (Lamentations 4:7).

4. Theological Significance

First, the sapphire passages reveal God's majesty. What lay beneath His feet looked like sapphire and was "like the very heavens in its clarity." Exodus compares the pavement to the clear sky; it does not say the sky was His floor. Isaiah 66:1 offers a related image of His majesty: "Heaven is My throne, And earth is My footstool." No part of creation stands above Him.

Second, the same stone shows how God values His people. The stone that pictured the pavement beneath His feet was set in the high priest's breastplate, where twelve stones together carried the names of Israel's tribes, one name on each. God connects the people He has redeemed with the splendor of His own dwelling.

Third, the stone traces a pattern of reversal. The king of Tyre was covered with precious stones, sapphire among them, "in Eden, the garden of God" (Ezekiel 28:13). Yet his heart was lifted up because of his beauty (Ezekiel 28:17), and God cast him down. Many readers hear in this lament an echo of an even greater fall behind the king's. Either way, the passage plainly shows glory lost through pride. Lamentations 4:7 likewise mourns people who once shone like sapphire and then darkened under judgment. Isaiah 54:11 moves in the opposite direction. Where pride brought the king of Tyre down, God promises to lay sapphire foundations for the afflicted.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 24:10–11 — The pavement under God's feet. Moses had just sprinkled the blood of the covenant on the people (Exodus 24:8). Then Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy elders climbed the mountain, "and they saw the God of Israel." Exodus does not describe God's form. It describes only what lay under His feet: something like a sapphire pavement "like the very heavens in its clarity." The text itself supplies the comparison: the pavement was as clear as the sky. The next verse is just as remarkable: "But on the nobles of the children of Israel He did not lay His hand. So they saw God, and they ate and drank." On the mountain where they saw that sapphire-like pavement, sinful men shared a meal in God's presence and lived. Exodus does not state the reason in so many words, but the order of the story is plain: the blood of the covenant was sprinkled first (24:8), and the meal on the mountain followed (24:11).

Ezekiel 1:26 and 10:1 — The throne in exile. Ezekiel saw his first vision beside a river in Babylon, far from the temple. A storm, living creatures, wheels, and a gleaming expanse filled the sky. Above them all was a throne "like a sapphire stone." On the throne sat "a likeness with the appearance of a man high above it." Ezekiel calls the whole scene "the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD" (Ezekiel 1:28). In chapter 10 the sapphire throne appears again, as God's glory prepares to leave the defiled temple. Together, these two visions show that God's throne is not confined to Jerusalem. He rules in Babylon, and He rules over the judgment of His own house.

Exodus 28:18 — A sapphire over the priest's heart. Only four chapters after the Sinai vision, God ordered "the second row shall be a turquoise, a sapphire, and a diamond" for Aaron's breastplate. Each of the twelve stones carried the name of a tribe (Exodus 28:21). Aaron bore those names over his heart whenever he entered the holy place (Exodus 28:29). His garments repeated the tabernacle's colors of blue, purple, scarlet, and gold, so the high priest walked into God's presence clothed like a small sanctuary. The elders had seen what looked like sapphire under God's feet. Now a sapphire, engraved with one tribe's name, was carried into His presence among the twelve stones that bore all Israel's names. Distance and nearness meet on the priest's chest.

Isaiah 54:11 — Foundations for the storm-tossed. God addresses Jerusalem as "afflicted one, Tossed with tempest, and not comforted." The address is honest about her condition. God does not reply with an explanation. He replies with a building project: "I will lay your stones with colorful gems, And lay your foundations with sapphires." Gates and walls of precious stones follow (Isaiah 54:12). The stone that pictured God's pavement and throne now appears in the foundations God promises to lay for His people. What was once seen from a distance becomes part of the promise of their home. This promise comes immediately after Isaiah 53, where the Servant is "wounded for our transgressions." In the order of Isaiah's prophecy, the city's restoration follows the Servant's suffering.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Read together, the sapphire passages tell one story. God's throne stands high above all He has made. A mediator carries God's people into His presence. In the end, God promises them a dwelling whose foundations are adorned with the same stone that pictured His throne. Christ fulfills each part of that story.

In Ezekiel's vision, a figure "with the appearance of a man" sits on the sapphire throne. The New Testament shows the risen Jesus seated on the throne at the Father's right hand. Revelation 4 also echoes Ezekiel's throne vision. The New Testament never names Ezekiel 1:26 as a direct prophecy of Christ, but Christians see the throne's fullest meaning in the One who took on human nature and now reigns.

Jesus is also the High Priest who "passed through the heavens" (Hebrews 4:14). He lives forever to intercede for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). Aaron carried Israel's names on stones. Christ carries His people in His own person.

The meal at Sinai looks forward to the table Jesus set when He said that the cup was "the new covenant in My blood" (Luke 22:20). Like the elders on the mountain, His people eat and drink before God and live.

Isaiah's sapphire city is not forgotten. Paul quotes Isaiah 54:1 and applies it to "the Jerusalem above" (Galatians 4:26–27). That is a direct quotation of a different verse in the same chapter. Revelation's link to Isaiah 54:11 is an echo rather than a quotation. John sees the new Jerusalem, where "the foundations of the wall of the city were adorned with all kinds of precious stones," and among them was "the second sapphire" (Revelation 21:19). Revelation uses the Greek name for the stone and does not cite Isaiah, but the image recalls Isaiah's promise. The story that began with a sapphire-like pavement under God's feet ends with sapphire adorning the foundations of the city where God dwells with His people forever.

7. Spirit & Life

Job 28 describes miners who cut shafts into the dark earth, where "its stones are the source of sapphires" (Job 28:6). Human skill can reach the deepest treasures in creation. Yet wisdom "cannot be valued in the gold of Ophir, In precious onyx or sapphire" (Job 28:16). After all the digging, God gives the answer: "Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom" (Job 28:28). People still dig hard for security through careers, savings, and achievements. The sapphire cannot buy what we need most. Wisdom comes from God and is received by fearing Him.

The breastplate speaks to anyone who feels small before a holy God. The stone that pictured the pavement beneath God's feet carried an ordinary tribal name, set among the twelve names on the breastplate, and Aaron was to bear them over his heart when he went into the holy place (Exodus 28:29). Christ does not go before the Father and leave His people behind. Your name is carried in.

Isaiah 54:11 also shows how God answers suffering. He calls His people what they are: afflicted, storm-tossed, uncomforted. Then He promises to build. A believer may not receive an explanation for every storm. God's answer is a promised foundation, laid with the same stone that pictured His throne. That foundation cannot be shaken.

Finally, the king of Tyre is a warning. Gifts God gives can become a source of pride. Beauty, talent, and position, held proudly, can be lost. Received humbly, they become part of what God is building.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Exodus 39:11 — "The second row, a turquoise, a sapphire, and a diamond." The craftsmen carried out exactly what God had commanded in Exodus 28. The sapphire was set in place as God specified.

Song of Solomon 5:14 — The bride describes her beloved: "His body is carved ivory Inlaid with sapphires." She reaches for the most costly materials she knows, ivory and royal gemstones, to say that he is worth more than all others.

Lamentations 4:7 — Jerusalem's Nazirites were once "like sapphire in their appearance." The next verse says they became blacker than soot. The comparison measures how far a people set apart for God had fallen.
