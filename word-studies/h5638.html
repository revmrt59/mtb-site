WINTER - (çᵉthâv, Strong's H5638)

Summary

The Hebrew word çᵉthâv means winter. It appears only once in the Bible, in the Song of Solomon's springtime invitation: "For lo, the winter is past, The rain is over and gone" (Song of Solomon 2:11). In ancient Israel, winter was the season of heavy, cold rains rather than snow and ice. Those rains soaked the ground, made travel hard, and kept people indoors. Yet without them there would be no flowers, figs, or grapes when spring arrived.

The word's single appearance carries real spiritual weight. The beloved speaks it to his bride as the reason she should rise and come away. The rainy season has ended, and the change of season is the occasion for his invitation. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, Song of Solomon 2:11 pictures the Lord who brings His people through hard seasons and calls them out of hiding into open fellowship with Him. The poem does not promise when any particular trial will end, but it points toward the final spring Scripture does promise, when God will dwell with His people and "the former things have passed away" (Revelation 21:3–4).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Çᵉthâv is a rare poetic word. Lexicons commonly connect it with the related Aramaic and Syriac words for winter. The more common Hebrew word for the cold half of the year is choreph, which Genesis 8:22 uses for "winter and summer." The Song's poet chose an unusual word for a moment of unusual beauty.

The land of Israel had two main seasons: a hot, dry summer and a cool, rainy winter. The winter rains, roughly from late autumn into early spring, were heavy and sometimes harsh. Roads turned to mud, shepherds sought shelter, and households stayed inside. Jesus could warn, "pray that your flight may not be in winter" (Matthew 24:20), because escaping in that season would be miserable and dangerous. The same rains, however, watered the crops. Winter in Israel was hard, but it was not barren.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Çᵉthâv has one sense: the winter season, understood as Israel's rainy time of year. The poet makes the meaning plain by placing it beside the common word for rain. "The winter is past" and "the rain is over and gone" say the same thing in two lines, as Hebrew poetry often does. Any further significance comes from the passage around the word, not from its dictionary meaning.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Because the word occurs only in Song of Solomon 2:11, its whole biblical use is set within one poem. The setting is a love song. The bride hears her beloved coming (2:8), sees him at the window (2:9), and hears him call her outside (2:10). Song of Solomon 2:11 begins with "For," so winter's passing is the reason for his call. The verses that follow (2:12–13) describe the spring that winter's end has opened: flowers, singing, the turtledove, green figs, and fragrant vines. The word winter is defined by what it has been holding back.

4. Theological Significance

God orders the seasons. At creation He set the lights in the sky "for signs and seasons, and for days and years" (Genesis 1:14). After the Flood He promised that "winter and summer" would not cease while the earth remains (Genesis 8:22). Ecclesiastes adds, "To everything there is a season, A time for every purpose under heaven" (Ecclesiastes 3:1). In Scripture, the coming and going of seasons shows the steady hand of God over time.

Seen this way, the end of winter in Song of Solomon 2:11 marks an appointed time reaching its fulfillment. Winter does not break apart at random. It runs the course God has given it, and then it gives way. The rain that confined people also prepared the fruit. Read with Scripture's wider teaching about the seasons, this suggests that God's hard seasons have a purpose and an end. The loss and the fruitfulness belong to one design.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:8–10: the voice that announces the new season. In the poem, the bride learns that winter is over by hearing it. "The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes Leaping upon the mountains" (2:8). He stands "behind our wall," gazing "through the lattice" (2:9). Then "My beloved spoke, and said to me: 'Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away'" (2:10). The announcement that winter has passed comes from his mouth as he arrives. The change of season and the coming of the beloved arrive together, and his word tells her what time it is.

Song of Solomon 2:11–13: winter past, spring arrived. "For lo, the winter is past" is the center of his appeal. The rainy season has ended, and that change of season is the occasion for his invitation. What follows is not a gradual thaw. "The flowers appear on the earth; The time of singing has come" (2:12). "The fig tree puts forth her green figs, And the vines with the tender grapes Give a good smell" (2:13). Every image depends on the winter that came before. Figs and grapes grow from ground the rain has soaked. The beloved repeats his call, "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away!" (2:13), so the invitation frames the whole description of spring.

Song of Solomon 2:14: the dove in the cleft of the rock. He next addresses her as "my dove, in the clefts of the rock, In the secret places of the cliff." The poem does not say why she is there or how long she has stayed. Perhaps the cleft had sheltered her through the rains, and a sheltered cleft would have been a good place to be in that season. Scripture itself knows a right kind of hiding: "Hide yourself, as it were, for a little moment, Until the indignation is past" (Isaiah 26:20). However, the beloved has just declared that the storm is past. Now he wants something more than her safety: "Let me see your face, Let me hear your voice; For your voice is sweet, And your face is lovely." Whatever shelter the cleft had given her, staying hidden now would keep her from him.

Genesis 8: the rains restrained and the dove sent out. The Song's picture can be set beside the end of the Flood as a thematic parallel. "The windows of heaven were also stopped, and the rain from heaven was restrained" (Genesis 8:2). Noah then sent out a dove, which returned with an olive leaf, so that "Noah knew that the waters had receded from the earth," and a week later did not return at all (Genesis 8:11–12). Genesis notes separately that the surface of the ground was dry, and later that the earth was dried (8:13–14). Song of Solomon 2 follows a similar order: the rain ends, "the voice of the turtledove Is heard in our land" (2:12), and the beloved calls his "dove" (2:14). The Song does not quote Genesis, and Israel's yearly rains were a blessing rather than a judgment. Still, both passages move from rain that shuts life in to a dove and a renewed earth, and Genesis 8:22 ties that renewal to God's promise of the seasons.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Scripture often presents the Lord as the bridegroom of His people, and the New Testament names Christ as the Bridegroom of the church. Read in that light, the voice that says "the winter is past" sounds like the gospel itself. Christ comes to a people separated from God and announces that the time of separation is ending. The new season does not begin with His people finding their way out. It begins when He arrives and speaks.

Many believers have also seen a deeper resonance in "the rain is over and gone." In the Psalms, overwhelming waters picture suffering under God's hand: "All Your waves and billows have gone over me" (Psalm 42:7). Isaiah's Servant "shall bear their iniquities" and "see the labor of His soul, and be satisfied" (Isaiah 53:11). The Song does not state this connection. It is a fitting gospel reflection rather than the poem's plain meaning. Even so, the Bridegroom can declare the storm finished for His bride because He went under it Himself.

The story ends in a spring that does not turn back. Seasons on earth repeat, and winter returns. At the end, though, "the tabernacle of God is with men," and "the former things have passed away" (Revelation 21:3–4). The Song's spring is a foretaste of that day, when nothing will again stand between God and His people.

7. Spirit & Life

Believers know seasons that feel like winter: prayer seems unanswered, joy is muted, and fruit is hard to see. Song of Solomon 2:11 does not pretend such seasons are unreal, and it does not promise when a particular hardship will lift in this life. What it shows is that winter is a season, not the final word. The same God who appointed "a time for every purpose under heaven" holds every season in His hands, and the last spring is certain: God will dwell with His people and "will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Revelation 21:3–4).

Winter's rain is also working. The figs of Song of Solomon 2:13 come from the soaked ground of Song of Solomon 2:11. A hidden season of waiting, sorrow, or quiet obedience can be the time when roots go deep. A believer may see little growth in such a time while God is preparing a harvest.

The end of winter is His to announce. The bride did not decide spring had come. She heard her beloved's voice. Christ speaks through His Word and by His Spirit, and His people learn to listen for that voice rather than forcing a new season or despairing that it will never come.

When He speaks, the right response is to come out. Shelter in the cleft of the rock may have been God's mercy for a time. But the One who calls, "Let me see your face, Let me hear your voice," wants to see the faces of His people and hear their voices. He delights in their presence and their prayer, and He welcomes them into fellowship with Him. Some believers keep hiding long after the storm has passed, out of habit, fear, or old hurt. Applied to them, the Bridegroom's words in this verse say that their voice is sweet to Him and their face lovely, and they echo His call to rise up and come away.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Çᵉthâv occurs only in Song of Solomon 2:11, which is discussed above. Other biblical references to winter use different words. Genesis 8:22 uses choreph for "winter and summer," and Matthew 24:20 uses a Greek word for winter. These passages help with the season's background but are not further uses of çᵉthâv.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2779 (choreph): the more common Hebrew word for the cold or autumn-winter season, as in Genesis 8:22. It is a related term for the same season, not a word from the same family as çᵉthâv.

  • H1653 (geshem, rain), canonical pairing: in Song of Solomon 2:11, çᵉthâv stands in parallel with the ordinary Hebrew word for rain. That pairing shows the winter meant here is Israel's rainy season.
