AMORITE, AMORITES - (ʼĔmôrîy, Strong's H0567)

Summary

The Amorites were one of the peoples living in Canaan before Israel entered the land. Genesis lists them among the descendants of Canaan, and the Old Testament remembers them for their powerful kingdoms, especially those of Sihon and Og east of the Jordan and the five-king alliance Joshua defeated in the south. Sometimes "Amorite" names a specific people or kingdom. At other times it stands for all the inhabitants of Canaan whom God drove out.

The word carries far more than history. God told Abram that his descendants would wait four generations for the land, "for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Genesis 15:16). That one sentence shows a God who is patient and whose judgment is measured. Over time the Amorites became Scripture's benchmark for idolatry. Kings like Ahab and Manasseh were condemned for acting like them, and Ezekiel told apostate Jerusalem that her father was an Amorite. The Amorite story teaches God's power to overthrow intimidating enemies. It also warns that God's own people can become the very thing He judged.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼĔmôrîy is a Hebrew name for a people group. It is translated "Amorite" or "Amorites." Genesis 10:16 places the Amorite among the sons of Canaan in the table of nations. The word appears from Genesis through the Prophets. It is most frequent in Numbers, Deuteronomy, and Joshua, where Israel meets the Amorites in battle. Later books use the name in historical summaries, in worship, and in prophetic rebuke.

The Amorites appear in several regions. Sihon, "king of the Amorites," ruled east of the Jordan (Numbers 21:21). Amorite hill country lay in the south of Canaan (Deuteronomy 1:7, 19–20), and five Amorite kings ruled cities in the southern hills (Joshua 10:5).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word names a people, and context shows how widely it is meant.

1. A specific people or kingdom, such as Sihon's realm (Numbers 21:21–31), the two kings east of the Jordan (Deuteronomy 3:8), or the southern coalition (Joshua 10:5).

2. A representative name for the inhabitants of Canaan as a whole, as in Genesis 15:16 and Amos 2:10, where "the land of the Amorite" means the whole Promised Land.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word often appears in the lists of nations God promised to drive out, such as "the Canaanites and the Hittites and the Amorites" (Exodus 3:8; Deuteronomy 7:1). In these lists the Amorite stands beside other peoples as part of the promise.

Elsewhere the Amorites stand alone as the representative enemy. Amos speaks of "the Amorite" as one towering figure: "Whose height was like the height of the cedars, And he was as strong as the oaks" (Amos 2:9). Gathering the people into a single giant makes the enemy look overwhelming, and so it makes God's victory larger.

In the historical books the name becomes a moral comparison. To act "according to all that the Amorites had done" (1 Kings 21:26) is to repeat the sins that cost them the land.

4. Theological Significance

The Amorites show God's justice moving at God's pace. He set the conquest centuries in advance and tied it to the moral condition of the people being judged. Leviticus 18:24–28 explains what Genesis 15:16 anticipates: the nations defiled the land through their abominations, and the land vomited out its inhabitants. The same chapter warns Israel that the land would do the same to them. The conquest was God acting as Judge, and His people were never exempt from the standard He applied.

The Amorites also show that His power cannot be resisted. The giants Israel feared were cut down "fruit above And... roots beneath" (Amos 2:9). The people whose gods tempted Israel became Scripture's measure of how far a heart can turn from the Lord.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 15:16: patience before judgment. God promised Abram the land and then told him he would not live to possess it: "But in the fourth generation they shall return here, for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete." Abram's descendants would wait while the Amorites were given time. God was in no hurry to judge. He let a culture's sin run its full course over centuries before He acted. Abram lived and died a sojourner, waiting on a timetable set partly by the moral condition of his neighbors. When Joshua finally crossed the Jordan, the conquest was the end of a long patience, announced centuries earlier (Genesis 15:13–16).

Joshua 10: heaven fights, and Gibeon is protected. Five "kings of the Amorites" joined forces against Gibeon (Joshua 10:5–6). The king of Jerusalem led them. Joshua marched to Gibeon's defense, and the Lord threw down great hailstones that killed more of the enemy than Israel's swords did (10:11). When Joshua prayed, the sun and moon stood still until the battle was won (10:12–14). Creation itself served its Creator in judging the Amorites.

The people Joshua rescued matter just as much. Second Samuel 21:2 explains that the Gibeonites "were not of the children of Israel, but of the remnant of the Amorites." Gibeon had sought peace with Israel and received an oath of protection (Joshua 9). So the same campaign that broke Amorite power also defended Amorites who had come over to the side of Israel's God. Even in this judgment there was a door of refuge, and God kept His people's promise to those who entered it.

Judges 1:34–3:5: the enemy left in place. Israel did not finish what God commanded. "The Amorites forced the children of Dan into the mountains, for they would not allow them to come down to the valley" (Judges 1:34). Joseph's tribes later put some of them to forced labor (1:35), but the Amorites stayed. Soon Israel lived among them, intermarried, and served their gods (3:5–6). God's rebuke came through a prophet: "do not fear the gods of the Amorites, in whose land you dwell." Yet the LORD said, "But you have not obeyed My voice" (6:10). These were the same words Joshua had used when he set the choice before Israel (Joshua 24:15). Israel kept the enemies it tolerated, and their gods became Israel's snare (3:5–6; 6:10).

Ezekiel 16:3, 45: a new family tree. By Ezekiel's day Jerusalem's idolatry had gone so far that God told the city her father was an Amorite and her mother a Hittite. In history, Jerusalem had been an Amorite city, the home of one of the five kings in Joshua 10. God was saying that after centuries as the city of His name, Jerusalem had returned to her origins. Her conduct revealed her true family resemblance. Kings had already crossed that line: Ahab "behaved very abominably in following idols, according to all that the Amorites had done" (1 Kings 21:26), and Manasseh "acted more wickedly than all the Amorites who were before him" (2 Kings 21:11).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Ezekiel's verdict on Jerusalem prepares for Jesus' words to the religious leaders who said, "Abraham is our father." He answered, "If you were Abraham's children, you would do the works of Abraham" (John 8:39), and He told them, "You are of your father the devil" (8:44). Both Ezekiel and Jesus judge spiritual family by what people do and love. Physical descent is not the test. Paul states the principle directly: "he is a Jew who is one inwardly; and circumcision is that of the heart, in the Spirit" (Romans 2:29).

The story runs the other way as well. Rahab heard what Israel did to "the two kings of the Amorites... Sihon and Og" (Joshua 2:10). That news brought a Canaanite woman to faith, and she later appears in the line of Christ (Matthew 1:5). Gibeon, a remnant of the Amorites, found shelter under Israel's oath. The news of God's judgment became the means by which outsiders came to Him. In the gospel this pattern reaches its fullness: Christ bore judgment so that people from every nation, Amorites included, might be brought into Abraham's family through faith.

The patience of Genesis 15:16 continues in the New Testament. Peter explains that the Lord's apparent delay is patience, because He is not willing that any should perish (2 Peter 3:9).

7. Spirit & Life

Israel learned to sing about the Amorites. Psalm 136:19 sets "Sihon king of the Amorites" right beside the refrain "For His mercy endures forever." The enemy that once made Israel's spies feel like grasshoppers (Numbers 13:29–33) became a line in a hymn of praise. Whatever looms as tall as cedars in a believer's life is measured against the God who uprooted the Amorite "roots beneath."

The more searching lesson comes from Judges. In battle, Israel feared the Amorites. Once the fighting ended, Israel was charmed by their gods and drawn into their ways. Sin that is tolerated does not stay small and quiet. It crowds the believer "into the mountains" and keeps them out of ground God has already given. Amos made the point sharply: God reminded Israel that He had destroyed the Amorite for them, and then charged them with living as Amorites. Remembering what God has already defeated is meant to keep us from going back to it.

Genesis 15:16 also teaches believers how to wait. Abram waited because God was giving others time to repent. When God's promises seem slow, His delay may be the same mercy toward someone else.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 14:13 — Mamre the Amorite and his brothers were allies of Abram, so the patriarch lived peaceably among the people whose judgment was still generations away.

  • Numbers 21:21–25 — Israel asked Sihon for peaceful passage. He refused and attacked, and Israel defeated him and took possession of his land.

  • Deuteronomy 1:27, 44 — The unbelieving generation complained that God meant to hand them over to the Amorites. When they then went up to fight without Him, the Amorites chased them like bees.

  • 1 Kings 9:20–21 — Solomon conscripted the remaining Amorites as forced laborers, showing that the incomplete conquest of Judges was still unfinished generations later.

  • Ezra 9:1 — After the exile, leaders grieved that Israel had intermarried with the peoples of the lands, Amorites among them, and so repeated the old compromise.

  • Nehemiah 9:8 — In the returned exiles' prayer of confession, the promise to give Abraham the land of the Amorites stands as proof that God "performed" His words.
