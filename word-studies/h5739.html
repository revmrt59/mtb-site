FLOCK, HERD, DROVE - (ʻêder, Strong's H5739)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻêder means a flock or herd: a gathered group of animals, usually sheep or goats, moving and grazing together under someone's care. In Genesis it counts Jacob's wealth and names the "droves" he sent ahead to Esau. In Proverbs it calls for careful stewardship. In the Song of Solomon it pictures a bride's beauty. In the prophets it becomes the name of Israel itself: "the LORD's flock" (Jeremiah 13:17).

Through this word Scripture shows a God who does not rule His people from a distance. He feeds His flock, carries the lambs, and holds careless leaders to account when they let His people be scattered and carried off. He promises to seek and gather them Himself. The prophets also use the word in a striking reversal: proud cities become pastures where defenseless flocks lie down in the ruins. In Micah 4:8 God calls Zion the "tower of the flock" and promises that "the former dominion shall come" to her. A few verses later Micah names Bethlehem as the town from which Israel's Ruler will come (Micah 5:2). Centuries later, shepherds keeping watch over their flock in the countryside around Bethlehem were the first to hear of Christ's birth (Luke 2:8–11), a fitting echo of the shepherd language that runs through these promises. Jesus then gathers His people into "one flock" under "one shepherd" (John 10:16).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Israel was a nation of herdsmen before it was a nation of cities. Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, and David all kept animals. A flock in that world was not a gentle scene to admire. It was a family's living, and it was always at risk from drought, thieves, lions, and bears. Sheep cannot defend themselves against such dangers.

ʻêder names the animals as a group. That is why the word appears where the whole company matters: flocks gathered at a well (Genesis 29:2–3), droves kept apart on the road (Genesis 32:16), and a people scattered or brought back together.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A flock or herd of domestic animals, most often sheep or goats, and sometimes cattle ("the herds of cattle are restless," Joel 1:18).

2. A separate group within a larger holding, a "drove." Jacob sent his animals "every drove by itself" (Genesis 32:16).

3. By extension, a people pictured as a flock under a shepherd's care: "the LORD's flock" (Jeremiah 13:17).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the patriarchal stories, ʻêder measures wealth and blessing. Jacob's growing flocks (Genesis 30:40) and Hezekiah's "folds for flocks" (2 Chronicles 32:28) are marks of God's provision.

Poetry uses the word for beauty. The beloved's hair is "like a flock of goats, going down from Mount Gilead." Her teeth are "like a flock of shorn sheep which have come up from the washing, every one of which bears twins, and none is barren among them" (Song of Solomon 4:1–2). A farmer's eye is at work in that picture. It sees whiteness, health, order, and completeness, with nothing missing from the group.

In the prophets, the word usually describes Israel in relation to God and to the leaders who were supposed to tend them. Joel 1:18 is a useful reminder that not every flock is a symbol. There the animals themselves groan under a real drought.

4. Theological Significance

When Scripture calls God's people a flock, it describes a God who takes on the daily, unglamorous work of a shepherd. He does not rule His people from a distance. Psalm 78 recalls that He guided His people "in the wilderness like a flock" (Psalm 78:52), a picture of a shepherd leading His people step by step and bringing them safely through (Psalm 78:53).

The image also settles who owns the flock. Kings, priests, and prophets were under-shepherds who had been entrusted with someone else's animals. When they failed, God did not give up His claim. Jeremiah 31:10 announces to the nations, "He who scattered Israel will gather him, and keep him as a shepherd does his flock." The God who disciplined His people by scattering them is the same God who gathers them again. Micah 2:12 adds the sound of that day. The regathered remnant, "like a flock in the midst of their pasture," will "make a loud noise because of so many people."

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 40:11 — The Shepherd's arm. Isaiah has just announced the Lord coming "with a strong hand" (40:10). The next verse shows what that strength does: "He will feed His flock like a shepherd; He will gather the lambs with His arm, and carry them in His bosom, and gently lead those who are with young." The same arm that rules also carries the weakest lambs, and the mighty God sets His pace by the ewes that are heavy with young.

Jeremiah 13:17, 20; Ezekiel 34:12; Zechariah 10:3 — The flock lost and visited. Jeremiah weeps "because the LORD's flock has been taken captive." He then asks Jerusalem's leaders where the flock is that was given to them, the beautiful sheep entrusted to their care (Jeremiah 13:20). The flock was a trust, and the leaders lost it. Ezekiel 34 condemns shepherds who fed themselves and left the sheep to wander. God answers that failure by stepping in Himself: "As a shepherd seeks out his flock on the day he is among his scattered sheep, so will I seek out My sheep and deliver them from all the places where they were scattered on a cloudy and dark day" (34:12). Zechariah 10:3 puts God's anger and His care side by side: "My anger is kindled against the shepherds… For the LORD of hosts will visit His flock, the house of Judah." God's jealous love for His people outlasts every failure of the people He put in charge of them.

Isaiah 17:2; 32:14; Zephaniah 2:14 — The flock in the ruins. The prophets use the flock to picture judgment on human pride. The cities of Aroer, in the oracle against Damascus, "will be for flocks which lie down, and no one will make them afraid." In Isaiah 32:14, "the palaces will be forsaken… the forts and towers" become "a pasture of flocks." In Nineveh, the capital of the empire that terrorized the nations, "the herds shall lie down in her midst," and birds lodge on the tops of her pillars. The irony is hard to miss. The most defenseless of animals rest undisturbed where the most aggressive powers once stood. Their walls could not save them, and God turns their fortresses into pasture.

Micah 4:8 — The tower of the flock. Micah speaks to the "tower of the flock," the stronghold of the daughter of Zion, and promises that "the former dominion" will come to her. A tower of the flock was a shepherd's watchtower, a lookout over the pasture. Micah uses that humble image for Jerusalem and attaches to it the promise that the kingdom will be restored. The same phrase also appears in Genesis as a place-name: after burying Rachel on the way to Bethlehem, Jacob "pitched his tent beyond the tower of Eder" (Genesis 35:19–21). A few verses after 4:8, Micah names Bethlehem as the town from which Israel's Ruler will come (5:2). Micah does not connect the Genesis place with his tower, so that link is an echo rather than a stated connection. What Micah does state is clear enough. The restored dominion promised to Zion and the Ruler from little Bethlehem, where David once kept his father's sheep (1 Samuel 17:15), belong to the same hope. God's king comes from the sheepfolds, not from imperial towers.

Malachi 1:14 — The flock and worship. "Cursed be the deceiver who has in his flock a male, and takes a vow, but sacrifices to the Lord what is blemished— For I am a great King." The flock was God's gift to the worshiper, and the sound male was standing in it. Keeping the best for himself and handing God the defective animal exposed what the man actually thought of God's greatness.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The shepherd line runs through David. Before he faced Goliath, David told Saul how "a lion or a bear came and took a lamb out of the flock" and how he went after it (1 Samuel 17:34). Guarding his father's animals was where God trained Israel's king. "He also chose David His servant, and took him from the sheepfolds… to shepherd Jacob His people" (Psalm 78:70–71). God's kind of king does not run from danger. He goes after the lamb.

Jesus fulfills that pattern and goes beyond it: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The hireling "sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees" (10:12). The hireling recalls the false shepherds of Jeremiah and Ezekiel. Jesus is the Lord who comes Himself to seek His scattered people, and the Son of David who lays down His life where David only risked his. The gathering that Micah and Jeremiah promised widens in Him to include the nations: "other sheep I have which are not of this fold… and there will be one flock and one shepherd" (John 10:16).

Micah's tower of the flock, his promise of a ruler from Bethlehem, and the shepherds "keeping watch over their flock by night" near Bethlehem in Luke 2:8 belong to the same story. Luke does not cite Micah 4:8, and he does not say where the shepherds' fields lay beyond placing them in the same country as Bethlehem. The connection is one of theme, not location. The first people to hear of the Shepherd-King's birth were men keeping the kind of night watch over a flock that a shepherd's watchtower was built for.

7. Spirit & Life

The safety of God's people has never rested on the strength of the sheep or the perfection of their leaders. Jeremiah's leaders lost the flock, and God came looking for it. A believer who has been wounded by careless shepherds can know that the Owner of the flock has not lost track of His own.

Jacob's road to meet Esau shows a contrast. Afraid of losing everything, Jacob first divided his people and animals into two companies, reasoning that "if Esau comes to the one company and attacks it, then the other company which is left will escape" (Genesis 32:7–8). That is the best a frightened human shepherd can do, which is to accept some losses. The word ʻêder does not appear in that plan. It appears a few verses later, when Jacob chose a present for Esau and sent it ahead "every drove by itself," with "some distance between successive droves" (32:16), hoping to appease his brother before he saw his face (32:20). One plan tried to limit losses, and the other tried to buy peace. Christ keeps His flock gathered as one and loses none of it: "they shall never perish; neither shall anyone snatch them out of My hand" (John 10:28). Where Jacob sent his droves ahead of him to face his brother, Jesus goes before His own sheep Himself (John 10:4). This contrast is an analogy drawn from Jacob's story rather than something Genesis teaches, but it fits what Jesus says.

For anyone entrusted with people, Proverbs 27:23 still applies: "Be diligent to know the state of your flocks, and attend to your herds." Knowing the condition of a flock means being close to it, noticing who is limping and who has strayed. Parents, pastors, and leaders cannot do that from a distance, and Peter calls elders to shepherd in the same way (1 Peter 5:2–3).

Malachi asks every worshiper what he is keeping back. Everything in our "flock" came from God. Offering Him the leftovers while saving the strong and healthy for ourselves tells Him He is a small king. He answers, "I am a great King."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 29:2–3, 8 — Flocks gather at the well where Jacob meets Rachel. The shepherds tell Jacob that they cannot water the sheep until all the flocks are gathered and the stone is rolled away (29:8). When Rachel arrives, Jacob himself rolls the stone away and waters her father's flock (Genesis 29:10).

  • Judges 5:16 — Deborah's song rebukes Reuben for staying among the sheepfolds, listening to shepherds pipe for the flocks, while others went to battle.

  • Job 24:2 — "They seize flocks violently and feed on them." Stealing a flock is listed among the crimes that rob the vulnerable of their living.

  • Jeremiah 51:23 — God pictures His "battle-ax," His instrument of judgment, breaking in pieces "the shepherd and his flock." The next verse separately names Babylon as the one God will repay (Jeremiah 51:24). In that judgment, no shepherd can protect his animals.

  • Song of Solomon 6:5–6 — The lover repeats the flock images from chapter 4. His delight in her has not faded.
