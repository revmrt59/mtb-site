AVITH - (ʻĂvîyth, Strong's H5762)

Summary

Avith was a city in the land of Edom, the nation descended from Esau. It appears only twice in Scripture, both times as "his city" of Hadad the son of Bedad, one of the early kings who "reigned in the land of Edom before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31, 35; 1 Chronicles 1:46). The meaning of the name is uncertain. Strong's and Smith's Bible Dictionary give the gloss "ruins," and older dictionaries proposed "wicked" or "perverse," but neither meaning can be confirmed. The city's exact location is also unknown.

Avith's importance lies in where Moses places it. Edom had a working monarchy, royal towns, and military campaigns while Israel still had no king at all. The list names several different localities for its kings, and Avith is named only for Hadad's reign. Many read this as a moving royal seat. Esau's line reached worldly strength before Israel had a king. God's promise of kings to Jacob's family moved more slowly toward a chosen city and, in the end, toward the everlasting throne of Christ.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Avith is a place: the royal city of Hadad son of Bedad, fourth in the list of Edomite kings (Genesis 36:35; repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:46). No other person or place in Scripture bears this name. This Hadad should not be confused with Hadar, a later king in the same list (Genesis 36:39), or with Hadad the Edomite who opposed Solomon (1 Kings 11:14).

The name's meaning is uncertain. Strong's and Smith's Bible Dictionary give "ruins," linking the name to a Hebrew root associated with overturning or ruin; the NAS lexicon calls its origin uncertain, and the Brown-Driver-Briggs entry gives the location without that derivation. Older works such as Hitchcock's dictionary give "wicked" or "perverse," drawing on a root meaning to bend or twist. Scripture offers no wordplay on the name, and it should not be treated as a comment on the city's character. Its location is lost. It was a city in Edom, whose homeland included Seir southeast of the Dead Sea, but its exact site is unknown.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Avith enters the record only through its king: "And when Husham died, Hadad the son of Bedad, who attacked Midian in the field of Moab, reigned in his place. And the name of his city was Avith" (Genesis 36:35). That one line shows Hadad attacking Midian in the territory of Moab, an example of regional conflict among Israel's neighbors. The text does not date the campaign beyond placing it before Israel had kings. When Hadad died, Samlah of Masrekah succeeded him (Genesis 36:36), and Avith is not named again, though that does not tell us the city ceased to exist.

The list shows no father-to-son succession. Bela ruled from Dinhabah, Jobab came from Bozrah, Husham from the land of the Temanites (a region), and Hadad from Avith (Genesis 36:32–35); later, Baal-Hanan has no city named. Many interpreters infer from this that early Edom's kingship was not hereditary and that the seat of power moved with each ruler. The Chronicler repeated the record centuries later, after the exile (1 Chronicles 1:46).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that these kings reigned "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). God had already promised Jacob that "kings shall come from your body" (Genesis 35:11). Yet Esau's descendants had kings and capitals first. This fits what Isaac foretold for Esau, a life of strength by the sword (Genesis 27:39–40). God gave Edom real land and real rulers outside the chosen line.

The meaning is an inference, but a well-supported one. The several localities named suggest that Edom's royal seat moved from place to place, though the list does not narrate every change. Nothing in the list points to a city God chose or a dynasty He promised to establish. Israel's king came later, but God eventually tied His promise to David's house and to Zion (2 Samuel 7:12–16).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never mentions Avith. It does develop the larger contrast between Esau and Jacob. Paul cites God's word before the twins were born, "The older shall serve the younger" (Romans 9:12), to show that God's purpose stands "not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11). Hebrews warns against being "like Esau, who for one morsel of food sold his birthright" (Hebrews 12:16).

Avith belongs to that story as a small piece of the early, visible success of Esau's line. The connection is thematic. Scripture does not make the city a type. Still, the pattern is clear. The kingdom that came first appears to have shifted from place to place, while the promised King from Jacob's line now reigns on a throne that will have no end.

5. Spirit & Life

Israelites reading this list knew that Edom had crowned kings while their own ancestors were still waiting on God's promise. Delay did not mean God had forgotten them. He gave Edom its kings in their time, and He kept His word to Jacob in His own time. Believers who see the world organize, prosper, and rule quickly can take courage from this. God's kingdom may come more slowly, but it does not pass to another seat when its King dies.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:31 — Dates the Edomite kings "before any king reigned over the children of Israel."

  • Genesis 36:32–36 — Places Avith within a list of kings associated with several different localities.

  • Genesis 36:35; 1 Chronicles 1:46 — The only two occurrences: Avith as Hadad's city and his attack on Midian in Moab.

  • Genesis 27:39–40; 35:11 — Isaac's word to Esau and God's promise of kings to Jacob.

  • Romans 9:10–13; Hebrews 12:16–17 — The New Testament's interpretation of Esau and Jacob.
