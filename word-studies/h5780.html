UZ - (ʻÛwts, Strong's H5780)

Summary

Uz is the name of three men in the biblical genealogies and of a land east of Israel. The land is by far the most important. The first is a grandson of Shem through Aram (Genesis 10:23; 1 Chronicles 1:17). The second is the firstborn son of Abraham's brother Nahor (Genesis 22:21). The third is a son of Dishan, one of the Horite clan chiefs of Seir, later the territory of Edom (Genesis 36:28; 1 Chronicles 1:42). The land of Uz is best known as the home of Job, and the prophets later name it in their oracles of judgment (Jeremiah 25:20; Lamentations 4:21). The meaning of the name is uncertain. Scholars have proposed several possibilities, but none is established, and Scripture never builds anything on the name's meaning.

The land of Uz matters because of what God did there. Scripture opens Job's story with the words "There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job" (Job 1:1). The man God held up as having "none like him on the earth" (Job 1:8) lived outside Canaan, apart from Israel's priesthood and temple. In Uz the Lord tested His servant, answered him out of the whirlwind, and restored him. Later, in Jeremiah's day, the land of Uz appears among the nations made to drink the cup of God's wrath (Scripture does not date Job's own life). That double portrait shows a God who seeks and delights in true faith wherever it is found, and who holds every land accountable. The cup that passed to Uz and Edom also points ahead to the cup Jesus accepted for His people.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name ʻÛwts (Strong's H5780) names three men and a land, whose histories should be distinguished:

  • Uz, son of Aram. A grandson of Shem listed in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:23). First Chronicles uses a condensed list that places Uz and his brothers directly after Aram among Shem's descendants (1 Chronicles 1:17).

  • Uz, son of Nahor. The firstborn of Abraham's brother Nahor and his wife Milcah (Genesis 22:20–21). The NKJV, like the KJV, spells this name "Huz" in Genesis 22:21, but the Hebrew name is the same.

  • Uz, son of Dishan. A descendant of "Seir the Horite who inhabited the land" (Genesis 36:20, 28; 1 Chronicles 1:42), from the people who lived in Seir before and alongside Esau's descendants (Deuteronomy 2:12, 22).

  • The land of Uz. Job's homeland (Job 1:1), later ruled by several kings (Jeremiah 25:20) and connected with "the daughter of Edom" (Lamentations 4:21).

The name's meaning is uncertain. Suggestions include "wooded," "counsel," and Arabic-based ideas such as "soft, sandy soil." These are older proposals found in Bible dictionaries (such as Smith's and Hitchcock's), not a settled derivation. The Bible offers no wordplay on the name. Readers should not build a spiritual message on any of these guesses.

The land's location is also debated. One view looks north toward Aram (Syria), based on Uz the son of Aram. Much of the biblical evidence points south and southeast, to the desert regions east of the Dead Sea near Edom and northern Arabia. Lamentations 4:21 links Uz directly with the daughter of Edom. A Horite named Uz lived in Seir. Some of Job's friends are plausibly associated with this region: Eliphaz is a Temanite, and Teman is an Edomite name (Genesis 36:11; Jeremiah 49:7), while Bildad is a Shuhite, perhaps descended from Shuah, the son Abraham sent "to the country of the east" (Genesis 25:2, 6). Jeremiah lists Uz separately from Edom (Jeremiah 25:20–21), so the two were associated but not identical. Which ancestor, if any, gave the land its name cannot be determined.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Land of Uz

Home of Job. The book of Job introduces Uz without explanation. The text simply says, "There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job; and that man was blameless and upright, and one who feared God and shunned evil" (Job 1:1). Job was "the greatest of all the people of the East" (Job 1:3), a wealthy herdsman whose world was exposed to raiders: Sabeans attacked his oxen and Chaldeans took his camels (Job 1:15, 17). He acted as a family priest, offering sacrifices for his household. After his children's feasts, he would "rise early in the morning and offer burnt offerings according to the number of them all," because, as he said, "It may be that my sons have sinned and cursed God in their hearts" (Job 1:5).

In Uz, Job lost his children, his wealth, and his health. Friends arrived from neighboring lands to mourn with him and then to argue with him (Job 2:11). The youngest speaker, Elihu, is called "the Buzite" (Job 32:2). Buz is the name of the brother of Nahor's son Uz (Genesis 22:21). That detail fits the book's setting among the eastern kin of Abraham's family, though the text does not say that these clans descend from those exact men.

Finally the LORD answered Job "out of the whirlwind" (Job 38:1). Through four chapters He displayed His wisdom in creation. Job was humbled, his friends were rebuked, and his fortunes were restored (Job 42:7–10). Scripture does not date Job's life. The patriarchal feel of the book, with a father offering sacrifices and wealth counted in flocks, suggests an early setting, but that is an inference.

Under the cup of wrath. In Jeremiah 25 the prophet is told, "Take this wine cup of fury from My hand," and make the nations drink it (Jeremiah 25:15). The list begins with Jerusalem and Judah (25:18). It then moves through Egypt, "all the mixed multitude, all the kings of the land of Uz, all the kings of the land of the Philistines," and on to Edom, Moab, Ammon, and others, including Dedan, Tema, and Buz (25:19–23). The phrase "all the kings" suggests that Uz was a group of tribal chiefdoms rather than a single kingdom. That would match the chieftain society the book of Job seems to describe, though the connection is an inference.

Linked with Edom. After Jerusalem fell, Lamentations turns on the people who celebrated its ruin: "Rejoice and be glad, O daughter of Edom, You who dwell in the land of Uz! The cup shall also pass over to you And you shall become drunk and make yourself naked" (Lamentations 4:21). The invitation to rejoice is bitter irony. Edom's gloating over Judah's disaster is condemned elsewhere (Psalm 137:7; Obadiah 10–14). The next verse sets the two daughters side by side: Zion's punishment will come to an end, while Edom's iniquity will be punished (Lamentations 4:22).

The Men Named Uz

Uz the son of Aram appears only in genealogy (Genesis 10:23; 1 Chronicles 1:17). He belongs to the spreading of the nations after the flood. Uz the son of Nahor was Abraham's nephew and the firstborn of a family that stayed in Mesopotamia. His brother Buz and his relatives Kemuel and Bethuel are listed with him, and Bethuel was Rebekah's father (Genesis 22:20–23). Uz the son of Dishan belongs to the Horite clans of Seir (Genesis 36:28; 1 Chronicles 1:42). Scripture records nothing more about any of the three. The repetition of the name across these peoples may reflect shared customs and settlement, or simply a common Semitic name. The text does not say which.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The plain statements of Scripture carry the main weight. A God-fearing man lived in Uz. The LORD called him "My servant Job" and said "there is none like him on the earth" (Job 1:8). God spoke to him from the whirlwind. Later, God sent the cup of His fury to the kings of that same land.

From these facts some careful implications follow. Outside Israel's covenant institutions and outside the land promised to Abraham, the true God was known, feared, and worshiped. (Many place Job before Sinai, but that early setting is a proposal, not a stated date.) Job had no tabernacle and no Levitical priest, yet his sacrifices and prayers mattered to God, and God accepted his prayer for his friends (Job 42:8–9). The LORD's attention was never confined to one nation's borders. Set side by side in the canon, Job's story and Jeremiah's oracle also warn that one righteous resident does not secure a land's future. Scripture does not describe what happened in Uz between Job's day and Jeremiah's. By the end of Judah's kingdom, however, Uz is listed among the nations under judgment and is tied to Edom's malice. God judges each generation and people by its response to Him, not by its past reputation.

Jeremiah's list also includes Judah, and in fact begins with Jerusalem. The LORD says, "I begin to bring calamity on the city which is called by My name" (Jeremiah 25:29). Uz was not judged because it was foreign, and Judah was not spared because it was chosen. The same holy God weighs them all.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Two connections are grounded in Scripture.

The first is the cup. The prophets repeatedly describe God's wrath as a cup that the guilty must drink (Jeremiah 25:15–28; Lamentations 4:21). Isaiah adds a turn of mercy: the LORD takes "the cup of trembling" out of His people's hand (Isaiah 51:17, 22). In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed, "if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me," and then yielded to His Father's will (Matthew 26:39). Uz is not a type of Christ. Still, the cup Uz was made to drink belongs to the same biblical line of imagery that comes to its height when the Son of God drinks the cup of judgment so that others need not.

The second concerns Job. James tells the church, "You have heard of the perseverance of Job and seen the end intended by the Lord—that the Lord is very compassionate and merciful" (James 5:11). The man from Uz becomes an example for Christian believers. His story also fits, as a thematic resonance rather than a stated fulfillment, with Jesus' words: "other sheep I have which are not of this fold; them also I must bring" (John 10:16). Long before the gospel went to the nations, God had a faithful servant in the land of Uz.

5. Spirit & Life

When God wanted to show what true faith looks like, He pointed to a man in Uz. Job had no special location or religious institution to rely on. What he had was a life of fearing God and turning from evil, kept up in ordinary habits like rising early to pray and sacrifice for his children. God noticed. He still notices faithfulness that no one else sees, wherever His people happen to live.

Uz also brought Job into his deepest trouble, and there God came to him. The whirlwind did not answer every question Job had asked, but it gave him God Himself, and that was enough for Job to say, "now my eye sees You" (Job 42:5). Believers who suffer far from comfort and familiar support can take courage from the fact that God's presence is not limited to the places where we expect it.

The juxtaposition in Scripture is a sober warning. The land remembered as Job's home later appears among nations under judgment and is tied to Edom's rejoicing over another people's ruin, though Scripture records no history in between. No family, church, or nation can live on a godly past. Each generation must turn to God for itself, and pride over the downfall of others invites the same cup to pass to us. Thanks be to God, Christ drank that cup for all who come to Him.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:23; 1 Chronicles 1:17: Uz, a descendant of Shem through Aram.

  • Genesis 22:20–21: Uz, the firstborn of Nahor, Abraham's brother, with his brother Buz.

  • Genesis 36:20, 28; 1 Chronicles 1:42: Uz, son of Dishan, among the Horites of Seir.

  • Job 1:1, 3, 5, 8: The land of Uz as Job's home; his faith, his priestly care for his family, and God's testimony about him.

  • Job 2:11; 32:2; 38:1; 42:7–10: Job's eastern visitors, God's answer from the whirlwind, and Job's restoration.

  • Jeremiah 25:15–29: "All the kings of the land of Uz" among the nations given the cup of God's wrath, a judgment that begins with Jerusalem.

  • Lamentations 4:21–22: Uz linked with the daughter of Edom, whose gloating will be answered by judgment.

  • Matthew 26:39; Isaiah 51:17, 22: The cup of wrath taken from God's people and accepted by Christ.

  • James 5:11: Job's perseverance and the Lord's compassion and mercy.
