AWAKE, STIR UP, ROUSE - (ʻûwr, Strong's H5782)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʻûwr means to wake up, to rouse, or to stir into action. It describes a sleeper coming awake, a warrior springing into battle, a love that should not be stirred before its time, and an eagle stirring its nest. Behind its many uses lies one idea: something at rest, dormant, or idle is roused and set in motion.

Scripture's most striking uses put God at the center of that movement. The LORD stirs up the spirit of a Persian king to send exiles home, and He stirs up discouraged builders to finish His house. His suffering people cry for Him to "awake," and He answers by calling them to awake as well. Isaiah's Servant is awakened by the Lord "morning by morning" to hear His voice. In Zechariah's most solemn use, the LORD of hosts calls His own sword to awake against His Shepherd, words Jesus applied to Himself on the night He was betrayed. God is the great Awakener. His stirring moves history, renews His people, and in Christ reaches all the way to the cross.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻûwr is a Hebrew verb used across the Old Testament, though most heavily in poetry and prophecy. Isaiah, the Psalms, the Song of Solomon, and Job account for more than half its uses. It appears in battle songs, prayers of lament, love poetry, prophetic visions, and the historical books that describe Israel's return from exile. The NKJV translates it with a wide range of English words, including "awake," "stir up," "raise," "rouse," "lift up," and "move." That range reflects one basic movement, from rest into alert action, applied to many settings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning of ʻûwr is to become awake or to make someone or something awake and active. Three senses stand out:

1. To wake from sleep. Zechariah says the angel "wakened me, as a man who is wakened out of his sleep" (Zechariah 4:1).

2. To rouse oneself to act. This sense usually appears as a summons, as when Deborah is told to awake and sing (Judges 5:12) or the psalmist cries to God, "Awake! Why do You sleep, O Lord?" (Psalm 44:23).

3. To stir up someone or something else. The object may be a person's spirit (Ezra 1:1), a nation (Isaiah 13:17), an emotion such as love or strife (Song of Solomon 2:7; Proverbs 10:12), a nest (Deuteronomy 32:11), or a weapon raised for battle (2 Samuel 23:18).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of ʻûwr depends heavily on who does the waking and who is woken. When God is the subject, the word describes His initiative: He rouses kings, armies, and His own people's hearts. When people address God with the word, it becomes a prayer for Him to act. When God addresses His people with it, it becomes a call to rise out of defeat. The word also appears in human relationships. Proverbs 10:12 says hatred stirs up strife. The Song of Solomon three times warns the daughters of Jerusalem, "Do not stir up nor awaken love until it pleases" (Song of Solomon 2:7; see 3:5; 8:4). The same Hebrew verb stands behind both "stir up" and "awaken" in that refrain, which makes the warning doubly emphatic: love is powerful, and it is not to be roused before its rightful time.

One grammatical pattern carries real weight. The command is often doubled: "Awake, awake." Deborah hears it four times in a single verse (Judges 5:12). Isaiah uses the doubled form three times in quick succession (Isaiah 51:9, 17; 52:1). In Hebrew, repeating a command signals urgency. The speaker is shaking the hearer, not making a mild suggestion.

4. Theological Significance

ʻûwr shows that God is the One who sets things in motion. Nations do not rise by accident. "Behold, I will stir up the Medes against them," God says of Babylon (Isaiah 13:17). Through Jeremiah He promises, "I will raise and cause to come up against Babylon an assembly of great nations from the north country" (Jeremiah 50:9). God even stirred up the spirit of the Assyrian king Pul to carry the northern tribes into captivity (1 Chronicles 5:26). The empires of the ancient world thought they marched by their own ambition. Scripture says the Lord roused them, sometimes to discipline His own people and sometimes to bring down the proud.

The same God welcomes His people's appeal for Him to rouse Himself. Psalm 121:4 says plainly that He "shall neither slumber nor sleep," and the psalmists knew it. Yet they still cry, "Awake! Why do You sleep, O Lord? Arise! Do not cast us off forever" (Psalm 44:23; see also 7:6; 35:23; 59:4). This is the honest language of people who trust God enough to protest His apparent silence. Scripture preserves these prayers, which shows that God receives bold, urgent pleading from people who appeal to His past faithfulness and ask Him to act again.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Ezra 1:1, 5 and Haggai 1:14 — God stirs the human spirit. The return from exile began inside a pagan king's heart: "the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia, so that he made a proclamation" (Ezra 1:1; repeated in 2 Chronicles 36:22). The Lord's stirring was the means by which "the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah might be fulfilled." God then moved the exiles themselves: those "whose spirits God had moved" rose up to go and build (Ezra 1:5). Years later the work had stalled. Haggai preached, and "the LORD stirred up the spirit of Zerubbabel... and the spirit of Joshua... and the spirit of all the remnant of the people; and they came and worked on the house of the LORD" (Haggai 1:14). In each case the decisive change happened inside people before anything changed outside them. God did more than arrange circumstances. He awakened wills.

Isaiah 51:9–52:1 — The cry turned around. Isaiah cries out, "Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD! Awake as in the ancient days" (Isaiah 51:9). The prophet recalls God's victories of old, when He "cut Rahab apart." God answers with the same words aimed in the opposite direction: "Awake, awake! Stand up, O Jerusalem, You who have drunk at the hand of the LORD the cup of His fury" (Isaiah 51:17). Then He calls Zion to awake and put on her own strength and beautiful garments (Isaiah 52:1). The people thought God was the one asleep. God shows that Jerusalem lay in a stupor of defeat. His people were not to wait passively. They were to rise and clothe themselves in the strength He was giving.

Isaiah 50:4 — The Servant awakened each morning. In contrast to the crashing stirrings of empires, the Servant speaks of a quiet, daily waking: "He awakens Me morning by morning, He awakens My ear to hear as the learned." The word appears twice in this one line. The purpose follows from the earlier part of the verse: the Lord gives Him "the tongue of the learned, that I should know how to speak a word in season to him who is weary." What the Servant hears each morning becomes what He says to the weary. The verses that follow describe Him giving His back to those who struck Him and not hiding His face from shame (Isaiah 50:5–6). His endurance in suffering grows out of His daily listening.

Zechariah 13:7 — The sword awakened against the Shepherd. This is the most startling use of the word in Scripture. The LORD of hosts says, "Awake, O sword, against My Shepherd, against the Man who is My Companion... Strike the Shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered." Elsewhere God rouses swords against wicked nations. Here He rouses the sword against the One who stands closest to Him, "My Companion." On the night before His crucifixion, Jesus quoted this verse about Himself: "All of you will be made to stumble because of Me this night, for it is written: 'I will strike the Shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered'" (Matthew 26:31; see Mark 14:27). The striking of the Shepherd was not a failure of God's plan. God Himself summoned that sword.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

ʻûwr traces a storyline that leads to Jesus. Through the Old Testament God's people cried for the arm of the LORD to awake and save them as in the days of old (Isaiah 51:9). A few chapters later Isaiah asks, "to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?" (Isaiah 53:1), and the answer is a suffering Servant. The connection is Isaiah's own sequence, not a statement in the New Testament, but it fits the pattern: God's awakened arm came in the form of the Servant who could say of the Father, "He awakens Me morning by morning," and who did not turn away from the blows that followed.

Zechariah 13:7, with Jesus' own application of it, takes the story to its depth. The God whom the psalmists begged to awake did awake, and His sword fell on His own Shepherd. Zechariah traces what follows: the Shepherd is struck, the sheep are scattered, and a remnant is brought "through the fire" and refined until they call on God's name and He says, "This is My people" (Zechariah 13:7–9). Isaiah 53 states plainly why the Servant suffered: "He was wounded for our transgressions," and "the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:5–6). Jesus did not need to be shaken out of rebellion or stupor as Zion did. He lived in willing, daily attention to His Father, and He went to the cross knowing whose sword was awakened against Him. Through His suffering, God's stirring of history, from Cyrus to the rebuilt temple, reaches its goal: a people gathered back to God by a Shepherd who was struck for them.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 50:4 gives every believer a picture of life with God. Jesus' ministry to the weary grew from ears opened each morning by His Father. Disciples are not sustained by occasional dramatic experiences alone. They need the quiet, repeated awakening that lets them hear before they speak. A believer who wants to offer "a word in season" to a tired friend must first be a listener.

Zechariah 4:1 adds that this hearing is God's gift. Zechariah says the angel "wakened me, as a man who is wakened out of his sleep." He compares the moment to being roused from sleep, and only then was he ready to see what God was showing him. Spiritual perception does not come from natural alertness alone. God must open eyes and ears, and believers can ask Him to do so.

Deuteronomy 32:11 shows that God's awakening is sometimes uncomfortable. The eagle "stirs up its nest," but the same verse continues with hovering, spreading out its wings, taking up its young, and carrying them. The disturbance and the carrying belong to one act of care. When God unsettles the comfortable life of His people, He has not let go of them. He is moving them toward a stronger trust in Him.

Isaiah 51–52 adds a needed word of honesty. When we cry for God to wake up, we may find that He is calling us to wake up. Haggai's builders learned the same thing: the Lord stirred their spirits, and then "they came and worked." Prayer for revival and obedience to God's stirring belong together.

Finally, the Song's refrain teaches restraint. Not everything should be awakened by force. Love has its proper time, and those who wait honor the way God designed it. Patience, too, is a response to God's timing.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Judges 5:12 — Deborah is summoned four times to awake and sing, and Barak to arise. The repeated cry rouses Israel's leaders to celebrate God's victory.

Job 14:12 — Job describes the dead lying down and not being roused from their sleep while the heavens remain. The word measures how final death seemed to him, and it makes later hope of resurrection all the more striking.

Psalm 57:8 — David tells his own soul, his harp, and his lyre to awake, and says he will awaken the dawn. Here a man stirs himself to praise instead of waiting for the right mood (repeated in Psalm 108:2).

Isaiah 64:7 — The prophet laments that no one stirs himself up to take hold of God. The verse shows that spiritual sleep can belong to God's people.

Habakkuk 2:19 — Woe is pronounced on anyone who tells a piece of wood to awake. An idol cannot be roused, unlike the living God, who awakens nations and hearts.

Joel 3:9, 12 — The nations are told to wake up their mighty men and be roused for the valley of decision. Their mobilization ends in God's judgment.
