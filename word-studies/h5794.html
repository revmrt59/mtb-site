STRONG, FIERCE, MIGHTY - (ʻaz, Strong's H5794)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻaz describes force that is hard to resist. It can mean a strong people or a fortified border, a strong east wind, deep waters, or a lion. It can also describe something harsh in a person: fierce anger, a hard face, a rough answer, an appetite that never has enough. The word itself does not decide whether that strength is good or evil. Everything depends on who holds it and how it is used.

Scripture uses ʻaz to show that every strength in creation answers to God. David's "strong enemy" could not stand against the Lord who rescued him (Psalm 18:17). The mighty waters of the Red Sea parted at His word, and the proud strength of nations is brought low. The word reaches its highest point in Song of Solomon 8:6: "love is as strong as death." That line points to a love that holds as firmly as death holds its captives. In Christ, God's love met death and overcame it.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻAz is a Hebrew adjective. It appears in the Law, the historical books, the Psalms, wisdom literature, and the prophets. It describes things that press hard against whatever stands in their way: armies, cities, animals, weather, water, and the passions of the human heart. English translations need several words to carry its range. The NKJV most often uses "strong," but it also uses "mighty," "fierce," "fortified," "roughly," and once, "greedy." That spread of translations shows how one Hebrew idea of unyielding force can show up in a battlefield, a storm, or a person's temper.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, ʻaz means strong in the sense of forceful, firm, and hard to resist. Context decides which side of that strength is in view.

1. Strong or mighty in power. This sense describes people, nations, animals, and natural forces that overpower whatever opposes them. Examples include the strong inhabitants of Canaan (Numbers 13:28), the "strong east wind" (Exodus 14:21), and the lion in Samson's riddle (Judges 14:18).

2. Fierce, harsh, or hard. This sense describes a disposition or manner that refuses to soften. Examples include the cruel anger of Simeon and Levi (Genesis 49:7), "a nation of fierce countenance" (Deuteronomy 28:50), and the rich man who answers the poor "roughly" (Proverbs 18:23).

3. Intense in inner force. This sense describes a drive or bond within a person that will not let go. Isaiah calls corrupt leaders "greedy dogs" (Isaiah 56:11). The Hebrew phrase behind "greedy" means strong of appetite: their hunger is the strong thing. The same intensity, turned to good, describes love in Song of Solomon 8:6.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When ʻaz describes an enemy, it usually stresses how helpless the threatened person is. The Psalms speak of "the mighty" who "gather against me" (Psalm 59:3), and David praises God for rescue from his "strong enemy" (Psalm 18:17). When it describes nature, the strength belongs to wind and water that only God can command. When it describes the human face or temper, it nearly always carries a warning.

Genesis 49 shows the range of the word within a single chapter. Jacob calls his firstborn Reuben the excellence of his power (49:3), yet that strength did not keep Reuben from losing his place. A few verses later Jacob curses the anger of Simeon and Levi because it was fierce (49:7). Strength appears in Jacob's blessing as both a gift and a danger.

The word can also be negated. Proverbs 30:25 calls ants "a people not strong," and the point of the proverb depends on that lack of strength.

4. Theological Significance

The uses of ʻaz teach that God alone owns strength. The spies looked at Canaan and saw that its people were strong (Numbers 13:28). They let that fact outweigh everything God had already done in Egypt. The prophets answer that fear. The Lord "rains ruin upon the strong, So that fury comes upon the fortress" (Amos 5:9). Through Ezekiel He says, "I will cause the pomp of the strong to cease" (Ezekiel 7:24). Isaiah adds an unexpected outcome. After God's judgment, the strong peoples themselves come to glorify Him (Isaiah 25:3). God humbles human strength so that the proud will finally honor the One whose strength is real.

The word also shows what strength becomes without righteousness. A cluster of passages uses ʻaz for a fierce face. Moses warned that a disobedient Israel would face "a nation of fierce countenance, which does not respect the elderly nor show favor to the young" (Deuteronomy 28:50). Isaiah foretold that Egypt would be handed to "a cruel master, And a fierce king will rule over them" (Isaiah 19:4). Daniel saw a coming king "Having fierce features, Who understands sinister schemes" (Daniel 8:23). In each case, strength without mercy turns into cruelty toward the weak. God allows these fierce powers to rise as instruments of judgment. Then He brings them down as well, because their strength was never their own.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 8:6 — love as strong as death. The bride asks, "Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm; For love is as strong as death." Death is the one power no one escapes. It takes kings and slaves alike, and it never gives back what it holds. The parallel line makes the same point: "Jealousy as cruel as the grave." Here jealousy means love's fierce refusal to share or surrender its beloved. The Song declares that true love grips with the same unbreakable hold. A seal on the heart and arm marks something as belonging to someone, permanently. The next verse adds that many waters cannot quench love (8:7). This is the same image of overwhelming water that ʻaz describes elsewhere. In this one place, the word that so often describes fierce anger and crushing armies describes the strength of love.

Exodus 14:21 with Nehemiah 9:11 and Isaiah 43:16 — strong wind, mighty waters. At the Red Sea, "the LORD caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind all that night." The wind was strong, but the Lord sent it and directed it. Later Israel remembered that God threw their persecutors into the deep "As a stone into the mighty waters" (Nehemiah 9:11). Isaiah, speaking to exiles, names God as the One "who makes a way in the sea And a path through the mighty waters" (Isaiah 43:16). The same waters that buried Pharaoh's army became a road for God's people. Isaiah uses that memory to promise a new deliverance from Babylon. God does not simply survive the forces that overwhelm His people. He commands them.

Psalm 18:17 (2 Samuel 22:18) — the strong enemy. David writes, "He delivered me from my strong enemy, From those who hated me, For they were too strong for me." In both versions of the song, ʻaz is the word behind "strong enemy." The closing words "too strong for me" translate a different Hebrew verb (Strong's H0553), so David states his enemy's strength and then confesses it a second time in other words. David was a warrior and a king, yet he does not hide that he was overmatched. The honesty of "too strong for me" makes the rescue clear. The deliverance was entirely God's, and David's praise depends on that confession.

Judges 14:14, 18 and Proverbs 30:25 — strength reversed. The Spirit of the Lord came on Samson, and he tore apart a lion. Later he found honey in its carcass and turned the scene into a riddle: "Out of the eater came something to eat, And out of the strong came something sweet." The Philistines gave their answer as a question: "What is stronger than a lion?" (14:18). Proverbs looks at strength from the opposite side. The ants are "a people not strong, Yet they prepare their food in the summer." The lion's strength ended up feeding someone else. The ants, who lack impressive strength, provide for themselves by wisdom.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of ʻaz moves toward Jesus. In the Gospels, Jesus describes His work with the image of a strong man: "No one can enter a strong man's house and plunder his goods, unless he first binds the strong man" (Mark 3:27). Mark was written in Greek, so the word there is different. The pattern, however, is the one David knew. An enemy too strong for us is overpowered by a stronger One. Paul says that at the cross Christ "disarmed principalities and powers" and triumphed over them (Colossians 2:15).

Jesus won that victory by becoming weak. Paul writes that "the weakness of God is stronger than men" (1 Corinthians 1:25). In Samson's riddle, sweetness came out of the strong. At the cross, the true sweetness of salvation came out of death itself, which had always been the great devourer.

Song of Solomon 8:6 finds its fullest echo here. The Song is about the love between a bride and her groom. Yet the whole Bible presents God's love for His people in the language of a husband's love. At Calvary that love went down into death and came out holding the keys. So death does not have the final word: "Death is swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). That is why Paul can list "death" first among the things that cannot "separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38–39).

7. Spirit & Life

These passages expose two kinds of strength at work in the human heart. One is the fierce strength of Simeon and Levi's anger and the hard face of oppressors. The other is the strength of appetite in Isaiah's shepherds, "greedy dogs Which never have enough," each one looking "for his own gain" (Isaiah 56:11). That inner hunger consumes everything and is never satisfied. Both are forms of strength that refuse to yield, and both destroy.

God offers a different way to be strong. Like the ants, believers are often a people not strong. Paul reminds the church that "not many mighty" were called (1 Corinthians 1:26). Yet God gives wisdom to prepare for what is coming, and His strength rests on those who stop pretending. The Lord told Paul, "My strength is made perfect in weakness," and Paul responded by boasting in his infirmities "that the power of Christ may rest upon me" (2 Corinthians 12:9). David's confession, "they were too strong for me," is a good place to begin praying. God does not ask us to match the strength of what threatens us. He asks us to trust the One whose love is as strong as death and stronger in the end.

The person who has been loved this way can lay down fierce anger and endless appetite. A heart sealed by the love of Christ has nothing left to grasp for.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 21:24 — Israel took the Amorite land up to the border of Ammon, which was "fortified." Even strong borders mark only the limits God allows.

  • Psalm 59:3 — "The mighty gather against me, Not for my transgression nor for my sin, O LORD." David faces strong enemies while innocent and brings his case to God.

  • Proverbs 18:23 — The poor man pleads, but the rich man answers "roughly." Here ʻaz describes hard, harsh speech toward someone who cannot answer back.

  • Proverbs 21:14 — A secret gift quiets anger, and a hidden bribe calms strong wrath. The proverb shows how volatile fierce anger can be.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H5797 (ʻoz), strength — a lexical relative. It is the noun form of the idea that ʻaz expresses as an adjective.

  • Canonical pairing: love and death in Song of Solomon 8:6 — the verse compares the strength of love with the strength of death. Romans 8:38–39 and 1 Corinthians 15:54 show that, in Christ, love prevails.
