GOAT, GOATS - (ʻêz, Strong's H5795)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻêz means goat. It refers to the ordinary goat of Israel's flocks, often the female, and sometimes the whole herd. Goats gave milk, meat, and hair for weaving. Because they were so common and so useful, the word appears wherever Israel's daily life appears: in a family's wealth, a gift, a meal for a guest, and a household's supply of milk.

Scripture also gives this ordinary animal some of its most serious assignments. In Genesis, goat meat, goat skins, and goat blood become tools of family deception. In Exodus, goat hair is woven into the tent that covered the tabernacle. In Leviticus and Numbers, the goat becomes a frequent sin offering, and on the Day of Atonement two goats are taken to deal with the sins of the whole nation. Daniel sees a charging goat that pictures the swift rise of Greece. Read together, these passages suggest a striking contrast: the animal people used to hide the truth is the animal God appointed to deal with sin honestly by sacrifice. That pattern points ahead to Jesus Christ, who entered God's presence "not with the blood of goats," as Hebrews puts it, but with His own blood.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Goats were everywhere in the biblical world. They thrived on rocky hillsides where cattle struggled, and a household could live on their milk (Proverbs 27:27). Their dark hair was spun into strong cloth for tents. A man's flocks of goats measured his wealth: Nabal owned "a thousand goats" (1 Samuel 25:2), and Jacob's prosperity under Laban was counted in speckled and spotted goats (Genesis 30:32–35).

Most uses of ʻêz fall in the Law of Moses, where goats are sacrificed, offered, or eaten. The rest appear in the stories of the patriarchs and the early kingdom, and in a few poetic and prophetic passages.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻêz means a goat, with no hidden or figurative meaning built into the word itself. Context decides whether it names a single animal, the herd, or a material taken from the animal:

1. The animal itself, as a single goat or a flock, as when Jacob sends Esau "two hundred female goats" (Genesis 32:14).

2. Goat hair used as a material, as in the "goats' hair" brought for the tabernacle (Exodus 25:4) or the "cover of goats' hair" Michal placed in David's bed (1 Samuel 19:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

One pattern matters for readers of English Bibles. The word often appears in phrases such as "a kid of the goats," meaning a young goat drawn from the herd. A related phrase in Daniel 8 is translated "a male goat." In these phrases ʻêz names the kind of animal, and another word specifies its age or sex. So when Leviticus 4:23 requires "a kid of the goats, a male without blemish," the law is specifying the animal precisely. God was not leaving the worshiper to guess what was acceptable.

The setting changes the goat's role. In family stories it is food, gift, or payment. In worship it is an offering "without blemish." In poetry it supplies a picture: the beloved's hair is like a flock of goats streaming down the slopes of Gilead (Song of Solomon 4:1; 6:5). In prophecy it becomes the symbol of an empire.

4. Theological Significance

In Israel's worship the goat appears most often as a sin offering, alongside its place in other offerings. When a ruler sinned unintentionally, he brought "a kid of the goats, a male without blemish" (Leviticus 4:23). A common person could bring a female goat (Leviticus 4:28) or, as an alternative, a female lamb (Leviticus 4:32). When the whole congregation failed without realizing it, they offered "one kid of the goats as a sin offering" (Numbers 15:24). The monthly offerings and the yearly feasts each included a goat for sin (Numbers 28:15, 30; 29:5–25). A goat was offered again and again throughout Israel's calendar, and each time it told Israel that sin, even unintentional sin, cannot be ignored before a holy God. It also told them that God Himself had provided a way to deal with it.

Numbers 7 makes the same point by repetition. For twelve days, twelve tribal leaders brought identical gifts for the dedication of the altar, and each included "one kid of the goats as a sin offering." Numbers records every list in full. The tribes differed in size and status, yet each leader stood before God with the same need and the same provision.

The goat also belonged at Passover. "You may take it from the sheep or from the goats" (Exodus 12:5). The animal we call "the Passover lamb" could be a young goat. On the night of judgment, what mattered was the blood on the doorposts, not which flock the animal came from.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 27, 37, and 38: goats and the family lie. Rebekah sent Jacob for "two choice kids of the goats" to make food "such as he loves" (Genesis 27:9), and she covered his smooth hands and neck with the goat skins (27:16). Jacob saw the danger clearly: "I shall seem to be a deceiver to him" (27:12). He went ahead anyway.

Years later, Jacob's sons dipped Joseph's tunic in the blood of a kid of the goats (37:31) and brought it to their father for him to identify. The man who once deceived his father with a goat was now deceived by his own sons with a goat.

Then Judah, one of those brothers, promised Tamar "a young goat from the flock" and left his signet, cord, and staff as a pledge (38:17). When she was condemned, she sent the pledge back with the words, "Please determine whose these are" (38:25). In Hebrew her request uses the same verb, meaning recognize, that the brothers used when they asked Jacob to identify the bloody tunic. Their request had led Jacob to accept a lie (37:33). Now Tamar asked Judah to recognize his own pledge, and he acknowledged the truth: "She has been more righteous than I" (38:26). The goat was only the promised payment. It was the pledge left in its place that identified Judah and brought him to confession.

Between these stories, Jacob sent Esau two hundred female goats as part of his gift on the way to meet the brother he had wronged (32:14). That gift was an open offering of peace, not a trick. In 1 Samuel 19:13, Michal used goats' hair to make a decoy in David's bed and saved the anointed king from Saul. Human schemes run through several of these accounts. Some are cruel, and some are desperate. Through these accounts God keeps His purposes moving and sometimes exposes what people hide, as He did with Judah.

Exodus 26: the goat-hair tent. The tabernacle's inner curtains were "fine woven linen and blue, purple, and scarlet thread; with artistic designs of cherubim" (Exodus 26:1). Over them went a tent of goats' hair (26:7), spun by the skilled women who offered their work willingly (35:26). Above the goat hair went two more coverings, of ram skins dyed red and of badger skins (26:14). Goat hair was valuable, but it was ordinary material compared with the embroidered cherubim beneath it. That beauty was seen from within, by those who drew near to serve.

Leviticus 16: two goats, one atonement. On the Day of Atonement the high priest took "two kids of the goats as a sin offering" from the congregation (16:5). One was killed, and its blood was carried behind the veil and sprinkled on the mercy seat (16:15). The high priest laid both hands on the head of the other goat and confessed over it all Israel's sins. Then he sent it away into the wilderness, where it would bear their sins to an uninhabited land (Leviticus 16:21–22). One goat shows that sin is paid for by a life given before God. The other shows that sin is carried away, far out of sight. Israel needed both pictures to understand one act of mercy.

Daniel 8: the swift goat. Daniel saw "a male goat" that "came from the west, across the surface of the whole earth, without touching the ground," with "a notable horn between his eyes" (8:5). It shattered the ram, which is identified as "the kings of Media and Persia" (8:20). At the height of the goat's strength the great horn was broken, and four horns grew in its place (8:8). Gabriel names the goat as Greece and the horn as its first king (8:21). Historians commonly match this to Alexander the Great's lightning conquests and the division of his empire. Long before any of it happened, God described the rise and fall of the empire in the plain image of a charging goat.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The goats of Israel's altar were never meant to be the final answer, and the New Testament says so directly. Christ entered the Most Holy Place "not with the blood of goats and calves, but with His own blood," and so obtained eternal redemption (Hebrews 9:12). Hebrews also says that the blood of bulls and goats could not take away sins (Hebrews 10:4). Seen in that light, every goat in Leviticus 4 and Numbers 28–29 was an honest admission that the work was not yet finished.

The two goats of Leviticus 16 find their meaning in Him together. Like the slain goat, His blood was presented before God. Like the goat sent away, He bore our sins and removed them, as far as the east is from the west (Psalm 103:12). On that day the body of the slain goat was carried outside the camp and burned (Leviticus 16:27). Hebrews draws the line directly: the bodies of the animals whose blood was brought into the sanctuary for sin were burned outside the camp, and so Jesus "suffered outside the gate" (Hebrews 13:11–12). The goat led away into the wilderness offers a further picture of the same movement away from the camp, though Hebrews does not mention it. The Passover permission in Exodus 12:5 belongs here too, because the blood that shelters God's people is fulfilled in Christ, our Passover (1 Corinthians 5:7).

The canon also turns the goat into a warning. Through Ezekiel, God says, "I shall judge between sheep and sheep, between rams and goats" (Ezekiel 34:17). God then rebukes the stronger members of the flock, who ate the good pasture and fouled the water the rest of the flock had to drink (Ezekiel 34:18–19), and He promises to judge "between the fat and the lean sheep" (Ezekiel 34:20). Ezekiel uses a different Hebrew word for the goats of verse 17, but the picture is the same flock. Jesus' words draw on the same picture when the Son of Man "will separate them one from another, as a shepherd divides his sheep from the goats" (Matthew 25:32). The goats on His left are those who did not feed, clothe, or visit "the least of these" (Matthew 25:45). In the Old Testament the goat could be a blameless offering. At the last judgment it stands for those who failed to care for the least. Scripture never explains why God chose the goat for sin offerings, so any link to the goat's stubborn nature is an analogy. The animals Ezekiel and Jesus describe, however, are identified by their own behavior. In Ezekiel the stronger members of the flock pushed and butted the weak ones (Ezekiel 34:21), an active harm. In Matthew the goats are condemned for what they left undone: they did not care for the least of Jesus' brethren.

7. Spirit & Life

Jacob went to his father covered in goat skin to receive a blessing under a false identity. God offers the opposite. In the tabernacle and on the altar, the goat became a covering that God Himself provided. Believers do not have to dress up as someone else to receive the Father's blessing. Christ has covered us honestly, by a sacrifice that faces sin instead of disguising it.

Judah's story shows how God deals with what we try to keep hidden. Judah told Tamar to wait until his son Shelah was grown, while privately fearing that Shelah would die (Genesis 38:11), and he later admitted, "I did not give her to Shelah my son" (Genesis 38:26). He also tried to settle the matter quietly through a friend sent with the goat to recover his pledge (Genesis 38:20–23). When his own pledge identified him, he had a choice, and he confessed. That confession marks a turning point in Judah's story. Later he offers himself in place of Benjamin (Genesis 44:33), and Judah becomes the tribe of the coming King. The account suggests that God often brings our hidden things back to us so that we can confess them and He can make something new of us.

The tabernacle's coverings teach us not to judge God's presence by what covers it. Glory can sit behind a plain exterior: a small congregation, an unremarkable life, a carpenter from Nazareth. Those who draw near see what passersby miss.

Finally, Ezekiel 34 and Matthew 25 search the heart. In Ezekiel the stronger members of the flock are judged for trampling the pasture and pushing the weak aside. In Matthew the goats are judged for failing to feed, clothe, and visit the least. Neither group is identified by its doctrine. Both are known by how they treated others in need. Those who have been covered by Christ's blood are called to become people who share the pasture, clean the water, and visit the prisoner, because they belong to the Shepherd.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 6:19 — Gideon prepared a young goat for the Angel of the LORD, and fire from the rock consumed it. His gift of hospitality became an offering God accepted.

  • Judges 13:15 — Manoah offered to prepare a young goat for his heavenly visitor, and he too learned that he was in God's presence.

  • 1 Samuel 16:20 — Jesse sent a young goat with David to Saul. The newly anointed shepherd entered the royal court carrying a gift from the flock.

  • 2 Chronicles 29:21 — Hezekiah's restoration of temple worship began with seven male goats as a sin offering for the kingdom and the sanctuary.

  • Ezekiel 43:22 — In Ezekiel's vision of the future temple, a kid of the goats without blemish cleanses the altar, carrying the sin offering into his picture of restored worship.

  • Proverbs 27:27 — Goats' milk is enough for the household and the maidservants. The steady care of a flock was one of the ordinary ways God fed His people.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H8163 (sa'ir), lexically distinct, often paired: Sa'ir means a male or hairy goat. It joins ʻêz in sin-offering phrases and names the goat sent away on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16). The same word is used for wilderness idols in Leviticus 17:7.

  • Greek eriphos and eriphion, thematic and canonical connection: Matthew uses eriphos for "the goats" the shepherd divides from his sheep (Matthew 25:32) and the related diminutive eriphion for the goats set on His left (25:33). The image carries forward from Israel's flocks, though the words are Greek.
