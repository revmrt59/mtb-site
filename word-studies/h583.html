ENOSH - (ʼĔnôwsh, Strong's H0583)

Summary

Enosh was the son of Seth and the grandson of Adam and Eve. He belongs to the third generation of the human story, before the flood. His name is the ordinary Hebrew word for "man," a word that often stresses human frailty and mortality, so it is usually understood as "mortal man." The connection to a root meaning "to be weak" is commonly proposed, though it is less certain than the plain sense "man." Genesis records only a few facts about him. He was born to Seth, he became the father of Cainan at ninety, he had other sons and daughters, and he died at 905 years old (Genesis 5:6–11). One sentence attached to his birth, however, has made his name memorable for every generation since: "Then men began to call on the name of the LORD" (Genesis 4:26).

That sentence comes immediately after Lamech's song of violent self-exaltation in Cain's line (Genesis 4:23–24). Genesis sets the two side by side. One family boasts of its own strength, and the other calls on the LORD. Enosh's line carried the promised "seed" God had appointed through Seth (Genesis 4:25), and Luke traces Jesus' ancestry back through him: "the son of Enosh, the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38). The Savior came through a family of mortal people who had learned to call on God's name.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

Enosh (Hebrew אֱנוֹשׁ) is a single individual: Seth's son, Adam's grandson, and the father of Cainan, who is called Kenan in some translations. The seven occurrences of this Strong's entry all refer to him. They appear in Genesis 4:26, Genesis 5:6–11, and 1 Chronicles 1:1. Luke 3:38 names the same man in Greek under a separately indexed entry (G1800). That verse belongs to the same person but is not counted among the seven Hebrew occurrences.

The name is spelled the same as the common Hebrew noun 'enosh (H0582), which means "man," "mankind," or "mortal man." That noun appears often in Old Testament poetry where human weakness is the point. Psalm 103:15 says that man's days are like grass, and Psalm 8:4 asks what man is that God should be mindful of him. Lexicons often connect the word to a root meaning "to be weak, frail, or sick." That derivation is probable but not certain. "Man" or "mortal man" is the safest statement of the meaning.

Genesis explains why Seth received his name (4:25). It gives no explanation for the name Enosh. Seth may have been acknowledging human mortality when he named his son, but that is an inference rather than a stated fact. It should not be treated as a summary of Enosh's life.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Enosh enters a world already marked by death. Abel had been murdered. Cain's descendants had built a city and developed new skills (Genesis 4:17–22). Their line reaches its low point when Lamech boasts to his wives that he has killed a man for wounding him and will be avenged seventy-sevenfold (4:23–24). Genesis then turns to Adam and Eve's new son. Eve says of Seth, "For God has appointed another seed for me instead of Abel, whom Cain killed" (4:25). Seth's son is Enosh, and with his birth the narrator notes that people began to call on the name of the LORD (4:26).

Genesis 5 places Enosh in the orderly genealogy from Adam to Noah. Seth fathered him at 105 (5:6). Enosh fathered Cainan at ninety, lived another 815 years, had sons and daughters, and died at 905 (5:9–11). The chapter's repeated refrain, "and he died," shows that God's word in Eden still stood, even over men who lived such long lives (compare 2:17; 3:19).

Centuries later, the Chronicler opens Israel's history with "Adam, Seth, Enosh" (1 Chronicles 1:1). Enosh appears there only as a name, but he is placed at the root of the story that leads to David and to Israel's worship. Luke later lists him among the ancestors of Jesus (Luke 3:38).

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states two things directly. Enosh belongs to Seth's line, which God appointed in place of Abel, and his lifetime marks the point at which people began to call on the LORD's name. The word "seed" in Eve's words echoes God's promise in Genesis 3:15 that the woman's seed would one day crush the serpent. That link is a strong inference from the way Genesis is written. Through Enosh, the line that carried that hope continued.

"Calling on the name of the LORD" later describes Abraham building altars and worshiping (Genesis 12:8) and Elijah appealing to God on Mount Carmel: "I will call on the name of the LORD; and the God who answers by fire, He is God" (1 Kings 18:24). The phrase involves prayer, worship, and open dependence on the God who has made Himself known. Genesis does not describe what this worship looked like in Enosh's day, and we should not read later tabernacle practices back into it. The wording "men began" suggests something shared among people rather than private devotion alone.

The covenant name LORD appears here even though Exodus 6:3 says the patriarchs did not know God by that name. Eve has already used the name in Genesis 4:1, so Genesis consistently presents the LORD of Moses' day as the same God whom the earliest believers called on. Some ancient Jewish interpreters read Genesis 4:26 negatively, as the beginning of idolatry. The surrounding contrast with Lamech supports the positive reading.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

Luke's genealogy runs backward from Jesus through David, Abraham, and Noah, then through "the son of Enosh, the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38). This is explicit New Testament testimony that Enosh stands in the ancestry of Christ. The Son of God entered the same mortal family whose frailty is written into its records.

Two further connections are thematic rather than stated outright. First, the Old Testament uses the word 'enosh in parallel with "son of man" (Psalm 8:4). Jesus, who called Himself "the Son of Man," fully shared the weakness of the humanity that word describes. Second, the practice that began in Enosh's day reaches its fullest promise in Joel 2:32, which Paul applies to faith in Christ in Romans 10:13: whoever calls on the name of the LORD will be saved. Calling on the name of the LORD began in Enosh's generation, and the same invitation still stands in the gospel.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Genesis places two responses to a broken world side by side. Lamech meets violence with louder violence and sings about his own strength. Seth's family begins to call on the LORD. Both families faced the same death and the same curse. The difference lay in where they looked for help.

Whatever Seth intended by the name, the word Enosh reminds every reader what we are: mortal, dependent, and made from dust. Scripture does not treat that weakness as a reason for despair. The psalmist who asks "What is man?" goes on to marvel at God's care (Psalm 8:4–5). People who know they are frail are ready to call on God, and God has never failed to answer those who call on His name in truth.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 4:23–26 – Lamech's boast contrasted with Seth's line; Enosh's birth and the beginning of calling on the LORD.

  • Genesis 4:25 – Seth as God's appointed "seed," connecting the line to Genesis 3:15.

  • Genesis 5:6–11 – Enosh's genealogy, lifespan, and death.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:1 – Enosh at the head of Israel's historical record.

  • Luke 3:38 – Enosh in the ancestry of Jesus Christ.

  • Genesis 12:8; 1 Kings 18:24 – Later uses of "calling on the name of the LORD."

  • Psalm 8:4; 103:15 – The common noun 'enosh describing mortal humanity.

  • Joel 2:32; Romans 10:13 – The promise to all who call on the LORD's name.
