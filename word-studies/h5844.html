WRAP, COVER, CLOTHE, VEIL - (ʻâṭâh, Strong's H5844)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʻâṭâh means to wrap, cover, or clothe, usually by drawing something around oneself like a cloak. Sometimes the covering is literal. Samuel appears "covered with a mantle," and a woman veils herself among the shepherds. Most of the time, though, Scripture uses the word as a picture. A person or thing becomes so fully surrounded by a condition that it serves as a garment, and everyone who looks can see it.

That picture becomes some of the most vivid language in the Old Testament. God covers Himself "with light as with a garment" (Psalm 104:2). When no one stands up to intercede for His people, He is "clad with zeal as a cloak" and comes to save them Himself (Isaiah 59:17). The wicked, by contrast, are wrapped in their own disgrace. Mourners and the unclean cover their lips. Even a dry valley of weeping can be covered with pools of rain. The word asks a searching question: what are you wrapped in? The gospel answers it, because those who were clothed in shame may now "put on Christ."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻâṭâh is a Hebrew verb that appears in Leviticus, 1 Samuel, Psalms, the Song of Solomon, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Micah. Psalms uses it more than any other book. Its setting is the everyday world of cloaks, mantles, and veils. These were garments a person wrapped around the body for warmth, modesty, mourning, or office. Because a wrapped garment covers the body and is the first thing others see, the verb readily became a way to describe identity and condition.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core idea is wrapping or covering, whether a garment drawn around the whole body or a covering over only part of it, such as the lips.

1. To wrap oneself in a garment or veil, or to cover part of the body. Examples are Samuel's mantle (1 Samuel 28:14) and the leper's covered mustache (Leviticus 13:45).

2. Figuratively, to be wrapped in a quality or condition, such as light, zeal, shame, or a curse (Psalm 104:2; Psalm 109:19).

3. To cover a surface, as rain covers a valley with pools (Psalm 84:6).

4. To grip or bundle up tightly, as in the NKJV's "surely seize" in Isaiah 22:17.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word shifts with whoever is doing the wrapping. When God wraps Himself, the garment reveals His glory and His purposes. When the wicked are wrapped, the garment is the shame their own conduct has earned. When God acts on nations or rulers, the wrapping becomes judgment, and it costs Him no effort.

One idiom needs explaining. Covering the mustache or lips was a recognized sign of grief, defilement, or shame. The leper in Leviticus 13:45 must "cover his mustache, and cry, 'Unclean! Unclean!'" Micah's false seers "shall all cover their lips; for there is no answer from God" (Micah 3:7). A covered mouth signaled a person cut off from fellowship, or a person with nothing left to say.

In Isaiah 22:17 the Hebrew repeats the verb for emphasis. The NKJV renders this doubled verb "surely seize." The repetition means the grip is certain and complete.

4. Theological Significance

This word shows that what covers a person reveals who that person is. God's garments are His own attributes. He never borrows light or zeal from outside Himself. When He is described as wrapped in them, Scripture is showing us His character made visible.

Human beings, by contrast, end up wearing what they have chosen. In Psalm 109 David prays that the man who loved cursing would find the curse clinging to him like clothing (Psalm 109:17–19). The prayer asks God for justice that fits the sin, handing the man over to the very thing he embraced.

Through all of this, God stays sovereign over the wrapping. He wraps Shebna for exile. He lets Nebuchadnezzar wrap himself in Egypt. He also wraps a valley of tears in rain. The verb covers judgment and mercy alike, and the Lord is the actor in both.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 104:2 and Isaiah 59:17: the garments of God. Psalm 104 praises the Creator "Who cover Yourself with light as with a garment, Who stretch out the heavens like a curtain." Before the psalm describes waters, mountains, and creatures, it shows God robed in radiance. The light of creation tells us something about the One who made it.

Isaiah 59 is set in a very different scene. Truth has fallen in the street, justice stands far off, and the Lord "saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor" (59:16). So He armed Himself. "He put on righteousness as a breastplate, And a helmet of salvation on His head; He put on the garments of vengeance for clothing, And was clad with zeal as a cloak" (59:17). The God who wears light in creation now wears zeal for rescue. Zeal is His burning commitment to His people and His name. In the moment of rescue, that zeal becomes the outer garment everyone can see. The movement from creation to redemption shows that God does more than shine. He also acts, and He acts alone when no one else will.

Psalm 109:19, 29: the curse that clings. David prays about a man who "loved cursing" (109:17). He asks, "Let it be to him like the garment which covers him, And for a belt with which he girds himself continually" (109:19). The prayer itself explains the image: David asks that the curse cling to the man like clothing. A garment goes with you everywhere, and a belt holds it tight. Later David asks that his accusers "cover themselves with their own disgrace as with a mantle" (109:29). The words "their own" matter. David asks God to act, yet the disgrace he prays for is woven from the accusers' own conduct.

Ezekiel 24:17, 22: grief left uncovered. God took Ezekiel's wife, "the desire of your eyes" (24:16), and then forbade the usual signs of grief: "do not cover your lips, and do not eat man's bread of sorrow" (24:17). People were stunned and asked why. God answered that when Jerusalem and its temple fell, Israel would do exactly as Ezekiel did and not cover their lips (24:22). The coming loss would be too overwhelming for ordinary mourning. The prophet's uncovered face, in a home that had lost its joy, became God's message to the nation.

Isaiah 22:17 and Jeremiah 43:12: wrapping as sovereign judgment. Shebna, the proud steward who carved himself a showy tomb, hears that the LORD "will surely seize you." The next verse says God will roll him up like a ball and toss him into a wide country (22:18, paraphrased). The man who built himself a monument is bundled up and thrown away. In Jeremiah 43:12, Nebuchadnezzar "shall array himself with the land of Egypt, as a shepherd puts on his garment." Egypt was the empire Judah trusted more than God, yet in this picture its conquest is as ordinary as a shepherd pulling on his cloak against the night air. Empires are small things in the Lord's hands.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah 59:16–17 sets up a pattern the gospel completes. No human intercessor could be found, so God Himself came to save. In Jesus, the Lord who wears light took on flesh and carried out salvation alone. The New Testament picks up Isaiah's armor when it calls believers to take "the helmet of salvation" (Ephesians 6:17), so the gear God wore in His saving work is shared with His people. Isaiah 59 does not name Christ, but the pattern of the God who comes alone when no one else can points directly toward Him.

The shame theme also leads to the cross. Psalm 89 laments David's royal heir: "You have covered him with shame" (89:45). Jesus, the Son of David, endured exactly that kind of covering. He was mocked, stripped, and crucified, and He despised the shame for the joy set before Him (Hebrews 12:2, paraphrased). The King took the garment of disgrace so that His people could take off theirs. Paul then describes salvation in the language of clothing: "as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27). He also gives a daily command: "put on the Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 13:14). Those Greek passages use different words, but they answer the question ʻâṭâh raises.

7. Spirit & Life

Every person wears something. Psalm 109 pictures sin, when it is loved, as clothing that will not come off. No one simply dabbles in cursing, bitterness, or pride for long. Those things wrap themselves around a life until others recognize the person by them. The good news is that Christ does more than advise us to change clothes. He takes our shame upon Himself and clothes us with Himself.

Psalm 84:6 gives believers a different picture. Those whose strength is in God "pass through the Valley of Baca," a valley of weeping and dryness, and "They make it a spring; The rain also covers it with pools." The same verb that wraps the wicked in disgrace here wraps a place of tears in water. God does not always lead His people around the valley. He covers it. Pilgrims on their way to His house find that the sorrowful stretch of road becomes the place where provision gathers.

The Song of Solomon adds a quieter note. The bride asks, "why should I be as one who veils herself By the flocks of your companions?" (1:7). She does not want to wander unrecognized among strangers. She wants to be with the one she loves. Love for God shares that longing: to be found near the Shepherd, known by Him, and not hidden among others' flocks.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

1 Samuel 28:14. "An old man is coming up, and he is covered with a mantle." From the medium's description, Saul perceives that it is Samuel. Earlier Saul had torn the edge of Samuel's robe, and Samuel said the LORD had torn the kingdom from him (1 Samuel 15:27–28), though the text does not say this mantle is that robe.

Psalm 71:13. "Let them be covered with reproach and dishonor Who seek my hurt." An aging believer trusts God to let the schemes of his enemies return upon them.

Leviticus 13:45. The leper covers his mustache and cries "Unclean!" His covered face publicly marked his separation from the community.

Micah 3:7. False seers cover their lips in shame "For there is no answer from God." Prophets who sold their words for profit end in silence.
