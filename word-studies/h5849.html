CROWN, SURROUND, ENCIRCLE - (ʻâṭar, Strong's H5849)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʻâṭar means to encircle or surround, and from that picture it came to mean to crown. A crown is a band that circles the head, so the same word can describe an army closing a ring around its target or a king receiving a wreath of honor. The word appears only seven times in the Old Testament, and nearly every use is striking. Saul's soldiers are "encircling" David to capture him, while David sings that the LORD surrounds the righteous with favor "as with a shield." God crowns human beings "with glory and honor," crowns His redeemed people "with lovingkindness and tender mercies," and crowns the year with His goodness.

In the three psalms that use this word for crowning, God is the One who crowns. He gives honor; His people do not seize it. Tyre, the wealthy "crowning city," presumed to hand out glory through trade, and the LORD brought its pride to dishonor. Hebrews takes up Psalm 8 and shows Jesus "crowned with glory and honor" after the suffering of death. The One who wore a crown of thorns now leads His people toward the glory God intended for them (Hebrews 2:10). Every believer lives inside that circle of grace.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻâṭar is a Hebrew verb, an action word. It is closely related to the far more common noun for "crown," ʻăṭârâh. The noun names the object. The verb describes the act of surrounding someone or of placing honor on them. One use comes from historical narrative (1 Samuel), five from poetry (Psalms and the Song of Solomon), and one from prophecy (Isaiah). In the poetic books the word becomes a way to describe what God does for people and for His creation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core idea is to put a ring around something. Context determines whether that ring threatens, protects, or honors.

1. To encircle or surround, whether to attack or to protect. Saul's men were "encircling David and his men to take them" (1 Samuel 23:26), while God surrounds the righteous with favor (Psalm 5:12).

2. To crown, literally or figuratively, meaning to place honor, beauty, or blessing on someone or something. Solomon's mother "crowned him" on his wedding day (Song of Solomon 3:11), and the psalmist tells God, "You crown the year with Your goodness" (Psalm 65:11).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes everything. When Saul does the encircling, the ring is a trap. When the LORD does it, the ring becomes a shield. Only one use describes a literal crown placed on a head, Solomon's wedding crown. The other four crownings are figurative. In three psalms God is the One who crowns (Psalms 8:5; 65:11; 103:4), and in a fourth He surrounds the righteous with favor (Psalm 5:12). Tyre is the exception among the figurative crownings, a proud city doing the crowning (Isaiah 23:8).

One grammatical detail is worth noticing. Isaiah 23:8 uses the verb to describe Tyre as an active crowner: "the crowning city." The form presents Tyre as the one who bestows crowns, not merely a city that wears one. This likely reflects Tyre's power to make its merchants into "princes" and perhaps to set up rulers in its trading colonies. Either way, the form explains why the word fits the city's pride.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these verses teach that honor is God's to give. The psalms that use this word never picture people crowning themselves. God crowns man, God crowns the redeemed, and God crowns the seasons.

Psalm 65:11 extends that generosity to all of creation: "You crown the year with Your goodness, And Your paths drip with abundance." The psalm celebrates rain, overflowing streams, and full harvests. The verb's basic sense of encircling suits the farming year well. Sowing, growing, and reaping make a complete cycle, and the psalmist pictures God's goodness surrounding that whole circle and setting the harvest on it like a wreath. God is no distant observer of nature. He is a host whose paths leave abundance behind them. The same God who protects and pardons also feeds His world, and the harvest table is one more crown He gives.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Encircled by Saul, surrounded by God (1 Samuel 23:26; Psalm 5:12). In the wilderness of Maon, Saul's army was on one side of the mountain and David on the other, and "Saul and his men were encircling David and his men to take them." The ring was closing. In Psalm 5, David uses the same verb for a different kind of ring: "For You, O LORD, will bless the righteous; With favor You will surround him as with a shield." The shield in that verse is the large kind that covers the whole body. David knew from experience what it was to be hemmed in by enemies, and though Psalm 5 does not name this chase, Maon illustrates the wider circle the psalm describes. Saul's men surrounded David, and God's favor surrounded them all. In 1 Samuel the chase ended because the LORD stepped in: a messenger reached Saul with news of a Philistine raid, and Saul turned back (1 Samuel 23:27–28).

Crowned with glory and honor (Psalm 8:5). David looks at the heavens and wonders why God would notice something as small as a human being. Then he answers: "You have made him a little lower than the angels, And You have crowned him with glory and honor." This crowning goes back to creation, where God made man in His image and told him to "have dominion" over the earth (Genesis 1:26–28). Human dignity is a gift placed on people by their Maker. Hebrews 2:6–8 quotes this psalm and then admits what everyone can see: "But now we do not yet see all things put under him." The crown was given, and human beings still bear that God-given dignity, but the full rule over creation that Psalm 8 describes is not yet seen.

From the pit to the crown (Psalm 103:4). David blesses the LORD "Who redeems your life from destruction, Who crowns you with lovingkindness and tender mercies." The two lines move from the lowest place to the highest. In the first line a life is pulled back from destruction. In the second the same life receives a crown. In the ancient world a crown meant power, conquest, or wealth. The crown God places on His redeemed is His steadfast love and His tender compassion. The greatest honor a forgiven person can wear is the mercy of God.

The crowning city brought low (Isaiah 23:8–9). Tyre was the trading capital of the Mediterranean, a city "Whose merchants are princes, Whose traders are the honorable of the earth." It made men great through commerce. Isaiah asks who planned Tyre's fall, and the answer comes at once: "The LORD of hosts has purposed it, To bring to dishonor the pride of all glory." Tyre took up a role that belongs to God, handing out honor on its own terms, and God removed it. Tyre is the exception among the figurative crownings: a city crowning on its own terms, and its story ends in judgment.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hebrews does not stop at "we do not yet see." It goes on: "But we see Jesus, who was made a little lower than the angels, for the suffering of death crowned with glory and honor, that He, by the grace of God, might taste death for everyone" (Hebrews 2:9). Psalm 8 says God crowned humanity, yet we do not yet see all things put under human rule. Hebrews shows where that crown of glory and honor rests in full: on the true Man, Jesus Christ. Hebrews gives the reason plainly: He was crowned "for the suffering of death."

The road to that crown passed through another one. When Adam sinned, the ground was cursed: "Both thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you" (Genesis 3:18). At His trial the soldiers "twisted a crown of thorns, put it on His head" (Mark 15:17). They meant it as mockery, yet the image fits the gospel. The King wore the fruit of the curse on the head that Psalm 8 says should be crowned with glory. The Gospels and Hebrews use Greek words, not the Hebrew verb, so this is a canonical connection rather than a repeated word. Its weight comes from Hebrews itself, which ties Christ's crowning to His death. He took the crown of thorns on the way to the crown of glory, and Hebrews says that through His sufferings God is "bringing many sons to glory" (Hebrews 2:10).

7. Spirit & Life

A believer can be hemmed in. The pressure may come from people, from circumstances, or from guilt, and the ring can feel as tight as Saul's army on the far side of the mountain. Psalm 5:12 gives a better reckoning: with favor God surrounds the righteous "as with a shield." The shield in that verse is the large kind that covers the whole body, so God's favor stands guard around His people as a great shield guards a soldier. David's escape in the wilderness of Maon illustrates the promise. He was surrounded, and he was kept.

The crown also tells a believer what to wear. Tyre offered honor that money could buy, and much of life still runs on that system of status, achievement, and being well thought of. Psalm 103 places a different crown on the redeemed. A crown sits on the head, the place of thought and identity. If God's lovingkindness is what crowns me, then my sense of who I am rests on His mercy and not on my record or anyone's approval. That is freeing. A crown I did not earn cannot be taken by someone who outperforms me.

Finally, these verses teach a believer to receive. In each psalm that uses this word for crowning, God is the One who crowns. We do not crown ourselves. We receive glory and honor through Christ, mercy in redemption, and goodness in each year's harvest, and we learn to give thanks for crowns we never could have placed on our own heads.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Song of Solomon 3:11 — "See King Solomon with the crown With which his mother crowned him On the day of his wedding, The day of the gladness of his heart." This is the only literal crowning among the word's uses, and it is a wedding crown given by a mother. The setting ties the act of crowning to love, family blessing, and joy rather than to warfare or politics.

The remaining occurrences (1 Samuel 23:26; Psalms 5:12; 8:5; 65:11; 103:4; Isaiah 23:8) are discussed above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H5850 (ʻăṭârâh), crown — the noun related to this verb, naming the crown itself. Isaiah 28:5 promises that "the LORD of hosts will be For a crown of glory and a diadem of beauty To the remnant of His people." There God Himself becomes His people's crown.

  • H2617 (chesed), lovingkindness, and H7356 (rachamim), tender mercies — a canonical pairing. These are the two words God uses to crown the redeemed in Psalm 103:4.

  • G4737 (stephanoō), to crown — a Greek thematic parallel, not a lexical relative. Hebrews 2:7, 9 uses it when quoting Psalm 8:5 and applying it to Jesus.
