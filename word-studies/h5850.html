CROWN - (ʻăṭârâh, Strong's H5850)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻăṭârâh means a crown: the gold circlet of a king, the festive wreath of a celebration, or anything that serves as a person's visible honor. In Scripture it sits on the heads of conquering kings, bridegrooms, and rescued men like Mordecai. It also moves beyond literal headgear. Proverbs calls a good wife, gray hair, and grandchildren a crown. The prophets warn that a proud nation's crown will fall and be trampled.

The word reaches its height in the prophets. Isaiah sees Ephraim's "crown of pride" wilting like a party garland, and then promises that "the LORD of hosts will be For a crown of glory" to His remnant (Isaiah 28:5). Ezekiel announces that the crown will be taken off Judah's last king and royal rule overthrown, "Until He comes whose right it is" (Ezekiel 21:27). Zechariah then sets a crown on a high priest as a sign of the coming Branch, who will be both priest and king. Followed through Scripture, this one word tells where lasting glory is found. It is not found in the honors people make for themselves. It is found in God Himself and in the King He has chosen. And it reaches the people He redeems, whom He calls "a crown of glory In the hand of the LORD" (Isaiah 62:3).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻăṭârâh is a Hebrew noun found only in the Old Testament. It appears in historical books (David's conquest of Rabbah, Esther), in poetry and wisdom literature (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Song of Solomon), and most often in the prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, Zechariah). A related verb means to crown. It appears in Song of Solomon 3:11, where Solomon's mother "crowned him."

In the ancient world a crown could be a heavy metal diadem or a simple wreath of leaves and flowers worn at feasts and weddings. Both kinds help explain the passages below. Hebrew also has another word for a royal crown, nēzer, which carries the idea of consecration. Some well-known "crown" verses use that word instead of this one.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻăṭârâh names something worn on the head that marks honor, rank, or joy. From there it came to mean whatever serves as a person's glory or adornment.

1. A literal crown or wreath worn as a sign of royalty, victory, or celebration. Examples are the Ammonite king's crown in 2 Samuel 12:30 and Solomon's wedding crown in Song of Solomon 3:11.

2. A figurative crown: a person, quality, or condition that brings someone honor and dignity. Examples are a wife, gray hair, and grandchildren in Proverbs, and Job's lost dignity in Job 19:9.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The literal crowns mark public triumph. When David took Rabbah, he took the Ammonite king's crown, "Its weight was a talent of gold, with precious stones. And it was set on David's head" (2 Samuel 12:30; compare 1 Chronicles 20:2). A talent was roughly seventy-five pounds. For that reason some interpreters think the crown was set on David briefly for a ceremony, or that it originally belonged to an idol. Either way, the transfer of the crown showed that rule had passed from one king to another. In the same way, Mordecai walked out of the king's presence in "a great crown of gold" after the king's new decree permitted the Jews to defend themselves (Esther 8:11, 15), before the day of their defense came in Esther 9. That crown made his reversal of fortune visible to the whole city.

The figurative uses ask what truly makes a person honorable. The prophetic uses add a further question: what happens when God removes the crown, and who will wear it next?

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats the crown as God's gift before it is anything else. Psalm 21:3 says of the king, "You set a crown of pure gold upon his head." The king's glory is received. It is not seized. That is why the crown can also be taken back. Jeremiah tells the king and the queen mother to humble themselves because their crown has come down (Jeremiah 13:18). The survivors in Lamentations confess that the crown has fallen from their head, and they own the cause: they have sinned (Lamentations 5:16).

Proverbs takes the symbol of royal rank and hands it to ordinary faithful people. Wisdom is pictured as a generous woman who "will place on your head an ornament of grace; A crown of glory she will deliver to you" (Proverbs 4:9). An excellent wife is her husband's crown (Proverbs 12:4). "The silver-haired head is a crown of glory, If it is found in the way of righteousness" (Proverbs 16:31). "Children's children are the crown of old men" (Proverbs 17:6). A farmer's wife, a white-haired grandfather, and a houseful of grandchildren carry a dignity that Proverbs ranks with a king's gold. The condition in 16:31 matters. Gray hair is a crown only on a life lived in righteousness, because the crown belongs to the faithfulness that the years have proved.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 28:1–5: the wilting garland and the LORD as crown. Isaiah opens with "Woe to the crown of pride, to the drunkards of Ephraim, Whose glorious beauty is a fading flower" (28:1). The picture is probably a festive wreath on the heads of drinkers, and possibly also Samaria itself, sitting like a garland on its hill above the valley. Flowers wilt within hours. Isaiah adds a second image: Ephraim's glory is like an early fig that an observer sees and "eats it up while it is still in his hand" (28:4). That glory will be "trampled underfoot" (28:3). Then the prophet turns: "In that day the LORD of hosts will be For a crown of glory and a diadem of beauty To the remnant of His people" (28:5). God does not hand the proud nation its garland back. He becomes the crown Himself. What judgment took away, God replaces with His own presence, and that crown cannot wilt.

Ezekiel 21:26–27: royal rule removed until the rightful King comes. Against Judah's last king, the "profane, wicked prince of Israel" (21:25), God commands, "Remove the turban, and take off the crown." The overthrow is repeated three times, and then comes a promise: "It shall be no longer, Until He comes whose right it is, And I will give it to Him." Ezekiel does not say what became of the physical crown. What he announces is that royal rule in David's line will be overthrown and not restored until the One to whom it rightfully belongs arrives, and God Himself will give it to Him. The line of David loses its throne, but the promise of rightful rule remains. The phrase echoes Jacob's blessing on Judah, that the scepter would remain "until Shiloh comes" (Genesis 49:10).

Zechariah 6:11–14: a crown on a priest's head. After the exile, Zechariah is told to make an elaborate crown and set it on the head of Joshua the high priest (6:11). This was startling. Israel kept the offices of priest and king apart. When King Uzziah tried to burn incense in the temple, the LORD struck him with leprosy (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). Joshua is not being made king. He serves as a sign of "the Branch," who will build the temple of the LORD, bear the glory, and sit and rule on His throne as a priest. Harmony will exist between the two roles (6:12–13). The elaborate crown was then to remain "for a memorial in the temple of the LORD" for Helem, Tobijah, Jedaiah and Hen, the men named in the passage (6:14), a lasting reminder of the promise the sign carried. Ezekiel's promise concerns rightful rule; Zechariah's sign shows priesthood and kingship joined in the coming Branch.

Ezekiel 16:12 and Isaiah 62:3: the bride crowned, and the bride as crown. Song of Solomon 3:11 shows a wedding crown: Solomon "with the crown With which his mother crowned him On the day of his wedding, The day of the gladness of his heart." Ezekiel takes up this wedding picture to describe God and Jerusalem. He finds her as an abandoned newborn, washes her, takes her as His own, and adorns her: "I put a jewel in your nose, earrings in your ears, and a beautiful crown on your head" (16:12). Isaiah goes further. Zion herself becomes the ornament: "You shall also be a crown of glory In the hand of the LORD, And a royal diadem In the hand of your God" (62:3). The surrounding verses speak of the land being "married" and of God rejoicing over His people as a bridegroom over a bride (62:4–5). God crowns His people, and then He holds them as His own crown.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The crown's path through Scripture leads to Jesus. Ezekiel promised that the overthrown kingdom would be given to the One "whose right it is" (Ezekiel 21:27). Zechariah's sign named a Branch who would be priest and king on one throne. Hebrews does not quote Zechariah's sign, but read in its light it shows that union in Jesus. Hebrews identifies Jesus as the priest in the order of Melchizedek, who was "king of righteousness" and "king of peace" and also "priest of the Most High God" (Hebrews 7:1–2). Jesus is the high priest seated at the right hand of the throne of God (Hebrews 8:1), the rightful heir and the faithful mediator together.

Before He received that crown, He wore another one. The soldiers twisted thorns into a crown and pressed it on His head (Matthew 27:29; John 19:2). Thorns first appear in Scripture as part of the curse (Genesis 3:18). Ephraim wore a garland of pride. The Son of David wore a wreath of curse. Through that humiliation, the promise of Isaiah 28:5 comes true for His people: the LORD Himself becomes their glory. Paul contrasts the athlete's wreath with the believer's prize: "they do it to obtain a perishable crown, but we for an imperishable crown" (1 Corinthians 9:25). The New Testament uses a different Greek word, but the hope is the same as Isaiah's. Human glory fades, and the glory God gives does not.

7. Spirit & Life

Job knew what it was to lose a crown: "He has stripped me of my glory, And taken the crown from my head" (Job 19:9). His reputation, his standing at the city gate, and his honor were all gone. Yet later, confident of his integrity, he says he would take the written charge against him and "bind it on me like a crown" (31:36). He would wear the accusation openly and walk toward God like a prince. Job spoke beyond what he knew, and he later repented of that (Job 42:6). But his confidence still says something true. A believer whose conscience is clean before God need not hide from false accusation. Honor that rests with God cannot be stripped away by slander.

The crown passages also expose the garlands we make for ourselves: success, admiration, being known as the one in control. Isaiah's picture is honest about such things. They look festive at the head of the valley, and they wilt by morning. The remedy he offers is not a sturdier garland. He offers the LORD Himself as the crown of the remnant, a glory that does not depend on how we look this week.

Isaiah 62:3 then reverses the direction. The redeemed are a crown "in the hand of the LORD." We do not keep ourselves on display. God holds us. The abandoned child of Ezekiel 16 becomes the beauty her rescuer holds up for all to see. There is quiet strength here for a believer who feels unremarkable. The faithful marriage, the white hair earned on the road of righteousness, and the grandchildren raised to know God are what Scripture calls crowns.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 14:24 — Proverbs says that the riches of the wise are their crown, while the foolishness of fools remains folly. Wealth adorns a wise person, but it cannot make a fool wise.

  • Ezekiel 23:42 — Sabeans brought from the wilderness "with men of the common sort" put bracelets on the wrists of the two unfaithful sisters and "beautiful crowns on their heads." This is a counterfeit of the crown God gave His bride in Ezekiel 16:12.

  • Jeremiah 13:18 — Jeremiah calls the king and queen mother to humble themselves because their crown of glory has come down. Pride on the throne brings the crown low.

  • Song of Solomon 3:11 — The daughters of Zion are invited to see the crowned bridegroom on "the day of the gladness of his heart." This verse shows the crown as a sign of joy.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H5145 (nēzer) — A distinct Hebrew word for a royal crown, linked with consecration. Psalm 89:39 laments that God has "profaned his crown by casting it to the ground," and Zechariah 9:16 pictures God's people as "the jewels of a crown." These are thematic parallels, not occurrences of ʻăṭârâh.
