EBAL - (ʻÊybâl, Strong's H5858)

Summary

Ebal is best known as Mount Ebal, the mountain facing Mount Gerizim across the valley at Shechem in the heart of the Promised Land. Moses commanded that when Israel entered Canaan, the curse of God's law would be set on Ebal and the blessing on Gerizim. On Ebal Israel was also to raise great plastered stones inscribed with the law and build an altar of uncut stones for burnt offerings and peace offerings. Joshua carried out that command after the victory at Ai. He read every word of the law, "the blessings and the cursings," before the whole assembly, including women, children, and foreigners living among them (Joshua 8:34–35). The same Hebrew name also belongs to two minor figures in the genealogies: a Horite son of Shobal (Genesis 36:23; 1 Chronicles 1:40) and a son of Joktan in the line of Shem (1 Chronicles 1:22). The meaning of the name is uncertain. Suggestions such as "bare," "bald," or "stone" have been proposed, but none is established.

Ebal matters because of what God put there. The mountain where Israel heard the curses of the law, and answered each one with "Amen" (Deuteronomy 27:15–26), is the same mountain where God commanded His altar to stand and His people to "rejoice before the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 27:7). Centuries later Paul quoted the final curse of that ceremony, "Cursed is everyone who does not continue in all things which are written in the book of the law," and then announced that "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us" (Galatians 3:10, 13). The mountain of the curse points ahead to the cross, where the curse was not ignored but borne.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Ebal names a mountain and two men. The three should be distinguished.

Mount Ebal is a mountain in central Canaan, standing opposite Mount Gerizim (Strong's H1630, a separately indexed name). The valley between them holds Shechem. Deuteronomy places the two mountains beyond the Jordan near the terebinth trees of Moreh (Deuteronomy 11:30), which ties them to the spot where God first appeared to Abram in the land and promised it to his descendants (Genesis 12:6–7). Five of the eight occurrences of the name refer to this mountain (Deuteronomy 11:29; 27:4, 13; Joshua 8:30, 33).

Ebal son of Shobal was a Horite, one of the descendants of Seir who lived in the land later associated with Edom (Genesis 36:20, 23). First Chronicles 1:40 repeats his name in its parallel list.

Ebal son of Joktan belongs to the line of Shem through Eber (1 Chronicles 1:20–22). The matching list in Genesis 10:28 reads "Obal," which Strong's indexes separately as H5745. The two lists are generally taken to name the same person under variant spellings, but Obal is a distinct lexical entry and is not counted among Ebal's occurrences.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. Proposed derivations include "bare," "bald," and "stone." These are scholarly suggestions, not settled findings, and Scripture offers no wordplay on the name. The mountain's significance comes from what God commanded there, not from what its name might mean. The same restraint applies to the genealogical Ebals, whose lives the text does not describe.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The command given in advance. Before Israel crossed the Jordan, Moses told the people that the land would open with a public ceremony: "you shall put the blessing on Mount Gerizim and the curse on Mount Ebal" (Deuteronomy 11:29, paraphrased). In Deuteronomy 27 he filled in the details. Large stones were to be set up and whitewashed: "on Mount Ebal you shall set up these stones, which I command you today, and you shall whitewash them with lime" (Deuteronomy 27:4). The words of the law were to be written on them very plainly (27:3, 8). On the same mountain Israel was to build an altar: "an altar of stones; you shall not use an iron tool on them. You shall build with whole stones the altar of the LORD your God, and offer burnt offerings on it to the LORD your God" (27:5–6). Then came a command that is easy to read past: "You shall offer peace offerings, and shall eat there, and rejoice before the LORD your God" (27:7).

Moses divided the tribes between the two mountains. Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Joseph, and Benjamin were to stand on Gerizim to bless. Reuben, Gad, Asher, Zebulun, Dan, and Naphtali were to stand on Ebal for the curse (27:12–13). The Levites would then call out twelve curses against hidden sins such as secret idolatry, contempt for parents, moving a neighbor's boundary, misleading the blind, and perverting justice for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow. After each one the people were to answer "Amen" (27:14–26). The list ends with a curse that gathers up the whole law: "Cursed is the one who does not confirm all the words of this law by observing them." And all the people shall say, "Amen!" (27:26).

The command fulfilled. Joshua 8 shows Israel doing exactly what Moses said. The timing matters. Israel had just been humiliated at Ai because Achan stole devoted things, and Achan's sin had been judged (Joshua 7). God then gave the second battle at Ai to His people (Joshua 8:1–29). Right after this restoration, Joshua built an altar on Mount Ebal of whole stones untouched by iron, offered burnt offerings and peace offerings, and wrote on the stones a copy of the law of Moses (8:30–32). The nation took its place: "Half of them were in front of Mount Gerizim and half of them in front of Mount Ebal, as Moses the servant of the LORD had commanded before" (8:33). The ark of the LORD, carried by the priests, stood between them, presumably in the valley.

Then Joshua read. "He read all the words of the law, the blessings and the cursings, according to all that is written in the Book of the Law. There was not a word of all that Moses had commanded which Joshua did not read before all the assembly of Israel, with the women, the little ones, and the strangers who were living among them" (8:34–35). The narrator stresses completeness twice. Nothing was softened, and no one was left out.

Mount Ebal is not named again after Joshua 8. Shechem, the valley beneath it, remained a place of renewal (Joshua 24:1, 25).

The genealogical Ebals. The other two men named Ebal appear only in lists. One is among the Horite clans of Seir (Genesis 36:23; 1 Chronicles 1:40), and the other is among the descendants of Joktan (1 Chronicles 1:22). Scripture records nothing about them beyond their names and families.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Ebal was the mountain of the curse, and also the mountain of the altar, the written law, and commanded rejoicing. Moses did not set the altar on Gerizim, the mountain of blessing, but on Ebal (Deuteronomy 27:4–7; Joshua 8:30). One textual note belongs here. The Samaritan Pentateuch reads "Gerizim" in Deuteronomy 27:4, while the Hebrew Masoretic text behind the NKJV and most English Bibles reads "Ebal." Joshua 8:30 also names Ebal as the place where the altar was built.

Taking the text as it stands, the arrangement teaches something that follows naturally from the passage. The law was written on the very mountain where its curses were confirmed, so Israel could never claim that God's standard was vague. Yet on that same ground God provided burnt offerings and peace offerings. Israel was still called to obey, and blessing and curse would follow their response (Deuteronomy 11:26–28; 27:9–10). Yet God did not leave sinners only with warnings; He provided sacrifice for them on the same ground. The people were called both to affirm the law's curses with "Amen" (27:26) and to eat and rejoice before the LORD (27:7).

The demand for whole, uncut stones repeats the older rule of Exodus 20:25: "if you use your tool on it, you have profaned it." Scripture gives the reason: human tooling would profane the altar. It is a fair inference that God wanted His altar received as He provided it, not improved by human craft. At the mountain of the curse, that detail carries particular weight.

The setting at Shechem also links Ebal to God's promise. Israel stood near where the LORD had first said to Abram, "To your descendants I will give this land" (Genesis 12:7, paraphrased). The ceremony declared that God had kept His word, and it called Israel to live under His word in the land He gave. The inclusion of "the stranger as well as he who was born among them" (Joshua 8:33) shows that the law's blessings and warnings reached beyond native-born Israel to everyone who lived under God's rule among His people.

The genealogical Ebals show that God's record includes peoples outside Israel's line, such as the Horites of Seir and the clans of Joktan. Scripture does not develop their significance further.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest New Testament connection is explicit. In Galatians 3:10 Paul writes, "For as many as are of the works of the law are under the curse; for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who does not continue in all things which are written in the book of the law, to do them.'" The words he quotes come from Deuteronomy 27:26, the closing curse pronounced in the Ebal ceremony. Paul's point is that no one is justified by the law, "for 'the just shall live by faith'" (Galatians 3:11). Then he gives the answer: "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us (for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree')" (Galatians 3:13). The curse Israel affirmed with its "Amen" at Ebal is the curse Christ took on Himself at the cross.

Beyond that explicit link lies a canonical pattern the text supports, though the New Testament does not expressly call it a type. On Ebal, God placed the sacrifice where the curse was declared. At Calvary, God dealt with the curse by sacrifice, in the person of His Son. The altar of uncut stones, which could not be improved by human tools, fits Paul's insistence that redemption comes through faith and not through "the works of the law." This is a strong canonical resonance rather than an identification Scripture spells out, but it rises naturally from the passages.

John 4 offers a further thematic connection. Standing near Shechem, the Samaritan woman said, "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain" (John 4:20), meaning Gerizim. Ebal is not mentioned, but the dispute over which mountain was the proper place of worship belongs to the same landscape. Jesus answered that "the hour is coming, and now is, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth; for the Father is seeking such to worship Him" (John 4:23). The question of where to build the altar gives way to the One who is Himself the meeting place between God and sinners.

5. Spirit & Life

Ebal will not let us choose between taking God's law seriously and enjoying God's presence. Israel heard every curse read aloud, and no sin was treated as small. Yet the same people were told to eat and rejoice on that mountain. Joy before God did not come from ignoring His holiness. It came from the altar He provided.

That is still how sinners come to God. We are not invited to rejoice because the law has been relaxed. We are invited because Christ became a curse for us. The gospel does not avoid Ebal. It goes straight to the place of the curse and puts the sacrifice there.

The uncut stones carry a quiet warning for anyone tempted to add something to the work God has done. We bring nothing to sharpen or refine His provision. We receive it.

The timing in Joshua also carries comfort. Ebal came after Achan's sin and the defeat it caused. God did not set His people aside after that failure. He brought them to His altar and His word, and He let them hear the whole truth with the whole assembly gathered, children and strangers included. Restoration in God's way always brings us back to both of these, His word and His sacrifice.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Deuteronomy 11:29–30 – Moses assigns the curse to Ebal and the blessing to Gerizim and locates the mountains near Moreh.

  • Deuteronomy 27:4–8 – Moses commands the plastered stones bearing the law and the altar of whole stones, with burnt offerings, peace offerings, and rejoicing on Ebal.

  • Deuteronomy 27:12–26 – The tribes are assigned to the two mountains, and the twelve curses close with the curse on all who fail to keep the whole law.

  • Exodus 20:25 – The earlier law forbidding tooled stones on God's altar.

  • Joshua 8:30–35 – Joshua builds the altar, writes the law, and reads every word before all Israel and the strangers among them.

  • Genesis 12:6–7 – God's promise to Abram at Shechem, the setting of the Ebal ceremony.

  • Galatians 3:10–13 – Paul quotes Deuteronomy 27:26 and declares that Christ redeemed us by becoming a curse for us.

  • John 4:20–24 – At the nearby mountain of Samaritan worship, Jesus teaches worship in spirit and truth.

  • Genesis 36:23; 1 Chronicles 1:40 – Ebal son of Shobal, a Horite.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:22 (compare Genesis 10:28, "Obal," H5745) – Ebal son of Joktan.
