ELAM - (ʻÊylâm, Strong's H5867)

Summary

Elam is the name of an ancient nation east of Babylon. It is also the name of several Israelites who had no connection to that nation. In Genesis, Elam is a son of Shem and the ancestor of a people who lived beyond the Tigris River in the highlands of what is now southwestern Iran, with Susa (Shushan) as their great city. The name is commonly explained as "highland," associated with the Mesopotamian name for the region, Elamtu, though specialists note a complex relationship with the Elamites' own name for their land. A link to the Hebrew word ʻalam ("hidden"), proposed in Strong's, remains unproven. Elam enters the Bible's story as an imperial power: Chedorlaomer, king of Elam, ruled the cities of the Jordan Valley until Abram defeated him to rescue Lot (Genesis 14). Later the prophets speak of Elam's famous archers. God summons them against Babylon, Isaiah names them among the forces that came against Jerusalem (where his rebuke is aimed at Judah's lack of trust), and through Ezekiel God pictures them lying disgraced in the Pit (Isaiah 21–22; Ezekiel 32:24).

The most important passage is Jeremiah's oracle in Jeremiah 49:34–39. God promises to "break the bow of Elam, the foremost of their might," to scatter its people to the four winds, and to "set My throne in Elam." Then He adds an unexpected promise: "I will bring back the captives of Elam." Isaiah likewise names Elam among the lands from which the Lord will recover His remnant (Isaiah 11:11). On the day of Pentecost, "Elamites" stand in Jerusalem and hear the wonderful works of God in their own language (Acts 2:9). Separately, the name Elam belonged to a Benjamite, a Korahite gatekeeper, and two large families that returned from Babylon with Zerubbabel. Members of one of those families later led Israel in confessing sin (Ezra 10:2). Together these records show a God who breaks proud strength, rules over the nations, and keeps a door of mercy open even to old enemies.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name ʻÊylâm (Strong's H5867) names an ancestor and his nation, as well as several Israelites who shared the name. These histories should be distinguished.

Elam the nation. In the Table of Nations, Elam is listed first among the sons of Shem, ahead of Asshur, Arphaxad, Lud, and Aram (Genesis 10:22; 1 Chronicles 1:17). His name became the name of a people and their land. That land lay east of the Tigris and south of Media, rising into the Zagros Mountains, and its capital was Susa, called Shushan in the Old Testament. Most biblical occurrences refer to this nation: Genesis 14, the prophetic oracles in Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel, and Daniel 8:2.

Israelites named Elam. Several Israelites carried the same name:

  • A Benjamite listed among the sons of Shashak (1 Chronicles 8:24).

  • A Levite, the fifth son of Meshelemiah, a Korahite gatekeeper in David's day (1 Chronicles 26:3).

  • The head of a family whose descendants, 1,254 people, returned with Zerubbabel (Ezra 2:7; Nehemiah 7:12). More of this family came later with Ezra (Ezra 8:7), and its members appear in the account of the mixed marriages (Ezra 10:2, 26).

  • "The other Elam," a second family that also numbered 1,254 returnees (Ezra 2:31; Nehemiah 7:34).

  • A leader who set his seal to the people's pledge (Nehemiah 10:14). He probably represented one of the returning families.

  • An Elam who took part in the dedication of Jerusalem's wall, listed among those standing with the priests and singers (Nehemiah 12:42).

Sharing a Strong's number does not make these people one person, and none of them should be confused with the nation.

The meaning of the name. The name Elam is commonly explained as "highland," an association with Elamtu, the Mesopotamian name for the mountainous country east of Mesopotamia. That fits the land's geography, though specialists note a complex relationship between that name, the Elamites' own name for their land, and the Sumerian term for the region. Strong's proposes a connection with the Hebrew root ʻalam, which carries the idea of something "hidden" or "concealed," but that remains an unproven lexical proposal. Scripture builds no wordplay on the name. For the Israelites called Elam, the Bible does not explain why the name was given. It may have been an old family name, or it may reflect a family's history in the east, but the text does not tell us.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Elam the Nation

An early imperial power (Genesis 14). Elam's first scene in the Bible's narrative is a war. Chedorlaomer, king of Elam, was allied with the kings of Shinar, Ellasar, and "nations" (Genesis 14:1). The five cities of the plain, including Sodom and Gomorrah, "served Chedorlaomer" for twelve years before rebelling in the thirteenth (14:4). Although Genesis 14:1 names Amraphel first, it is Chedorlaomer whom the cities served and whom they rebelled against, so he was clearly the overlord. In the fourteenth year he and his allies swept south through the Transjordan, defeating the Rephaim, Zuzim, Emim, and Horites, then attacking "all the country of the Amalekites, and also the Amorites" (14:5–7). Then they routed the kings of the plain in the Valley of Siddim (14:9–10) and carried off Lot along with the plunder of Sodom. Abram armed his trained servants, pursued the invaders north, struck them by night, and brought back Lot, the people, and the goods (14:14–16). On his return Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, met and blessed him (14:17–20).

Archers in the prophets (Isaiah 21–22). By Isaiah's time Elam was known for its bowmen. In the oracle against Babylon, God summons Elam and Media to go up and besiege the city (Isaiah 21:2), using them as His instruments of judgment. In the very next chapter, Elam takes up the quiver and joins the forces gathered against "the Valley of Vision," Jerusalem (Isaiah 22:6). In one chapter Elam serves God's purpose against Babylon, and in the next it is part of the threat against His own city. The empires of the world shift their alliances freely, but none of them moves outside the Lord's rule.

Under the cup and in the Pit (Jeremiah 25; Ezekiel 32). Jeremiah lists "all the kings of Elam" among the rulers who must drink the cup of the Lord's wrath (Jeremiah 25:25). Ezekiel's lament over Egypt takes the reader down into the realm of the dead, where the great powers lie with their slain. There Elam appears: "There is Elam and all her multitude, All around her grave, All of them slain, fallen by the sword, Who have gone down uncircumcised to the lower parts of the earth, Who caused their terror in the land of the living; Now they bear their shame with those who go down to the Pit" (Ezekiel 32:24). The nation that once spread terror is now silent and ashamed.

The oracle against Elam (Jeremiah 49:34–39). Jeremiah dates this word to "the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah king of Judah" (49:34). It is the fullest treatment of Elam in Scripture. The Lord of hosts promises, "Behold, I will break the bow of Elam, The foremost of their might" (49:35). He will bring the four winds against Elam and "scatter them toward all those winds; There shall be no nations where the outcasts of Elam will not go" (49:36). He will send disaster, His fierce anger, and the sword (49:37). Then comes the decisive word: "I will set My throne in Elam, And will destroy from there the king and the princes" (49:38). The oracle does not end in ruin, however: "But it shall come to pass in the latter days: I will bring back the captives of Elam," says the LORD (49:39).

A province of the empire (Daniel 8). Daniel 8:2 places Daniel's vision of the ram and the goat at the citadel of Shushan, in the province of Elam; whether he was there in person or saw himself there in the vision is debated. Elam's own history was complex: Elamite kingdoms revived after Assyria's decline and were later absorbed into the Persian Empire. Shushan, a related place name indexed separately in Strong's as H7800, later becomes the setting of the book of Esther and the place where Nehemiah served the Persian king (Esther 1:2; Nehemiah 1:1). These passages do not use the name Elam itself. They show the old Elamite capital serving as a seat of Persian power while God's people lived in exile.

At Pentecost (Acts 2:9). The New Testament names the people once more, using the related Greek word for "Elamites." Among the crowd in Jerusalem were "Parthians and Medes and Elamites, those dwelling in Mesopotamia." Luke describes this crowd as devout Jews and proselytes gathered from many nations (Acts 2:5, 10), and each of them heard the apostles speaking in his own language.

Israelites Named Elam

The Benjamite Elam (1 Chronicles 8:24) and the gatekeeper Elam (1 Chronicles 26:3) appear only in genealogies. The gatekeeper's line served at the entrances of the Lord's house, a humble post the Chronicler thought worth recording.

The returning families carry more of the story. Two groups called by the name Elam each numbered 1,254 (Ezra 2:7, 31). Together they make up one of the larger blocks of people in the return under Zerubbabel. Decades later, seventy more males "of the sons of Elam" came with Ezra under Jeshaiah the son of Athaliah (Ezra 8:7). Members of this family were also involved in the community's failure. When Ezra learned of the intermarriage with the peoples of the land, it was "Shechaniah the son of Jehiel, one of the sons of Elam" who spoke up: "We have trespassed against our God, and have taken pagan wives from the peoples of the land; yet now there is hope in Israel in spite of this" (Ezra 10:2). Six men of the sons of Elam are later named among those who had taken foreign wives (Ezra 10:26). A Jehiel appears in that list, but the text does not say whether he was Shechaniah's father. Later still, an Elam sealed the people's written pledge (Nehemiah 10:14), and an Elam stood among the participants when the rebuilt wall was dedicated with music and rejoicing (Nehemiah 12:42).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that the Lord governs Elam. He calls it up against Babylon (Isaiah 21:2), passes judgment on its kings (Jeremiah 25:25), and declares that He will break its bow, scatter its people, and remove its rulers (Jeremiah 49:35–38). Elam is not a free agent in history. Even its famous archers draw their bows under the authority of the Lord of hosts.

Genesis 14 shows this from the start. The eastern alliance led by Elam's king had subdued kingdoms from Bashan to the edge of the wilderness. Yet it fell to a man whom God had called out of Ur and to whom He had promised the land. The text does not call the episode a contest between empires and God's chosen man, but the outcome makes the point. The man who held God's promise defeated the greatest military power in the narrative. Melchizedek's blessing gives the credit for the victory to "God Most High," Possessor of heaven and earth (Genesis 14:19–20).

Jeremiah 49 reveals the order of God's dealings with Elam. First He breaks the bow, the thing Jeremiah calls "the foremost of their might." Then He sets His throne in Elam and removes the king and the princes. Only after that does He promise to restore Elam's fortunes. The bow stands for everything Elam relied on. God does not simply weaken it; He breaks it and takes the throne Himself. The text states this directly. A reasonable implication follows: God's rule over the nations is not limited to Israel's borders, and His throne can stand in a Gentile land.

The closing promise is significant. Jeremiah's oracles against the nations do not always end with restoration, but Elam's does (49:39). Isaiah, too, names Elam among the places from which the Lord will recover the remnant of His people when He sets His hand "again the second time" (Isaiah 11:11). Scripture clearly states that God's purposes for the "latter days" include Elam. How and when each promise is fulfilled is a matter of interpretation, which the next section addresses.

The Israelite families named Elam show God's faithfulness on a different scale. More than 2,500 people bearing that name came back to rebuild the altar, the temple, and the wall. They were not a model community, and Ezra 10 records their sin without softening it. Yet one of them was the man who first said aloud, "There is hope in Israel." It is a striking fact that a name once tied to Abram's imperial enemy belonged to so many of God's restored people. The Bible makes no comment on this, so it should not be pressed into a lesson. It can still be noticed.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament links Genesis 14 to Christ directly. Hebrews recalls that Melchizedek met Abraham "returning from the slaughter of the kings," Chedorlaomer and his allies, and blessed him (Hebrews 7:1). It then presents Melchizedek's priesthood as a pattern for the priesthood of Jesus. Elam's defeat is the setting in which that priest-king appears. The victory over the eastern kings and the blessing of God Most High belong to the same scene.

Isaiah 11:11 belongs to a chapter about the Branch from the stem of Jesse. The Spirit of the Lord rests on Him, and He judges the poor with righteousness (Isaiah 11:1–5). Just before the promise about Elam, Isaiah says that the Root of Jesse will stand as a banner to the peoples and that the Gentiles will seek Him (11:10). Paul quotes this passage in Romans 15:12 to show that Christ came so the Gentiles might glorify God. The regathering from Elam is therefore part of a messianic hope that the New Testament explicitly applies to Jesus.

At Pentecost, Elamites are among the first people to hear the gospel (Acts 2:9). Luke does not cite Jeremiah 49 or Isaiah 11 in that passage. Luke identifies the crowd as devout Jews and proselytes; some from Elam may have been of Elamite descent, but the text does not identify them as descendants of the people Jeremiah said would be scattered. So Acts 2 should not be presented as the stated fulfillment of Jeremiah 49:39. Still, the connection across the canon is real and worth seeing. People from the very region Jeremiah addressed are now gathered in Jerusalem, where they hear the Spirit-given word in their own languages. The God who promised to set His throne in Elam is now proclaiming His exalted Son, who sits at His right hand (Acts 2:33–36), to people from Elam. That is a strong thematic link rather than a declared fulfillment, and it is a link the whole Bible supports.

The story of Elam begins with a king from the east carrying captives away from the Jordan Valley. It ends, as far as Scripture follows it, with people from Elam hearing that Jesus is Lord and Christ. That arc is not called typology anywhere, but it matches what the Bible teaches about God's purpose for the nations.

5. Spirit & Life

Jeremiah 49 shows something consistent in the way God deals with pride. Elam's bow was its glory, the "foremost of their might." God did not negotiate with that strength. He broke it, and then He set His throne in the place where the king and princes had ruled. Individuals and churches have their own versions of Elam's bow: a skill, a reputation, money, influence, or anything else that serves as the real source of their confidence. When God breaks such things, He is not being careless. He is taking His rightful throne. The same oracle that announces the breaking also promises restoration, so His purpose in judgment is not merely destructive.

Elam also shows how far God's mercy reaches. This nation oppressed Abram's family, joined in threatening Jerusalem, and ended in the Pit among the uncircumcised. Yet God did not close Elam's story with the words "until I have consumed them" (Jeremiah 49:37). He added "But" (49:39). No one is too far east, too hostile, or too long estranged to be addressed by the God who says "I will bring back." People from Elam at Pentecost heard the gospel in their own language, a sign that the God of Israel had no intention of keeping His good news to one people.

The returning families named Elam offer a closer lesson. They traveled hundreds of miles to rebuild God's city, and some of their men still compromised in their homes (Ezra 10:2, 26). Shechaniah did not excuse his family's sin, and he did not despair over it either. "We have trespassed against our God... yet now there is hope in Israel in spite of this" (Ezra 10:2). Honest confession and real hope belong together. Saying both is how God's people return to Him after failure.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:22; 1 Chronicles 1:17 — Elam named as a son of Shem, the ancestor of the nation.

  • Genesis 14:1–17 — Chedorlaomer, king of Elam, rules the cities of the plain, captures Lot, and is defeated by Abram.

  • Hebrews 7:1 — Connects Abraham's return "from the slaughter of the kings" to Melchizedek and Christ's priesthood.

  • Isaiah 11:10–11 — The Root of Jesse as a banner to the Gentiles; the Lord recovers His remnant "From Elam and Shinar."

  • Isaiah 21:2; 22:6 — Elam summoned against Babylon, then arrayed against Jerusalem.

  • Jeremiah 25:25 — The kings of Elam must drink the cup of God's wrath.

  • Jeremiah 49:34–39 — The central oracle: the bow broken, the people scattered, God's throne set in Elam, and latter-day restoration promised.

  • Ezekiel 32:24 — Elam, once a source of terror, lies in shame in the Pit.

  • Daniel 8:2 — Shushan located in the province of Elam during the exile.

  • Acts 2:9 — Elamites hear the gospel at Pentecost.

  • 1 Chronicles 8:24; 26:3 — An Israelite Benjamite and a Korahite gatekeeper named Elam.

  • Ezra 2:7, 31; 8:7; Nehemiah 7:12, 34 — Two large returning families named Elam.

  • Ezra 10:2, 26 — A son of Elam confesses the people's sin and holds out hope; members of the family are named among the offenders.

  • Nehemiah 10:14; 12:42 — An Elam seals the people's pledge, and an Elam takes part in the wall's dedication.
