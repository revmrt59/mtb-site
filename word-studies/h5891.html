EPHAH - (ʻÊyphâh, Strong's H5891)

Summary

Ephah is a name carried by three different people in the Old Testament. The best known is Ephah the son of Midian, a grandson of Abraham through Keturah, whose descendants became an Arabian tribe also called Ephah (Genesis 25:4; 1 Chronicles 1:33; Isaiah 60:6). The other two belong to the tribe of Judah: a woman named Ephah who was the concubine of Caleb and bore him three sons (1 Chronicles 2:46), and a man named Ephah, one of the sons of Jahdai (1 Chronicles 2:47). The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is often linked with the Hebrew word for "darkness" or "gloom," a noun related to the verb ʻuph, but the link is not settled. This proper name should not be confused with the "ephah" that appears throughout the Old Testament as a dry measure of grain, which is a separate Hebrew word.

Ephah the Midianite is the one whose story Scripture carries forward. He was born into Abraham's family but not into the line of promise. Abraham gave gifts to Keturah's sons and sent them away to the east so that nothing would cloud Isaac's place as heir (Genesis 25:5–6). Centuries later Isaiah sees Ephah's descendants coming back. They arrive at Zion among long camel caravans, alongside those from Sheba bringing gold and incense, and the caravans "proclaim the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). A family branch once sent away from the center of God's work is pictured returning to worship Abraham's God. Matthew's account of the wise men from the East who bring gold and frankincense to the child Jesus (Matthew 2:1–11) gives an early glimpse of the ingathering Isaiah foresaw, and Revelation completes it with the nations bringing their glory into the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:24–26).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H5891 is a single Hebrew name used for three separate people, and they must be kept apart.

Ephah son of Midian. He is the first-listed of Midian's five sons: "And the sons of Midian were Ephah, Epher, Hanoch, Abidah, and Eldaah. All these were the children of Keturah" (Genesis 25:4; repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:33). That makes him a grandson of Abraham and Keturah and a nephew of Isaac and Ishmael. As often happens in Genesis, his name later stands for the people descended from him. When Isaiah 60:6 speaks of "the dromedaries of Midian and Ephah," it means a tribe or clan of Midianite stock, not the individual himself.

Ephah, Caleb's concubine. In Judah's genealogy, "Ephah, Caleb's concubine, bore Haran, Moza, and Gazez" (1 Chronicles 2:46). This Caleb is Caleb the son of Hezron (1 Chronicles 2:18, 42), a descendant of Judah. He should not be confused with Caleb the son of Jephunneh, Joshua's companion.

Ephah son of Jahdai. The next verse lists "the sons of Jahdai" as "Regem, Jotham, Geshan, Pelet, Ephah, and Shaaph" (1 Chronicles 2:47). Jahdai appears without explanation. He is placed among Caleb's descendants, but the text does not say exactly how he is related to Caleb.

Scripture does not say whether the two Judahite Ephahs had any connection to the Midianite tribe. Midianite and Kenite families did live among Israel at times, and the name might reflect that kind of mixing. It might also simply be a name in common use. The text leaves the question open, and so should we.

The meaning of the name. The origin of ʻÊyphâh is obscure. Many link it to the Hebrew noun for "darkness" or "gloom" (Strong's H5890). The noun itself is related to the Hebrew verb ʻuph (H5774). The safest statement is that the meaning is uncertain, with "darkness" the most commonly suggested sense. Scripture builds no wordplay on the name, and nothing in these passages suggests that any of the three people should be understood through its meaning.

Not the measure. English Bibles also use "ephah" for a dry measure of grain, as in the ephah of flour in many offering laws. That is a different Hebrew word (Strong's H374). Every passage about bushels and baskets belongs to that word, not to this name.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Ephah the Midianite and his tribe

Ephah's story begins at the end of Abraham's life. After Sarah's death, Abraham took Keturah as a wife, and she bore Midian among other sons (Genesis 25:1–2). Midian's sons, with Ephah named first, are listed in Genesis 25:4. The following verses explain what happened to this whole branch of the family: Abraham gave everything he had to Isaac, gave gifts to the sons of his concubines, and while he was still living sent them away from Isaac toward the east country (Genesis 25:5–6). Ephah's line begins with a departure from the land of promise.

The Chronicler repeats the list almost word for word in 1 Chronicles 1:33. Writing for a people rebuilding after exile, he traces the whole family of Abraham, the branches that left as well as the line that stayed.

Ephah is not named again until Isaiah 60, but his wider people, the Midianites, appear often. The most painful memory is from the days of Gideon, when Midianite raiders invaded Israel year after year: "they would come up with their livestock and their tents, coming in as numerous as locusts; both they and their camels were without number; and they would enter the land to destroy it" (Judges 6:5). Ephah is not named in Judges, and Scripture does not say whether that clan took part in those particular raids; it simply belonged to the wider Midianite people.

Outside the Bible, Assyrian records from the eighth century BC mention an Arabian tribe called Hayappa that paid tribute to Assyria, and some scholars, including archaeologist Eshchar Gihon in a study of the Midianite site of Qurayyah, identify it with biblical Ephah. The tribute record does not by itself prove the extent of Ephah's trade or borders. If that identification is right, Ephah was a known trading people of northwestern Arabia in Isaiah's own century, which fits Isaiah's picture of camel caravans loaded with goods.

Isaiah 60 opens with Zion called to arise because her light has come and the glory of the LORD has risen on her, while darkness covers the earth and nations come to her light (Isaiah 60:1–3). Then the caravans appear: "The multitude of camels shall cover your land, The dromedaries of Midian and Ephah; All those from Sheba shall come; They shall bring gold and incense, And they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6).

Ephah, Caleb's concubine

Her only appearance is 1 Chronicles 2:46. She bore Caleb three sons, Haran, Moza, and Gazez, and the verse adds that "Haran begot Gazez," so the name Gazez appears for both a son and a grandson. Several names in this part of the genealogy are also place names in Judah, a reminder that the Chronicler is recording clans and settlements as well as individuals. Her name was kept because her sons became part of Judah's family record.

Ephah, son of Jahdai

He appears only in 1 Chronicles 2:47, fifth in a list of six brothers. Nothing more is said about him.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Ephah came from Abraham through Keturah and that Abraham sent Keturah's sons away so Isaac alone would inherit (Genesis 25:4–6). God had said that Abraham would be "a father of many nations" (Genesis 17:4), and Midian's sons are part of how that promise was kept. Yet God was equally clear that the promised line would run through Isaac. Ephah's departure is the result of that choice.

Isaiah 60:6 then states that Ephah's people will come to Zion with gold and incense and will praise the LORD. Isaiah does not explain this by pointing back to Genesis 25. Putting the two passages side by side is an inference, but it is a well-grounded one. Isaiah names descendants of Abraham who had lived outside Israel. In the very next verse he names Kedar and Nebaioth (Isaiah 60:7), sons of Ishmael (Genesis 25:13), another branch Abraham sent away. Sheba in Isaiah 60:6 may also belong to Keturah's family, since Genesis 25:3 lists a Sheba among her grandsons, though the Bible knows more than one Sheba and this connection is less certain. Even setting Sheba aside, Isaiah gathers Abraham's sent-away descendants around Zion in worship.

So sending Ephah away was never God's final word to him. God chose Isaac so that through Abraham's promised seed "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). The choice of one son was the means by which blessing would reach the others. In Isaiah's vision Ephah does not displace Isaac or become the heir. Ephah comes to the place where the God of Isaac dwells and joins in praising Him.

The Judahite Ephahs carry no stated theological weight. Their presence in the genealogies shows the Chronicler's care for Judah's whole family, including a concubine and an otherwise unknown son.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest development of Isaiah 60:6 comes in Matthew 2. "Wise men from the East came to Jerusalem," saying, "we have seen His star in the East and have come to worship Him" (Matthew 2:1–2). When they found the child, they "fell down and worshiped Him. And when they had opened their treasures, they presented gifts to Him: gold, frankincense, and myrrh" (Matthew 2:11). Matthew does not quote Isaiah 60 here, and he does not say the wise men were Midianites. Still, the connection is not forced. Isaiah foresees people from the East bringing gold and incense in honor of the LORD's glory, and Matthew shows people from the East bringing gold and frankincense to the newborn King. Christians have long read Matthew 2 as an early, partial fulfillment of Isaiah's ingathering. The glory Isaiah saw rising over Zion is present in this child, and the nations have begun to come.

There is also a reversal of setting. Abraham sent Ephah's family toward the east. Matthew opens his Gospel with worshipers coming from the east. This is a connection of theme rather than a direct link Matthew draws, but it fits the story the Bible tells as a whole.

Revelation completes the picture. In the New Jerusalem, "the nations of those who are saved shall walk in its light, and the kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into it. Its gates shall not be shut at all by day (there shall be no night there). And they shall bring the glory and the honor of the nations into it" (Revelation 21:24–26). John's wording draws heavily on Isaiah 60, which also speaks of open gates and of the LORD as everlasting light (Isaiah 60:11, 19–20). The caravans of Ephah belong to a vision that reaches its end in the city where the Lamb is the light.

Readers can set Isaiah's camels in striking canonical contrast with the camels of Judges. In Gideon's day Midian's camels were "without number" and came "to destroy" the land (Judges 6:5). In Isaiah the camels of Midian and Ephah again cover the land, but this time they carry tribute and worship. Isaiah does not mention Gideon, so this is a canonical comparison rather than an established allusion, though the same people, the same animals, and the same image of a land covered by them appear in both passages. God does more than remove a threat. He can turn the very things that once brought ruin into offerings to Himself.

5. Spirit & Life

Ephah's two main appearances, sent away in Genesis 25 and returning in Isaiah 60, show something about God's heart. Abraham's household sent Keturah's sons off with gifts so the promise would stay with Isaac. That was a real separation, and Scripture does not soften it. But God's purposes for Abraham's family were wider than the tent of Isaac. The God who chose one son had the others in view, and His plan was for them to come back as worshipers. Anyone who has felt like an outsider to the people of God, someone born on the wrong branch or living far to the east of the promise, can take Ephah's story seriously. God's choice of Israel was the road by which the nations would reach Him, and that road runs through Christ.

The direction of the gifts changes too. In Genesis Abraham gives gifts to Ephah's family as they leave. In Isaiah Ephah's people bring gifts to the LORD as they arrive. Their wealth came from trade, the caravans and goods that made them prosperous. In Isaiah's vision those same resources are carried to Zion and offered up, and the people who carry them "proclaim the praises of the LORD." Worship in Scripture includes the voice and the treasure together. The wise men did the same when they knelt before Jesus and opened their treasures. When someone comes to know the King, the ordinary means of life, a trade or a skill or an income, become things to lay before Him.

The camels carry a quieter encouragement. Israel remembered Midian's camels as a plague that left the land stripped. Read alongside that memory, God's promise through Isaiah shows Him bringing Midianite caravans as friends and fellow worshipers. He is able to redeem what once caused harm.

The two Judahite Ephahs, a concubine and an otherwise unknown son, are named and remembered in the record of God's people, a quiet dignity given without any recorded biography.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 25:1–6 — Ephah's birth as Midian's son through Keturah, and Abraham sending Keturah's sons east while Isaac inherits. The starting point of Ephah's story.

  • Genesis 25:4; 1 Chronicles 1:33 — The two lists naming Ephah as Midian's first-listed son.

  • 1 Chronicles 2:46 — Ephah, concubine of Caleb son of Hezron, mother of Haran, Moza, and Gazez.

  • 1 Chronicles 2:47 — Ephah, one of the sons of Jahdai in Judah's genealogy.

  • Isaiah 60:1–7 — Zion's glory drawing the nations. The caravans of Midian and Ephah bring gold and incense and praise the LORD (60:6), alongside Ishmael's sons Kedar and Nebaioth (60:7).

  • Judges 6:5 — Midian's countless camels as raiders, a canonical contrast with Isaiah's picture.

  • Genesis 12:3; 17:4 — God's promise that Abraham would father many nations and that all families of the earth would be blessed through him.

  • Matthew 2:1–11 — Wise men from the East worship the child Jesus with gold, frankincense, and myrrh, an early fulfillment of Isaiah's ingathering.

  • Revelation 21:24–26 — The nations bring their glory and honor into the New Jerusalem, the completion of Isaiah 60's vision.
