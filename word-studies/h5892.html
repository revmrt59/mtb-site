CITY, TOWN - (ʻîyr, Strong's H5892)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻîyr is the Old Testament's ordinary word for a city or town. It can name a great walled capital like Jerusalem, a small country town, or a modest outpost. Sometimes it stands for the people who live there. In most uses, an ʻîyr is a gathered and defended community: people living together, usually behind walls and gates, and looking to those defenses for safety.

Scripture follows this word from the first city Cain built to the prophets' visions of a restored Jerusalem. Human cities often express the desire to be secure and famous apart from God, as at Babel, and the prophets warn that walls cannot save a city God has given up. Yet God does not abandon the city. He set apart cities of refuge where a person who had killed someone by accident could flee and live. He scattered the Levites' cities through every tribe, chose Jerusalem as "the city of God," and promised to rebuild ruined towns. The story reaches its goal in Jesus, born in Bethlehem, David's family town, which Luke calls "the city of David" (Luke 2:4). The stronghold of Zion in Jerusalem, where David reigned, bore the same title, and the King born in the one is heir to the throne of the other. The story ends in the holy city, New Jerusalem, where God dwells with His people. Psalm 127:1 states the lesson that runs through it all: "Unless the LORD guards the city, the watchman stays awake in vain."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻîyr is one of the most common nouns in the Hebrew Old Testament. It appears in nearly every book. The heaviest concentrations are in Joshua, which records the conquest and division of the land city by city, and in Jeremiah, which announces the fall of Judah's cities. In the ancient Near East, what usually set a city apart from a village was its fortification. Walls, gates and bars allowed farmers from the surrounding fields to retreat inside when enemies came (compare Deuteronomy 3:5).

Strong's dictionary describes the word as "a place guarded by waking or a watch" and links it to a Hebrew word meaning to wake. That derivation is uncertain, so the meaning of the word should be drawn from how Scripture uses it. In a few verses the NKJV renders words grouped under this entry as "anguish" (Jeremiah 15:8), "terror" (Hosea 11:9), or "awake" (Malachi 2:12). These come from similar-looking Hebrew words or difficult readings, and they do not change the word's ordinary meaning of city.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A settled community of any size, walled or unwalled, from a fortress like Jericho (Joshua 6:3) to small rural towns (Deuteronomy 3:5).

2. The people of a city, considered together. When news of the ark's capture reached Shiloh, "all the city cried out" (1 Samuel 4:13).

3. A fortified section within a larger city, such as the stronghold of Zion that became "the City of David" (2 Samuel 5:7, 9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses are plain history. Cities are captured, built, listed, allotted and burned. The long lists in Joshua 15–21 record each tribe's inheritance and should be read as real geography. They do not carry hidden symbolism.

The word gains weight when Scripture tells us whose city it is or what marks it. In Hebrew, "city of" phrases identify the owner or the defining feature: "cities of refuge," "the City of David," "the city of God," "the City of Truth." The title tells the reader what the city is for.

The city gate was also the public court. Elders sat there to hear cases (Joshua 20:4; Deuteronomy 21:19), so justice and city life were bound together. Deuteronomy ties blessing and curse to the city itself: the faithful would be blessed in the city, and the unfaithful cursed there (Deuteronomy 28:3, 16).

4. Theological Significance

A city concentrates whatever its people love. It can concentrate rebellion, as at Babel and Sodom, or righteousness, as in Isaiah's promise that Jerusalem would again be "called the city of righteousness, the faithful city" (Isaiah 1:26). That promise answers Isaiah's earlier lament, "How the faithful city has become a harlot!" (Isaiah 1:21, using a related Hebrew word for city). The prophets speak of a city's relationship to God in the language of marriage. Jerusalem's idolatry is unfaithfulness to her Husband. Babylon is pictured as a proud queen addressed as one "Who dwell securely," who says, "I shall not sit as a widow," until widowhood comes "in a moment, in one day" (Isaiah 47:8–9).

The second great truth is that security belongs to God. Israel was warned about "your high and fortified walls, in which you trust" (Deuteronomy 28:52). God swore, "I abhor the pride of Jacob... Therefore I will deliver up the city And all that is in it" (Amos 6:8). Walls protect only those whom the Lord protects.

The third truth is that God shapes community life around His justice and mercy. In Genesis 18, Abraham asks whether God will destroy a city that contains righteous people, and God agrees to spare it for their sake (Genesis 18:24–26). God weighs a city by the people in it, and He is patient with it longer than people deserve.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The first cities: Cain and Babel (Genesis 4:17; 11:4–8). God sentenced Cain to be a wanderer, and Cain "went out from the presence of the LORD" to live east of Eden (Genesis 4:16). Genesis then records that he built a city and named it after his son Enoch (Genesis 4:17). Genesis does not give his reason. Many readers see an attempt to make his own permanence where God had given none, a reading that fits the sentence of wandering, though the text records only the act and the name. At Babel the motive is stated outright: "Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth" (Genesis 11:4). God had told humanity to fill the earth (Genesis 9:1). The builders gathered to resist that command and to seek a name of their own making, so God scattered them, and "they ceased building the city" (Genesis 11:8).

Abraham stands in contrast. He "dwelt in the land of promise as in a foreign country, dwelling in tents" (Hebrews 11:9), "for he waited for the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God" (Hebrews 11:10). The difference between these cities is who builds them and whose name they carry.

Cities of refuge and Levite cities (Numbers 35; Joshua 20–21). Among the forty-eight cities given to the Levites, God appointed "six cities of refuge, to which a manslayer may flee" (Numbers 35:6). They were for one "who kills a person accidentally or unintentionally," as "your refuge from the avenger of blood" (Joshua 20:3). The fugitive stood "at the entrance of the gate of the city," declared his case to the elders, and they would "take him into the city as one of them, and give him a place, that he may dwell among them" (Joshua 20:4). That welcome at the gate was a provisional shelter, not a verdict. The slayer stayed "until he stands before the congregation for judgment, and until the death of the one who is high priest in those days" (Joshua 20:6). If the congregation judged the killing unintentional, it returned him to the city of refuge to remain there until the high priest died (Numbers 35:12, 24–25). Only then could he "return and come to his own city and his own house" (Joshua 20:6).

Blood was never treated lightly, yet God made sure that a grieving family's rage would not add innocent blood to the land. The cities were spread across the country, from Kedesh in Galilee to Hebron in Judah (Joshua 20:7), so that no one was too far from safety. The refuge cities belonged to the Levites, and the Levite cities were themselves scattered through every tribe (Joshua 21). As a result, the men charged with teaching God's law lived among all of Israel. Mercy and the knowledge of God were placed within reach of every Israelite.

Jerusalem, the city of God (2 Samuel 5–6; Psalms 46; 48; 87; Zechariah 8:3). David captured Zion and named it "the City of David" (2 Samuel 5:7, 9). He then brought the ark of God there (2 Samuel 6:12, 16). The king's throne and the place of God's presence now stood in one city. The psalms celebrate it: "There is a river whose streams shall make glad the city of God, The holy place of the tabernacle of the Most High" (Psalm 46:4). "Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised In the city of our God" (Psalm 48:1). "Glorious things are spoken of you, O city of God!" (Psalm 87:3).

Jerusalem's glory never came from its stones. After judgment and exile, God promised, "I will return to Zion, And dwell in the midst of Jerusalem. Jerusalem shall be called the City of Truth" (Zechariah 8:3). The city becomes holy because God dwells there.

Isaiah's two cities (Isaiah 24–26). Isaiah sets two cities side by side. One is left unnamed, and it stands for all proud human society: "In the city desolation is left, And the gate is stricken with destruction" (Isaiah 24:12). "You have made a city a ruin, A fortified city a ruin... It will never be rebuilt" (Isaiah 25:2). Then God's people sing, "We have a strong city; God will appoint salvation for walls and bulwarks" (Isaiah 26:1). In God's city, salvation itself serves as the wall. This contrast reaches its fullest form at the end of the Bible, where Babylon falls (Revelation 18) and the holy city descends (Revelation 21).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The angels announced to the shepherds that a Savior, Christ the Lord, had been born "in the city of David" (Luke 2:11). Luke has already named the town: Joseph went "to the city of David, which is called Bethlehem, because he was of the house and lineage of David" (Luke 2:4). This is Bethlehem, David's family town, where Samuel anointed him (1 Samuel 16:4, 13), not the stronghold of Zion in Jerusalem that David captured and called "the City of David" (2 Samuel 5:9). The two places belong together because they belong to one king. David's line began in Bethlehem, and in Zion he reigned and brought the ark, uniting throne and presence. The Son of David is born in David's town as heir to David's throne, the King in whom God Himself has come to dwell with His people.

The cities of refuge point toward Christ as well. Hebrews speaks of believers "who have fled for refuge to lay hold of the hope set before us," a hope that enters "the Presence behind the veil," where Jesus has entered for us as "High Priest forever" (Hebrews 6:18–20). The parallel goes further than Hebrews states outright. The slayer whose case had been judged went home free at the death of the high priest, and believers are set free by the death of their great High Priest.

Isaiah 61:4 promises that those whom the Lord's Anointed comforts "shall rebuild the old ruins" and repair "the ruined cities." Jesus read the opening of that chapter in Nazareth and said, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled" (Luke 4:21). The rebuilding promised by the prophets (Amos 9:14; Ezekiel 36:35) belongs to the restoration He began.

The story ends in a city. Abraham's hope is answered when John sees "the holy city, New Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Revelation 21:2), and hears, "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3). Humanity left a garden, and the first city was built by Cain after he went out from the LORD's presence (Genesis 4:16–17). God now brings down a city of His own building, with a river flowing through it (Revelation 22:1–2, echoing Psalm 46:4). The bride imagery answers Isaiah's harlot city made faithful again. Ezekiel's vision closes by naming its city for the Lord's presence there (Ezekiel 48:35).

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 127:1 speaks to anyone building a home, a church or a life: "Unless the LORD builds the house, They labor in vain who build it; Unless the LORD guards the city, The watchman stays awake in vain." The psalm does not dismiss the watchman. The watchman still stays awake. The psalm simply refuses to let him trust in his own alertness. Prayerful watchfulness and real dependence on God belong together.

Proverbs carries the image inward: "Whoever has no rule over his own spirit Is like a city broken down, without walls" (Proverbs 25:28). An undisciplined temper or appetite leaves a person open to every attack. The proverb nearby honors the one who rules his spirit above the one who captures a city (Proverbs 16:32, paraphrased). Self-control is the wall God helps His people rebuild.

Believers also live between two cities. Like Abraham, they wait for the city God is building, so no earthly city can claim their final loyalty. Yet God told the exiles in Babylon to seek the peace of the city where He had sent them and to pray for it (Jeremiah 29:7). The pilgrim who knows where home is can serve the place where he lives without fearing it or worshiping it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 107:4, 7 — The wanderers "found no city to dwell in" until the Lord led them "to a city for a dwelling place." God gives His people a settled home.

  • Jonah 4:11 — God asks whether He should not pity Nineveh, "that great city," with its many people and animals. His compassion extends even to an enemy city.

  • Ecclesiastes 9:14–15 — A poor wise man delivers a besieged city, and afterward no one remembers him. Wisdom accomplishes what walls cannot.

  • Nehemiah 11:1–2 — To repopulate "Jerusalem, the holy city," the people "cast lots to bring one out of ten to dwell" there (11:1). Others needed no lot: "the people blessed all the men who willingly offered themselves to dwell at Jerusalem" (11:2). Some came by lot and some volunteered, and together they filled a ruined place that God had promised to restore.

  • Ezekiel 48:35 — The visionary city receives a new name that announces the Lord's presence there. That is the destiny of every city God claims.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7151 (qiryah) — A distinct Hebrew word for city, used mostly in poetry. Isaiah uses it in his lament over "the faithful city" in Isaiah 1:21, and ʻîyr appears in the promise of restoration in Isaiah 1:26.

  • H6726 (Tsiyyon, Zion) — Paired with ʻîyr when the stronghold of Zion becomes the City of David (2 Samuel 5:7).

  • G4172 (polis) — The Greek word for city. It carries the canonical story forward in Hebrews 11:10 and Revelation 21:2. This is a thematic connection between the Testaments, and the two words are not lexical relatives.
