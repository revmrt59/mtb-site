ACHBOR - (ʻAkbôwr, Strong's H5907)

Summary

Achbor is the name of two men in the Old Testament. The first is an Edomite remembered only as the father of Baal-Hanan, one of the early kings of Edom who reigned before Israel had a king (Genesis 36:38–39; 1 Chronicles 1:49). The second, and far more significant, is Achbor the son of Michaiah, a trusted official of King Josiah of Judah. When the Book of the Law was found in the temple, Josiah sent Achbor with four other men to ask the LORD what this book meant for the king and the nation (2 Kings 22:12–14). The name means "mouse," or possibly a desert rodent such as the jerboa. Animal names were common in the ancient Semitic world, and the meaning says nothing about the character of either man.

Achbor of Judah is usually identified as the father of Elnathan, whom Jeremiah twice calls "the son of Achbor" (Jeremiah 26:22; 36:12). Jeremiah does not give Achbor's own father's name, so the identification is probable rather than stated, but it is widely held. Elnathan served the wicked King Jehoiakim, yet he was one of the officials who begged the king not to burn Jeremiah's scroll (Jeremiah 36:25). The father stood near a king who trembled at the Word of God. The son stood near a king who cut it apart and threw it in the fire. Between these two scenes lies the story of Judah's last revival and its final rejection of God's Word, and of a God whose Word could not be destroyed.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name עַכְבּוֹר (ʻAkbôwr) means "mouse." It comes from the ordinary Hebrew word for a mouse or small desert rodent, and the jerboa is sometimes suggested. Israelites and their neighbors often gave children animal names. Rachel ("ewe") and Jonah ("dove") are familiar examples. Scripture builds no wordplay on the name Achbor, and it would be a mistake to turn "mouse" into a statement about either man.

The seven occurrences of the name belong to two separate people:

  • Achbor of Edom, father of Baal-Hanan king of Edom (Genesis 36:38–39; 1 Chronicles 1:49). He lived in the region of Seir, southeast of the Dead Sea, at an early and undated time before Israel's monarchy.

  • Achbor of Judah, son of Michaiah, an official in Jerusalem under Josiah in the late seventh century BC (2 Kings 22:12, 14). He is usually identified as the father of Elnathan (Jeremiah 26:22; 36:12), though the link is probable rather than stated.

The parallel account in 2 Chronicles 34:20 names the same official "Abdon the son of Micah." Abdon is a separately indexed name (H5658). Most interpreters regard the two forms as variants of one man's name, since the lists, the setting, and the father's name (Micah being a short form of Michaiah) line up closely. Readers looking for Achbor in Chronicles will find him under that spelling.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Achbor of Edom. Genesis 36 lists the kings who reigned in Edom before any king ruled over Israel (Genesis 36:31). After Saul of Rehoboth died, "Baal-Hanan the son of Achbor" reigned in his place, and when Baal-Hanan died, Hadar followed him (Genesis 36:38–39; compare 1 Chronicles 1:49–50, where the successor is Hadad). Scripture does not say Achbor himself was a king. No explicit father-to-son succession is identified in this list, though not every king's father is named, and Achbor's only role is to identify his son.

Achbor of Judah. In Josiah's eighteenth year, Hilkiah the priest found the Book of the Law while the temple was being repaired (2 Kings 22:3–10). When Shaphan read it to the king, Josiah tore his clothes (22:11). He then "commanded Hilkiah the priest, Ahikam the son of Shaphan, Achbor the son of Michaiah, Shaphan the scribe, and Asaiah a servant of the king" (22:12) to go and inquire of the LORD, "for great is the wrath of the LORD that is aroused against us, because our fathers have not obeyed the words of this book" (22:13). The five men went to Huldah the prophetess in Jerusalem (22:14). Her answer confirmed that judgment would come on Judah but promised that Josiah, because his heart was tender and he humbled himself, would be gathered to his grave in peace (22:15–20). Josiah then gathered the people, read the book to them, and led them in renewing their pledge to obey it (23:1–3). Achbor is not named again in that reform, but he was part of the mission that set it in motion.

Elnathan the son of Achbor. If this Elnathan is the son of Josiah's official, as is usually held, the next scenes follow the family a generation later, when Josiah's son Jehoiakim ruled very differently. When the prophet Urijah fled to Egypt, "Jehoiakim the king sent men to Egypt: Elnathan the son of Achbor, and other men who went with him to Egypt" (Jeremiah 26:22). They brought Urijah back, and Jehoiakim killed him with the sword (26:23). Some years later, Elnathan sat with the princes in the scribe's chamber when Baruch read Jeremiah's scroll (36:12–15). These officials were afraid, told Baruch and Jeremiah to hide, and reported the matter to the king (36:16–20). As the scroll was read, Jehoiakim cut it piece by piece and burned it (36:23). Scripture records that Elnathan, Delaiah, and Gemariah "implored the king not to burn the scroll; but he would not listen to them" (36:25).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The Edomite Achbor belongs to the record of Esau's descendants. God had promised that Esau too would become a people (Genesis 25:23; 27:39–40), and Genesis 36 shows that promise kept. Edom had kings while Israel was still waiting for the kings God had promised Jacob (Genesis 35:11). Esau's line got kingship first, but the promise of God's chosen King ran through Jacob.

Achbor of Judah is more important. Scripture states plainly that he was one of the men Josiah trusted to seek the LORD at the gravest moment of his reign. This tells us what the king thought of him. Anything more about Achbor's personal faith is inference.

The larger significance comes from how the books of Kings and Jeremiah fit together. In 2 Kings 22, a king hears God's Word, tears his clothes, and sends men to inquire of the LORD. In Jeremiah 36, a king hears God's Word and burns it, and the text pointedly notes that neither he nor his servants were afraid or tore their garments (36:24). Achbor's household stands on both sides of that contrast. Other families appear in both scenes as well. Shaphan and his son Ahikam went with Achbor to Huldah. Ahikam later protected Jeremiah from death (Jeremiah 26:24), and Shaphan's son Gemariah joined Elnathan in pleading for the scroll (36:12, 25). The men who once carried Josiah's question to the prophetess left sons who still had some regard for the prophetic word when the throne had turned against it.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No New Testament passage mentions Achbor, and he is not a type of Christ. The scenes he and his son witnessed still belong to a story that leads to Christ. Josiah's fear was well-founded. The book named a wrath that Judah's fathers had earned, and even Josiah's repentance only postponed it (2 Kings 22:16–20). The Law could expose the nation's guilt, but it could not remove it. Scripture later says that Christ redeemed His people from the curse of the Law by becoming a curse for them (Galatians 3:13). He also answers the question Josiah sent Achbor to ask.

The burned scroll also points ahead. God told Jeremiah to take another scroll and write all the former words again, and many similar words were added (Jeremiah 36:27–32). A king could destroy ink and parchment, but he could not undo what God had spoken. God's spoken and written word is not the same as the eternal Word, yet the two are joined across the canon: the Word who was with God and was God became flesh (John 1:1, 14), was rejected by many who heard Him, and declared that His words will not pass away (Matthew 24:35). This is a matter of thematic resonance within the whole canon rather than an explicit fulfillment of these chapters.

5. Spirit & Life

These chapters show that God does not leave His people without His Word, even when they have neglected it for generations. A forgotten book in the temple was enough to bring a king to his knees, and God answered his humility with mercy (2 Kings 22:19). The same God sent warning after warning through Jeremiah, and when the warning was burned, He had it written again.

Elnathan's life is sobering. Scripture does not record his age or upbringing, and it does not tell us why he pleaded for the scroll, and it would go beyond the text to say his father's influence caused it. It is a fair observation, though, that the son of a man who helped receive God's Word could not stand by quietly while that Word was thrown into the fire. Yet the same Elnathan had earlier brought Urijah home to his death. His recorded actions are mixed: he carried out an ungodly king's order against a prophet and also pleaded against burning the scroll, and a conscience that draws a line at one point cannot by itself save anyone. Elnathan's partial courage leaves us with a plain question: how will we respond when God speaks, as Josiah did or as Jehoiakim did? The answer that brings life is humility before the Word and trust in the mercy God offers to those who tremble at it.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:31, 38–39; 1 Chronicles 1:49–50 — Achbor of Edom, father of King Baal-Hanan, in the list of Edom's early kings.

  • 2 Kings 22:8–20 — The Book of the Law is found, and Achbor joins the delegation to Huldah. Her answer brings both judgment and mercy.

  • 2 Kings 23:1–3 — The reform and renewed commitment to God's law that followed.

  • 2 Chronicles 34:20 — The parallel account, which names the official "Abdon the son of Micah."

  • Jeremiah 26:20–24 — Elnathan son of Achbor brings Urijah back from Egypt, and Ahikam protects Jeremiah.

  • Jeremiah 36:11–26 — Elnathan hears the scroll read and pleads with Jehoiakim not to burn it.

  • Jeremiah 36:27–32 — God commands that the burned scroll be written again.
