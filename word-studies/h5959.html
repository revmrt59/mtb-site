VIRGIN, MAIDEN - (ʻalmâh, Strong's H5959)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻalmâh means a young woman or maiden. The NKJV translates it "virgin" or "maiden." The Old Testament uses the word only seven times. In ancient Israel a young woman who had not yet married was customarily expected to be a virgin, and that association stands behind the NKJV's "virgin." The word itself, though, focuses on her youth and stage of life. What each passage says about a particular woman, such as whether she was married or living at home, comes from that passage's own details rather than from the word alone.

The word's few uses carry great weight. A maiden comes to a well and becomes the wife through whom Abraham's family continues. A maiden stands by the Nile and keeps the infant Moses alive. Maidens play timbrels when God's procession enters His sanctuary, and in the Song of Solomon they love the king for the fragrance of his name. Most important of all, Isaiah names an ʻalmâh as the Lord's own sign to the house of David: "the virgin shall conceive and bear a Son, and shall call His name Immanuel" (Isaiah 7:14). Matthew and Luke state plainly that Mary conceived Jesus as a virgin by the Holy Spirit, and Matthew declares Isaiah's promise fulfilled in Him. God repeatedly chose young women with little social power to carry His purposes forward, and He gave His greatest sign of "God with us" through one of them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻalmâh is a Hebrew noun for a young woman. It appears in three kinds of writing. Two uses come from historical narrative (Genesis 24 and Exodus 2), four from poetry and wisdom literature (Psalm 68, Proverbs 30, and the Song of Solomon), and one from prophecy (Isaiah 7).

Hebrew has a second word for an unmarried woman, bethulah, which is often more specifically "virgin." The two words overlap. For example, Rebekah is called a bethulah in Genesis 24:16 and an ʻalmâh in Genesis 24:43. Bethulah can also stretch to describe a woman mourning "the husband of her youth" (Joel 1:8). None of the seven uses of ʻalmâh clearly describes a married woman, but the word names a young woman rather than a legal status.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻalmâh means a young woman or maiden. The word names her youth and stage of life rather than giving a medical description. In Israel's moral world an unmarried young woman was expected to be a virgin, and that customary expectation is why the NKJV often renders the word "virgin," as it does in Isaiah 7:14. The seven verses themselves say different amounts about the women they describe. Genesis 24 makes clear that Rebekah was unmarried and a virgin (24:16), and Exodus 2 shows Moses' sister as a young girl, but the maidens of Psalm 68 and the Song appear in groups with no details about marriage, home or a coming wedding. The word should not be made to supply those details on its own. In Isaiah 7:14 the mother of the sign is called an ʻalmâh, and Matthew and Luke state plainly that Mary conceived as a virgin by the Holy Spirit (Matthew 1:18–25; Luke 1:26–35). The meaning stays essentially the same across all seven uses, so the word does not need to be divided into separate senses. Context decides whether a passage stresses youth, purity, beauty, or readiness for marriage.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the narratives, the ʻalmâh is an individual girl who acts: Rebekah draws water (Genesis 24:43), and Moses' sister runs to call his mother (Exodus 2:8). In the poetry the word is usually plural and describes a group, such as maidens in worship (Psalm 68:25), maidens who love the king (Song of Solomon 1:3), or maidens "without number" in a royal court (Song of Solomon 6:8). Proverbs 30:19 speaks of "the way of a man with a virgin"; many readers take this as a picture of courtship, though the verse does not explain it further. Isaiah 7:14 stands apart because there a single ʻalmâh becomes a sign given by God Himself.

4. Theological Significance

The passages that use ʻalmâh reveal a consistent habit of God. He places His purposes in the hands of people the world overlooks. In the ancient world, an unmarried girl usually had little status or protection of her own. Yet the family line that carried God's promise to Abraham, the survival of Israel's future deliverer, and the sign of God's own presence among His people all pass through young women of this kind. Paul describes the same pattern when he writes that God chose "the weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty" (1 Corinthians 1:27).

The word also brings purity and joy together. The maidens in Israel's poetry are glad. In Psalm 68 they play timbrels in God's procession, and in the Song the poet ties their love to the king's name: "Therefore the virgins love you" (Song of Solomon 1:3). As the poem presents it, their love is a response to who he is.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 7:14: the maiden as God's sign. Syria and the northern kingdom of Israel had joined forces against Jerusalem, and King Ahaz's heart "and the heart of his people were moved as the trees of the woods are moved with the wind" (Isaiah 7:2). God sent Isaiah to tell Ahaz that the plot "shall not stand," and He added a warning: "If you will not believe, Surely you shall not be established" (7:7, 9). Then the Lord invited Ahaz to ask for a sign, "either in the depth or in the height above" (7:11). Ahaz refused with pious-sounding words: "I will not ask, nor will I test the LORD!" (7:12). Isaiah answered by asking whether the house of David would "weary my God also" (7:13). Second Kings records that Ahaz, under attack from these same two kings, sent silver and gold to the king of Assyria and asked him for rescue (2 Kings 16:7–9). Isaiah 7 does not say when that appeal was made or what Ahaz was thinking when he refused the sign. What the chapter shows is the refusal itself, set against God's warning in 7:9.

Isaiah then turned from the king to the whole royal family: "Hear now, O house of David!" (7:13). "Therefore the Lord Himself will give you a sign: Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a Son, and shall call His name Immanuel" (7:14). God's answer to a faithless king was the promise of a child born to a maiden, a child whose name means "God with us." In Isaiah's own day the sign included a time limit: before such a child could tell good from evil, the two invading kings would be gone (7:16). But the promise did not stop there. The name Immanuel returns in Isaiah 8:8–10, and Isaiah 9:6–7 announces a Child to be born who will be called "Mighty God" and will reign on David's throne forever. These passages show that the sign looked far beyond Ahaz's crisis.

Genesis 24:43 and Exodus 2:8: maidens at the water. Abraham's servant stood "by the well of water" and asked God to show him which ʻalmâh was the right wife for Isaac. He would know her when she offered water to him and to his camels. Rebekah came and did exactly that, which led her to leave her home for a man she had never met. Through her, the line of promise continued.

Exodus 2 sets a similar scene at the Nile. Moses' sister, whom later passages name as Miriam, watched the basket among the reeds. When Pharaoh's daughter found the baby, the girl spoke up and offered to find a nurse. "So the maiden went and called the child's mother" (Exodus 2:8). In a land where Pharaoh had ordered Hebrew boys killed, a young girl arranged for Moses to be nursed by his own mother and paid from Pharaoh's household. In both stories the future of God's people depended on a girl at the water's edge. In both, she showed a readiness and courage that God used to preserve His promises.

Song of Solomon 1:3 and Psalm 68:25: maidens who love and rejoice. "Because of the fragrance of your good ointments, Your name is ointment poured forth; Therefore the virgins love you." The word "therefore" matters here. The maidens love the king because of who he is, and his name, which stands for his character and reputation, spreads like perfume. Psalm 68:25 shows the same gladness directed toward God. As His victorious procession enters the sanctuary, "Among them were the maidens playing timbrels." The maidens are at the center of the celebration, joyfully taking part in the praise of the triumphant Lord.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Matthew explains the birth of Jesus by quoting Isaiah: "So all this was done that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet, saying: 'Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and bear a Son, and they shall call His name Immanuel,' which is translated, 'God with us'" (Matthew 1:22–23). Centuries before Jesus was born, Jewish translators rendering Isaiah into Greek had used parthenos, the ordinary Greek word for a virgin, and Matthew follows that wording. Mary was betrothed to Joseph, and "before they came together, she was found with child of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 1:18). Because betrothal was a binding commitment, Matthew already calls Joseph "her husband" and Mary his "wife" (1:19–20), yet Joseph "did not know her till she had brought forth her firstborn Son" (1:25). Luke adds Mary's own words, "I do not know a man," and the angel's answer that the Holy Spirit would come upon her (Luke 1:34–35). In her, the fullest meaning of Isaiah's word came to light. Her virginity showed that this salvation came entirely from God's intervention. Human ability and royal strength contributed nothing.

The movement of the word across Scripture is striking. It begins with a girl drawing water so that Abraham's family can continue, and it reaches its height in a maiden who carries the eternal Son. The house of David failed through kings like Ahaz, yet God kept His promise to David through the quietest means imaginable.

The poetry of the maidens also points forward. Paul writes, "I have betrothed you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin to Christ" (2 Corinthians 11:2). In a comparable way, the church stands where the maidens of the Song stood, drawn by the King's name and waiting with undivided love for His appearing.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 7, read alongside Matthew 1 and Luke 1, places two responses to God side by side. In Isaiah, Ahaz had a throne, an army, and a direct offer from God, and he refused the sign God offered. In the Gospels, Mary had none of these, and she received the promise: "Let it be to me according to your word" (Luke 1:38). God was not hindered by the king's refusal. He gave a sign anyway, and it came through a young woman with no royal power. That should encourage anyone who feels small or unimportant. Rebekah was drawing water, and Miriam was watching her brother. Neither could see how much depended on her faithfulness in that ordinary moment. God sees what we cannot see in such moments.

The Song of Solomon also shows the reason behind genuine devotion: "Your name is ointment poured forth; Therefore the virgins love you." The best worship begins with a growing knowledge of the Lord's character. When believers come to know Christ's name, His mercy, His faithfulness, and His victory, love follows. Paul's "godly jealousy" for a church kept pure for Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2) reflects this same longing. He wants a people whose affection has not been divided by lesser loves.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 30:19 – Agur lists "the way of a man with a virgin" among things too wonderful for him, beside the flight of an eagle and a ship's path at sea. If the phrase describes courtship, as many readers take it, then the attraction between a man and a young woman belongs among the wonders God built into creation. Paul calls the union of husband and wife "a great mystery" that speaks "concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32), so on that reading Agur's wonder can lead a reader on to marvel at Christ's pursuit of His bride. That is a meditation suggested by one possible reading of the proverb; Agur does not draw the connection himself.

  • Song of Solomon 6:8 – "There are sixty queens And eighty concubines, And virgins without number." Against a crowded royal court, the king goes on to say that his beloved is the only one (6:9). Faithful love singles out one person from many.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1330 (bethulah) – A distinct Hebrew word, often more specifically "virgin," that overlaps with ʻalmâh. Both words describe Rebekah (Genesis 24:16, 43). Bethulah can also describe a woman mourning her husband (Joel 1:8).
