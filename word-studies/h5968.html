WRAPPED, FAINT, INLAID - (ʻâlaph, Strong's H5968)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʻâlaph means to wrap or cover something completely. It appears only five times in the Old Testament, and it moves in two directions. In Genesis, Tamar wraps herself in a veil, and in the Song of Solomon the beloved's body is "inlaid with sapphires," covered with jewels. In three prophetic passages, the same word describes people who faint, overcome by weakness as though something has closed over them.

Those three passages about fainting tell a sober story. Jerusalem's sons collapse under "the fury of the LORD," Israel's strongest young people faint when God withholds His word, and Jonah wilts under a wind God Himself sent. Each time, the collapse comes when God acts. Human strength gives out under His discipline, and it gives out when people are cut off from His voice. Read in the light of the whole Bible, these verses point toward the One who drank the cup of wrath for His people and who satisfies the thirst that no water can reach.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻâlaph is a Hebrew verb with a basic sense of enwrapping or covering. Its five uses come from Genesis, the Song of Solomon, Isaiah, Amos, and Jonah: one in narrative, one in love poetry, and three in the prophets. In the narrative and the poetry, something is physically covered. In all three prophetic uses, a person faints.

Hebrew dictionaries connect the two meanings by treating fainting as an experience of being overcome or covered over, the way a person who swoons feels darkness close in. That link reasonably explains how one verb came to carry both meanings, but it should not be pressed into every verse. Context tells us which meaning a writer intends.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To wrap or cover thoroughly. Tamar "wrapped herself" in a veil (Genesis 38:14), and the beloved's body is "inlaid," or overlaid, with sapphires (Song of Solomon 5:14).

2. To faint or grow weak, to be overcome until strength fails. Jerusalem's sons "have fainted" (Isaiah 51:20), Israel's youth "shall faint from thirst" (Amos 8:13), and Jonah "grew faint" (Jonah 4:8).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The covering sense first appears in Genesis 38:14. Judah had withheld his son Shelah from Tamar, so she "took off her widow's garments, covered herself with a veil and wrapped herself," and sat by the road to Timnah. The wrapping hid her identity, and that disguise set up the exposure of Judah's wrongdoing. When he finally saw the truth, he admitted, "She has been more righteous than I" (Genesis 38:26). The story also carries the family line forward, because Tamar's son Perez stands in the line of David.

Wherever the word means fainting, the subject is a person, and the cause is named in the verse or the surrounding lines. Heat, thirst, and divine rebuke all appear as causes. The prophets never use the word for simple tiredness at the end of a day. They use it for people brought to the end of their strength.

4. Theological Significance

The three prophetic uses share a pattern. In every case God is the One acting. He pours out His fury on Jerusalem, He sends a famine of His word on Israel, and He prepares the east wind for Jonah. The fainting that follows comes from His hand. It is not random misfortune.

That pattern teaches something about God and about us. Human strength is real, but it was never meant to stand on its own. When God disciplines, the strongest people fold. When God goes silent, even "strong young men" collapse. These verses show God as holy, because His rebuke is not light. They also show that His acts do not all end the same way. Jerusalem's fainting is followed by a promise that the cup of His fury will be taken from her hand and given to those who afflicted her (Isaiah 51:22–23). Jonah's fainting is followed by a question that presses on his heart. Amos, by contrast, offers those who faint no promise of quick relief (Amos 8:11–13), and those who swear by "the sin of Samaria," the god of Dan and "the way of Beersheba" "shall fall and never rise again" (Amos 8:14). God can discipline in order to restore, and He can judge with finality, and these verses keep both in view. Where He disciplines His own, as with Jerusalem and Jonah, He has not stopped working for their good.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 51:20 — caught in the net of God's rebuke. Isaiah pictures Jerusalem after judgment: "Your sons have fainted, they lie at the head of all the streets, like an antelope in a net; they are full of the fury of the LORD, the rebuke of your God." An antelope is built for speed, but once a net falls over it, the animal is tangled and helpless. Jerusalem's sons lie in the most public places of the city, unable to rise. Isaiah explains why: they are "full of the fury of the LORD." The judgment is not happening near them. It fills them, and the result is complete collapse. The chapter then turns. God promises to take "the cup of trembling" out of Jerusalem's hand so that she will never drink it again, and to put it into the hand of those who afflicted her (Isaiah 51:22–23). The same God who brought the collapse announces its end for Jerusalem.

Amos 8:11–13 — fainting in the famine of the word. Amos makes the most searching use of this verb. God announces, "I will send a famine on the land, not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the LORD" (8:11). People "shall run to and fro, seeking the word of the LORD, but shall not find it" (8:12). Then: "In that day the fair virgins and strong young men shall faint from thirst" (8:13).

The thirst in Amos 8:13 comes right after God has said what kind of thirst He means. The physical and the spiritual are bound together, but Amos 8:11 names the deeper deprivation: the loss of hearing God's words. Amos chose the very people a nation counts as its future and its strength. If the fair virgins and strong young men faint, no one is left standing. Israel had refused the prophets God sent (Amos 7:10–13), and now the word they rejected would be withheld. A people that silences God's voice eventually discovers it cannot live without that voice.

Jonah 4:8 — overcome so his heart could be exposed. After Nineveh repents, Jonah sits outside the city, angry at God's mercy. God provides a plant for shade, then a worm to destroy it, and then "God prepared a vehement east wind; and the sun beat on Jonah's head, so that he grew faint." Jonah answers, "It is better for me to die than to live." Each element is something God "prepared," the same verb the book uses for the fish, the plant, and the worm. Jonah's faintness is part of a lesson God is deliberately teaching. God then asks whether Jonah is right to be angry about the plant, and He sets Jonah's pity for one plant beside His own pity for a city of more than a hundred and twenty thousand people (Jonah 4:9–11).

Jonah's prayer from the fish uses a similar picture, but with a different Hebrew verb, ʻâṭaph (Strong's H5848), so Jonah 2:7 is a parallel rather than another use of ʻâlaph. Praying from the fish's belly (Jonah 2:1), Jonah looks back on sinking into the sea and says, "When my soul fainted within me, I remembered the LORD; And my prayer went up to You" (Jonah 2:7). In that earlier distress he turned to God in prayer. Under the sun he turns to self-pity. The same weakness can drive a person toward God or deeper into resentment.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah's picture of a people "full of the fury of the LORD" belongs to a larger biblical image of God's wrath as a cup (Isaiah 51:17). In Isaiah 51:22–23, God takes that cup out of Jerusalem's hand and gives it to her oppressors; that is the promise as Isaiah gives it. The New Testament invites a further comparison that Isaiah does not state. In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed, "let this cup pass from Me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as You will" (Matthew 26:39), and then He went to the cross. The cup in Gethsemane is a canonical comparison, but the truth that Christ bore God's judgment for others is taught plainly elsewhere: "He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities; The chastisement for our peace was upon Him" (Isaiah 53:5), and those justified by His blood "shall be saved from wrath through Him" (Romans 5:9). The One who never deserved to collapse under God's rebuke carried it, so that those who were tangled in that net could be set free.

Amos himself holds out no promise of relief for those who faint, but the wider biblical story does answer the famine he describes. John calls Jesus the Word made flesh (John 1:14), and Jesus said, "He who believes in Me shall never thirst" (John 6:35). As a gospel application, the thirst Amos describes, a thirst for words from God, finds its answer in a Person who is Himself God's final word to His people (Hebrews 1:1–2).

Jonah's story points to Christ in His own words. Jesus named "the sign of the prophet Jonah" and said that "a greater than Jonah is here" (Matthew 12:39–41). Jonah fainted, resenting God's mercy toward a pagan city. Christ gave Himself so that mercy could reach the nations. Even Tamar's veil connects to this story, since Matthew names her in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:3).

7. Spirit & Life

These verses tell the truth about human strength. Isaiah shows Jerusalem's sons lying faint in the streets, Amos shows the young and strong collapsing, and Jonah shows a prophet wishing to die. Scripture does not flatter our endurance. That honesty is a mercy, because it keeps us from building our lives on strength that will fail.

Amos speaks to any believer who has grown careless about God's voice. Spiritual faintness rarely starts with a crisis. It starts when we stop listening. A person can keep running "to and fro," busy and active, and still be dying of thirst. Amos was not warning about a shortage of Bibles. He was warning a people who had refused to listen until listening was no longer offered. Hearing God now, while He speaks, is a gift not to be presumed upon.

Jonah's two collapses ask a searching question: when I reach the end of my strength, which way do I turn? In his prayer from the fish, Jonah recalled that when his soul fainted, he remembered the LORD. Under the sun, his faintness led him to argue for his own way. God sometimes lets us faint so that what is in our hearts comes to the surface. When that happens, His questions are better than our answers. The God who asked Jonah about the city is the God who gives power to the weak (Isaiah 40:29), and Christ died for us "when we were still without strength" (Romans 5:6).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Song of Solomon 5:14 — "His body is carved ivory inlaid with sapphires." The bride describes her beloved as precious material covered with jewels. Here the verb's covering sense serves beauty instead of disguise, picturing a surface wholly adorned.

The remaining four occurrences, Genesis 38:14, Isaiah 51:20, Amos 8:13, and Jonah 4:8, are discussed above.
