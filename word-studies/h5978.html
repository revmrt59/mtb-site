WITH ME, BY ME, FROM ME - (ʻimmâd, Strong's H5978)

Summary

ʻImmâd is a Hebrew preposition meaning "with," closely related to the much more common ʻim. It carries the ending "me" (ʻimmâdî, "with me"). Context decides what being "with me" means. It can describe a person standing beside the speaker, an action aimed at the speaker for good or harm, something the speaker carries inwardly, or something that belongs to the speaker. With the prefix "from," it marks movement away from the speaker. English readers seldom see one consistent word, because the NKJV renders it as "with me," "by Me," "toward me," "within me," "Mine," and even "take." Job uses it repeatedly, speaking of God's arrows "within me" and also of God knowing "the way that I take." Noticing the word lets readers hear how personally these speakers place God's presence, God's anger, and God's knowledge right beside themselves.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

ʻImmâdî places someone or something in relation to the speaker. Like the English "with," its basic sense is closeness or association, and Hebrew uses that closeness in several directions. A companion can be "with me." Kindness or harm can be done "with me," meaning toward me. Pain, honor, or a way of life can be "with me" as part of my own experience. An animal can be "with Me" in the sense that it belongs to God. When the speaker adds "from" (mēʻimmâdî), the word shows departure from the speaker or a request made of the speaker. The word rarely changes the logic of a sentence. Its role is relational: it tells the reader where things stand in relation to the person talking.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Presence and accompaniment ("with me," "by Me")

In Genesis 3:12 Adam blames "the woman" whom God gave to be with him (paraphrase). The word that once described companionship becomes part of an excuse. In Deuteronomy 5:31 God tells Moses to stand there by Him (paraphrase). While the people stay at a distance, Moses is stationed beside God to receive the commandments.

Action directed toward the speaker ("toward me," "with me," "me")

Hebrew often speaks of doing something "with" a person where English says "to" or "toward." Lot thanks the angels for the mercy shown to him (Genesis 19:19, paraphrase). Jacob says God did not allow Laban to harm him (Genesis 31:7, paraphrase). Job turns the same pattern into complaint: "You renew Your witnesses against me, And increase Your indignation toward me" (Job 10:17). He also asks whether God would contend with him in great power (Job 23:6, paraphrase). Context decides whether the action is kind or hostile.

What is "with" the speaker: inner experience, conduct, belonging ("within me," "take," "Mine")

Sometimes ʻimmâdî describes what a person carries. Job cries, "For the arrows of the Almighty are within me" (Job 6:4). In happier days, his honor was fresh within him (Job 29:20, paraphrase). In Job 23:10, "He knows the way that I take," the Hebrew reads more literally "the way that is with me." This means Job's settled path of life, not a single step. In Psalm 50:11, God says, "the wild beasts of the field are Mine," literally "with Me." Here ʻimmâdî expresses possession.

Separation or source, with "from" ("from me," "of me")

Samuel tells Saul what will happen when he departs from him that day (1 Samuel 10:2, paraphrase). Jonathan tells Saul that David earnestly asked permission of him to go to Bethlehem (1 Samuel 20:28, paraphrase). In both cases, mēʻimmâdî marks the speaker as the point of departure or the one approached.

3. Practical Reading Insight

Job uses ʻimmâdî more than any other speaker, and his uses reveal his struggle. God's arrows are "with" him (6:4). God's indignation increases "toward" him (10:17). He wonders whether God would contend "with" him (23:6). Then, in chapter 23, Job admits he cannot find God anywhere, forward or backward, left or right (23:8–9, paraphrase). Yet he declares, "But He knows the way that I take." The way that is "with" Job, his whole manner of life, is fully known to God, even when God seems absent. That conviction lets Job say, "When He has tested me, I shall come forth as gold."

Psalm 50:11 makes a related point from God's side. "The wild beasts of the field are Mine" stands parallel to "I know all the birds of the mountains." The God who needs no sacrifices from Israel owns the animals because nothing in His creation lies outside His knowledge.

4. Reading Guidance

Don't expect one English word to signal ʻimmâdî. "Within," "toward," "Mine," and "take" can all represent it. Don't treat the longer form as automatically more intense than the ordinary ʻim either. Job 10:17 uses both forms for "with me" in a single verse. Let the context, not the form alone, determine whether "with me" means companionship, hostility, inner experience, or ownership.
