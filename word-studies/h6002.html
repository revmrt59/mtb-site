AMALEK - (ʻĂmâlêq, Strong's H6002)

Summary

Amalek is first the name of a grandson of Esau, born to Eliphaz by his concubine Timna (Genesis 36:12), and then, far more often, the name of the desert people descended from him. The meaning of the name is uncertain. Proposed derivations exist, but none is established, and Scripture itself never builds on the name's meaning. The Amalekites lived as nomads and raiders in the Negev and Sinai regions south of Canaan. They enter Israel's story as the first nation to attack God's newly redeemed people. At Rephidim, shortly after the Red Sea and the water from the rock, "Amalek came and fought with Israel" (Exodus 17:8). Moses later told Israel what made that attack so wicked. Amalek struck "the stragglers at your rear, when you were tired and weary; and he did not fear God" (Deuteronomy 25:18).

Because of that attack, the LORD declared that He "will have war with Amalek from generation to generation" (Exodus 17:16), and the conflict runs through the Old Testament. It appears in the raids of the Judges era, in Saul's disobedience that cost him the kingdom (1 Samuel 15), in David's rescue of the captives of Ziklag (1 Samuel 30), and in the defeat of the last Amalekite remnant in Hezekiah's day (1 Chronicles 4:43). Many readers also see the conflict echoed in Esther, where Haman "the Agagite" seeks to destroy the Jews. The story of Amalek shows a God who sees the weary when others prey on them. Victory over His enemies comes through dependence on Him, not human strength. The king God approves is one who shares God's judgments fully, finds his strength in the LORD, and protects the weak. That pattern finds its fullest expression in the Son of David, who will reign until every enemy is put under His feet.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The name Amalek refers to two connected realities. The first is an individual: Amalek, son of Eliphaz and grandson of Esau, born to Eliphaz's concubine Timna (Genesis 36:12). He is listed among the chiefs descended from Eliphaz in Edom (Genesis 36:16), and the Chronicler includes him in the same family line (1 Chronicles 1:36). This places Amalek's origins inside the wider family of Abraham. Through Esau he was a distant relative of Jacob's sons, and that makes his descendants' later hostility toward Israel all the more striking.

Most passages use Amalek for the nation that bore his name. In most of its occurrences, "Amalek" is a collective name for the Amalekite people, much as "Israel" can mean either Jacob or his descendants. A related gentilic word, "Amalekite" (H6003), is indexed separately. It appears in passages such as Numbers 14:45, 1 Samuel 30:1, and 2 Samuel 1:8 and 13, and it helps fill out the story, but it is a distinct lexical entry. Genesis 14:7 also mentions "the country of the Amalekites" in Abraham's day, before Esau's grandson was born. The most natural explanation is that the narrator uses the region's later, familiar name, but the text does not settle the question.

The meaning of the name Amalek is uncertain. Some scholars have proposed connections to roots suggesting "dweller in the valley" or to a word related to labor or trouble, but these suggestions are speculative. The Bible offers no wordplay on the name. Its theological weight comes entirely from what Amalek did and how God responded, not from what the name might mean.

Geographically, the Amalekites belonged to the southern wilderness. The spies reported that "the Amalekites dwell in the land of the South" (Numbers 13:29, paraphrasing the setting), and Saul's campaign ranged "from Havilah all the way to Shur, which is east of Egypt" (1 Samuel 15:7, described). Their raids were mobile and often made on camels (Judges 6:5; 1 Samuel 30:17), though they were not only wanderers; Saul came to "a city of Amalek" (1 Samuel 15:5). The Song of Deborah's reference to Ephraim's "roots" in Amalek (Judges 5:14), together with "the mountains of the Amalekites" in Ephraim (Judges 12:15), suggests that the name was attached to some territory farther north as well. The exact sense of those references is unclear.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The First Attack at Rephidim (Exodus 17)

Israel had just crossed the Red Sea, been fed with manna, and received water from the rock at Rephidim. Then the first enemy appeared: "Now Amalek came and fought with Israel in Rephidim" (Exodus 17:8). Israel had not invaded Amalek's land. Amalek came to Israel. This was Israel's first battle as a free people, and God arranged it in a way the nation would never forget. Moses told Joshua, "Choose us some men and go out, fight with Amalek. Tomorrow I will stand on the top of the hill with the rod of God in my hand" (17:9).

The battle in the valley turned on what happened on the hill: "when Moses held up his hand, that Israel prevailed; and when he let down his hand, Amalek prevailed" (17:11). Moses could not keep his hands raised on his own. "Aaron and Hur supported his hands, one on one side, and the other on the other side; and his hands were steady until the going down of the sun" (17:12). Joshua's soldiers did real fighting, and "Joshua defeated Amalek and his people with the edge of the sword" (17:13). Still, the outcome plainly depended on the uplifted rod of God.

Then the LORD gave Moses a command that looked far beyond that day: "Write this for a memorial in the book and recount it in the hearing of Joshua, that I will utterly blot out the remembrance of Amalek from under heaven" (17:14). Moses built an altar and named it "The-LORD-Is-My-Banner" (17:15), explaining, "Because the LORD has sworn: the LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation" (17:16). The Hebrew behind "the LORD has sworn" is difficult and is often rendered literally as "a hand upon the throne of the LORD." Interpreters debate whether this describes God's raised hand in an oath or Amalek's hand raised against God's throne. Either way, the attack is treated as more than a border skirmish. It touched the LORD's own honor and rule.

Wilderness Warnings (Numbers)

When the spies returned from Canaan, they listed the Amalekites among the peoples of the land, living in the South (Numbers 13:29). After Israel refused to enter the land and then tried to go up in their own strength against Moses' warning, the Amalekites and Canaanites drove them back as far as Hormah (Numbers 14:43–45, using the related gentilic). The same enemy who was defeated when Moses' hands were raised won easily when Israel went out without the LORD.

Balaam, hired to curse Israel, instead pronounced blessing. In his final oracles he looked toward Amalek and declared that Amalek was "first among the nations" but that his end would be destruction (Numbers 24:20, paraphrased). "First" may refer to Amalek's place as the first nation to attack Israel, or to its standing among the peoples of the region. Earlier in Balaam's oracles he said Israel's king would be "higher than Agag" (Numbers 24:7), a name later borne by the Amalekite king of Saul's day.

The Command to Remember (Deuteronomy 25)

Forty years after Rephidim, Moses gave the new generation details Exodus had not recorded: "Remember what Amalek did to you on the way as you were coming out of Egypt, how he met you on the way and attacked your rear ranks, all the stragglers at your rear, when you were tired and weary; and he did not fear God" (Deuteronomy 25:17–18). Amalek had picked off the exhausted, the slow, and the lagging, the people least able to defend themselves. Scripture gives the root of that cruelty in five plain words: "he did not fear God."

The command that follows contains a deliberate tension. Israel must remember, and Israel must "blot out the remembrance of Amalek from under heaven." Moses closes with "You shall not forget" (25:19). This duty would come due "when the LORD your God has given you rest from your enemies all around." It was therefore a task for a settled nation under God's rule, not for wandering tribes.

Raiders in the Days of the Judges

During the Judges period, Amalek usually appears as a partner in other nations' oppression. Eglon of Moab gathered "the people of Ammon and Amalek" and seized the City of Palms (Judges 3:13). In Gideon's day the Midianites, Amalekites, and people of the East swept in at harvest time "as numerous as locusts," with countless camels, and destroyed Israel's produce until the people were badly impoverished (Judges 6:3–5, 33; 7:12). The pattern from Deuteronomy continued: Amalek fed on Israel's weakness. When Israel cried out again, the LORD reminded them that He had delivered them from the Amalekites among others (Judges 10:12). His record of rescue was the ground for their repentance.

Saul's Failure (1 Samuel 14–15, 28)

Saul fought the Amalekites early in his reign and "delivered Israel from the hands of those who plundered them" (1 Samuel 14:48, paraphrasing the sense). Then came the decisive test. Through Samuel the LORD recalled "what he did to Israel" on the way up from Egypt, speaking of Amalek, and commanded Saul to devote Amalek entirely to destruction (15:2–3). This was the judgment announced at Rephidim, now placed in the hands of Israel's king.

Before attacking, Saul warned the Kenites to withdraw, because they had shown kindness to Israel when Israel came out of Egypt (15:6). God's judgment was not blind hostility toward everyone in the region. It distinguished kindness from cruelty. Saul struck the Amalekites from Havilah to Shur (15:7). But he spared Agag the king and the best of the sheep, oxen, and fatlings, destroying only what was "despised and worthless" (15:8–9, described).

Saul then set up a monument to himself at Carmel (15:12), greeted Samuel by claiming he had obeyed, and blamed the people for keeping the animals "to sacrifice to the LORD" (15:15, 20–21). Samuel answered that obedience is better than sacrifice and that rebellion is like the sin of witchcraft and stubbornness like idolatry (15:22–23). Saul admitted the real reason: "I feared the people and obeyed their voice" (15:24). The man who would not fully carry out God's judgment on a nation that "did not fear God" turned out to fear people more than God. Samuel himself put Agag to death before the LORD at Gilgal (15:32–33). The kingdom was torn from Saul and given to "a neighbor of yours, who is better than you" (15:28).

The issue returned at the end of Saul's life. On the eve of his death, in the scene at En Dor, Saul heard that the LORD had turned from him because he did not obey the LORD's voice or execute His fierce wrath on Amalek (1 Samuel 28:18). Saul's failure with Amalek is presented as the hinge of his rejection.

David's Victory at Ziklag (1 Samuel 30)

While David was away, the Amalekites raided Ziklag, burned it, and carried off the women and children, including David's own wives (1 Samuel 30:1–5). His men talked of stoning him, but "David strengthened himself in the LORD his God" (30:6). He asked the LORD whether to pursue and received the answer that he would surely overtake them and recover all (30:8).

Two details in this chapter connect directly back to Deuteronomy 25. First, David's men found a sick Egyptian slave whom his Amalekite master had abandoned three days earlier (30:11–13). Amalek was still discarding the weak. David fed the man, and the abandoned slave led him to the raiders. Second, two hundred of David's men were too exhausted to cross the Brook Besor. When some of the fighters wanted to keep the spoil from them, David refused and made it a lasting rule that those who stayed with the supplies would share equally with those who went into battle (30:21–25). Amalek attacked the weary. David protected and honored them.

David struck the raiders, and only four hundred young men escaped on camels (30:17). "David recovered all" (30:18–19, described), and he sent gifts from the spoil to the elders of Judah (30:26). Saul spared Amalek's best for himself and his people. David took Amalek's spoil and gave it away.

Saul's Death and David's Kingdom (2 Samuel 1, 8; 1 Chronicles 18)

David had just returned "from the slaughter of the Amalekites" (2 Samuel 1:1) when an Amalekite arrived with Saul's crown and bracelet and claimed to have killed the wounded king (1:6–10). His account does not match 1 Samuel 31, where Saul falls on his own sword, so he was probably lying in hope of a reward. David had him executed for claiming to have struck the LORD's anointed (1:14–16). Saul's crown, lost over Amalek, came to David in an Amalekite's hand. Later, as king, David dedicated to the LORD the silver and gold taken from Amalek and other nations (2 Samuel 8:11–12; 1 Chronicles 18:11).

The Last Remnant and the Psalmist's Prayer

The Chronicler records that in Hezekiah's days five hundred men of Simeon went to Mount Seir and "defeated the rest of the Amalekites who had escaped," settling there afterward (1 Chronicles 4:42–43, described). This is the final historical defeat of Amalek recorded in the books that use the name.

Amalek also lives on in Israel's prayers. Psalm 83 lists a confederacy of nations conspiring against God's people: "Gebal, Ammon, and Amalek; Philistia with the inhabitants of Tyre" (Psalm 83:7). In that psalm the nations seek to wipe out Israel's name (83:4, described), the very fate God had decreed for Amalek. Amalek had become a byword for those who plot against the people of God.

The Echo in Esther

The word Amalek does not appear in Esther, but the book's opponent is "Haman, the son of Hammedatha the Agagite" (Esther 3:1, described). The Agagite designation (H91, separately indexed) is widely understood to link Haman to Agag, the Amalekite king Saul spared. Mordecai, meanwhile, is introduced as a Benjamite descended from a man named Kish (Esther 2:5), the same tribe and family name as Saul, son of Kish. Whether this Kish is Saul's father or a later relative is not stated, but the echo is hard to miss. A Benjamite of the house of Kish now stands against an Agagite. Haman plots to destroy all the Jews and is himself destroyed. When the Jews defend themselves, the text pointedly notes, more than once, that they did not lay a hand on the plunder (Esther 9:10, 15–16). Saul kept the spoil of Amalek. Mordecai's generation refused it.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly why God set Himself against Amalek. Amalek attacked a people God had just redeemed. It came out of its way to do so, it targeted the weary, and it "did not fear God" (Deuteronomy 25:18). The LORD's sworn war (Exodus 17:16) was not ethnic hatred. It was His settled judgment on a people who preyed on the vulnerable and defied Him, a judgment carried out over many generations, during which Amalek kept raiding. Even then the judgment was discriminating. The Kenites who had shown kindness were spared (1 Samuel 15:6). The command to destroy Amalek was a specific judicial sentence given to Israel at a particular time, not a general license for violence. It belongs to the same God who later sent His Son to bear judgment for sinners.

The Amalek narratives also serve as a test of kingship in Israel. This is a strongly supported reading of how Samuel is arranged, not a claim the text states in so many words. The king of God's people must carry out God's purposes fully and fear God more than people. Saul's partial obedience over Amalek is the reason Scripture gives for the loss of his kingdom (1 Samuel 15:23, 28; 28:18). David's conduct at Ziklag shows the opposite: he strengthens himself in the LORD, asks God's direction, rescues the captives, protects the weary, and gives away the spoil. The contrast prepares the reader for God's choice of David's house.

Finally, Amalek shows that God keeps His word across long stretches of time. He told Moses to write the judgment in a book (Exodus 17:14) and commanded Israel not to forget (Deuteronomy 25:19). Rephidim, the Judges era, Saul, David, and Hezekiah's day lie centuries apart, yet the same word is still being worked out. If the Esther connection is accepted, God also completes through Mordecai what Saul left undone. That is a reasonable canonical inference rather than an explicit statement. Either way, God's purposes are not cancelled by human failure.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never mentions Amalek, so the Christ-centered connections here rest on canonical patterns rather than direct New Testament interpretation. Several of them are well grounded.

The first concerns the king who triumphs. Balaam's oracle sets Amalek's doom beside the rise of a ruler from Jacob, a "Star" and "Scepter" (Numbers 24:17–20, described), and mentions a king higher than Agag (24:7). David partly fulfilled this, but Israel came to read the oracle as pointing beyond him to the Messiah. The New Testament announces that Christ "must reign till He has put all enemies under His feet" (1 Corinthians 15:25, described). Amalek's long story, ending in complete defeat, fits within that larger promise that every enemy of God's kingdom will fall.

The second is the contrast between Saul and David, which points forward to the Son of David. At Ziklag David recovers everything that was stolen, rescues a discarded slave, and insists that the weary share fully in the victory. These actions resemble Christ's mission to proclaim liberty to the captives (Luke 4:18, quoting Isaiah 61) and His welcome to those who labor and are heavy laden (Matthew 11:28). Scripture does not call Ziklag a type of Christ, but the resemblance grows out of the shape of David's kingship as God's chosen king.

The third is intercession. At Rephidim, victory in the valley depended on Moses' raised hands on the hill. The battle was won by the sword, yet it was decided by a mediator standing before God. This thematic resonance should not be pressed into a formal type. Still, it is fitting that the fighter in the valley was Joshua, whose name in Greek is the same as Jesus' (compare Hebrews 4:8), and that believers now have a Savior who "always lives to make intercession" for them (Hebrews 7:25). Unlike Moses, His hands never grow heavy.

Christian writers have long treated Amalek as a picture of the flesh, the persistent sinful nature that wars against the Spirit (Galatians 5:17) and must be put to death rather than spared (Colossians 3:5). This is an application rooted in later interpretation, not an identification Scripture makes. It is a useful lesson when kept in proportion, especially in light of Saul's attempt to keep the "best" of what God had condemned.

5. Spirit & Life

Deuteronomy 25 reveals that God noticed the stragglers. When Amalek struck the tired and weary at the back of the line, the LORD took it personally, and He did not let the wrong be forgotten. The people who fall behind are not invisible to Him. Exploiting weakness is an act of contempt toward God Himself, because it shows that the attacker "did not fear God." David's rule at the Brook Besor shows what God's people should look like instead: the weary are not abandoned, and they share fully in the victory.

Rephidim shows how God's battles are won. Joshua had to fight, but Moses had to stand before God with the rod in his hand, and even Moses needed Aaron and Hur to hold his arms up. Prayer and action belong together, and no believer can sustain either one alone. Sometimes the most important help we can give another servant of God is the help Aaron and Hur gave: standing beside him and holding him up until the sun goes down.

Saul's story is a sober warning about partial obedience. He did most of what God commanded, kept what he valued, and dressed up his choice in the language of worship. Samuel's answer has not changed: obedience is better than sacrifice. The root of Saul's failure was fear of people, and the cure is the fear of God that Amalek lacked. Whatever God has condemned in our lives is not improved by sparing its best parts.

The whole history of Amalek also teaches God's patience and faithfulness. He told Israel to remember, and He Himself remembered. A word He spoke in the wilderness still stood in David's day, in Hezekiah's day, and, if we follow the echo into Esther, in Persia. When His servants fail, as Saul did, His purpose does not fail. Like David at Ziklag, believers facing loss and attack can strengthen themselves in the LORD their God, because the LORD who swore to have war with Amalek (Exodus 17:16) has also promised that He will finish what He began.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:12, 16; 1 Chronicles 1:36 – Amalek's origin as grandson of Esau through Eliphaz and Timna, and his place among Edom's chiefs.

  • Exodus 17:8–16 – The unprovoked attack at Rephidim, the victory tied to Moses' raised hands, the written memorial, the altar "The-LORD-Is-My-Banner," and the LORD's war "from generation to generation."

  • Numbers 13:29; 14:43–45 – Amalek in the South and Israel's defeat when it went up without the LORD.

  • Numbers 24:7, 20 – Balaam's oracle on Amalek's prominence and coming ruin, with mention of Agag.

  • Deuteronomy 25:17–19 – The moral verdict: Amalek attacked the weary and "did not fear God," and Israel is commanded to remember.

  • Judges 3:13; 6:3–5, 33; 7:12; 10:12 – Amalek as a raider allied with Moab and Midian, and God's reminder of past deliverance.

  • 1 Samuel 15:1–35 – Saul's partial obedience, the sparing of Agag, Samuel's verdict, and the rejection of Saul's kingship.

  • 1 Samuel 28:18 – Saul's failure with Amalek named as the reason for his downfall.

  • 1 Samuel 30:1–26 – David's strength in the LORD, the abandoned Egyptian slave, the recovery of all, and the shared spoil at the Brook Besor.

  • 2 Samuel 1:1–16; 8:12; 1 Chronicles 18:11 – The Amalekite with Saul's crown, and Amalek's spoil dedicated to the LORD.

  • 1 Chronicles 4:42–43 – The last remnant of Amalek defeated by Simeonites in Hezekiah's day.

  • Psalm 83:7 – Amalek named in the confederacy plotting against God's people.

  • Esther 2:5; 3:1; 9:10, 15–16 – Mordecai the Benjamite of Kish's line against Haman the Agagite, and the refusal of plunder (a related canonical echo; the name Amalek does not appear).
