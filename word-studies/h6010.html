VALLEY - (ʻêmeq, Strong's H6010)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻêmeq means a valley in the sense of a broad, open lowland. It describes the wide, fairly flat land between ranges of hills, where farmers grew grain and armies could gather in great numbers. In Scripture these valleys are where harvests ripen and where kings meet. They are also where Israel faced some of its most frightening enemies: iron chariots in the plains, the Midianite horde in Jezreel, and Goliath in the Valley of Elah.

The valleys of the Bible also become places where God makes Himself known. When the Syrians said the LORD ruled only the hills, He answered by winning in the valley (1 Kings 20:28). Several valleys were renamed for what God did there. The Valley of Achor, where Achan's sin brought death, is promised back to Israel as "a door of hope" (Hosea 2:15). The Valley of Berachah is named for the blessing offered after God's deliverance. The prophets also look ahead to a final valley, the Valley of Jehoshaphat, where the Lord will judge the nations. A believer who walks through low and exposed places can learn from these valleys that God rules the lowland as fully as the heights.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻêmeq is a common Hebrew word for geography. Most of Israel's people lived in the central hill country. The broad valleys, such as Jezreel, Elah, Rephaim and Aijalon, were the open plains below. These valleys were good farmland and natural roads. Because they were open ground, they were also the places where large armies and chariots could fight.

Hebrew uses other words for other kinds of valleys, such as narrow ravines and wide rift valleys. Some familiar "valley" passages use those other words. Psalm 23:4, "the valley of the shadow of death," and Ezekiel's valley of dry bones are two examples. Neither passage uses ʻêmeq, and this study does not treat them as part of its evidence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A broad valley, open plain or lowland, as when Saul and the men of Israel "encamped in the Valley of Elah" (1 Samuel 17:2) to face the Philistines. The next verse sets the scene more closely: the two armies stood on mountains on either side, "with a valley between them" (1 Samuel 17:3). There the NKJV's "valley" translates a different Hebrew word, so the broad Valley of Elah is the larger setting and the valley named in that verse is the ground between the two ridges.

2. A word used as part of a place name, such as the Valley of Shaveh, the Valley of Achor or the Valley of Rephaim. In one place the NKJV keeps the Hebrew word itself in the town name Emek Keziz (Joshua 18:21).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Many uses are simply geography. In Joshua, valleys mark tribal boundaries and territories (Joshua 13:19, 27; 15:7–8). Other uses describe the land's fruitfulness. The psalmist writes, "The valleys also are covered with grain; They shout for joy, they also sing" (Psalm 65:13). The bride in the Song calls herself "the lily of the valleys" (Song of Songs 2:1), comparing herself to a flower growing in fertile lowland.

A frequent use is military. The valley is where an enemy gathers and where Israel is most exposed. The Midianites "encamped in the Valley of Jezreel" (Judges 6:33), as many as locusts (Judges 7:12). The Philistines spread out in the Valley of Rephaim (2 Samuel 5:18, 22). Israel camped in the Valley of Elah, then stood on one mountain while the Philistines stood on another, with a valley between them (1 Samuel 17:2–3).

Several valleys were given new names after God acted there, including Achor and Berachah. The name of such a valley tells what God did in it.

4. Theological Significance

The valley is where Israel's own strength ran out. Judah took the hill country, "but they could not drive out the inhabitants of the lowland, because they had chariots of iron" (Judges 1:19; see Joshua 17:16). The infantry tactics that worked in the hills were useless against chariots on open ground. Israel could not fully possess the land God had promised without help beyond its own strength. God later gave that help. Deborah's song recalls Issachar following Barak "into the valley" (Judges 5:15) against Sisera, whose nine hundred chariots God overthrew (Judges 4).

First Kings 20 makes the point in plain words. After their defeat in the hills, the Syrian advisers reasoned that Israel's gods were mountain gods and planned to fight on level ground (1 Kings 20:23). God answered through a man of God: "Because the Syrians have said, 'The LORD is God of the hills, but He is not God of the valleys,' therefore I will deliver all this great multitude into your hand, and you shall know that I am the LORD" (1 Kings 20:28). The Syrians tried to limit God to a region. God treated that claim as a challenge to His own name, and He answered it in the very terrain where Israel was weakest.

God's valley victories carry a warning as well. Isaiah says the LORD will rise up "as in the Valley of Gibeon," where He once fought for Joshua (Isaiah 28:21; Joshua 10:12). This time, however, He will act against His own disobedient people, and Isaiah calls it His "unusual act." The God who fights for His people in the valley cannot be used as a tool by people who refuse Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The Valley of Achor (Joshua 7:24–26; Hosea 2:15; Isaiah 65:10). After Jericho fell, Achan secretly kept goods that God had devoted to destruction. Because of his sin, Israel was defeated at Ai. Joshua brought Achan and his household into a valley and asked, "Why have you troubled us?" Achan was stoned there, and a great heap of stones was raised over him. The place was then called the Valley of Achor, which means Trouble (Joshua 7:25–26). The valley stood on Judah's border as a lasting monument to the cost of unfaithfulness (Joshua 15:7).

Centuries later, Hosea described Israel as an unfaithful wife. He announced that God would break Israel's bow "in the Valley of Jezreel" (Hosea 1:5). Then the tone changes. God says He will draw Israel into the wilderness and speak tenderly to her, and adds, "I will give her her vineyards from there, And the Valley of Achor as a door of hope; She shall sing there, As in the days of her youth" (Hosea 2:15). God does not choose a fresh location. He returns to the place that recorded Israel's shame and makes it the way back in. Isaiah adds that Achor will become "a place for herds to lie down, For My people who have sought Me" (Isaiah 65:10). The valley of execution becomes a place of rest.

The King's Valley (Genesis 14:17–24). In the first battle story of the Bible, the Valley of Siddim is full of asphalt pits. When the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah flee, some fall there and the rest escape to the mountains (Genesis 14:10). After Abram rescues Lot, the king of Sodom comes out to meet him in "the Valley of Shaveh (that is, the King's Valley)" (Genesis 14:17). In that valley Melchizedek, priest of God Most High, brings bread and wine and blesses Abram, and Abram gives him a tenth of everything. Abram then refuses Sodom's wealth, down to a thread or a sandal strap, so that no one could say a pagan king had made him rich (Genesis 14:23). In this first developed valley story, the open plain becomes a place of worship and of clear loyalty to God.

The Valley of Berachah (2 Chronicles 20). A huge alliance of enemies came against Judah. Jehoshaphat admitted that Judah had no power against them and that their eyes were on God (2 Chronicles 20:12). He sent singers ahead of the army. Then the LORD set the enemy armies against one another, and Judah never had to fight. The people spent three days gathering spoil. On the fourth day they assembled in a valley to bless the LORD, and they named it the Valley of Berachah, or Blessing (2 Chronicles 20:26). The kind of place where an enemy usually gathers became known by the name of the praise offered there.

The Valley of Decision (Joel 3). God declares, "I will also gather all nations, And bring them down to the Valley of Jehoshaphat; And I will enter into judgment with them there" (Joel 3:2). The name Jehoshaphat means the LORD judges. In Joel 3:12 the Lord says He will sit there to judge. A broad valley is wide enough for the whole world to stand in, and Joel pictures exactly that: "Multitudes, multitudes in the valley of decision! For the day of the LORD is near in the valley of decision" (Joel 3:14). The decision in this verse belongs to God. It is His final verdict on the nations, and no one in the crowd can escape it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The valleys of Scripture point toward Christ in several ways. Melchizedek, the priest who blessed Abram in the King's Valley, becomes the pattern for Christ's priesthood: Psalm 110:4 and Hebrews 7 declare Jesus a priest forever after his order. In the Valley of Elah, the armies of Israel stood frozen until a young shepherd from Bethlehem went down alone to fight the battle no one else could win. That picture matches the Son of David who faced sin and death on behalf of His people.

Achor offers another connection, which Scripture does not state directly but which the passages support. In Achor, sin brought trouble and judgment that ended in death under a heap of stones, yet God later promised that same valley as a door of hope. Paul names what Christ bore: "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). Achor is a historical picture, not Paul's subject, but it fits his teaching. Because Christ bore the curse of the law in our place, the trouble that sin brings can become a door of hope. Joel's valley also looks forward to the judgment Christ Himself will carry out. The Father has committed all judgment to the Son (John 5:22), and God has appointed a day when He will judge the world by that Man (Acts 17:31). Joel's own book contains the gospel promise that whoever calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved (Joel 2:32; Acts 2:21). The way of escape is offered before the multitudes gather in the valley.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 84 describes pilgrims whose hearts are set on the road to God's house: "As they pass through the Valley of Baca, They make it a spring; The rain also covers it with pools" (Psalm 84:6). The meaning of "Baca" is uncertain. It may mean weeping, or it may refer to trees that grow in dry ground. Either way, the valley is hard country. The psalm says that as the pilgrims pass through, they make it a spring, and the rain covers it with pools. The verse does not explain how the springs come, but its picture is clear: the hard valley does not stay the same when people bound for God's house walk through it. As an application, those who walk with God through a dry place can leave it better for others than they found it.

David's battles in the Valley of Rephaim teach how to face a valley. When the Philistines came, David asked the LORD whether to go up, and he won (2 Samuel 5:19–20). When they returned to the same valley, he asked again, and God gave him a completely different plan: he was to circle behind them and wait for the sound of marching in the mulberry trees (2 Samuel 5:23–24). Yesterday's victory did not replace today's prayer. A familiar trouble still calls for fresh dependence on God. It is in the open, exposed place where we are outmatched that God shows He is God of the valleys too.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 37:14 — Jacob "sent him out of the Valley of Hebron," and Joseph's errand began the road to betrayal, slavery and the saving of his family.

  • 2 Samuel 18:18 — Absalom set up a pillar to himself in the King's Valley. Abram had refused to be enriched by a king in that same valley, while Absalom built a monument to his own name.

  • Psalm 60:6 (also Psalm 108:7) — God declares, "I will divide Shechem And measure out the Valley of Succoth," claiming ownership of the land down to its broad plains.

  • Jeremiah 49:4 — Ammon is rebuked for boasting in its fruitful valleys and trusting its treasures, as if no one could come against it.

  • Micah 1:4 — When the LORD comes, the mountains melt under Him and the valleys split, like wax before the fire and like waters poured down a steep place. Even the broad, solid ground of the earth cannot stand before Him.
