ANAH - (ʻĂnâh, Strong's H6034)

Summary

Anah is the name of two closely related men among the Horites, the people who lived in the mountains of Seir, south of the Dead Sea, before Esau's descendants took the land. The first Anah was a son of Seir the Horite and one of the chiefs of his people (Genesis 36:20, 29). The second was his nephew, a son of Zibeon (Genesis 36:24). This younger Anah became the father of Aholibamah, one of Esau's wives, and of a son named Dishon (Genesis 36:2, 25). The name is generally connected with a Hebrew root meaning "to answer." Some connect it instead with a root meaning "to be afflicted" or "humbled." Its meaning is therefore probable rather than certain, and Scripture does not build any lesson on it.

Genesis pauses its list of names to remember one thing about the younger Anah: he "found the water in the wilderness as he pastured the donkeys of his father Zibeon" (Genesis 36:24). In dry country, a find like that could be remembered for generations. Through his daughter's marriage, Anah's family became part of Esau's household and the Edomite nation. The Bible keeps his name in both Genesis and 1 Chronicles. That small record shows a God who gave Seir to Esau as surely as He gave Canaan to Israel, and who keeps account of peoples outside the chosen line.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Two individuals bear this name, and both appear only in the genealogies of Genesis 36 and their parallel in 1 Chronicles 1.

Anah, son of Seir. He is listed among "the sons of Seir the Horite who inhabited the land: Lotan, Shobal, Zibeon, Anah, Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan" (Genesis 36:20–21). Genesis 36:29 names him again among the chiefs of the Horites. 1 Chronicles 1:38 repeats the list.

Anah, son of Zibeon. He is the nephew of the first Anah. "These were the sons of Zibeon: both Ajah and Anah. This was the Anah who found the water in the wilderness..." (Genesis 36:24; compare 1 Chronicles 1:40). The writer's added note, "This was the Anah who...," reads like a deliberate effort to tell this man apart from his uncle. Genesis 36:25 follows with "the children of Anah: Dishon and Aholibamah the daughter of Anah." 1 Chronicles 1:41 likewise names Dishon as his son. This Dishon is a different man from the Dishon who was a son of Seir (Genesis 36:21). Families in the ancient world often reused names across generations, and the repetition here does not mean the genealogy contradicts itself.

Genesis 36:2 calls Esau's wife "Aholibamah the daughter of Anah, the daughter of Zibeon the Hivite." Since Genesis 36:24 makes Anah the son of Zibeon, this is best read as daughter of Anah and granddaughter of Zibeon. Hebrew genealogies regularly use "daughter" and "son" for later descendants.

The name. ʻĂnâh is generally linked to the Hebrew root meaning "to answer." A connection with the similar-sounding root "to be afflicted" or "humbled" has also been proposed. The derivation is probable but not settled. The text makes no wordplay on the name, and it should not be read as a description of either man's life.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Horites were the earlier inhabitants of Seir, the rugged highland later called Edom. Moses explains, "The Horites formerly dwelt in Seir, but the descendants of Esau dispossessed them and destroyed them from before them, and dwelt in their place" (Deuteronomy 2:12). The elder Anah belonged to that older ruling order. He was one of the clan chiefs of a people whose land later passed to Esau's descendants; Scripture does not date that whole displacement to his lifetime.

Genesis records one link between the younger Anah's family and the newcomers: his daughter Aholibamah married Esau. Scripture does not record what political stance the family as a whole took. She bore him Jeush, Jaalam, and Korah, and each of them became a chief of Edom (Genesis 36:5, 14, 18). This Korah is an Edomite and should not be confused with the later Levite who rebelled against Moses. Through this marriage, Horite blood and Horite standing passed into the families that ruled Edom.

The one story told about Anah comes in Genesis 36:24. While he was doing ordinary work, pasturing his father's donkeys, he found something worth remembering in the wilderness. The Hebrew word, yemim, appears only here, and translators have long disagreed about it. The KJV renders it "mules." Many modern versions read "hot springs." The NKJV translates it "the water." Many modern translations prefer springs or water. In the dry land around the Arabah, finding dependable water was the kind of event that would keep a man's name alive in his clan's memory. Israel's law later forbade crossbreeding livestock (Leviticus 19:19), but that law says nothing directly about this verse, and the meaning of yemim should be decided on its own terms. What the text makes plain is that Anah's find was well enough known to identify him.

The Chronicler, writing centuries later, copies these names again (1 Chronicles 1:38–41). He sets Israel's story inside the wider family of nations, Edom included.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states that the Horites once held Seir and that Esau's descendants replaced them "just as Israel did to the land of their possession which the LORD gave them" (Deuteronomy 2:12). In the same chapter the Lord tells Israel not to provoke Edom, because He had given Mount Seir to Esau as a possession (Deuteronomy 2:5). Anah's family stands at the point of that handover. One Anah led the people who were passing away, and the other gave a daughter to the family that was rising.

A careful inference can be drawn from where Genesis places this chapter. Esau settles quickly. He marries into the local nobility, gathers wealth, and produces chiefs, and Genesis notes that Edom had kings "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). Jacob's family, by contrast, faces famine, a descent into Egypt, and long years of slavery before Israel possesses Canaan as a nation. Anah's marriage alliance with Esau belongs to this picture of early, visible success. Genesis does not condemn Anah. It does let readers see that the line carrying God's promise often moves more slowly than the nations around it.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

No Scripture connects Anah himself with Christ, and his finding of water should not be turned into a type of Christ as the living water. The larger story he belongs to does reach forward. The prophets speak of judgment on Edom's pride (Obadiah 1–4). Amos also foresees a restored house of David that would possess "the remnant of Edom" along with the nations called by the Lord's name (Amos 9:11–12). James cites that prophecy in Acts 15:16–17 when he explains the Gentiles' welcome into the church. The descendants of Anah's daughter were part of a people the prophets did not write off. That hope belongs to Edom as a whole and not to Anah personally, but it is the setting in which his name is preserved.

5. Spirit & Life

Anah's name can easily be skimmed past, and that makes it worth noticing that God kept it. Genesis records which Anah was which, who his children were, and the day he found water while watching his father's donkeys. This is not Israel's family, yet the Lord counted Seir as His gift to Esau and set its borders with care (Deuteronomy 2:5). People outside the chosen line were not outside God's attention.

Anah's moment of discovery came during plain, assigned work. Scripture shows a similar pattern more than once. Moses was keeping Jethro's flock when the Lord met him (Exodus 3:1), and David was with the sheep when Samuel sent for him (1 Samuel 16:11). No divine calling is recorded for Anah, and that difference should be kept clear. Still, the Bible's habit of placing significant moments in ordinary duties is real, and it gives weight to faithful daily work.

Esau's quick rise through alliances with families like Anah's also carries a sober note. A people can gain land, leaders, and resources early, while the people of promise are still waiting. Genesis does not treat early success as proof of God's approval.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:2 – Identifies Aholibamah as Anah's daughter and Zibeon's granddaughter, and Esau's wife.

  • Genesis 36:14, 18 – Aholibamah's sons become chiefs of Edom, carrying Anah's family into Esau's nation.

  • Genesis 36:20–21, 29 – Anah son of Seir, a Horite chief.

  • Genesis 36:24 – Anah son of Zibeon, distinguished by his finding of water while pasturing donkeys.

  • Genesis 36:25 – Anah's children, Dishon and Aholibamah.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:38, 40–41 – The Chronicler's faithful repetition of both Anahs and Dishon.

  • Deuteronomy 2:5, 12 – The Lord's gift of Seir to Esau and the dispossession of the Horites.

  • Genesis 36:31 – Edom's kings before Israel's, the backdrop for Esau's rapid establishment.
