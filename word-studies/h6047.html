ANAMIM, ANAMITES - (ʻĂnâmîym, Strong's H6047)

Summary

The Anamim were an ancient people descended from Mizraim, the Hebrew name for Egypt, who was a son of Ham and a grandson of Noah. Their name appears only twice in the Bible. Both times it comes in the same line of the same genealogy: "Mizraim begot Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim" (Genesis 10:13; 1 Chronicles 1:11). Scripture tells us nothing else about them. We do not know where they lived with any certainty, what they did, or what became of them.

That silence is part of what makes them worth noticing. Genesis 10, often called the Table of Nations, records how Noah's family grew into the peoples of the world after the Flood. The Anamim have no story, yet God placed their name in His Word, and the Chronicler copied it again centuries later. Their brief appearance shows that the God of the Bible keeps account of every people on earth, including those history has forgotten. It also prepares for the purpose that runs through the whole Bible: God is gathering a redeemed people "out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation" (Revelation 5:9).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻĂnâmîym is a Hebrew proper noun, the name of a people group. It belongs to the genealogical lists of Genesis 10 and 1 Chronicles 1, which trace the descendants of Noah's three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth. The Anamim fall within the family of Ham, through his son Mizraim: "The sons of Ham were Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan" (Genesis 10:6).

Their exact location is unknown. Because they are counted among Egypt's descendants, they almost certainly lived somewhere within Egypt's sphere. Historical proposals have placed them in the oases west of the Nile or in the Nile Delta, but there is no settled agreement.

Hitchcock's dictionary of Bible names offers glosses such as "fountain," "answer," and "affliction," while Brown-Driver-Briggs treats the locality as unknown and the form as doubtful. Proposed meanings are guesses based on possible roots. The Bible uses the word only as a name, and nothing about the Anamim should be preached from a supposed word picture.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻĂnâmîym names a people descended from Mizraim, the Anamites. It has one sense. The NKJV keeps the Hebrew form, "Anamim," in both places it appears.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The ending -im in Hebrew is the usual masculine plural, and it matters here. In this part of the genealogy, Mizraim's "sons" all carry it: Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim, and the others that follow in Genesis 10:14. These are names of peoples, not individual men. When Genesis says that "Mizraim begot Ludim, Anamim…," it is telling us that a whole people traced its origin back to the family that became Egypt.

This shows how the Table of Nations works. "Begot" in this list can describe the rise of tribes and nations as well as a father's literal sons. The chapter maps the spread of humanity after the Flood, family by family and people by people, and it closes by saying so: these were the families of Noah's sons, and from them the nations spread across the earth after the Flood (Genesis 10:32, paraphrased).

The word never appears in narrative, law, poetry, or prophecy. Both occurrences sit in the same genealogical line, with identical wording.

4. Theological Significance

The Anamim are a small window onto a large truth: God is Lord over the origins and boundaries of every nation. Genesis 10 does more than record ethnic history. It places all peoples, Israel's neighbors and its enemies, the famous and the obscure, within one family descended from Noah and under one God.

Moses later puts this into a song: "When the Most High divided their inheritance to the nations, When He separated the sons of Adam, He set the boundaries of the peoples" (Deuteronomy 32:8). Paul says the same thing to the philosophers of Athens, who prided themselves on being a people apart. God "has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26). The Anamim had their own appointed time and place. What became of them is not recorded, but they were never outside God's knowledge or rule.

This list also makes a claim about the Bible itself. Genesis does not move from creation into myth. It roots the story of redemption in real human families who spread across real lands, and a people as obscure in surviving records as the Anamim stands in that record alongside Egypt and Canaan.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:13 — the Anamim among the nations. The Anamim appear in a chapter that sets up a quiet contrast. A few verses earlier, Ham's line produces Nimrod: "Cush begot Nimrod; he began to be a mighty one on the earth" (Genesis 10:8). Nimrod gathers power into a kingdom whose "beginning... was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar" (10:10). Babel then becomes the scene of Genesis 11, where people try to build a city and a tower so they will not be scattered, and God scatters them anyway.

As a literary observation, the plain list of Mizraim's descendants stands beside Nimrod's empire-building. After the Flood God told Noah's family to fill the earth (Genesis 9:1), and the Table of Nations shows peoples spreading out and becoming nations. Genesis does not record the Anamim's own choices, but the arrangement sets the scattering of families beside the pride of Babel. Nobody remembers their monuments. Their names were recorded all the same.

This passage also presents Egypt genealogically, as a family of related peoples. The same verse and the one after it trace the Philistines to Mizraim's line through the Casluhim (Genesis 10:14). The nations that later trouble Israel in Exodus and Judges are first introduced here as relatives in Noah's household.

1 Chronicles 1:11 — the Anamim remembered after the exile. The Chronicler repeats the line from Genesis word for word. He was writing for Israelites who had come back from Babylon to a small, weakened land, and he begins his history with Adam and the whole Table of Nations before he reaches David or the temple. Read in that setting, the list can remind a weakened community that their story belongs within God's dealings with all humanity. The God who chose Israel is the God who ordered every nation, down to the Anamim. Israel's calling had a worldwide setting from the very beginning.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Genesis 10 comes right before the call of Abram in Genesis 12. The order matters. First the reader sees the scattered nations, and then God calls one man and promises that through him all the families of the earth will be blessed (Genesis 12:3, paraphrased). The "families" of that promise are the families just listed in Genesis 10, and the Anamim are among them. God chose Abraham in order to bless the nations.

That promise reaches its fulfillment in Christ. The heavenly song of Revelation 5:9 praises the Lamb because "You were slain, And have redeemed us to God by Your blood Out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation." The Table of Nations lists who was scattered, and Revelation shows those peoples gathered again around the throne. What Babel divided, the blood of Christ gathers.

Mizraim's line also has a place in that hope. Through Isaiah, God speaks of a day when He will say, "Blessed is Egypt My people" (Isaiah 19:25, paraphrased). At Pentecost, people from Egypt and "the parts of Libya adjoining Cyrene" heard the gospel in their own languages (Acts 2:10, paraphrased). We cannot say whether any of them descended from the Anamim, since the tribe's location is uncertain. Acts 2 shows people from regions associated with Mizraim's line hearing the gospel in Jerusalem, though it does not say the gospel was then carried first to those regions.

7. Spirit & Life

The Anamim are a people of whom history remembers almost nothing. There are no deeds and no surviving inscriptions we can confidently link to them, and Scripture contains no narrative or oracle about them beyond these genealogies. Yet the Holy Spirit placed their name in Genesis, and then placed it there again in Chronicles. Human history usually keeps only the names of conquerors and builders. God's record keeps everyone.

This matters to anyone who feels hidden or small. A believer's worth before God does not depend on being noticed by the world. The same Lord who counted the Anamim numbers the hairs of our heads and knows every sparrow that falls (Matthew 10:29–30, paraphrased). Being forgotten by people is not the same as being forgotten by Him.

The Anamim also widen the believer's view of mission. If God tracked every obscure people from the beginning, then no group today lies beyond His concern or His gospel. The Church carries the message to peoples the world overlooks because God never overlooked them. Their name stands in Scripture as a small pledge of the multitude John saw, gathered from every nation.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word occurs only in Genesis 10:13 and 1 Chronicles 1:11, and both have been discussed in Section 5. The two verses read the same: "Mizraim begot Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim." The Chronicler's copy keeps the Genesis line unchanged.
