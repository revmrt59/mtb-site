FAWN, YOUNG - (ʻôpher, Strong's H6082)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻôpher means a fawn, the young of a deer or gazelle. It appears only five times in the Bible, and every use is in the Song of Solomon, where it serves as a picture of love. Three times the bride uses it for her beloved. She calls him "a young stag" who comes leaping over the mountains and whom she urges to hurry back to her. Twice the bridegroom uses it for the bride, comparing her beauty to "two fawns, twins of a gazelle." The same small animal pictures his eager strength and her tender loveliness.

That pairing gives the word unusual spiritual depth. Scripture calls God the Husband of His people and Christ the Bridegroom of the church. Read in that light, the leaping young stag pictures a Lord who crosses every distance to reach those He loves. The quiet fawns feeding among the lilies picture a people at rest and cherished in His care. The Song ends with the bride's cry, "Make haste, my beloved," and the Bible itself ends with the church's matching cry: "Even so, come, Lord Jesus!"

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻôpher belongs to the poetry of the Song of Solomon, a book of love songs between a bridegroom and his bride, the Shulamite. The word never appears in law, history, or prophecy. Each time it occurs, it is set beside the gazelle, an animal known for speed, grace, and alertness. In the hill country of Israel, a young deer was a familiar sight: slender, quick, easily startled, and lovely to watch. The poet uses that common sight to say something about the people he describes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻôpher has one plain meaning: the young of a deer or gazelle, a fawn. The NKJV translates it as "fawns" when the bridegroom describes the bride (Song of Solomon 4:5; 7:3). When the bride describes the bridegroom, the NKJV joins it to the word for stag and translates the phrase "young stag" (Song of Solomon 2:9, 17; 8:14). The word names the animal and its youth. Ideas such as energy, grace, and tenderness come from how the poet uses the image.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

All five uses are comparisons introduced by "like." The poet never tells the reader that the beloved is a stag. Instead, he points to something about a young deer and invites the reader to see that quality in a person.

The usage splits along the line between the two speakers, and the emphasis changes with each of them. In the bride's mouth, the image is full of motion. The young stag leaps, turns, and hurries over the mountains. In the bridegroom's mouth, the image is still and gentle: twin fawns "which feed among the lilies" (Song of Solomon 4:5). The animal and the word are the same, but they point to different qualities. The bridegroom is strong and comes swiftly. The bride is pictured as tender and at rest.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show love that moves toward the person it loves. In each of the young stag passages the movement belongs to the beloved: he comes, or the bride calls him to come, and her expectation rests on his coming. Scripture describes the Lord in the same way. "Your Maker is your husband," Isaiah tells Israel (Isaiah 54:5), and through Hosea God promises, "I will betroth you to Me forever... In lovingkindness and mercy" (Hosea 2:19). The God of the Bible takes the first step toward His people.

The fawn passages also show that delight goes both ways. The bride admires the strength of the one who comes to her, and the bridegroom treasures the tender beauty of the one he comes to. The Lord does not merely put up with His people. He looks at them with pleasure, and He wants their love in return.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:8–9: the stag at the wall. The bride hears him before she sees him: "The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes Leaping upon the mountains, Skipping upon the hills." Hills that would slow anyone else are no obstacle to him. Verse 9 then changes pace: "My beloved is like a gazelle or a young stag. Behold, he stands behind our wall; He is looking through the windows, Gazing through the lattice." The one who cleared the mountains stops at her wall. He does not break through it. He looks in and, in the verses that follow, calls her to rise and come away with him. His strength brings him close, and his love waits for her answer.

Song of Solomon 2:17 and 8:14: from Bether to the mountains of spices. The young stag appears twice more, both times in a closing plea from the bride. In 2:17 she says, "Until the day breaks And the shadows flee away, Turn, my beloved, And be like a gazelle Or a young stag Upon the mountains of Bether." The words "until the day breaks" may place her in the night, waiting for the shadows to lift, though the exact time of day is uncertain. Bether may be the name of a rugged place whose location is unknown. Many readers connect it with a Hebrew root meaning to cut in two and take it to mean division or separation. If that reading is right, it fits a scene of waiting with mountains between the lovers, but neither the setting nor the meaning of the name is certain. The book's final verse repeats the same plea with two changes: "Make haste, my beloved, And be like a gazelle Or a young stag On the mountains of spices" (8:14). "Turn" has become "Make haste," and the mountains of Bether have become mountains of spices, a place of fragrance associated with delight. The Song closes while the bride is still calling out to him and before he has arrived. Its last word is longing.

Song of Solomon 4:5 (with 7:3): fawns among the lilies. The bridegroom says, "Your two breasts are like two fawns, Twins of a gazelle, Which feed among the lilies." The point of the comparison is the impression the image gives, not a detailed matching of body parts. Twins suggest balance and symmetry, and fawns suggest softness and delicacy. Song of Solomon 7:3 repeats the comparison to twin fawns but does not mention lilies, so the feeding scene belongs to 4:5. There the setting adds a quiet, peaceful picture: young fawns grazing among the lilies, nourished in a place of beauty. The bridegroom is the one who sees his bride that way, tender, lovely and at rest.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament gives the reason for reading this love poetry beyond the marriage of a husband and wife. Paul quotes the creation teaching on marriage and then says, "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). Heaven celebrates because "the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7). The Song is first a true song of human love. Within the whole Bible, it also anticipates Christ and His bride.

Seen this way, the young stag leaping over the mountains pictures the Son of God coming down to His people across every distance of sin and estrangement. Read devotionally, the movement from the mountains of Bether to the mountains of spices can picture the church's own hope. The Song does not tell the church's history, but the church does wait "until the day breaks," and it looks toward the day when "the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3). The Song's last line and the Bible's last prayer echo each other. Christ says, "Surely I am coming quickly," and the church answers, "Even so, come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20).

The word "young" may add one more thought. Isaiah observes that "even the youths shall faint and be weary" (Isaiah 40:30), because natural youth does not last. Christ, however, is "alive forevermore" (Revelation 1:18), a priest "according to the power of an endless life" (Hebrews 7:16). This is an analogy rather than a direct teaching of the Song. Even so, it fits well: the Bridegroom's love never ages or grows tired.

7. Spirit & Life

Elsewhere the Bible uses deer to describe what God does for believers. "He makes my feet like the feet of deer, And sets me on my high places" (Psalm 18:33; 2 Samuel 22:34). Habakkuk, facing failed crops and empty stalls, says, "The LORD God is my strength; He will make my feet like deer's feet" (Habakkuk 3:19). In those passages God gives His people the ability to stand on dangerous heights. In the Song, the Beloved crosses the heights himself to reach His bride. Both pictures are true, and read together they suggest an order. Before He strengthens us to climb, He has already come over the mountains to find us.

The fawns feeding among the lilies offer a quiet picture of rest, and Psalm 23 supplies the teaching that belongs with it. "The LORD is my shepherd," David says, and "He makes me to lie down in green pastures" (Psalm 23:1–2). The sheep rest because of the Shepherd who leads them. David goes on to say that even in "the valley of the shadow of death" he will fear no evil, "For You are with me" (Psalm 23:4). The psalm does not promise a life without danger. It promises the Shepherd's presence in the middle of it. A believer can be fragile and still rest, because that rest depends on the One who watches over the flock.

The same word holds both sides of the Christian life together. The young stag pictures His strength and coming, and the fawns picture our rest and nourishment. Read together, the two images suggest that His coming over the mountains is what makes our quiet feeding possible. Even while we enjoy that rest, the Song's closing plea invites us to keep longing for Him. A heart that knows it is cherished does not grow content with distance. It prays, "Make haste, my beloved."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All five occurrences of ʻôpher are treated above: Song of Solomon 2:9, 2:17, 4:5, 7:3, and 8:14. Song of Solomon 7:3 repeats the comparison of 4:5, "Your two breasts are like two fawns, Twins of a gazelle." The repetition suggests that the bridegroom's delight in his bride has not faded as the Song moves toward its close.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H0355 (hind, female deer), a thematic image-family connection rather than a lexical relative. It lies behind the image of feet made like the feet of deer (Psalm 18:33; Habakkuk 3:19), where God enables His people to stand on the heights.

  • Gazelle (H6643, H6646) and stag (H0354), distinct words paired with ʻôpher in the comparisons, not relatives of the word. A gazelle is mentioned in all five comparisons (Song of Solomon 2:9, 17; 4:5; 7:3; 8:14). The stag appears only in Song of Solomon 2:9, 2:17 and 8:14, where it is joined to ʻôpher in the phrase translated "young stag." In Song of Solomon 4:5 and 7:3 the bride is compared instead to twin fawns of a gazelle.
