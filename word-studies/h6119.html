HEEL, FOOTSTEPS, REAR - (ʻâqêb, Strong's H6119)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻâqêb means heel. It names the back of the human foot or the hoof of an animal. Because the heel is the part of us that touches the ground and trails behind, the word also came to mean footsteps (the tracks a person leaves and the path a person walks) and the rear of something. The NKJV translates it as heel, footsteps, steps, hooves, rear guard, and "at last."

ʻâqêb appears only thirteen times, but it sits at the center of some of Scripture's most important moments. In Genesis 3:15, God promises that the woman's Seed will bruise the serpent's head while the serpent bruises His heel. Hebrew uses the same verb for both blows; the difference lies in where each one lands, on the head or on the heel. Christians have long seen in that promise the first announcement of the gospel, a victory that would come through real suffering, just as Hebrews 2:14 says Christ destroyed the devil "through death." In Psalm 41:9 a trusted friend "has lifted up his heel," and Jesus applies those words to Judas. Jacob is born gripping his brother's heel, and his name, Jacob, echoes that heel-grasping scene. In Psalm 77:19, God leads Israel through the sea, "and Your footsteps were not known." Across these passages the heel shows where we are weak and exposed. It also shows how God works to save, often in ways no human eye can trace.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻâqêb is an ordinary Hebrew noun for a body part. Its uses fall into clear settings. Some are narrative, such as Jacob's birth and Joshua's battle at Ai. Some are prophetic blessings, such as Jacob's words over his sons in Genesis 49. Others are poetry: Deborah's victory song, Job, the Psalms, and the Song of Solomon. One occurrence is in Jeremiah's prophecy of judgment. The heel is low, close to the dust, and behind us, where we cannot see it. Nearly every figurative use grows out of those plain physical facts.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The heel or hoof itself. This is the literal back of the foot, as in Genesis 3:15 and Genesis 25:26, or an animal's hoof, as in the galloping horses of Judges 5:22.

2. Footsteps or path. Because the heel leaves a print, the word can stand for the tracks a person leaves and the course of a person's life. Psalm 56:6 speaks of enemies who "mark my steps."

3. The rear. The heel is the back of the body, so the word can mean the rear guard of an army (Joshua 8:13). In Genesis 49:19 the NKJV renders it "at last," though the line can also picture Gad striking the rear of his attackers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the heel changes with what happens to it. When something strikes the heel or catches it, the heel is a point of weakness. The serpent bruises it (Genesis 3:15). Dan, pictured as a viper, bites a horse's heels "so that its rider shall fall backward" (Genesis 49:17). In Job 18:9 a net catches the wicked man by the heel. A heel can also be used against someone. To "lift up" the heel against a friend is an idiom for betrayal (Psalm 41:9). The picture may be of turning one's back and walking out, or of kicking or trampling the friend, and either way the meaning is a deliberate, insulting rejection.

When the word refers to footsteps, the focus moves to a person's path. Enemies watch that path (Psalm 56:6) or mock it (Psalm 89:51), and in the Song the footsteps of a flock show the way to the shepherds' tents (Song of Solomon 1:8). In Psalm 77:19 the footsteps are God's own, and nobody could trace them.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these passages present a picture of human life. We walk close to the dust, we cannot see behind us, and we can be struck, snared, or betrayed at the place we least protect. Genesis 3 places that weakness right beside the curse on the ground. The serpent is sentenced to eat dust, and the heel is the part of us in constant contact with that dust.

God does not stay at a safe distance from that weakness. He announces that His victory over the serpent will come through a Seed who is wounded in the heel. God also has "footsteps" of His own (Psalm 77:19). His are not vulnerable like ours. His path through the sea could not be traced. The God who cannot be trapped chose to save people who are trapped so easily, and, as the New Testament makes clear, He did it by sending One who would suffer for them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 3:15: the bruised heel and the crushed head. God tells the serpent, "He shall bruise your head, And you shall bruise His heel." The same Hebrew verb stands behind both uses of "bruise," so the verse does not set a light blow against a heavy one. The contrast lies in where each blow lands. The heel is a vulnerable place, low to the ground and behind a person, where a snake can strike unseen, and a blow there is a real and painful injury. Genesis 49:17 shows what a strike at the heel can do: the viper "bites the horse's heels So that its rider shall fall backward." Even so, a wound to the heel is a different kind of wound from a blow to the head. The verse promises that the Seed will suffer and that the serpent will be defeated, and it ties the two together. The New Testament fills in how the promise is kept. Christ shared "flesh and blood," "that through death He might destroy him who had the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14). Read in that light, the heel wound points to the cross, and the cross was the means of victory.

Psalm 41:9 and John 13:18: the lifted heel. David writes, "Even my own familiar friend in whom I trusted, Who ate my bread, Has lifted up his heel against me." Eating bread together was a sign of peace and friendship. The betrayal is bitter because it comes from someone who sat at the same table. At the Last Supper, Jesus quotes this line about Judas: "He who eats bread with Me has lifted up his heel against Me." He says it so "that the Scripture may be fulfilled," and He prefaces it with "I know whom I have chosen." Jesus was not caught off guard. John notes that He was troubled in spirit as He spoke of the betrayal (John 13:21), and He walked toward the cross knowing who would betray Him.

Genesis 25:26: Jacob grasping the heel. Jacob is born holding Esau's heel, and his name echoes the Hebrew word for heel. Later Esau complains that Jacob has lived up to his name by twice taking what was his (Genesis 27:36). Hosea recalls the scene with a play on words: "He took his brother by the heel in the womb, And in his strength he struggled with God" (Hosea 12:3). There "took … by the heel" translates a related Hebrew verb (Strong's H6117) meaning to seize by the heel, not the noun ʻâqêb itself, but the echo of Jacob's name is plain. Jacob spent years grabbing from behind, trying to pull ahead by his own cleverness. God chose him anyway. At Peniel a Man wrestled with him until daybreak, touched his hip, and renamed him Israel, and Jacob said, "I have seen God face to face" (Genesis 32:24–30). The heel-grabber became a man who clung to God for a blessing he could not take by force.

Psalm 77:19: footsteps that were not known. Asaph begins this psalm unable to sleep and wondering whether God's mercy has ended. He turns to the Red Sea: "Your way was in the sea, Your path in the great waters, And Your footsteps were not known." The footsteps are poetic language for God's hidden path. The road He opened for His people was real, but no one could see or trace the One who made it. The very next verse says God led His people like a flock "by the hand of Moses and Aaron" (Psalm 77:20). God Himself was unseen, but His people were led safely through.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The heel connects the start of the story to the cross. Genesis 3:15 promises a wounded Seed. Psalm 41:9, applied by Jesus in John 13:18, shows the betrayal that set His suffering in motion. Psalm 89:51 laments that enemies "have reproached the footsteps of Your anointed." The mockery was aimed at the path God's anointed king walked, not only at the king. In Jesus that path became one of humility, suffering, and obedience, and the world despised it. Colossians 2:15 shows the result: at the cross He disarmed the powers and triumphed over them.

The promise also reaches Christ's people. "The God of peace will crush Satan under your feet shortly" (Romans 16:20). Paul names God as the One who crushes Satan, and His people share in that victory as the enemy is put under their feet. What Christ won at the cross, God Himself will bring to completion. Peter adds that Christ suffered and left an example so that we would follow in His steps (1 Peter 2:21). Anyone who walks the path of the Anointed can expect scrutiny like that His footsteps received.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 77:19 speaks directly to anyone who cannot see what God is doing. Israel could not trace God's footsteps through the sea, but they reached the other side. When God's ways are hidden beneath deep waters, we can still look back at where He has already brought us. Our inability to trace His steps does not mean He has stopped walking with us.

Song of Solomon 1:8 offers related counsel. When the bride does not know where the shepherd is, she is told, "Follow in the footsteps of the flock." This is a love poem, and its first meaning is about a bride and her shepherd. As a picture, though, it fits Hebrews 6:12, which tells us to "imitate those who through faith and patience inherit the promises." When Christ seems hard to find, we can walk where His faithful people have walked, in Scripture, prayer, and the fellowship of the church.

Jacob's story warns against grasping. We do not get ahead by pulling others back. God may have to wound our self-reliance, as Jacob was wounded at Peniel, before we learn to cling to Him. Psalm 56:6 reminds us that enemies still "mark my steps." David answers that danger with trust (Psalm 56:3–4). Gad's blessing adds hope: a troop shall tramp upon him, "But he shall triumph at last." Defeat need not be the final word.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joshua 8:13: The "rear guard" is placed west of Ai. This is the spatial sense, the back of the army.

  • Judges 5:22: Deborah's song describes pounding horses' hooves in the scene of the Canaanite defeat.

  • Job 18:9: Bildad says of the wicked man, "The net takes him by the heel, And a snare lays hold of him." The next verse says the trap is hidden "on the ground," which pictures sin's sudden and unseen consequences.

  • Jeremiah 13:22: Jerusalem asks why disaster has come and hears, "For the greatness of your iniquity Your skirts have been uncovered, Your heels made bare." The image is public shame and the stripping away of all protection. This verse uses a different picture from the serpent passages and is best read on its own terms.

  • Genesis 49:19: "But he shall triumph at last." The NKJV's "at last" translates this word for heel or rear, so the line may also picture Gad pressing on the heels of the troop that raided him. On either reading, Gad turns defeat around.
