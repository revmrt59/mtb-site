SWEET - (ʻârêb, Strong's H6156)

Summary

The Hebrew adjective ʻârêb means sweet, pleasant or delightful. It describes something that is a pleasure to experience. It is not the usual word for the taste of sugar or honey. Hebrew normally uses another word for that kind of sweetness. ʻârêb names the satisfaction a person feels in something, and because it describes that felt pleasure, the word is neither good nor evil in itself. Everything depends on what is being enjoyed.

The word appears only twice in the Old Testament, and the two uses point in opposite directions. In Proverbs 20:17, bread gained by deceit is "sweet to a man" until his mouth fills with gravel. Sin really does taste good at first, and it lies about what comes after. In Song of Solomon 2:14, the bridegroom calls his hidden "dove" out of the cliffs: "Let me hear your voice; For your voice is sweet." Love takes delight in the beloved's voice. Taken together, these two verses ask every reader where they look for sweetness. Read with the rest of Scripture, the Song also illustrates that the Lord, whom the Song has long helped believers to see as the Bridegroom of His people, delights to hear the voices of those He loves.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻârêb comes from a small family of Hebrew words built on a verb meaning to be pleasant or to be sweet. Both of its uses are in Israel's poetry and wisdom writing. One is a short proverb about honest and dishonest gain. The other is a love song in which a man and a woman long for each other.

This word should not be confused with mâthôq (H4966), the ordinary Hebrew word for something sweet to the taste. The answer the men of the city gave to Samson's riddle uses mâthôq: "What is sweeter than honey?" (Judges 14:18). So does Psalm 19:10, where God's judgments are sweeter than honey. ʻârêb moves in a different direction. It describes the pleasure a thing gives the person who enjoys it, whether that is a meal, a voice, or a gain.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻârêb means sweet, pleasant, or agreeable to the one experiencing it. The pleasure is felt and personal. In one verse bread gained by deceit is pleasant to the man who got it dishonestly. In the other, the voice is pleasant to the one who loves the speaker. The word itself does not decide whether the pleasure is good. That depends on the source of the pleasure and where it leads. For that reason the same adjective can describe the passing thrill of fraud and the honest joy of love.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Proverbs 20:17, the sweetness belongs to the person enjoying dishonest gain: the bread is "sweet to a man." The proverb is honest about how sin feels in the moment. Then the word "afterward" turns the verse around, and that turn is the point of the proverb.

In Song of Solomon 2:14, the sweetness belongs to the beloved's voice, and it is part of the reason for the bridegroom's request. "Let me hear your voice; For your voice is sweet." His desire to hear her follows from the delight he already finds in her. The word sits beside "lovely," which describes her face. Together the two words show how fully he takes pleasure in her.

4. Theological Significance

The two uses of ʻârêb show that Scripture takes desire seriously. Human beings are drawn by what they find pleasant, and God does not pretend that sin has no appeal. Hebrews 11:25 speaks plainly of "the passing pleasures of sin." The danger in sin's sweetness is that the pleasure is real while its promise is false. James traces the same path from enticement to death: "sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death" (James 1:15).

The Song shows the other side of the same capacity for delight. Delight was never the problem. God made people to enjoy what is good, and He Himself takes pleasure in His people. The verb related to ʻârêb carries this thought in other passages. The psalmist prays that his meditation would be sweet to the Lord (Psalm 104:34). Malachi looks ahead to a day when the offering of Judah and Jerusalem will again be pleasant to the Lord (Malachi 3:4). The God of Scripture can be pleased, and He finds sweetness in the worship and nearness of His people.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 20:17 — Sweet bread, a mouth full of gravel. "Bread gained by deceit is sweet to a man, But afterward his mouth will be filled with gravel." The proverb does not call such bread bitter. It admits the bread is sweet, and that honesty is what gives the warning its force. A man who gets ahead through lies, cheating, or a hidden scheme often enjoys the result at first. The meal tastes like provision, and that is exactly how sin presents itself: as something that will sustain him.

"Afterward" changes everything. The same mouth that enjoyed the bread fills with gravel, grit that breaks teeth and gives no nourishment. Lamentations 3:16 uses the same picture of grief: "He has also broken my teeth with gravel." The image follows the experience from the first bite to what remains once the bread has been taken in. Proverbs 9:17 gives a separate example that names theft outright. Folly invites the simple with the words "Stolen water is sweet," and the next verse adds that "her guests are in the depths of hell" (9:18). Revelation 10:9–11 has a similar shape, though for a very different purpose. The angel tells John to take and eat the little book, which will be "as sweet as honey in your mouth" but will make his stomach bitter. John eats it and finds it sweet in his mouth and then bitter in his stomach (10:10). Only then is he told, "You must prophesy again" (10:11). There the bitterness accompanies John's prophetic commission rather than any forbidden pleasure, so the likeness lies only in the movement from a sweet taste to a bitter aftermath. Proverbs 20:17 teaches the reader to judge bread by its afterward and not only by its first taste.

Song of Solomon 2:14 — The dove in the cleft of the rock. "O my dove, in the clefts of the rock, In the secret places of the cliff, Let me see your face, Let me hear your voice; For your voice is sweet, And your face is lovely." Doves in that land nest high in rock crevices, out of reach and hard to see. The bridegroom's beloved is hidden like that. The verses just before this one describe him calling her to rise and come away because winter is past and the flowers have appeared (2:10–13). He wants her out in the open, face to face with him.

His request carries no rebuke. He does not ask her to come out so he can correct her. He asks to see her face and to hear her voice, because her voice is sweet to him and her face is lovely. The poem does not say why she is in the secret places of the cliff. The sweetness here belongs to love, and unlike the bread gained by deceit, it does not turn to gravel.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pattern of Proverbs 20:17 reaches back to Eden. The woman saw that the tree "was pleasant to the eyes" (Genesis 3:6). The Hebrew word there is different, but the experience is similar: something forbidden looked delightful, and it brought death. Ever since, people have reached for bread they were never meant to take. Jesus answers that hunger with Himself: "I am the bread of life. He who comes to Me shall never hunger" (John 6:35). The bread of deceit is sweet and then ruins the one who eats it. The Bread of Life never turns to gravel.

The Song points toward Christ along a different line. Paul calls the union of husband and wife a picture of Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:31–32), and Christians have long heard in the Song's bridegroom the voice of the Lord who loves His people. In this reading, the dove's hiding place takes on added meaning. God once put Moses "in the cleft of the rock" and covered him with His hand while His glory passed by (Exodus 33:22). Paul writes of Israel's wilderness journey that "that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4). The Song does not make this connection itself. Still, it rings true to the rest of Scripture that believers are sheltered in Christ, and from that place their voices are welcome before God.

Scripture also treats the voice of God's people as an offering. "Let my prayer be set before You as incense" (Psalm 141:2). Hosea calls the repentant to return with "the sacrifices of our lips" (Hosea 14:2), and Hebrews urges believers to "continually offer the sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of our lips" (Hebrews 13:15). In heaven the elders hold "golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints" (Revelation 5:8). Under the old covenant, sacrifices God accepted rose as a pleasing aroma. The Song, read this way, pictures that pleasure in terms of love: the voice itself is sweet to the one who hears it.

7. Spirit & Life

These two verses work like a mirror. Each of us is pursuing something we find sweet, and what we enjoy shows what has hold of our hearts. Proverbs 20:17 gives us a question to ask before the first bite: what will this be afterward? A dishonest shortcut, a secret indulgence, or a gain we could not explain to God all taste like provision. They do not stay that way.

Warnings alone seldom break the pull of sin's sweetness. A greater delight does. Psalm 34:8 invites us to "taste and see that the LORD is good," and the Song, read as a picture of the Lord and His people, suggests something many believers have never let themselves believe: the Lord finds them sweet as well. Many Christians pray from a distance, embarrassed by failure and unsure of their welcome. The bridegroom's call to his hidden dove illustrates the welcome Scripture promises them, and Hebrews grounds that welcome in Christ Himself. Because our High Priest sympathizes with our weaknesses, "Let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (Hebrews 4:15–16). Prayer then stops being a duty performed before a reluctant listener. It becomes a conversation that the One listening wants to have. A person who knows that the Lord delights to hear them has less reason to go looking for the bread of deceit.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

ʻârêb occurs only in Proverbs 20:17 and Song of Solomon 2:14, and both verses are discussed above. Their contrast is the main lesson of the word: the passing sweetness of sin set against the lasting delight of love.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H4966 (mâthôq), distinct word: the common Hebrew word for something sweet to the taste, as in "What is sweeter than honey?" (Judges 14:18) and Psalm 19:10. It is useful as a contrast. It is not a form of ʻârêb.

  • Thematic parallels: Proverbs 9:17 (stolen water and secret bread) and Genesis 3:6 (the pleasant-looking fruit) share the image of a pleasure that turns harmful. Revelation 10:9–11 (sweet in the mouth, bitter in the stomach, then the call to prophesy again) shares only the sensory sequence of sweetness followed by bitterness; there both the sweetness and the bitterness accompany John's commission to prophesy, not a forbidden pleasure. Psalm 141:2 and Revelation 5:8 picture prayer as pleasing incense before God. These passages use different words but shed light on the two uses of ʻârêb.
