BED, GARDEN TERRACE - (ʻărûwgâh, Strong's H6170)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻărûwgâh names a prepared planting bed: a raised or terraced plot of ground that someone has marked out, worked, planted, and tended. It appears only four times in the Old Testament, twice in the Song of Solomon and twice in Ezekiel. Those two settings could hardly be more different. In the Song, the bride compares her beloved's face to "a bed of spices" (5:13), and she says he has gone to "the beds of spices" of his garden (6:2). In Ezekiel 17, a vine planted in a "garden terrace" turns its roots toward Egypt for water, and God declares that it will wither in the very bed where it was planted.

Taken together, these passages show two sides of the same image. A cultivated bed is a chosen place. In the Song it is a place of fragrance, beauty, and love. In Ezekiel it becomes a place of judgment. There the vine's roots bending away toward another source of water picture Zedekiah's offense: he broke the oath he had sworn to the king of Babylon, which God calls "My oath," and sent to Egypt for horses and troops (Ezekiel 17:15–19). Ezekiel's failed vine leads to Jesus, the True Vine, who remained faithful to the Father and obeyed Him even to the cross. By application, the Song's fragrant beds invite the believer to seek His face and to give Him a guarded, tended place in the heart.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻărûwgâh is an ordinary farming and gardening word. It describes a deliberate plot rather than open country. Hebrew has a separate word for the open field (śādeh) and another for a garden in general (gan). ʻărûwgâh points more narrowly to the bed or terrace inside a garden, the shaped strip of soil where something is intentionally grown. In the hill country of Israel, terraced beds held soil and water on slopes, so the picture is one of ground that has been worked and protected.

All four uses appear in figurative writing. Two belong to love poetry, and two belong to a parable that God commands Ezekiel to tell (Ezekiel 17:2).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one basic meaning: a cultivated bed or terrace of planted ground. The NKJV translates it as "bed" or "beds" in the Song of Solomon. In Ezekiel 17 it becomes "garden terrace," a phrase that brings out the shaped, raised character of the plot. The setting determines what grows there, whether spices and lilies or a vine. The dictionary meaning stays the same: ground that someone has prepared on purpose.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Song of Solomon the bed is always a "bed of spices." Spice beds needed careful tending, and their fragrance could fill a garden. The poet uses that image for beauty that is rich, valued, and delightful to the senses.

In Ezekiel the bed is the place where a vine "had been planted" (17:7). The emphasis falls on where the vine was placed and on what it does with that placement. The word itself simply names the planted bed; it does not mark out a boundary the vine crossed. The fault lies in what the vine did from that bed. Ezekiel explains that the bending roots picture Zedekiah breaking the oath he had sworn to the king of Babylon and sending to Egypt for horses and troops (17:15–19).

4. Theological Significance

Both settings show that God's gifts come with a place. In Ezekiel the place was a terrace of good soil beside "many waters" (Ezekiel 17:5, 8). In the parable the eagle planted the vine there, and God held Zedekiah to the oath that came with that planting. When Zedekiah broke faith and sought Egypt's help, God treated it as treachery against Himself. He calls the oath Zedekiah swore to Babylon "My oath" and "My covenant" (17:19), showing that He stood behind that oath and the discipline it placed on Judah. For Zedekiah, faithfulness meant keeping his sworn word and trusting God rather than Egypt's army, even when that meant living as a humbled king.

The Song shows the delightful side of the same truth. A garden bed exists for fragrance and fruit. The bride invites her beloved to enjoy what the garden yields: "Let my beloved come to his garden And eat its pleasant fruits" (Song 4:16). He answers, "I have come to my garden" (5:1). The image fits a wider biblical pattern: God plants with purpose and comes looking for what has grown.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:13 — the beloved's face. The daughters of Jerusalem ask the bride why her beloved is better than any other, and she answers with a portrait of him. "His cheeks are like a bed of spices, Banks of scented herbs. His lips are lilies, Dripping liquid myrrh." She has just searched the city for him without finding him (5:6–8). In his absence, she remembers his face as something tended and fragrant, like a raised bed of spices whose scent rises to meet you. Her longing makes the description vivid. She is describing someone she loves and knows closely.

Song of Solomon 6:2 — where she says he has gone. When the daughters of Jerusalem ask where he has gone (6:1), she answers. "My beloved has gone to his garden, To the beds of spices, To feed his flock in the gardens, And to gather lilies." Her answer recalls the earlier image of the enclosed garden. In chapter 4 he called her "a garden enclosed," a "fountain sealed" (4:12). She then invited him to come to "his garden" (4:16), and he replied, "I have come to my garden" (5:1). Now, after searching for him, she describes him as gone to his garden and its beds of spices rather than into wild country. The poem does not say that this is the same setting as chapter 4, and it does not describe a meeting there, but the repeated garden language links her answer to that earlier invitation. She adds at once, "I am my beloved's, And my beloved is mine" (6:3).

Ezekiel 17:7, 10 — the vine that reached away. God's parable is explained in 17:11–21. Nebuchadnezzar, the first great eagle, took Jerusalem's king to Babylon and set up Zedekiah, "the king's offspring," under oath, "that the kingdom might be brought low and not lift itself up, but that by keeping his covenant it might stand" (17:13–14). Judah could have survived as a humble, low-spreading vine. Instead, "this vine bent its roots toward him, And stretched its branches toward him, From the garden terrace where it had been planted, That he might water it" (17:7). The second eagle was Egypt, and Zedekiah sent envoys asking for "horses and many people" (17:15). God's verdict in verse 10 is that when the east wind strikes, the vine will wither in the very terrace where it grew. Egypt's help would fail: "Nor will Pharaoh... do anything in the war" (17:17). The place of provision became the place of judgment. God names the reason plainly: Zedekiah "despised the oath by breaking the covenant" (17:18) and turned to Egypt for help (17:15).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Ezekiel 17 does not end with the withered vine. In Ezekiel 17:22–24 the Lord says He will take a tender twig from the top of the cedar and plant it Himself on a high mountain of Israel, where it will become a majestic cedar sheltering birds of every kind. Where human kingship failed, God promises a King of His own planting. Read in the light of the whole Bible, this line of hope reaches David's greater Son.

Jesus' own words stand in clear contrast to the failed vine: "I am the true vine, and My Father is the vinedresser" (John 15:1). Zedekiah broke his oath and reached toward Egypt rather than accept a humbling place. Jesus accepted the Father's will in Gethsemane, prayed that the Father's will be done rather than His own, and did not ask for the legions of angels the Father could have provided (Matthew 26:39, 53). His appointed place included the cross. The New Testament does not cite Ezekiel 17 here, but the contrast is real and instructive. One vine broke faith and withered under the east wind. The other stayed rooted in obedience, and now every branch that abides in Him bears fruit (John 15:4–5).

The Song's bed of spices fits the same story from another direction. Christians have long read the Song as a picture of Christ and His people, and the beloved's fragrant face fits that reading well. Paul says God has given "the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). For the believer who reads the Song this way, the face the bride longed to see points to the face where God's glory is made known.

7. Spirit & Life

Ezekiel's vine was not ruined by bad soil. Its problem lay in the direction of its roots. Roots are hidden, and that is where the betrayal happened. Zedekiah's sin was specific: he broke a sworn oath and looked to Egypt's army instead of to God. The passage does not teach that a believer must stay in a particular job, congregation or season of life no matter what, and it is not a ruling on marriage, which Scripture addresses in its own passages. What it does show is that keeping faith with God, and by application with promises made before Him, matters more than escaping a hard place. A person can keep every outward promise while the heart's roots quietly stretch toward another source of security: money, approval, an exit plan, a powerful friend. Jeremiah describes the opposite kind of life: "a tree planted by the waters, Which spreads out its roots by the river, And will not fear when heat comes" (Jeremiah 17:8). The question for any hard season is where your roots are drinking from. Trusting God where faithfulness has placed you is safer than running to an Egypt He has not sent you to.

The Song adds a tenderness that Ezekiel's warning lacks. Spice beds do not grow by accident. They are dug, cleared of stones, planted, watered, and guarded. When the bride invited her beloved into his garden, he answered that he had come (4:16–5:1). Later, asked where he has gone, she says he has gone to his garden and its beds of spices (6:2), words that recall that earlier invitation. As a pastoral comparison, life with Christ works in a similar way. Unguarded, overgrown hearts are seldom places of close fellowship. Time set apart for Him, attention protected from noise, and a heart kept for Him alone become like the beds of spices where He comes "to feed his flock... And to gather lilies." The fragrance that rises from such a life starts with Him, the One whose face is itself "a bed of spices."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because ʻărûwgâh occurs only four times, every occurrence has been treated in Section 5: Song of Solomon 5:13 and 6:2, and Ezekiel 17:7 and 17:10. Readers who want to follow the rooted-tree image further can turn to Psalm 1:3, Jeremiah 17:8, and Colossians 2:7 ("rooted and built up in Him"). Those verses use different words but illuminate the same picture.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1588 (gan), garden: a distinct word for a garden in general. It appears alongside ʻărûwgâh in Song of Solomon 6:2, where the beloved goes "to his garden, To the beds of spices."

  • H7704 (śādeh), field: a distinct word for open country. It provides a contrast that clarifies the cultivated, tended character of ʻărûwgâh. This is a thematic distinction, not a lexical relationship.
