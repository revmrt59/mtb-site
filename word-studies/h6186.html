ARRANGE, SET IN ORDER, ARRAY, PREPARE - (ʻârak, Strong's H6186)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʻârak means to set things in a row: to arrange, lay out, or put in order. Scripture uses it when Abraham lays the wood on the altar, when priests set out the showbread and tend the lamps, and when armies draw up their battle lines. It is also used for laying out a legal case, preparing a table for a meal, and placing two things side by side to compare them or set a price. The common thread is deliberate placement. Something is put where it belongs, in relation to everything else.

This ordinary word carries some of the Old Testament's richest statements about God. David confesses, "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies" (Psalm 23:5). He rests his dying hope on an everlasting covenant God has "ordered" in every detail (2 Samuel 23:5). Isaiah asks what could ever be set in a row beside God for comparison (Isaiah 40:18). The word shows a God who does nothing carelessly. He arranges His promises, sets His table for His people, and will one day lay out the deeds of the unrepentant before their own eyes.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻârak is a common Hebrew verb used across the Law, the historical books, poetry, and the prophets. Its settings are concrete and practical. It belongs to the tabernacle, where wood, bread, and lamps had to be set out properly. It belongs to the battlefield, where soldiers formed ranks. It belongs to the household table and the courtroom. Because the word moves so easily between these settings, English translations render it in many ways: "array," "order," "prepare," "value," "compare," "present," and "direct." The variety comes from the contexts. The idea underneath stays the same.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, ʻârak means to put things in their proper arrangement. Context determines what is being arranged:

1. To lay out or set in order objects, such as wood on an altar or bread on a table: "Abraham built an altar there and placed the wood in order" (Genesis 22:9, paraphrased).

2. To draw up in battle formation, the most frequent use: armies "set in battle array" (Joel 2:5).

3. To prepare a table or meal: "You prepare a table before me" (Psalm 23:5).

4. To set words or arguments in order, as in a legal case: "Set your words in order before me; take your stand" (Job 33:5).

5. To compare or assess value, by setting things side by side, as when a priest sets the value of a vow (Leviticus 27:8, 12).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The military sense dominates the historical books. Israel and the Philistines draw up battle lines across from each other at the valley where Goliath challenged Israel (1 Samuel 17:2, 21), and Joab's armies form ranks against Ammon and Syria (2 Samuel 10:8–10). Jeremiah applies the same language to the nations God would send against Babylon (Jeremiah 50:9, 14, 42).

In the Law, the word governs how worship is carried out. The priests arrange the wood and the pieces of the sacrifice on the altar (Leviticus 1:7–8, 12). Aaron keeps the lamps in order from evening until morning (Leviticus 24:3–4), and the showbread is set in order before the LORD every Sabbath (Leviticus 24:8).

The comparison sense stands out in poetry. Job says that neither gold nor topaz can equal wisdom (Job 28:17, 19). Nothing can be set in the same row with it. The same idea, applied to God Himself, becomes one of the highest statements of His uniqueness.

4. Theological Significance

God is approached on His terms. Abraham's careful ordering of the wood on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22:9) comes before any law of sacrifice was given. Later, the same verb runs through the priestly instructions for altar, lamp, and table. Coming near to a holy God was never casual. Each element had its place because the God being approached had told His people how to come. Hebrews carries this forward: "let us have grace, by which we may serve God acceptably with reverence and godly fear. For our God is a consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:28–29).

God cannot be placed in a row with anything. Comparison means setting two things side by side, and Scripture uses ʻârak to say that no such line can be formed with God. "To whom then will you liken God? Or what likeness will you compare to Him?" (Isaiah 40:18). "For who in the heavens can be compared to the LORD?" (Psalm 89:6). Isaiah aimed his question at idol-makers, who were trying to do exactly what cannot be done. Angels, kings, and carved gods may stand in a row with one another. The LORD stands apart from all of them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 23:5: the table God sets. Everywhere else in the psalm, the Shepherd leads and protects. In verse 5 He becomes the host who sets the table: "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies; You anoint my head with oil; My cup runs over." The enemies are present, yet they cannot interrupt the meal. That confession answers an old doubt. In the wilderness Israel asked whether God could prepare a table in the desert (Psalm 78:19). David, who had known his own wilderness years, says that He can and that He has.

The prophets use the same verb for tables set by people who had abandoned the LORD. Isaiah condemns those "Who prepare a table for Gad, And who furnish a drink offering for Meni" (Isaiah 65:11), gods of fortune and fate. Ezekiel pictures unfaithful Jerusalem on "a stately couch, with a table prepared before it, on which you had set My incense and My oil" (Ezekiel 23:41). Ezekiel's detail is painful. God's own gifts had been laid out to entertain other lovers. Every heart is sitting at some table, either the one God has prepared or one we have set for something else.

2 Samuel 23:5: the covenant God ordered. In his last words, David admits that his house is not what it should be before God. His family history bears that out. Yet he rests on the everlasting covenant God made with him, a covenant "ordered" in every detail and made secure (paraphrased). David's hope did not depend on his household holding together. It depended on God having already arranged everything the promise required.

Psalm 5:3 and Job: ordered words. "In the morning I will direct it to You, And I will look up" (Psalm 5:3). The verb is the one used for laying out an altar or a legal case, and David does not say which picture he has in mind. Either way, he arranges his prayer deliberately and then watches for God to answer. Job uses the word as courtroom language. He says he has prepared his case (Job 13:18) and longs to lay it out before God and fill his mouth with arguments (Job 23:4, paraphrased). Job wanted a hearing. He did not want silence or blind fate. Elihu then challenges Job in the same terms: "Set your words in order before me" (Job 33:5).

Psalm 50:21 and Psalm 40:5: what God orders, and what we cannot. In Psalm 50, God tells the wicked that His silence led them to think He was like them, but He will rebuke them and set their deeds in order before their eyes (paraphrased). Judgment will come as a fully arranged case, and the guilty will be unable to dispute it. Psalm 40:5 uses the same verb in the opposite direction. David says God's thoughts toward His people are so many that they cannot be set in order and recounted (paraphrased). God can lay out every sin. No one can lay out all His kindness.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The ordered covenant David leaned on reaches its fulfillment in Jesus, the Son of David. Hebrews speaks of "the God of peace who brought up our Lord Jesus from the dead, that great Shepherd of the sheep, through the blood of the everlasting covenant" (Hebrews 13:20). The Shepherd of Psalm 23 and the everlasting covenant of 2 Samuel 23 meet in one Person. Paul describes the same secure arrangement of grace. Those God foreknew, He predestined, called, justified, and glorified, with no link in the chain missing (Romans 8:29–30).

The table moves forward too. Jesus tells His disciples, "I go to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2), and Scripture's story ends at a feast: "Blessed are those who are called to the marriage supper of the Lamb!" (Revelation 19:9). The Greek of John is not ʻârak, but the pattern of the host who makes ready for His guests carries straight through.

The lamps the priests kept in order also find an echo. In Psalm 132:17 God promises to prepare a lamp for His Anointed, using the same verb as the priestly lamp-tending of Leviticus 24. And the God who cannot be compared is made known in the Son, who is "the brightness of His glory and the express image of His person" (Hebrews 1:3). The One beyond comparison has shown His face.

7. Spirit & Life

Prayer can be deliberate without becoming stiff. David's morning practice in Psalm 5 was to arrange what he brought to God and then lift his eyes in expectation. Many believers pray scattered thoughts and then walk away without waiting for anything. The psalm teaches both halves: lay out your heart before the Lord, and then look up for His answer.

Job's example gives sufferers permission to bring God an honest, reasoned case. Job did not throw words around. He wanted to stand before God and speak, and Scripture records that longing without condemning it.

The battle-line verses keep believers realistic. God's people have always lived where lines are drawn against them, and Paul names the true opponents: "we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers" (Ephesians 6:12). The armor is God's, and so is the victory.

Underneath it all is great comfort. The God who will one day set every unrepented sin in order is the same God whose thoughts of mercy toward His people cannot be counted. Those who come to Christ will not face that arranged prosecution. They are guests at a table He has already set.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 14:8. The kings of the plain "joined" battle in the Valley of Siddim, the first use of the battle sense in Scripture.

  • Joshua 2:6. Rahab hid the spies under stalks of flax she had laid in order on her roof. An ordinary household arrangement became the means of their protection.

  • 1 Chronicles 12:33. The men of Zebulun who came to David were "expert" in war, skilled at forming ranks with an undivided heart.

  • Proverbs 9:2. Wisdom has "furnished" her table and invites the simple to come in. Her table stands in deliberate contrast to the table of folly later in the chapter.

  • Leviticus 27:8. When a poor person made a vow, the priest set its value according to what that person could afford. God's law made room for the poor.

  • Joel 2:5. The coming army is "Like a strong people set in battle array," a picture of the Day of the LORD advancing in disciplined ranks.
