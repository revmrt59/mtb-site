ARKITE - (ʻArqîy, Strong's H6208)

Summary

"Arkite" is the name of an ancient people descended from Canaan, the son of Ham. The Bible uses the word only twice, both times in the same short line of a family record: "the Hivite, the Arkite, and the Sinite" (Genesis 10:17; 1 Chronicles 1:15). The Arkites are linked with the old coastal city of Arqa in what is now northern Lebanon. Ancient records outside the Bible know this city as Irqata. The name places Genesis's account of the nations in real geography, among real peoples on a real coastline.

The Arkites take part in no biblical story. No battles, prophets, or other events involving them are recorded, and they are not named again. Their presence is still worth noticing. God's Word records an obscure, distant people when it lists the families descended from Noah, and the Chronicler copies that same line for Israel centuries later, after the exile. Scripture teaches that the Most High "set the boundaries of the peoples" (Deuteronomy 32:8). The Arkites stand in the record as a quiet witness that He knows and governs every nation, including those far from the center of the story.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻArqîy is a Hebrew gentilic noun, meaning a word that names a people by their place or ancestor. English handles the same idea with words like "Texan" or "Athenian." The NKJV renders it "Arkite" in both of its occurrences.

The Arkites are associated with Arqa, modern Tell Arqa in northern Lebanon. The fourteenth-century BC Amarna letters, diplomatic correspondence from the ancient Near East, mention the city as Irqata. Both biblical occurrences sit inside genealogies. Genesis 10 is the "Table of Nations," a map of the peoples who spread out after the flood. First Chronicles 1 repeats much of that table at the opening of a history written for Israel after the Babylonian exile.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one meaning: an Arkite, a member of the people descended from Canaan and connected with the city of Arqa. It is a proper name for a people group. It carries no figurative or theological sense of its own, and Scripture never uses it to describe a quality or an action.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Both uses appear in the identical phrase "the Hivite, the Arkite, and the Sinite" (Genesis 10:17; 1 Chronicles 1:15). In each case the Arkite is one name in a list of the sons of Canaan, which begins, "Canaan begot Sidon his firstborn, and Heth" (Genesis 10:15). The list then gives a string of peoples, ending with "the Arvadite, the Zemarite, and the Hamathite" (10:18).

The singular "the Arkite" stands for the whole people, much as "the Jebusite" in the previous verse stands for the whole Jebusite population. The word does not appear in narrative, poetry, prophecy, or the New Testament. Everything it contributes comes from the purpose of the genealogies that contain it.

4. Theological Significance

The genealogy that names the Arkites teaches that the nations have a history under God. Genesis 10 does not present the world's peoples as random tribes emerging from nowhere. They are families, traced to fathers, spread across lands, and this Canaanite subsection concludes that "the families of the Canaanites were dispersed" (10:18) before the chapter continues to its close at 10:32. Human diversity flows from a common origin, and Scripture records that origin as part of God's dealings with the world after the flood.

Moses later gives the theological reason behind such a list: "When the Most High divided their inheritance to the nations, when He separated the sons of Adam, He set the boundaries of the peoples according to the number of the children of Israel" (Deuteronomy 32:8). The settling of peoples in their lands was God's doing. Moses ties it to His purposes for Israel, which means the map of the nations was drawn with His redemptive plan already in view. A people in the coastal region of northern Lebanon belongs to that ordered world as surely as Egypt or Babylon does.

The Arkites also belong to the line of Canaan, which carries a heavy shadow. Just before the Table of Nations, Noah says, "Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants he shall be to his brethren" (Genesis 9:25). Much of Canaan's later history in Scripture is a story of opposition and judgment. Even so, God's record names Canaan's families with care. His knowledge of a people does not depend on their standing with Israel, and His record of them does not wait for their repentance.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:15–19: the Arkites among the sons of Canaan. The Arkites appear in the middle of Canaan's family. The list moves from Sidon and Heth through the Jebusite, Amorite, and Girgashite, then to the Hivite, Arkite, and Sinite, and finally to the Arvadite, Zemarite, and Hamathite. Genesis then describes "the border of the Canaanites" running "from Sidon as you go toward Gerar, as far as Gaza," and on toward Sodom and Gomorrah (10:19). Arqa lay north of Sidon. Together with the names that follow it, the Arkite seems to point to the northern reaches of Canaan's family, the coastal and inland regions north of the territory Israel actually occupied. The wider inheritance promised in Scripture reaches toward Lebanon (Genesis 15:18–21; Joshua 13:5–6), so these northern peoples were not simply outside the promise.

That detail matters. When Moses later lists the nations Israel would face in the land, he names Hittites, Girgashites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites (Deuteronomy 7:1). The Arkites are not individually named there, and Scripture records no story of Israel with them. Their place in Scripture at least shows that God's account of the nations includes them.

The specificity is also significant. Arqa was a real city, known to the scribes of the Amarna age as Irqata. Genesis is not describing an imaginary world. When the Bible lays out the background for God's call of Abraham, it uses the names of actual peoples living in actual places. The history of redemption begins on solid ground.

1 Chronicles 1:15: the Arkites remembered after the exile. The Chronicler writes for a people coming home from Babylon. They had lost their king and their independence, and much of their land lay in ruins. He begins his history with Adam and the whole family of nations before narrowing to Abraham, Israel, David, and the temple. Within that opening sweep he repeats the same line: "the Hivite, the Arkite, and the Sinite."

To a weakened people, this opening said something. The God of Israel was no local deity of a small province. He was the Lord who had seen the whole spread of humanity, down to the clans along a distant coastline. Israel's story belonged to His larger story of all peoples. Nothing about the exile had shrunk Him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Table of Nations sets up what follows. Genesis 10 scatters the families of the earth, Genesis 11 shows their pride at Babel, and Genesis 12 calls Abraham with God's promise that in him all the families of the earth would be blessed. The nations, including Canaan's line, are the reason for Abraham's calling. The list that names the Arkites describes the world God meant to bless.

That purpose comes to light in Christ. In Athens, Paul tells the philosophers that God "has made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, and has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26). He goes on to say that God did this so that people would seek Him (17:27). Paul's sermon echoes Genesis 10 and Deuteronomy 32: one origin, many nations, boundaries set by God. Paul then names the goal, which is that the nations would seek and find the God who made them. In the same sermon he calls all people to repent because of the Man God raised from the dead (17:30–31).

The Gospels contain a striking glimpse of Canaan's family meeting Jesus. In the region of Tyre and Sidon, a woman Matthew calls "a woman of Canaan" begs Jesus for mercy, and He heals her daughter (Matthew 15:21–28). The text does not identify her people any further, and she cannot be called an Arkite. Still, she comes from the same Canaanite family that Genesis 10 traces back to Sidon, Canaan's firstborn. A line that begins under Noah's curse ends, in her case, with the commendation of her faith by the Son of God.

7. Spirit & Life

Readers often skip genealogies, but the Arkites show why they deserve attention. Here is a people whose deeds Scripture does not narrate, yet God's Word preserved their name through the flood account, through the exile, and into the Chronicler's history. God's attention does not depend on prominence. He knows the coastal clans, the minor families, and the names nobody else would think to record.

This speaks to believers who feel peripheral. The Arkites lived north of the land Israel actually held, and no battles with them are recorded, and God still knew where they lived and who their father was. The same God, Paul says, determines "the boundaries of their dwellings" for every nation today. The place a person lives, the people they come from, and the times they live in are not accidents. They are part of God's arrangement, with His stated aim that people would seek Him.

This also corrects a narrow view of God's interest. The returned exiles might have wanted a history about Israel alone. The Chronicler made them begin with the whole world. Believers today need the same correction. God's purposes include peoples we overlook, and the gospel's reach is as wide as the Table of Nations.
