BED, COUCH, BEDSTEAD - (ʻeres, Strong's H6210)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻeres simply means a bed or couch: the frame or furnished piece of furniture where a person sleeps, rests, recovers from sickness, or reclines at ease. The NKJV renders it "bed," "couch," and "bedstead." It is not a common word, but Scripture places it where people are least guarded. There is the giant king's iron bed, the sufferer's sleepless bed, the sick man's bed, the seductress's perfumed bed, the bridal bed, and the ivory-trimmed couches of a careless nation.

These verses show that the place where we rest also shows what is in our hearts. God meets the weak on the bed of illness and stays with the weeping through the night. The same bed can also become a refuge from God. Amos warns that comfortable couches dulled Israel to coming judgment, and Proverbs shows a decorated bed laid as a trap. In Psalm 132 David refuses the comfort of his own bed until the LORD has a dwelling place. That oath invites comparison with the Son of David who had "nowhere to lay His head" (Luke 9:58).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻeres is a Hebrew noun for a bed or couch. It appears in Deuteronomy, Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Song of Solomon, and Amos. In several of these settings the bed is a substantial piece of furniture rather than a simple sleeping mat. Og's bed is made of iron. The seductress spreads her bed with Egyptian linen. The wealthy in Samaria stretch out on couches beside beds inlaid with ivory. Proposed links to a root about weaving or spreading are uncertain, so the meaning is best learned from how the word is used.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A bed for sleeping or lying down, including the bed of a person who is sick or grieving (Job 7:13; Psalm 6:6; 41:3).

2. A couch for reclining at leisure or at feasts, often with a sense of comfort or luxury (Amos 6:4).

The two senses differ in how the furniture is used, not in the object itself, which is always a real piece of furniture.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Hebrew poetry likes to say things twice, and ʻeres often stands beside another word for bed in matching lines. In Job 7:13 "bed" is paired with "couch." In Psalm 6:6 "I make my bed swim" is paired with "I drench my couch with my tears." In Amos 6:4 "beds of ivory" is paired with "your couches." The pairing gives the line emphasis: every place of rest is affected, whether by grief or by indulgence.

The description added to the bed often carries the point. Og's bed is iron. David speaks of "the comfort of my bed" (Psalm 132:3) and refuses it; Job only hopes his bed will comfort him (Job 7:13). The seductress's is covered in "Egyptian linen." The bride's is "green." The word stays the same, while these added details show whether the bed stands for terror, comfort, seduction, or delight.

4. Theological Significance

Because the bed is where a person is most private, these passages show God's presence in places no one else sees. He is Lord of the night as well as the day. He strengthens the sick on their beds (Psalm 41:3), and He also sees the couches where people ignore Him (Amos 6:4). Nothing done or suffered in private is outside His concern.

The passages also show God's judgment working against false security. The things people trust for rest, such as a giant king's iron bed or Samaria's luxurious couches, cannot stand when the LORD acts. The materials themselves point in different directions. Iron suggests human might, while ivory and imported linen suggest wealth and outward show. The bride's "green" bed suggests life and growth. In these passages, trust in strength or luxury ends in ruin, while the love God gives flourishes.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 3:11 — Og's iron bedstead. Israel's first generation had refused to enter Canaan because they feared the giants (Deuteronomy 1:28). Moses now records that "only Og king of Bashan remained of the remnant of the giants. Indeed his bedstead was an iron bedstead," nine cubits long, which is roughly thirteen feet. Then he asks, "Is it not in Rabbah of the people of Ammon?" The question means the bed could still be seen. The furniture of the most fearsome enemy had become a museum piece. Each time Israel remembered that enormous bed, they could remember that the LORD had already defeated the giant who slept in it.

Job 7:13 and Psalm 6:6 — the bed that gives no rest. Job says, "My bed will comfort me," but the next verse tells how God terrifies him there with dreams. David groans, "All night I make my bed swim; I drench my couch with my tears." In both passages, the place meant for relief becomes the place where suffering is felt most sharply. Night removes distractions, and the sufferer is left alone with his pain and with God.

Psalm 41:3 — God at the sickbed. Into that darkness comes this promise: "The LORD will strengthen him on his bed of illness; You will sustain him on his sickbed." The promise belongs to the one who "considers the poor" (41:1). The psalm does not promise that the sick person will never lie down in weakness. It promises that the LORD will be present on the very bed where weakness keeps him. Psalm 41:3 answers Job's lonely bed: God does not wait until the sufferer gets up.

Amos 3:12 and 6:4 — the couch that cannot save. Amos pictures the wealthy "Who lie on beds of ivory, Stretch out on your couches," feasting on choice lambs and calves. A few verses later he says they are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph, meaning the nation itself (6:6). Their comfort had numbed them. Amos 3:12 gives the outcome: "As a shepherd takes from the mouth of a lion Two legs or a piece of an ear, So shall the children of Israel be taken out Who dwell in Samaria— In the corner of a bed and on the edge of a couch!" Under Israel's law, a shepherd brought back the remains of a torn animal as evidence (Exodus 22:13). In Amos's comparison, what is "taken out" of Samaria will be as scant as those scraps. He does not say no one will survive, but the little rescued, like the couch they trusted for comfort, shows how much was lost.

Proverbs 7:16 and Song of Solomon 1:16 — two beds. The adulteress invites, "I have spread my bed with tapestry, Colored coverings of Egyptian linen." Her bed is costly and carefully arranged, and it leads the simple young man toward death (7:27). In the Song, the bride delights in her beloved: "Behold, you are handsome, my beloved! Yes, pleasant! Also our bed is green." Her bed needs no imported decoration. "Green" here suggests something leafy and fresh, like a growing tree, and it describes love that is alive and welcome. These two passages set the stolen intimacy of Proverbs against the faithful love of the Song.

Psalm 132:3 — the bed David refused. David vows, "Surely I will not go into the chamber of my house, Or go up to the comfort of my bed," until he finds "a place for the LORD" (132:5). David's bed was not sinful. It was his rightful rest as king. Yet he could not settle comfortably while the ark, the sign of God's presence, had no settled home (compare 2 Samuel 7:1–2). He set aside a good comfort for something better.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Psalm 132 does not end with David's vow. The LORD answers with His own oath that a son of David will sit on the throne (132:11). David gave up his bed so that God might have a dwelling place. Jesus, the greater Son of David, said, "Foxes have holes and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay His head" (Luke 9:58). Luke does not connect these words with Psalm 132, but the two make a fitting thematic comparison: the Son of David had no settled place to rest, and in Him the Word "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14).

Psalm 41 also points to Christ. The same psalm that promises God's help at the sickbed later describes a trusted friend who "lifted up his heel" against the speaker (41:9). Jesus applied that line to Judas (John 13:18). In the Gospels, Jesus also brings God's sustaining help to the sickbed directly: He heals the paralytic, who then picks up his bed and walks home (Mark 2:11–12). Amos's warning is also carried forward. In Revelation 2:22, the risen Christ says, "I will cast her into a sickbed" against a false prophetess who will not repent. That passage uses a Greek word for bed, not ʻeres, but it repeats the pattern Amos showed: the bed of sinful pleasure becomes the place of judgment.

7. Spirit & Life

The bed is where a person stops performing. In daytime we keep busy and present ourselves to others. At night, in bed, we face what is really in our hearts. Psalm 4:4 tells the troubled to "meditate within your heart on your bed, and be still." The bed can be where people plot evil (Micah 2:1), wallow in sloth, or cry out to God.

Psalm 6 and Psalm 41 encourage anyone who has spent long nights weeping or lying ill. Tears that soak the couch are not a sign that God has left. Psalm 6 ends with the confidence that "the LORD has heard" (6:9). Psalm 41 promises strength on the bed itself, and Psalm 139:8 says that even if "I make my bed in hell, behold, You are there."

Amos and Psalm 132 bring a sharper challenge. Comfort itself is not the danger. The danger is comfort that keeps us from caring about what God cares about. The leaders in Samaria were lying at ease while the nation was falling apart. David would not lie down while God's house was unbuilt. Every believer has to decide which of those two pictures their own rest resembles. Sometimes faithfulness means getting up from our couch, giving up sleep, ease, or rightful comfort, because something concerning God's presence matters more.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Every occurrence of ʻeres falls within the passages developed in Section 5: Deuteronomy 3:11; Job 7:13; Psalms 6:6; 41:3; 132:3; Proverbs 7:16; Song of Solomon 1:16; and Amos 3:12; 6:4.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H4904 (mishkab), bed, place of lying down. This is a distinct Hebrew word paired with ʻeres in parallel lines, as in Job 7:13 ("my couch") and Psalm 41:3 ("his sickbed"). It also appears in the wider bed imagery of Psalm 4:4.

  • G2825 (klinē), bed, couch. This is a Greek thematic parallel, not a lexical relative. It is used of the sickbed of judgment in Revelation 2:22.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Scripture sometimes uses other words for "bed" to picture death. Job says, "If I make my bed in the darkness" (Job 17:13), and Ezekiel describes nations whose "bed" is set "in the midst of the slain" (Ezekiel 32:25). ʻeres itself is not used this way, but those passages show that every earthly rest is temporary. The final rest of the body lies in the hands of the God who raised Jesus from the grave.
