DO, MAKE, PERFORM - (ʻâsâh, Strong's H6213)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʻâsâh is the Old Testament's everyday word for action. It means to do, make, perform, prepare, or produce, and English translations render it in dozens of ways: "made," "did," "done," "show," "deal," "keep," "execute," "yield." God uses it when He makes the sky and the stars. A cook uses it when preparing a meal, a craftsman when building an altar, a nation when committing a sin, and a tree when bearing fruit. The word has no moral color of its own. Its meaning depends on who acts and what is done.

This ordinary verb carries some of Scripture's largest themes. God is "the LORD our Maker" (Psalm 95:6), and because He made all things, He has the right to rule them and to receive our worship. Israel was called to hear God's word and do it. Instead, people kept trying to make their own gods and their own names. The Old Testament's answer comes through the same verb. In Ezekiel 36, God promises Israel that He will cleanse them, give them a new heart, put His Spirit within them, and cause them to do His will (Ezekiel 36:25–27). The New Testament connects that promise with Jesus Christ, who did the Father's will, and with the Spirit He gives.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻâsâh appears throughout the Hebrew Old Testament, in every kind of writing: creation accounts, building instructions, law, narrative, psalms, wisdom, and prophecy. It is heaviest where Scripture is most concerned with action. It dominates the tabernacle chapters of Exodus, the commands of Deuteronomy, the verdicts on kings ("he did evil" or "he did what was right in the sight of the LORD"), and Ezekiel's oracles of judgment and restoration.

English readers often miss how often the word repeats, because translators choose whatever English verb fits the sentence. When Isaiah's vineyard "brought forth" wild grapes and Ezra resolved "to do" the Law, the same Hebrew verb stands behind both.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, ʻâsâh means to bring something about by action. Context supplies the nuance. The main senses are:

1. To make or fashion something. This covers God making the firmament (Genesis 1:7), Noah building the ark (Genesis 6:14), and a craftsman carving an idol (Isaiah 44:15).

2. To do, perform, or carry out. People do commands or do evil, keep a feast, execute judgment, or perform a vow (Leviticus 18:5; Judges 2:11).

3. To prepare or offer. A meal is prepared (Genesis 18:7–8) and sacrifices are offered (Leviticus 9:7).

4. To produce or yield. Land and plants bring forth growth (Genesis 1:11; Jeremiah 17:8).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes everything. With God as subject, ʻâsâh describes creation, wonders, judgment, and deliverance. Moses sings of the God who is "fearful in praises, doing wonders" (Exodus 15:11). With people as subject, it measures faithfulness. They either do what God commands or do what is right in their own eyes (Judges 17:6).

One combination deserves attention. Joined with the Hebrew word chesed (steadfast, loyal love), ʻâsâh becomes "show kindness" or "deal kindly." Abraham's servant asks God to show kindness to his master (Genesis 24:12). God shows mercy to thousands who love Him (Exodus 20:6). David asks whether anyone remains from Saul's house to whom he may show the kindness of God (2 Samuel 9:1, 3, 7). In this expression, kindness takes concrete action, whether God or a human being shows it. David's kindness meant bringing a lame man named Mephibosheth to the king's table for the rest of his life.

4. Theological Significance

God the Maker has the right to rule. The psalmist's call to worship rests on God's making: "The sea is His, for He made it; And His hands formed the dry land. Oh come, let us worship and bow down; Let us kneel before the LORD our Maker" (Psalm 95:5–6). Ownership follows authorship. The same truth humbles us: "Can a man be more pure than his Maker?" (Job 4:17). It also makes us equals, because rich and poor share one Maker (Proverbs 22:2). And it gives joy: "Let Israel rejoice in their Maker" (Psalm 149:2). The God who made us has not left us to ourselves.

God acts freely and cannot be stopped. "But our God is in heaven; He does whatever He pleases" (Psalm 115:3; compare Psalm 135:6). In Isaiah 46:10, God declares that His counsel will stand and that He will do all His pleasure. God is never a spectator in history. What He intends, He does.

God's love takes action. When ʻâsâh is joined to chesed, God's character appears in deeds. He shows His love by keeping His promises, rescuing, providing, and forgiving.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 1–2 and Exodus 25–40: the made world and the made sanctuary. Genesis closes its creation account with repeated words of making: "God saw everything that He had made, and indeed it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). He "ended His work which He had done" (Genesis 2:2) and blessed the seventh day, "because in it He rested from all His work which God had created and made" (Genesis 2:3). Many readers hear Exodus echoing this pattern, though the text does not announce the parallel. God commands, "let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8), and the verb then runs through chapter after chapter of construction. At the end, "all the work of the tabernacle... was finished. And the children of Israel did according to all that the LORD had commanded Moses; so they did" (Exodus 39:32). Moses saw what they had done and blessed them (Exodus 39:43), much as God saw His work and blessed the seventh day. Read alongside Genesis, the tabernacle looks like a small, ordered world within a broken one. It was made where God chose to live among His people.

Isaiah 44 and Psalm 115: making gods. God made human beings, and human beings turned around and made gods. Isaiah describes a man who burns half a log to bake bread, and "the rest of it he makes into a god... Prays to it and says, 'Deliver me, for you are my god!'" (Isaiah 44:17). The same wood supplies fuel for his cooking and material for his god, and ʻâsâh is the verb for making that god (Isaiah 44:15, 17). Psalm 115 traces the result. The idols have eyes, "but they do not see," and ears, "but they do not hear" (Psalm 115:5–6). Then the psalm adds, "Those who make them are like them; So is everyone who trusts in them" (Psalm 115:8). The God who "does whatever He pleases" (Psalm 115:3) stands opposite the idol that can do nothing.

Genesis 11:4 and 2 Samuel 7:9: who makes the name? At Babel, the people said, "let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered" (Genesis 11:4). God scattered them. To David, a shepherd He had chosen, God said, "I... have made you a great name" (2 Samuel 7:9). Human pride tries to manufacture its own security and legacy. God gives a lasting name to the one He chooses.

Leviticus 18:5 and Ezekiel 36:27: from demand to gift. The law set life before Israel through doing: "You shall therefore keep My statutes and My judgments, which if a man does, he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5). Israel's history proved that the people did not do them. Ezekiel lists the abominations they "committed," and the same verb appears again and again. Then God addresses "the house of Israel" and uses ʻâsâh to describe what He will do for His holy name’s sake (Ezekiel 36:22). God will gather His scattered people, sprinkle clean water on them and cleanse them "from all your filthiness and from all your idols" (Ezekiel 36:25). He will give them "a new heart" and take out "the heart of stone" (Ezekiel 36:26). Then He will put His Spirit within them and cause them to walk in His statutes, so that they will keep His judgments and do them (Ezekiel 36:27). The requirement has not been lowered. What changes is the source of the doing. God Himself cleanses the people, renews their hearts, and takes up the work they could not do on their own.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of ʻâsâh moves from God's making, through human failure to do His will, to God doing what we could not. Jesus stands at the center of that movement. The psalmist says, "I delight to do Your will, O my God" (Psalm 40:8). Hebrews 10:5–7 places the psalm's declaration on the lips of Christ in its own wording: "To do Your will, O God." Isaiah's Servant "had done no violence" (Isaiah 53:9). Jesus said, "My food is to do the will of Him who sent Me, and to finish His work" (John 4:34). He is the one person who fully did what Leviticus 18:5 required.

Paul explains how His doing becomes ours: "For what the law could not do in that it was weak through the flesh, God did by sending His own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, on account of sin: He condemned sin in the flesh, that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us who do not walk according to the flesh but according to the Spirit" (Romans 8:3–4). Paul does not quote Ezekiel here, but the connection is strong. First God acted in His Son, dealing with sin itself by condemning it in the flesh of Christ. Then the Spirit produces in believers the obedience the law required. What Ezekiel promised to Israel, a cleansed people with a new heart and God's Spirit within, Paul describes as the work of the Son and the Spirit in all who belong to Christ.

The pattern of the made sanctuary also points forward. The tabernacle was the dwelling God commanded His people to make. In Christ, God came to live among us in person (John 1:14), and the New Testament speaks of believers as His dwelling place.

7. Spirit & Life

The same verb describes the fruitful life. Jeremiah describes the one who trusts the LORD as "a tree planted by the waters, Which spreads out its roots by the river... Nor will cease from yielding fruit" (Jeremiah 17:8). The tree does not strain to produce fruit. Its fruit comes from where it is planted. Psalm 1:3 gives the same picture and uses ʻâsâh for the outcome: "whatever he does shall prosper." Isaiah's vineyard is the warning on the other side. God dug it, cleared it, planted the choicest vine, and "expected it to bring forth good grapes, But it brought forth wild grapes" (Isaiah 5:2). Isaiah himself explains the picture. The vineyard is "the house of Israel," and God "looked for justice, but behold, oppression; For righteousness, but behold, a cry for help" (Isaiah 5:7). The wild grapes were the oppression of the weak and the cries of those who were wronged, in place of the justice and righteousness God was looking for. Good care alone did not change what the vine produced.

In Ecclesiastes the Preacher looks back on his own works: "Then I looked on all the works that my hands had done... And indeed all was vanity and grasping for the wind" (Ecclesiastes 2:11). He had made gardens and pools and gathered great wealth (Ecclesiastes 2:4–8). His heart rejoiced in his labor, and that was his reward (Ecclesiastes 2:10), yet he judged that there was "no profit under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 2:11). His account carries a warning for anyone who makes work the ultimate goal: work done apart from the Maker cannot give what only He gives.

For a believer, then, the freedom is real. We do not have to make a name, manufacture a god, or earn a life through restless effort. We are called to stay rooted in Christ, where the Spirit does in us what God promised. That life still looks like David at the table with Mephibosheth: love that acts, in concrete kindness to people who cannot repay it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 6:22 — Noah did everything God commanded him. It is an early picture of faith expressed in obedience.

  • Psalm 103:10 — God has not dealt with us according to our sins. Here God's "doing" is mercy that holds back the judgment we deserve.

  • Psalm 126:3 — The returning exiles confess that the LORD has done great things for them, and they are glad.

  • Jeremiah 22:3 — Judah's king is told to execute judgment and righteousness and to rescue the oppressed. Justice is something rulers are expected to do.

  • Micah 6:8 — The LORD requires His people to do justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with their God.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1254 (bārāʼ), "create." This is a distinct verb. In its sense create, it is used in Scripture only with God as subject. It stands beside ʻâsâh in Genesis 2:3: "all His work which God had created and made."

  • H3335 (yātsar), "form, fashion." This is a distinct verb of shaping. It is paired with ʻâsâh in Psalm 95:5: "He made it; And His hands formed the dry land."

  • H2617 (chesed), "steadfast love, kindness." This is a frequent canonical pairing in which ʻâsâh chesed means "show kindness" (Genesis 24:12; 2 Samuel 9:1).
