ESAU - (ʻÊsâv, Strong's H6215)

Summary

Esau was the firstborn twin son of Isaac and Rebekah, the older brother of Jacob, and the ancestor of the Edomites who settled in the mountain country of Seir. Genesis ties his name to his appearance at birth: "the first came out red. He was like a hairy garment all over; so they called his name Esau" (Genesis 25:25). The name is therefore usually understood as "hairy" or "rough." That meaning rests on the wordplay of Genesis itself, and the precise linguistic derivation of the name is not firmly established. His redness at birth anticipates his second name, Edom ("red"), which Genesis explicitly ties to the red stew he begged from Jacob (Genesis 25:30).

Esau's story is one of the most human in Genesis. He was a skilled hunter and his father's favorite. He traded his birthright for a single meal, lost his father's blessing to his brother's deception, vowed revenge, and then, twenty years later, ran to embrace the brother he had once meant to kill. God gave him a land of his own and later forbade Israel to take so much as a footstep of it. Yet Scripture remembers Esau chiefly as a man who did not value what God valued. The prophets speak of the "house of Esau" as the proud and violent enemy of Jacob, and the New Testament names him in three passages. Paul shows through Esau that God's saving purpose rests "not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11); Hebrews 11:20 counts Isaac's blessing of Esau among acts of faith; and Hebrews 12 warns believers not to be a "profane person like Esau, who for one morsel of food sold his birthright" (Hebrews 12:16). Esau stands as a sobering picture of a person who wanted the blessing but despised the birthright, and who wept when it was too late.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Esau is a single historical individual: the elder twin of Isaac and Rebekah, grandson of Abraham, and brother of Jacob (Genesis 25:24–26). He lived in the patriarchal period and became the father of a people. Genesis says it outright: "Esau is Edom" (Genesis 36:1, 8). Because of this, the name Esau is used in two ways in Scripture. In Genesis it refers to the man himself. In Deuteronomy, Jeremiah, Obadiah, and Malachi it often stands for his descendants, the nation of Edom, as in "the descendants of Esau, who live in Seir" (Deuteronomy 2:4) and "the house of Esau" (Obadiah 1:18). Readers should keep the two uses distinct, even though the prophets deliberately let the brothers' story echo in the nations' story. Edom (Strong's H123) and Seir (Strong's H8165) are separately indexed names. They are closely linked to Esau, but their occurrences are not part of this entry. In the New Testament the Greek form of his name is indexed separately as G2269.

The meaning of the name comes from Genesis 25:25. At birth the child was "like a hairy garment all over; so they called his name Esau." The Hebrew word for "hairy" (se'ar) sounds like the name of the region where Esau later lived, Seir, so the narrative links the man, his appearance, and his homeland. "Hairy" or "rough" is the sense the text itself points to, and it is the meaning traditionally given. Whether the form ʻÊsâv actually derives from that root is uncertain. The connection is best described as a biblical wordplay rather than a proven etymology.

A second wordplay attaches to the color red. The baby "came out red" (Genesis 25:25), and years later the famished hunter asked Jacob for "that same red stew," which is why "his name was called Edom" (Genesis 25:30). Esau's hairiness also figures in the plot of Genesis 27, where Jacob disguises himself with goatskins so that his blind father will take his smooth hands for Esau's (Genesis 27:11, 16, 22–23).

Esau's family relationships are recorded in some detail. He married Judith and Basemath, daughters of Hittites (Genesis 26:34), and later Mahalath, a daughter of Ishmael (Genesis 28:9). The genealogy of Genesis 36 lists his wives as Adah, Aholibamah, and Basemath (Genesis 36:2–3). The two lists do not match exactly, possibly because some women bore more than one name. Scripture does not explain the difference, and it should not be forced into a tidy solution.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Twins in Conflict (Genesis 25:19–28)

Rebekah had been barren until Isaac prayed for her. When she conceived, the children "struggled together within her," and the Lord told her that two nations were in her womb and that "the older shall serve the younger" (Genesis 25:22–23; quoted in Romans 9:12). Esau was born first, with Jacob holding his heel. As the boys grew, Esau became "a skillful hunter, a man of the field," while Jacob stayed among the tents. Isaac loved Esau because he enjoyed the game Esau brought him, and Rebekah loved Jacob (Genesis 25:27–28). The family's favoritism set the stage for much of the sorrow that followed.

The Birthright Sold (Genesis 25:29–34)

Esau came in from the field exhausted and saw Jacob cooking stew. He asked for some of the red stew, and Jacob demanded his birthright in exchange. Esau answered that he was about to die, so what use was a birthright to him? Jacob made him swear, and Esau sold it for bread and lentil stew. He ate, drank, rose, and went his way. Genesis then gives its own verdict: Esau despised his birthright (Genesis 25:34). Later law defines the firstborn's right as a double portion (Deuteronomy 21:17), and the right carried leadership in the family. In Abraham's family it also stood tied to the promises God had sworn. Esau treated all of it as worth less than one hot meal.

Marriages That Grieved His Parents (Genesis 26:34–35; 28:6–9)

At forty, Esau married two Hittite women, and Genesis says they were a grief of mind to Isaac and Rebekah (Genesis 26:34–35). Later, when Esau saw that Isaac had sent Jacob away to take a wife from their own relatives and had charged him not to marry a Canaanite, Esau understood that the Canaanite women displeased his father. He then went to Ishmael and married Mahalath, Ishmael's daughter (Genesis 28:6–9). The gesture shows Esau trying to win approval by copying the outward form of what his father wanted. He added a wife from Abraham's line. No seeking of the God of Abraham is recorded.

The Blessing Lost (Genesis 27)

When Isaac was old and nearly blind, he sent Esau to hunt game so that he could bless him before he died. Rebekah overheard and arranged for Jacob to impersonate his brother. Jacob received the blessing of the firstborn: abundance, rule over nations, and lordship over his brothers (Genesis 27:27–29). The deception was real sin. Genesis never excuses it, and Jacob would later be deceived himself.

Esau returned too late. When he learned what had happened, he cried "with an exceedingly great and bitter cry" and begged his father to bless him also (Genesis 27:34). He accused Jacob of supplanting him twice, first the birthright and now the blessing (Genesis 27:36), though the first had been his own choice. Isaac could not take back what he had spoken. At Esau's pleading and weeping (Genesis 27:38), Isaac gave him a sober word: in the NKJV his dwelling would be "of the fatness of the earth" (many other translations read the Hebrew preposition as "away from" the fatness, and the sense is debated); he would live by his sword, and serve his brother, but in time he would break his brother's yoke from his neck (Genesis 27:39–40). Hebrews 11:20 counts Isaac's blessing of both sons "concerning things to come" as an act of faith.

Esau's grief turned into hatred. He planned to kill Jacob once their father had died (Genesis 27:41). Rebekah learned of it and sent Jacob away to Laban in Haran (Genesis 27:42–45; 28:5).

Reconciliation (Genesis 32–33)

Twenty years later, Jacob returned to Canaan and sent messengers ahead "to Esau his brother in the land of Seir, the country of Edom" (Genesis 32:3). The messengers reported that Esau was coming with four hundred men. Jacob was terrified. He prayed for deliverance "from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau" (Genesis 32:11) and sent waves of livestock ahead as gifts. That same night he wrestled with God at Peniel.

What happened next surprised Jacob and still surprises readers. Esau ran to meet him, embraced him, fell on his neck, kissed him, and they wept together (Genesis 33:4). At first Esau declined the gifts, saying in effect that he had enough, and only accepted them when Jacob urged him (Genesis 33:9–11). Jacob told Esau that seeing his face was like seeing the face of God, because Esau had received him with favor (Genesis 33:10). Esau offered to travel with him or leave men to escort him, and then returned to Seir (Genesis 33:12–16). Whatever Esau lacked spiritually, he showed real generosity here. The brothers met once more to bury their father Isaac together (Genesis 35:29).

Settlement in Seir and Esau's Descendants (Genesis 36)

Both brothers had become too wealthy for the land to support them together, so Esau moved his household, his livestock, and all his goods away from Jacob and settled in Mount Seir (Genesis 36:6–8). Genesis 36 records his sons, grandsons, and the chiefs of Edom (Genesis 36:9–19, 40–43), and it notes that kings reigned in Edom before any king reigned over Israel (Genesis 36:31). Among Esau's grandsons was Amalek, son of Eliphaz by his concubine Timna (Genesis 36:12). Amalek's descendants became some of Israel's most persistent enemies. First Chronicles preserves the same line in Israel's official genealogies (1 Chronicles 1:34–35).

Esau's Descendants and Israel's Journey (Deuteronomy 2; Joshua 24)

Centuries later, as Israel approached the promised land, God gave Moses a firm command: "You are about to pass through the territory of your brethren, the descendants of Esau, who live in Seir; and they will be afraid of you. Therefore watch yourselves carefully. Do not meddle with them, for I will not give you any of their land, no, not so much as one footstep, because I have given Mount Seir to Esau as a possession" (Deuteronomy 2:4–5). Israel was to buy food and water from them (Deuteronomy 2:6, 29). Moses also records that the descendants of Esau had dispossessed the Horites who previously lived in Seir, just as Israel would do in Canaan, and that the Lord had done this for them (Deuteronomy 2:12, 22). Joshua's final sermon recounts the same history: God gave Isaac both Jacob and Esau, gave Mount Seir to Esau, while Jacob and his children went down into Egypt (Joshua 24:4). The law also told Israel not to abhor an Edomite, "for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7).

The House of Esau in the Prophets (Jeremiah, Obadiah, Malachi)

Later history between the two nations was bitter. In the prophets, "Esau" names the nation that gloated over Judah's collapse. Jeremiah announces that God will bring "the calamity of Esau" upon him and make Esau bare, uncovering his hiding places (Jeremiah 49:8, 10).

Obadiah gives the fullest picture. God asks, "Will I not in that day … Even destroy the wise men from Edom, And understanding from the mountains of Esau?" (Obadiah 1:8). The charge is specific: "For violence against your brother Jacob, Shame shall cover you, And you shall be cut off forever" (Obadiah 1:10). When foreigners entered Jerusalem's gates and cast lots for it, Edom "stood on the other side" and was "as one of them" (Obadiah 1:11). Edom gazed on Judah's disaster, rejoiced over it, spoke proudly, looted, and stood "at the crossroads To cut off those among them who escaped" (Obadiah 1:12–14). The verdict follows the principle of justice: "As you have done, it shall be done to you" (Obadiah 1:15). Then the image reverses Genesis: "The house of Jacob shall be a fire, And the house of Joseph a flame; But the house of Esau shall be stubble" (Obadiah 1:18). The book closes with deliverers coming to Mount Zion to judge the mountains of Esau and with the declaration that the kingdom will belong to the Lord (Obadiah 1:21).

Malachi, at the close of the Old Testament, begins with God's assurance of His love for Israel by pointing to the two brothers: He loved Jacob and hated Esau, and He laid Esau's mountains waste (Malachi 1:2–3). Paul later quotes this line: "As it is written, 'Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated'" (Romans 9:13).

Esau in the New Testament

Three New Testament passages name Esau: Romans 9:13, Hebrews 11:20, and Hebrews 12:16. In Romans 9, Paul explains that God's promise has not failed even though many in Israel have not believed. He points to the twins: "for the children not yet being born, nor having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works but of Him who calls," it was said to Rebekah, "The older shall serve the younger" (Romans 9:11–12). In Hebrews 12, the writer warns the church against any "profane person like Esau, who for one morsel of food sold his birthright. For you know that afterward, when he wanted to inherit the blessing, he was rejected, for he found no place for repentance, though he sought it diligently with tears" (Hebrews 12:16–17). Between these, Hebrews 11:20 names Esau in the record of Isaac's faith: "By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning things to come."

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God chose Jacob, the younger, to carry the line of promise, and that He announced this before the twins were born (Genesis 25:23; Romans 9:11–12). Paul's point is that the promise depends on God who calls, not on birth order, natural strength, or human achievement. Esau had every natural advantage: he was firstborn, strong, skilled, and loved by his father. None of it made him the heir of the promise. Interpreters differ on how far Romans 9 speaks of individual salvation and how far it speaks of the roles of peoples in history. Malachi's original words clearly have the two nations in view. On either reading, Paul's central claim stands: God's purpose is His own, and it cannot be earned. The word "hated" in Malachi and Romans belongs to a Hebrew way of expressing choice by sharp contrast. Genesis uses similar language when Leah is described as unloved compared with Rachel (Genesis 29:31). The word marks God's choosing of Jacob for the line of promise, and this contrast idiom is a legitimate part of its meaning. It is not the whole meaning, however, for Malachi goes on to speak of Esau's mountains laid waste, "the Territory of Wickedness," and "the people against whom the LORD will have indignation forever" (Malachi 1:3–4). That is real rejection and judgment, answering Edom's wickedness. It does not describe a petty divine grudge.

It is equally clear from Scripture that God did not abandon Esau. He gave Esau a homeland and defended it against His own chosen people (Deuteronomy 2:5; Joshua 24:4). He gave Esau's descendants victory over the Horites as He would give Israel victory in Canaan (Deuteronomy 2:12, 22). He told Israel to treat the Edomite as a brother (Deuteronomy 23:7). From this a careful implication follows: God's choosing of one line for redemption does not mean indifference toward the others. He rules the boundaries of every nation and keeps His word to those outside the chosen line.

Scripture also holds Esau and his descendants responsible for their own choices. Genesis says Esau despised his birthright (Genesis 25:34), and Hebrews calls him profane (Hebrews 12:16). Edom's judgment in Obadiah rests on concrete acts of violence, gloating, and betrayal against a brother (Obadiah 1:10–14). God's sovereign choice and human accountability stand side by side in these texts, and neither cancels the other.

The movement across the canon is sobering. The man Esau forgave his brother (Genesis 33:4). His descendants refused Israel passage (Numbers 20:14–21, under the name Edom) and later cheered at Jerusalem's fall (Obadiah 1:11–12). Amalek, Esau's grandson, founded a people who attacked Israel in the wilderness (Genesis 36:12; Exodus 17:8–16). Scripture does not draw a single explicit line from Esau's indifference to his descendants' hatred. Still, the canonical arc invites a contrast: the man who despised the birthright stands at the head of a people who, over generations, came into open hostility toward the people who carried the promise. Scripture does not judge the inner state of every descendant or trace a hereditary chain of cause.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest New Testament use of Esau comes in Hebrews 12. There the writer sets Esau's choice beside the inheritance believers have received. Esau sold his firstborn's right for one meal. Christians, by contrast, have come "to Mount Zion" and "to the general assembly and church of the firstborn who are registered in heaven" (Hebrews 12:22–23). The warning is explicit: do not do what Esau did with what God has given you in Christ. The firstborn's privilege that Esau threw away now belongs to everyone joined to Jesus, who is Himself "the firstborn among many brethren" (Romans 8:29; see Colossians 1:15). The privilege is far greater, and so is the loss of treating it lightly.

Romans 9 places Esau within a pattern that runs through Genesis and beyond: God repeatedly passes over the elder or the stronger to choose the younger or the unexpected. Abel was accepted rather than Cain, Isaac rather than Ishmael, Jacob rather than Esau, and later Ephraim was set before Manasseh and David was taken from the sheepfold. This is strongly supported canonical development, and it prepares for the gospel, where God's saving grace comes to those who have no claim on it (Romans 9:16).

Obadiah's vision ends with the kingdom belonging to the Lord and deliverance on Mount Zion (Obadiah 1:17, 21). The New Testament announces that this kingdom has drawn near in Jesus. One small detail in the Gospels is worth noticing. Among the crowds who came to hear Jesus were people from Idumea, the Greek name for the land of Edom (Mark 3:8). Residents of Esau's former country stood among those drawn to Israel's Messiah. That detail fits the broader promise that the nations will be brought under the Lord's rule in mercy as well as judgment.

There is also a thematic resonance, though Scripture does not draw it explicitly. Esau, famished, gave up his inheritance for food. Jesus, hungry after forty days in the wilderness, refused to turn stones into bread and answered from Scripture that man does not live by bread alone (Matthew 4:1–4). Where Esau valued the immediate over what God had promised, the true Son trusted His Father's word over His own hunger.

5. Spirit & Life

Esau's life confronts a danger that is easy to underestimate: treating holy things as ordinary. He did not curse God or bow to idols that Genesis records. He simply valued a meal more than the birthright. Hebrews calls this "profane." The word describes a person for whom nothing is sacred, for whom God's promises carry no more weight than his appetite. Esau's question, what use is a birthright to a dying man (Genesis 25:32), is the question of every heart that measures life by what it feels right now. The letter to the Hebrews was written to believers tempted to drift away from Christ under pressure, and it places Esau before them as a warning.

His tears teach something just as important. Esau wept loudly over the lost blessing (Genesis 27:34, 38), and Hebrews says he "sought it diligently with tears" (Hebrews 12:17). Interpreters debate whether the "repentance" he could not find refers to a change of heart in Esau or a change of mind in Isaac. On either reading, his tears were for what he had lost. Genesis never shows him sorrowing over having despised what God had attached to the birthright. Paul distinguishes the "godly sorrow" that produces repentance from the "sorrow of the world" that produces death (2 Corinthians 7:10). It is possible to grieve the consequences of sin bitterly and still not turn to God. Esau wanted his father's blessing without having valued his father's God.

At the same time, Scripture refuses to let us make Esau a simple villain. In Genesis 33 he behaves better than Jacob might have deserved. He runs, embraces, weeps, forgives, and declines gifts. Jacob, who had wrestled with God the night before, saw something of God's own favor in his brother's welcoming face (Genesis 33:10). The lesson is uncomfortable. A person can be generous, warm, and forgiving and still live without regard for God. Natural decency is a good gift, and Scripture honors it in Esau. But it is not the same thing as faith, and it cannot replace receiving the inheritance God offers.

God's dealings with Esau also reveal His character. He chose Jacob, and He also guarded Esau's land, commanding Israel not to touch one footstep of it (Deuteronomy 2:5). He holds nations to account for how they treat their brothers. Obadiah's charge against Edom, "you should not have gazed on the day of your brother" (Obadiah 1:12), searches the heart of anyone who has quietly enjoyed another's downfall. Gloating over a brother's ruin is the opposite of the heart Esau showed in Genesis 33. It is the attitude that God judged in his descendants.

Esau's story finally drives us to the grace of God in Christ. If the promise depended on natural advantage, Esau had it. If it depended on moral record, Jacob the deceiver would not have qualified. It rests "not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11). That is humbling and freeing at once. No one earns the birthright, and no one who has received it in Christ should ever treat it as something to sell.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 25:21–28 — The oracle before the twins' birth, Esau's birth and naming as "hairy," and the family's divided loyalties.

  • Genesis 25:29–34 — Esau sells his birthright for stew and is named Edom; Genesis says he despised his birthright.

  • Genesis 26:34–35; 28:6–9 — Esau's marriages, his parents' grief, and his attempt to please his father by marrying Ishmael's daughter.

  • Genesis 27:1–45 — The stolen blessing, Esau's bitter cry, Isaac's word about his future, and Esau's vow to kill Jacob.

  • Genesis 32:3–21; 33:1–16 — Jacob's fear and Esau's gracious reconciliation with his brother.

  • Genesis 35:29; 36:1–43 — The brothers bury Isaac together; Esau settles in Seir; "Esau is Edom"; his descendants include Amalek and the chiefs and kings of Edom.

  • Deuteronomy 2:4–8, 12, 22, 29; Joshua 24:4 — God gives Mount Seir to Esau and forbids Israel to take any of it.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:34–35 — Esau's line preserved in Israel's genealogies.

  • Jeremiah 49:8–10 — The "calamity of Esau" announced.

  • Obadiah 1:8–21 — Judgment on the house of Esau for violence against Jacob, ending with the kingdom belonging to the Lord.

  • Malachi 1:2–3 — "Jacob I have loved … Esau I have hated" as assurance of God's love for Israel.

  • Romans 9:10–16 — Paul uses Esau and Jacob to show that God's purpose rests on His call, not on works.

  • Hebrews 12:15–17, 22–23 — Esau as the "profane person" who sold his birthright and could not recover the blessing, contrasted with the church of the firstborn.

  • Mark 3:8 — People from Idumea (Edom's land) among the crowds who came to Jesus.
