SMOKE, SMOKING - (ʻâshân, Strong's H6227)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻâshân simply means "smoke," the dark, rising cloud given off by fire. Scripture uses it for ordinary things such as a chimney, a burning city, or a stinging irritant in the eyes. The biblical writers also notice what smoke does. It rises high and can be seen from far away. It hides what is behind it. Then it thins out and disappears. Each of these qualities becomes a way of teaching about God and about human life.

The most striking uses of the word surround God's presence. A smoking oven passes between Abram's divided animals when God binds Himself to His promise. Sinai is wrapped in smoke "like the smoke of a furnace" when the LORD comes down. The temple fills with smoke as the seraphim cry "Holy." Isaiah later promises a cloud and smoke resting over Zion as a covering and shelter for God's cleansed people. Smoke also carries a sharp contrast. The wicked "vanish away" into smoke (Psalm 37:20), yet the smoke of final judgment "shall ascend forever" (Isaiah 34:10). The prosperity of evil does not last, but God's justice against it does.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻâshân is a common Hebrew noun for physical smoke. In Israel's world, smoke was part of daily life. It rose from cooking fires, ovens, chimneys, and altars. In wartime, smoke rising over a captured city could be seen from far away, sometimes while the battle around it was still going on (Joshua 8:20–21). The word appears in narrative, poetry, wisdom sayings, and prophecy. Its symbolic uses depend on these familiar experiences: the sting of smoke in the eyes, the dark column on the horizon, and the way smoke drifts off in the wind.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻâshân means smoke, the visible cloud produced by burning. The NKJV translates it "smoke" in every occurrence except Genesis 15:17, where an oven of smoke becomes "a smoking oven." The word has one basic meaning. Its figurative uses come from what smoke is like: dense and frightening, fragrant when incense burns, irritating, visible at a distance, or quickly gone.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Smoke as a battlefield signal. At Ai, Joshua stretched out his spear toward the city at the LORD's command, and the men in ambush rose, entered the city while its defenders were out chasing Joshua's army, took it, and set it on fire (Joshua 8:18–19). When the men of Ai looked back, "the smoke of the city ascended to heaven" (Joshua 8:20), and they had no way to flee. Joshua and all Israel saw that the ambush had taken the city, turned back, and struck them down (Joshua 8:21–22). At Gibeah the smoke was explicitly the "appointed signal" planned ahead of time (Judges 20:38). When the Benjamites "looked behind them," they saw "the whole city going up in smoke to heaven" (Judges 20:40). Israel's army turned, and Benjamin panicked and fled (Judges 20:41–42). In both stories the smoke rose while the fighting was still under way. At Ai it showed Israel that the city was taken and prompted the counterattack; at Gibeah it was the agreed signal for Israel to turn. Both times it marked the moment the battle turned against the enemy.

Smoke as something that vanishes. The psalms and prophets use smoke as a picture of what has no lasting substance. The enemies of the LORD "shall vanish. Into smoke they shall vanish away" (Psalm 37:20). Hosea groups smoke with other things that pass quickly, saying that Ephraim, the northern kingdom of Israel, will be "like the morning cloud... like chaff... And like smoke from a chimney" (Hosea 13:3).

Smoke as an irritant. "As vinegar to the teeth and smoke to the eyes, So is the lazy man to those who send him" (Proverbs 10:26). A messenger who will not do his job is as hard to put up with as smoke in the eyes.

Smoke from the nostrils. Hebrew often speaks of anger through the image of the nose, as in a snorting rage. David's song of deliverance pictures God this way: "Smoke went up from His nostrils, And devouring fire from His mouth" (Psalm 18:8; 2 Samuel 22:9). Job 41:20 gives a similar description of Leviathan: "Smoke goes out of his nostrils." God draws the lesson Himself: no one dares stir up Leviathan, so "Who then is able to stand against Me?" (Job 41:10).

4. Theological Significance

When God appears, smoke both shows His presence and hides it. Everyone at Sinai could see that the LORD was on the mountain, yet no one could see Him. The smoke was the visible sign that the LORD had come down on the mountain (Exodus 19:18). What kept the people back was the boundary God Himself commanded. Moses was to set bounds around the mountain, and the LORD warned the people not to break through "to gaze at the LORD, and many of them perish" (Exodus 19:12–13, 21–24). God made Himself known, and through that boundary He protected the people from coming near in a way that would destroy them.

Smoke also shows that God's anger is a response to something. In Psalm 18, the smoke from God's nostrils is not random rage. David cried out in his distress, and God came down to rescue him (Psalm 18:6–17). God's anger here moves Him to save His servant.

Isaiah 65:5 turns this image around. God describes people who practice pagan rituals and still tell others, "Keep to yourself, Do not come near me, For I am holier than you!" God's verdict is, "These are smoke in My nostrils, A fire that burns all the day." In Psalm 18 the smoke comes out of God's nostrils. Here self-righteous religion is smoke that goes into them. Such people claim to be holier than others. To God it is a constant, stinging offense, much like the smoke in the eyes of Proverbs 10:26.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 15:17 — The smoking oven between the pieces. Abram asked how he could know he would inherit the land (Genesis 15:8). God told him to cut animals in half and lay the halves opposite each other. Jeremiah 34:18–20 shows what this ritual could mean. The leaders of Judah made a covenant by passing between the parts of a calf, and when they broke it, God gave them over to death. Walking between the pieces said, in effect, Let this happen to me if I break my word. In Genesis 15, Abram never walked through. Instead, "there appeared a smoking oven and a burning torch that passed between those pieces." Then Genesis adds, "On the same day the LORD made a covenant with Abram" (Genesis 15:18). Only the signs of God's presence passed between the pieces. God took the initiative and bound Himself alone to keep the promise. If the ritual carried the meaning Jeremiah describes, God was placing Himself under the sanction of His own word, though Genesis does not say so. The certainty of Abram's future rested entirely on God.

Exodus 19:18 and Isaiah 6:4 — Smoke and holiness. At Sinai, "the LORD descended upon it in fire. Its smoke ascended like the smoke of a furnace, and the whole mountain quaked greatly." The smoke of Genesis 15 came from one small oven. At Sinai it covered the entire mountain, and God commanded bounds to keep the people from coming close (Exodus 19:12, 21). Centuries later, Isaiah saw the Lord in the temple. The seraphim called out God's holiness, and "the posts of the door were shaken by the voice of him who cried out, and the house was filled with smoke." In both scenes, smoke and shaking come together when God's holiness is revealed. Isaiah responded by crying out over his unclean lips (Isaiah 6:5). The smoke showed the distance between a holy God and sinful people.

Isaiah 4:5 — Smoke becomes shelter. Isaiah 4 looks ahead to a Zion washed of its filth (4:4), when "the Branch of the LORD shall be beautiful and glorious" (4:2). Then "the LORD will create above every dwelling place of Mount Zion, and above her assemblies, a cloud and smoke by day and the shining of a flaming fire by night. For over all the glory there will be a covering." This promise recalls the pillar of cloud and fire that led Israel out of Egypt (Exodus 13:21–22), and it echoes the smoke of Sinai. Its purpose, however, is new. At Sinai God set bounds to keep the people back from the smoking mountain. In Zion the cloud and smoke rest over every home and every gathering as "a shelter from storm and rain" (4:6). The holiness that once required a boundary now covers and protects God's people.

Psalm 37:20 and Isaiah 34:10 — Two kinds of smoke. The wicked "shall vanish. Into smoke they shall vanish away" (Psalm 37:20). The judgment on Edom is described differently: "It shall not be quenched night or day; Its smoke shall ascend forever" (Isaiah 34:10). Both pictures are true together. The wicked themselves, with all their apparent power, will drift off like smoke in the wind. God's judgment on them will stand like a column of smoke that never fades. What looks strong now will not last, and the justice that seems slow now will not end.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The movement from Sinai to Zion is one of the main threads of this word's story, and Hebrews 12 brings it to Christ. "You have not come to the mountain that may be touched and that burned with fire, and to blackness and darkness and tempest" (Hebrews 12:18). Instead, "you have come to Mount Zion... to Jesus the Mediator of the new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling" (Hebrews 12:22, 24). Hebrews does not use the word "smoke," but it describes the same mountain of Exodus 19, and its Mount Zion fits the hope Isaiah 4 held out. The holy God has not changed. Hebrews still warns, "See that you do not refuse Him who speaks" (Hebrews 12:25). What has changed is that a Mediator now stands between God and His people. Isaiah tied the covering over Zion to the day of "the Branch of the LORD," a title the prophets elsewhere use for the coming Messiah.

Genesis 15 points in the same direction. God passed between the pieces alone and bound Himself to His promise. Genesis does not say that He took a penalty on Himself, but the New Testament shows how far God went to keep His word. Paul writes that "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us," so "that the blessing of Abraham might come upon the Gentiles in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:13–14). The God who alone passed between the pieces is the God who, in His Son, bore the curse so that the promise to Abraham would stand for His people. Seeing the oven and torch as a foreshadowing of the cross is a connection drawn from the whole Bible, and it rests on Paul's plain statement.

Smoke also marks the end of history. Joel foretold wonders in heaven and earth, "blood and fire and pillars of smoke," before the great and awesome day of the LORD (Joel 2:30). Peter quoted this prophecy on the Day of Pentecost (Acts 2:19). The outpoured Spirit shows that the last days have begun. Revelation takes up Isaiah 34:10 for final judgment: "the smoke of their torment ascends forever and ever" (Revelation 14:11). It uses the same picture for fallen Babylon (Revelation 19:3). The smoke Isaiah saw rising over Edom becomes Scripture's final picture of judgment that does not end.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 37 was written for people troubled by evildoers who seem to prosper. Its answer is not that evil is weak today. Its answer is that evil does not last. The powers that intimidate believers, whether people, systems, or fears that seem to block out the sky, are smoke in the psalm's picture. They can sting and blind for a while, but the wind of God's purposes will carry them away. Isaiah 51:6 says that even the heavens will vanish like smoke, while God's salvation lasts forever. A believer can stand with confidence by building life on what God says will remain.

Psalm 18 adds something personal. God's anger can be on the side of His child. David was surrounded by "the pangs of death" (Psalm 18:4), and God came down in smoke and fire to rescue him. A believer can pray knowing that God takes the threats against His people seriously.

Isaiah 65:5 also warns against a quieter danger. "I am holier than you" is not only the attitude of pagans. It can creep into any heart that measures itself against others. God calls that attitude smoke in His nostrils. Isaiah 4 offers a different picture: those who remain in Zion are "called holy" after the Lord has washed them (Isaiah 4:3–4), and His covering rests over them (Isaiah 4:5). In that picture, holiness is not achieved by keeping others at a distance. It comes from God's washing and is lived under His covering.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 68:2 — The psalmist asks God to drive His enemies away as smoke is driven away, which is a prayer based on how easily wind scatters smoke.

  • Psalm 102:3 — A suffering man says his days are used up like smoke. The same image that comforts the righteous about the wicked here describes the frailty of a person crying out to God.

  • Song of Solomon 3:6 — The poem asks, "Who is this coming out of the wilderness Like pillars of smoke," perfumed with myrrh and frankincense? The next verse points to Solomon's couch, carried with sixty valiant men around it (3:7). Here smoke belongs to a fragrant royal procession.

  • Isaiah 9:18 — Wickedness burns like a fire that devours the briers and thorns and kindles the thickets of the forest, which "mount up like rising smoke." The next verse adds that, through the wrath of the LORD, the people themselves "shall be as fuel for the fire" (Isaiah 9:19).

  • Isaiah 14:31 — Philistia is told that smoke is coming from the north, a warning that an invading army is on its way.

  • Nahum 2:13 — The LORD declares He will burn Nineveh's chariots in smoke. The weapons the empire relied on will end as smoke.
