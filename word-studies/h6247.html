CARVED, PLATE - (ʻesheth, Strong's H6247)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʻesheth appears only once in the Bible, in the bride's praise of her beloved in Song of Solomon 5:14: "His body is carved ivory inlaid with sapphires." It describes a worked slab or plate of solid material with a smooth, finished surface. The NKJV translates it "carved," which emphasizes skilled workmanship. Other translations say "bright" or "polished," which emphasize the gleam of the finished piece. Both ideas belong to the picture of a solid block of ivory, shaped and smoothed until it shines.

The image says a great deal. In Solomon's world, ivory was a costly luxury used for a king's throne and palaces, and elsewhere in Scripture sapphire appears in scenes of God's throne. By describing the center of her beloved's body this way, the bride gives him a kingly beauty and presents him as priceless, strong, and without flaw. Christians have long read the Bridegroom of the Song as a picture of Christ. Read that way, this one rare word offers a fitting picture of what the New Testament says about the Son of God: His love for His people is firm and pure, and He reigns in heavenly glory.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻEsheth is a Hebrew noun connected to the verb ʻashath (H6245), which carries the idea of being smooth, sleek, or shiny. Because the noun occurs only once, translators rely heavily on its setting to understand it.

That setting is a love poem. In Song of Solomon 5:10–16 the Shulamite describes her beloved from head to foot. Each part of his body is compared to precious materials: gold, beryl, ivory, sapphire, marble, and cedar. She is answering the question the daughters of Jerusalem ask in 5:9, "What is your beloved more than another beloved?" ʻEsheth is part of her answer, and it comes at the center of the description, where she speaks of his body.

Ivory had to be imported, so only the wealthy could afford it. The stone called "sapphire" in English Bibles was a deep blue gem, and it may refer to what is now called lapis lazuli. Both materials signaled great value.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻEsheth means a fabricated slab, block, or plate: a solid piece of material that has been worked and given a smooth, gleaming finish. The word holds two ideas together:

1. Solidity: a block or plate of material that is firm and whole.

2. Finish: a surface that has been worked by a skilled hand until it is smooth and bright.

The NKJV's "carved" draws attention to the craftsmanship. "Polished" and "bright" draw attention to the shine. With only one occurrence, the poem's own description of a solid, finished, gem-set piece of ivory is the best guide.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Since the word appears only in Song of Solomon 5:14, its usage is defined by that single sentence. It is joined to "ivory," so the phrase means something like a worked plate of ivory. It describes the beloved's "body," which renders a Hebrew word (meʻim) for the inward parts or abdomen. Elsewhere in Scripture the same word often names the place where deep feeling is felt, much as English speaks of the "heart."

The surrounding verses use similar crafted imagery. His hands are "rods of gold set with beryl" (5:14), and his legs are pillars of marble (5:15). The bride pictures him as a finished work of art made from the finest materials. ʻEsheth contributes the idea of a smooth, unbroken surface at the very center of that portrait.

4. Theological Significance

Although this word describes a human lover in a love poem, its imagery opens up truths about God's ways that appear throughout Scripture.

First, Scripture treats love as something that sees and praises. The bride does not love vaguely. She studies her beloved closely and finds language worthy of him. God's people are called to the same attentive praise of the One they love.

Second, the materials matter. Ivory belongs to the king's world, and sapphire appears elsewhere in Scripture in visions of God's throne. The Song does not say it is drawing on those throne scenes, but a reader who knows them will hear the echo. Joined in one image, the two materials give the beloved a kingly beauty and a glory beyond ordinary admiration. This is one reason the church has heard the voice of Christ and His people in the Song: the language reaches higher than ordinary praise of a man.

Third, the image puts beauty and strength together. An ʻesheth is lovely, but it is also a solid block. The picture fits what Scripture teaches elsewhere: God's goodness is not fragile, and His love has substance and weight.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:14 in its setting. The verse comes after one of the saddest moments in the Song. In 5:2–6 the beloved knocks at night, and the bride hesitates to rise; when she finally opens the door, he has gone. She searches the city, is mistreated by the watchmen, and then is asked why her beloved is so special. Her answer, in verses 10–16, is not a complaint about his absence. It is praise.

Against this background, the description of his meʻim as a solid, polished plate of ivory stands out. Her heart yearned for him when he put his hand by the latch (5:4, where "heart" renders the same word, meʻim). She rose to open, but he had turned away and was gone; her heart leaped up when he spoke, and she sought and called him without finding him (5:5–6). The text does not describe his feelings directly. The contrast is one the reader sees by setting the two passages side by side: the bride's delay in 5:3–6, and the whole, gleaming image she chooses for his inner being in 5:14. She ends by saying, in effect, that he is altogether lovely, her beloved and her friend (5:16).

Ivory and the throne of the king (1 Kings 10:18; Psalm 45:8). Solomon "made a great throne of ivory, and overlaid it with pure gold." Psalm 45, a royal wedding song, speaks of the king's garments coming "out of the ivory palaces." Ivory was a royal luxury, used for a king's throne and the rooms of his palace. When the bride uses an ʻesheth of ivory to describe her beloved, she gives him a kingly beauty. The verse does not name him king by using ivory, but the material carries that association.

Sapphire and the glory of God (Exodus 24:10; Ezekiel 1:26). When Moses and the elders of Israel "saw the God of Israel," there was "under His feet as it were a paved work of sapphire stone." Ezekiel saw "the likeness of a throne, in appearance like a sapphire stone," and on it "a likeness with the appearance of a man high above it." The Song does not point to these scenes, but for a reader of the whole Bible the bride's sapphires recall them: the ivory plate is set with stones that elsewhere appear in visions of God's throne.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament does not quote Song of Solomon, but it does present Christ as the Bridegroom of His people (Ephesians 5:25–32). Psalm 45, the wedding song of the king from the "ivory palaces," is applied directly to the Son in Hebrews 1:8–9. With that warrant, reading the royal beloved of Song of Solomon 5 as a picture of Christ follows a well-traveled path in the church. It is a canonical reading of the poem, not the poem's stated meaning.

Ezekiel's sapphire throne is occupied by one with "the appearance of a man." The New Testament speaks of Jesus in a way that invites comparison with both the royal ivory and the sapphire throne. Hebrews 1:8 applies to the Son the words "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever," and Hebrews 7:26 describes Him as a High Priest who is "holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners, and has become higher than the heavens." Neither the Song nor Ezekiel names Him, but the bride's picture of a worked ivory plate set with sapphires offers a fitting image of the One whose human life was flawless and whose throne is in heaven.

Christ's purity also reaches His people. He sanctifies and cleanses His bride so that He may present her "a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing" (Ephesians 5:27). The polished, unblemished Bridegroom intends a bride without blemish.

7. Spirit & Life

The most searching insight here comes from placing the bride's word beside her own delay. She yearned for him but did not rise in time. Many believers know that experience: real love for Christ mixed with sluggishness, delay, and divided attention. Yet when she speaks of his inward parts, she describes something solid and smooth, an unbroken slab of the finest material. The Song does not draw that contrast for us, but the image invites it.

God speaks of His own heart the same way. In Jeremiah 31:20, after rebuking Ephraim, the Lord says, "My heart yearns for him; I will surely have mercy on him." The word for "heart" there is the same word for inward parts. "Though I spoke against him," the Lord says, "I earnestly remember him still." The New Testament shows the same steady love in Christ. He "loved the church and gave Himself for her" (Ephesians 5:25), and "having loved His own who were in the world, He loved them to the end" (John 13:1). Scripture states this directly. The Song's image does not prove it, but it gives believers a fitting picture of it: a solid, gleaming ʻesheth at the center of the beloved. Christ's love is a tender feeling, and it is also a fixed reality that does not crack.

This is why the bride's praise, given while her beloved is still absent, is a model for faith. She describes his worth from what she knows. A believer in a dry or regretful season can do the same thing: recall what Christ is, speak it, and let that praise turn the heart back toward Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

ʻEsheth occurs only in Song of Solomon 5:14. The best companion reading is the whole description in Song of Solomon 5:10–16, the bride's portrait of her beloved from his head of "finest gold" to his legs like marble pillars.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H6245 (ʻashath), a verb associated with being smooth, sleek, or shining. ʻEsheth is connected to this root, which explains translations such as "polished" and "bright."

  • Canonical pairing: In Song of Solomon 5:14, ʻesheth is joined with ivory (H8127, shen) and sapphire (H5601, sappir). The ivory links to royal imagery in 1 Kings 10:18 and Psalm 45:8, and the sapphire links to throne imagery in Exodus 24:10 and Ezekiel 1:26.
