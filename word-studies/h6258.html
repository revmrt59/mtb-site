NOW - (ʻattâh, Strong's H6258)

Summary

ʻAttâh is the ordinary Hebrew word for "now." It points to the present moment in contrast to what came before or what lies ahead. Very often, especially in the phrases "and now" or "now therefore," it does more than tell time. It works as a hinge in speech, moving from what has been said to what must happen because of it. A speaker reviews history, states a grievance, or recalls a promise, and then ʻattâh turns the listener toward a command, a request, a verdict, or a fresh act of God.

That small hinge carries a great deal of biblical weight. God uses it to pronounce judgment, as in "So now you are cursed" (Genesis 4:11). He also uses it to break into Israel's deserved ruin with grace: "But now, thus says the LORD... 'Fear not, for I have redeemed you'" (Isaiah 43:1). Through Joel He calls people to repent while judgment is still approaching. Caleb, David, and Daniel use the same word to take hold of God's past words and actions in the present. Again and again, the word shows that what God has done and promised is meant to be answered in the moment one is living.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʻAttâh is a Hebrew adverb used throughout the Old Testament, from Genesis to Malachi. It belongs mostly to spoken language: conversations, negotiations, prayers, speeches by leaders, and prophetic oracles. That is why it clusters in the narratives of Genesis and 1–2 Samuel and in the prophets Isaiah and Jeremiah. People say "now" when they are talking to someone and want something to happen.

It should be kept distinct from broader Hebrew time words such as yôm ("day") or ʻēt ("time, season"). Those words can describe an era. ʻAttâh names the present, actionable moment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʻAttâh means "now," the present as distinct from past or future. Context brings out three main uses:

1. The present moment. This is plain temporal "now," often set against an earlier time. Caleb says, "just as my strength was then, so now is my strength for war" (Joshua 14:11).

2. "Now therefore," a logical turn. Here the word introduces a consequence, command, or appeal based on what has just been said. An example is "Now therefore, thus says the LORD of hosts: 'Consider your ways!'" (Haggai 1:5).

3. A starting point that reaches forward. Joined with words meaning "from" and "forever," it marks the present as the beginning of something lasting. The NKJV renders it this way in Psalm 113:2, where the LORD's name is blessed from this time forth and forevermore.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In narrative, ʻattâh often moves a conversation toward a decision. Abimelech asks Abraham to swear an oath "now" (Genesis 21:23). Joseph's brothers plot, "Come therefore, let us now kill him" (Genesis 37:20). Abigail turns from pleading to offering her gift (1 Samuel 25:26–27).

On God's lips, the word announces a change in how He will deal with people. Sometimes the change is judgment, as with Cain. Sometimes it is a call to obedience, as in Haggai. Sometimes it is sudden mercy, as in Isaiah 43:1. In prayer, it marks the moment the petitioner moves from recalling God's acts to asking for help.

The most important grammatical pattern is the pairing with the simple Hebrew "and," usually rendered "and now" or "now therefore." That pairing often introduces an inference, appeal, or consequence drawn from what came before, though it can also mark a transition in time or a contrast, as in Isaiah 43:1's "But now." Context decides. In 2 Chronicles 10:11 the "now" construction introduces Rehoboam's comparison of his harsh policy with his father's.

4. Theological Significance

Across these uses, God shows Himself as One who acts in the present and expects to be answered in the present. His dealings are never abstract. When Cain sheds Abel's blood, God's verdict arrives immediately: "So now you are cursed from the earth" (Genesis 4:11). When the returned exiles let God's house lie in ruins, His word comes as "Now therefore... Consider your ways!" (Haggai 1:5). Sin has consequences, and God names them in the present.

Yet God's "now" is not limited to verdicts. The same word can introduce grace that has no basis in what came before. God uses it to call people to repentance while judgment is near, and His people use it to hold Him to His word. Behind every "now therefore" stands a "because." God has spoken, acted, delivered, or promised, and that history obliges and invites a response today.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 42:23–43:1 — the "now" of unearned redemption. Isaiah 42 ends in fire. The prophet asks who gave Jacob to the plunderers and answers, "Was it not the LORD, He against whom we have sinned?" (42:24). God "has poured on him the fury of His anger," and still Israel "did not take it to heart" (42:25). By every human logic, the next word should be final destruction. Instead comes "But now, thus says the LORD, who created you, O Jacob... 'Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; You are Mine'" (43:1). Nothing in Israel's response explains the turn. The only basis is the identity of the One who created and formed them. Here ʻattâh marks the point where God's mercy overrides a history of failure.

Joel 2:12 and Daniel 9 — the open window of repentance. Joel describes the LORD's army sweeping over the land like an unstoppable judgment. Then, with the threat still hanging in the air, God speaks: "'Now, therefore,' says the LORD, 'Turn to Me with all your heart, With fasting, with weeping, and with mourning'" (Joel 2:12). The day of the LORD is coming, yet the present moment is held open for return. That is God's heart laid bare. He announces judgment so that people will come back to Him before it falls.

Daniel prays as someone who understood that open window. He confesses generations of rebellion, then turns: "And now, O Lord our God, who brought Your people out of the land of Egypt with a mighty hand... we have sinned, we have done wickedly!" (Daniel 9:15). A few verses later he uses the word again to ask God to hear and to let His face shine on His desolate sanctuary (9:17). Daniel's "now" rests on God's past rescue from Egypt and appeals to God's mercy in the present. Ezra's community follows the same path after he recounts the prophets' commands (Ezra 9:12). They say, "now," let us make a covenant to put things right (10:3), and Ezra answers, "now," make confession to the LORD (10:11).

Joshua 14:10–11 and 2 Samuel 7:25, 29 — claiming old promises today. Caleb waited forty-five years on a promise Moses swore to him (Joshua 14:9). Standing before Joshua, he piles up the word: "And now, behold, the LORD has kept me alive, as He said... and now, here I am this day, eighty-five years old" (14:10). He adds, "so now is my strength for war" (14:11). The long delay had not weakened the promise. Caleb treats the present as the time to receive it, and he asks for the hill country (14:12).

David does the same with the promise of a lasting house and throne (2 Samuel 7:12–16). He does not simply admire what God said. He turns it into a request: "Now, O LORD God, the word which You have spoken concerning Your servant and concerning his house, establish it forever and do as You have said" (7:25). He repeats the turn in 7:29, asking God to bless his house. Faith takes God's spoken word and brings it back to Him as a present petition.

Exodus 19:5 and Joshua 24 — the "now therefore" of decision. At Sinai, God first reminds Israel how He carried them out of Egypt on eagles' wings (Exodus 19:4). Only then does He say, "now therefore," if you will indeed obey My voice, you will be My special treasure (19:5). Grace comes first, and the call to obey follows from it. Joshua's farewell has the same order. After rehearsing God's faithfulness from Abraham onward, he says, "now therefore," fear the LORD and serve Him (24:14), and then, put away the foreign gods (24:23). Samuel does likewise when Israel receives a king (1 Samuel 12:7, 13). Past deliverance does not let a person live on memories. It calls for exclusive loyalty now.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets use ʻattâh to tie present distress to God's coming kingdom. Micah tells a battered remnant that the LORD will reign over them in Zion from now on and forever (Micah 4:7). Of the Ruler from Bethlehem he says that "now" He will be great to the ends of the earth (5:4). Isaiah promises that the Child's government and peace will have no end, upheld from that time forward and forever (Isaiah 9:7). The fulfillment lay ahead of the prophets, yet they speak of it with present certainty. Gabriel takes up that hope when he announces that Mary's Son will receive David's throne and reign forever (Luke 1:32–33).

The pattern of Isaiah 43:1, where God breaks into a history of sin with redemption, reaches its fullest expression in the gospel. After showing that all the world stands guilty, Paul writes, "But now the righteousness of God apart from the law is revealed" (Romans 3:21). He uses the same kind of turn when he tells Gentiles who were once far off that they have now been brought near by the blood of Christ (Ephesians 2:13). Joel's open window also becomes Paul's announcement that now is the accepted time, now is the day of salvation (2 Corinthians 6:2). The Hebrew word is not present in these Greek texts. Still, the God who spoke Israel's "but now" has spoken it decisively in Jesus.

7. Spirit & Life

This word refuses to let faith drift into the past or the future. A believer can treasure what God did and hope in what He will do, and still never answer Him today. Every "now therefore" in Scripture presses that point. Because God redeemed Israel at the Red Sea, Israel was called to obey at Sinai. Because God kept Caleb alive, Caleb asked for his mountain. Hebrews carries the same urgency when it warns that "today," if you hear His voice, you must not harden your heart (Hebrews 3:7).

There is deep comfort here as well. Isaiah 43:1 means that a person's worst chapter does not have the last word when God says "but now." Joel 2:12 means that judgment announced is also mercy offered, and the present hour is still open for return. Daniel shows what to do with that knowledge. Confess honestly, appeal to what God has already done, and ask Him to act now. David and Caleb show that God's promises are not museum pieces. They are words to bring back to Him with confidence, asking Him to do as He has said.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 22:12 — After Abraham raises the knife, the Angel of the LORD declares that now He knows Abraham fears God, since he did not withhold his only son.

  • Genesis 45:5, 8 — Joseph tells his frightened brothers not to be grieved now, because God sent him ahead to preserve life. He adds that it was not they who sent him there, but God.

  • Exodus 32:32 — Moses intercedes after the golden calf, asking God to forgive Israel's sin now, or else to blot Moses out of His book.

  • 2 Samuel 12:23 — When his child has died, David explains that now there is no reason to fast. He cannot bring the boy back, but he will go to him.

  • Isaiah 64:8 — In a lament over sin, the prophet turns with "But now," confessing that the LORD is their Father and they are the clay in the Potter's hands.

  • Jonah 4:3 — Jonah, angry at God's mercy to Nineveh, asks the LORD to take his life now. His "now" exposes a heart out of step with God's compassion.
