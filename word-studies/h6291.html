GREEN FIGS, EARLY FIGS - (pag, Strong's H6291)

Summary

The Hebrew word pag means the green, early figs that start to form on a fig tree at the turn from winter to spring, well before the main summer harvest. It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 2:13, where the beloved calls the woman he loves out into a world coming back to life: "The fig tree puts forth her green figs, And the vines with the tender grapes Give a good smell. Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away!" The figs are not ripe and not ready to eat. Their appearance still proves that winter is over and that the tree is alive and moving toward harvest.

That single verse carries a great deal. The green fig is a sign of a new season, a small fruit that promises more, and a reason to rise and come away with the one who loves you. Read alongside the rest of Scripture's fig imagery, it also says something about God's heart. Hosea describes God finding Israel like the first-ripe figs of the season, and then recalling that Israel went away to Baal Peor. Jesus went looking for fruit on a leafy tree and found none. Taken together, these pictures show a God who delights in early, real fruit and is not fooled by leaves.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Fig trees were part of daily life in ancient Israel. Their fruit was a staple food, and a family sitting under its own fig tree became a familiar picture of peace (1 Kings 4:25). Hebrew had several words connected with the tree. The ordinary word for the fig tree or its fruit is tə'ēnâ (H8384). A separate word, bikkurah, names the first-ripe fig. Pag is narrower than either. It names the young green fruit that forms early, before the tree has ripened anything.

Fig growers know this early crop well. It forms on the previous year's wood and swells as spring arrives, often as the new leaves are opening.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Pag means an early, unripe fig: green, tender, still forming on the branch. The NKJV renders it "green figs." The word describes the fruit's stage of growth. It is not yet sweet and not yet harvested, but it is clearly on its way.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The single use of pag sits inside a carefully arranged list of signs in Song of Solomon 2:11–13. Winter is past. The rain is gone. Flowers appear, singing begins, and the turtledove is heard. The last two signs come from the orchard: green figs on the fig tree and blossoming vines that "Give a good smell." The green figs are part of the evidence the beloved offers for his invitation. He does not ask her to come away because the harvest is in. He asks because life has visibly begun.

Other passages that speak of early figs use different Hebrew or Greek words. Those passages are not uses of pag, but they treat the same fruit and help us see how Scripture thinks about it.

4. Theological Significance

God takes pleasure in beginnings that are real. Hosea 9:10 puts the image into God's own mouth: "I saw your fathers As the firstfruits on the fig tree in its first season." Those first-ripe figs were a delicacy, and God recalls finding Israel with that kind of delight. The verse turns at once: "But they went to Baal Peor, And separated themselves to that shame." The delight of that first finding makes the betrayal all the more painful. Jeremiah 24:2 likens the good basket of figs, representing the exiles God would keep and restore, to "the figs that are first ripe." Scripture does not portray a God who values only finished maturity. He looks at early fruit and sees both what it is and what it promises.

The same imagery also measures whether a profession of faith is real. A fig tree is planted for fruit, and leaves without fruit leave the purpose of the tree unmet. Scripture's fig imagery ranges from tender delight to sober judgment, and both come from the same expectation: God looks for genuine life.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:10–13 — the green figs and the call to come away. The speaker announces, "For lo, the winter is past, The rain is over and gone" (2:11). In Israel the cold rains marked the dormant season, so their end meant the land could breathe again. The green figs are the orchard's testimony that the change is real. The invitation stands on both sides of this list of signs: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away" (2:10, 13). The man reads the first green fruit as a call to closer fellowship, not as a reason to wait for summer.

Mark 11:12–14 — leaves without fruit. Jesus, hungry, saw "a fig tree having leaves" and "went to see if perhaps He would find something on it. When He came to it, He found nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs" (Mark 11:13). Many interpreters explain the scene this way: the main harvest was months off, but a tree in full leaf was expected to show early figs as well. If that reading is right, the leaves advertised a fruitfulness the tree did not have. Mark places the incident around Jesus' cleansing of the temple (Mark 11:15–21), and the arrangement makes the tree a picture of a religious center full of activity and empty of the fruit God was seeking. The healthy tree of Song of Solomon 2:13 "puts forth her green figs." The tree in Mark 11 put forth only leaves.

Isaiah 28:4, Nahum 3:12, and Revelation 6:13 — figs that are quickly lost. These passages use figs at different stages. Isaiah compares Ephraim's proud glory to "the first fruit before the summer, Which an observer sees; He eats it up while it is still in his hand." That first-ripe fig is gone in a moment. Nahum says Nineveh's strongholds are "fig trees with ripened figs: If they are shaken, They fall into the mouth of the eater." His point is how easily the fortified city will fall to its attackers. Revelation describes stars falling "as a fig tree drops its late figs when it is shaken by a mighty wind." The Greek word behind "late figs" can describe unripe figs, and the Greek translation of the Old Testament uses the same word for the Song's green figs. In the Song those figs announce spring and an invitation; in Revelation a mighty wind tears them from the tree. The mechanisms differ: Isaiah's fig is taken in hand and eaten at once, while Nahum's and Revelation's figs fall when the tree is shaken. What the three pictures share is a sudden loss of something that seemed secure.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song is a poem of human love, and Christians have long heard in it a resonance with Christ, whom the New Testament calls the Bridegroom of His people (John 3:29; Ephesians 5:25–32). The invitation to "come away" in a season of new life echoes the way Jesus calls people to Himself. That connection is a resonance, not a stated fulfillment, but it fits the gospel.

Jesus Himself used the fig tree to teach. "Learn this parable from the fig tree: When its branch has already become tender and puts forth leaves, you know that summer is near" (Matthew 24:32). Tender growth is a sign that something larger is coming. In Luke 13:6–9 a vinedresser pleads for one more year for a barren fig tree so he can dig around it and fertilize it. The tree in Mark 11, by contrast, shows that this patience has limits when the fruit never comes.

Alongside these pictures of judgment, Micah and Zechariah use the fig tree to picture the peace God has promised. Micah says that "everyone shall sit under his vine and under his fig tree, And no one shall make them afraid" (Micah 4:4), and Zechariah 3:10 pictures neighbors inviting one another there. Read within that larger story, the green fig can stand as a first sign of the tree whose shade would one day mean safety under the reign of God's King.

7. Spirit & Life

Christ does not wait for your harvest before He calls you near. A believer in the early days of repentance, or someone just coming out of a long spiritual winter, often feels that the fruit is too green to count. The picture in the Song points the other way. The beloved sees the first green figs and says, "Rise up." Jesus does not break a bruised reed (Matthew 12:20), and Hosea 9:10 recalls God's delight at finding Israel like first-ripe figs, before they went to Baal Peor. Small, real beginnings matter to Him because He sees the harvest they carry.

The fig tree also raises a searching question. Leaves are easy to produce: religious words, visible activity, a full calendar. As an illustration, think of Adam and Eve reaching for fig leaves to cover their shame (Genesis 3:7). Genesis does not set leaves against fruit, but the picture of covering instead of change fits the warning of Mark 11. The life God looks for begins out of sight, in honest repentance, love, and fellowship with Christ, and that hidden life is what gives outward profession its meaning. Jesus gives the direct teaching on how fruit grows: "As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in Me" (John 15:4). Early faith grows toward maturity by staying joined to Christ, and the one who abides in Him "bears much fruit" (John 15:5).

Seasons of dormancy are not necessarily seasons of loss. The Song shows the green figs only once winter has passed; it does not describe the branch during the winter. Still, the growers' detail in Section 1 offers a possible illustration: the early crop comes from wood that lived through the cold. In that light, God can preserve through a dormant season what He intends to bring out in spring.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Pag occurs only in Song of Solomon 2:13. The passages below use other words for figs and fig trees.

  • Jeremiah 24:2 — The "very good figs, like the figs that are first ripe" represent the people God would watch over for good, early fruit as a picture of favor.

  • 1 Kings 4:25 — Under Solomon, Judah and Israel "dwelt safely, each man under his vine and his fig tree," a historical picture of the peace the prophets later promised.

  • Zechariah 3:10 — In the coming day, people invite their neighbors under vine and fig tree, so the peace is shared.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H8384 (tə'ēnâ), fig tree or fig — A distinct Hebrew word for the tree and its fruit. It is not a lexical relative of pag, but it appears in the same verse: "The fig tree puts forth her green figs" (Song of Solomon 2:13).

  • H1063 (bikkurah), first-ripe fig — A different Hebrew word for early fruit that has ripened. It belongs to the same image family and is reflected in Hosea 9:10 and Isaiah 28:4.

  • H1612 (gephen, vine) and H8384 (tə'ēnâ, fig tree) — A canonical pairing of two distinct words that together picture settled peace (1 Kings 4:25; Micah 4:4). The pairing also appears in Song of Solomon 2:13.
