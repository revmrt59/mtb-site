BIND, BOUND, PRISONER - (ʼâçar, Strong's H0631)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʼâçar means to bind. Scripture uses it for the ropes Samson's enemies tied around him, the bronze fetters placed on Judah's last kings, and the cell where Joseph was "confined." The same verb also describes hitching animals to a cart or chariot so they are ready for a task. Most strikingly, it describes a person "binding" his own soul with a vow before the Lord. In each case, something is fastened to something else. The context shows whether that bond is a punishment, a preparation, or a promise.

This plain word carries much of the Bible's story. Kings who threw off the Lord's rule ended up chained by Assyria and Babylon. Elihu tells Job that God sometimes holds the proud "in the cords of affliction" to open their ears. Psalm 118 commands worshipers to "bind the sacrifice with cords to the horns of the altar." The prophets promised that the Lord's Servant would open the prison to those who are bound, and Jesus announced in Nazareth that this promise was fulfilled in Him. Taken together, these passages do not picture people as simply free and unattached. The question is what holds them: sin and its consequences, or the Lord they have willingly bound themselves to.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼâçar is a common Hebrew verb drawn from everyday life. Shepherds tied animals, farmers hitched cows to carts, soldiers harnessed chariot horses, and jailers bound prisoners. The word appears across the Old Testament, from the Joseph narrative in Genesis to Hosea. It clusters in two places. Numbers 30 uses it again and again for vows. Judges 15–16 uses it for the repeated attempts to tie up Samson. A related Hebrew noun means a bond or binding obligation, and Numbers 30 pairs that noun with this verb in its language about vows.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To tie, restrain, or imprison. This sense covers physical binding with ropes or fetters, or holding someone in custody, as when Joseph is "confined" in Pharaoh's prison (Genesis 39:20) or Samson is "bound … with bronze fetters" (Judges 16:21). The participle form often simply means "prisoners."

2. To harness, hitch, or make ready. Animals are fastened to a vehicle for a task, as when Pharaoh "made ready his chariot" (Exodus 14:6) or the Philistines hitch cows to a cart carrying the ark (1 Samuel 6:7, 10). The word extends naturally to getting a battle ready, as in "who will set the battle in order?" (1 Kings 20:14; compare 2 Chronicles 13:3).

3. To bind oneself by obligation. A person fastens himself to a promise, as when a man "swears an oath to bind himself by some agreement" (Numbers 30:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject and the setting determine what the binding means. When enemies do the binding, it means humiliation and loss of freedom, as with Hoshea, Manasseh, Jehoiakim, and Zedekiah. When God's servant does the binding, it shows delegated authority. Joseph, once a prisoner, later binds Simeon (Genesis 42:24) and is given power "to bind his princes at his pleasure, and teach his elders wisdom" (Psalm 105:22). When the worshiper binds, the act is consecration (Psalm 118:27). When a person binds his own soul, the bond is a vow (Numbers 30).

Harnessing often signals human military confidence that God overturns. Pharaoh "made ready his chariot" and drove toward the sea that would swallow it (Exodus 14:6). Jeremiah's taunt to Egypt, "Harness the horses" (Jeremiah 46:4), opens a prophecy of Egypt's defeat. The poetry of the Song of Songs reverses the usual picture: "A king is held captive by your tresses" (Song of Songs 7:5). The one who normally binds others is himself bound, and he is held by love.

4. Theological Significance

These passages reveal a God who rules over every bond. Job confesses that God "loosens the bonds of kings" and binds a belt around their waist (Job 12:18, paraphrased). In Scripture, the power to imprison and the power to release both belong to the Lord, who "gives freedom to the prisoners" (Psalm 146:7).

A sobering pattern runs through Israel's history. Kings who would not be held by the Lord's word ended up held by foreign iron. Hoshea secretly sought help from Egypt, so "the king of Assyria shut him up, and bound him in prison" (2 Kings 17:4). Chronicles says plainly that "the LORD brought upon them" the Assyrian captains, who "bound [Manasseh] with bronze fetters" (2 Chronicles 33:11). Jehoiakim suffered the same fate (2 Chronicles 36:6). Zedekiah watched his sons die, was blinded, and was "bound … with bronze fetters" (2 Kings 25:7). Psalm 149 pictures God's saints given the honor "to bind their kings with chains" (Psalm 149:8–9). Here, however, Judah's own kings were led away bound. For these kings, throwing off God's rule did not bring freedom. It handed them over to harsher masters.

Yet God also binds in order to heal. Elihu explains that when people are "bound in fetters, held in the cords of affliction" (Job 36:8), God is showing them their sin and opening their ears to instruction (Job 36:9–10). Those who stay angry "when He binds them" (Job 36:13) miss what He is doing. The same restraint that destroys the rebel can rescue the proud. Hebrews describes this kind of discipline as a father's love: "whom the LORD loves He chastens," and He does it "for our profit, that we may be partakers of His holiness" (Hebrews 12:6, 10).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 30 — Binding the soul. In this chapter, the verb describes vows again and again. A man who makes a vow or "swears an oath to bind himself by some agreement … shall not break his word; he shall do according to all that proceeds out of his mouth" (Numbers 30:2). The Hebrew pictures a bond placed on the soul itself. A spoken promise to God is treated like a rope on the inner life, and it holds until the promise is kept. God takes human words this seriously, and He Himself keeps every word He speaks. The chapter's provisions for a father or husband to confirm or annul a woman's vow (Numbers 30:3–15) show how carefully God guards such bonds.

Judges 15–16 — Ropes that could not hold. The men of Judah came "to arrest Samson" (Judges 15:10). Samson's own countrymen tied him with two new ropes, but when the Spirit of the Lord came on him, the ropes became like burned flax (Judges 15:13–14). Delilah tried fresh bowstrings, then new ropes, then weaving his hair into a loom, and each attempt to bind him failed. The binding finally succeeded only after Samson told Delilah the secret of the Nazirite separation he had carried from birth, his hair was shaved, and "the LORD had departed from him" (Judges 16:17–20). Then "they bound him with bronze fetters, and he became a grinder in the prison" (Judges 16:21). No Philistine rope could hold the man while the Lord was with him. What made him a prisoner was his own compromise, not the strength of his enemies.

Psalm 118:27 — The sacrifice bound to the altar. This psalm celebrates "the stone which the builders rejected" (v. 22) and the one who comes in the name of the Lord (v. 26). Its procession ends with a command: "Bind the sacrifice with cords to the horns of the altar." (Some translators read the line as binding a festival procession with branches, but the NKJV reflects a long-standing reading.) In the NKJV picture, the offering is fastened to the place of sacrifice so that it is fully given and cannot pull away. The crowds quoted this same psalm as Jesus entered Jerusalem (Matthew 21:9).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The redemptive story moves from captivity to release, and Isaiah carries that movement. The Servant of the Lord will say "to the prisoners, 'Go forth'" (Isaiah 49:9). The Spirit-anointed one will proclaim "the opening of the prison to those who are bound" (Isaiah 61:1). Both uses of "prisoners" and "bound" in these verses come from ʼâçar. The exile had made Israel a nation of the bound, from Zedekiah in Babylon to Jeremiah, who was found at Ramah "bound in chains" among the captives (Jeremiah 40:1). Isaiah 61:1 also joins healing (literally binding up) the brokenhearted with opening the prison. The Messiah binds up one kind of wound while He breaks another kind of bond.

In Nazareth, Jesus read this passage and said, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). This is stated fulfillment. Read beside Psalm 146:7, it claims for Jesus the work the psalm assigns to the Lord: "The LORD gives freedom to the prisoners." Luke's wording follows the Greek translation of Isaiah and includes "recovery of sight to the blind" (Luke 4:18). That phrase fits the sad history of this word, since both Samson and Zedekiah were blinded and then bound.

The one who frees the bound first let Himself be bound. Mark records that the council "bound Jesus, led Him away, and delivered Him to Pilate" (Mark 15:1). Psalm 118's sacrifice fastened to the altar finds a fitting echo here. This is a connection Scripture invites rather than one the New Testament states outright. Cords held Jesus' hands, but His obedience to the Father held Him to the cross. He took the fetters that rebels like Zedekiah earned so that captives could hear "Go forth."

Joseph's story previews this reversal. The man "confined" in Egypt's dungeon (Genesis 39:20) was raised to "bind his princes at his pleasure" (Psalm 105:22). God turns the bound one into the one who binds. Jesus goes further and gives His people authority: "whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven" (Matthew 16:19). That saying uses a Greek word, not ʼâçar, but the pattern carries over. God shares His authority over bonds with those He has freed.

7. Spirit & Life

The gospel does not move believers from being bound to being unattached. It transfers them from one bond to another. Christ breaks the fetters of sin, and those He frees then fasten themselves to Him. Romans 12:1 asks believers to "present your bodies a living sacrifice." Psalm 118:27 offers a picture of what that involves: tying a wavering will to the altar so it does not slip off when the fire gets hot. Numbers 30 adds that promises made to God are real bonds. A believer's words before the Lord should be sincere and kept.

Samson shows where real danger lies. The world's ropes did not overpower him. His weakness came from inside, when he let go of what bound him to God. For a believer, guarding that inner bond matters more than fearing outside opposition.

The bonds God allows can be a mercy. Elihu's "cords of affliction" are meant to open ears. A believer who feels hemmed in should ask what God is teaching before resenting the restraint. Above all these bonds stands the one the Song of Songs pictures, where "a king is held captive by your tresses." The Song's picture suggests that the richest freedom is to be held fast by love, and the Lord who was bound for His people holds them in exactly that way.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 49:11 — Jacob's blessing pictures Judah's ruler binding his donkey's colt to the choice vine, an image of abundance in the coming king's reign. Christ later rides such a colt into Jerusalem.

  • Genesis 46:29 — Joseph made ready (harnessed) his chariot to meet Jacob. In this verse the harnessing is in the service of a reunion.

  • 1 Kings 18:44 — Elijah tells Ahab, "Prepare your chariot," because the rain God promised is coming.

  • Nehemiah 4:18 — Each builder had his sword girded (bound) at his side. Rebuilding the wall and readiness for battle went together.

  • Ezekiel 3:25 — "They will put ropes on you and bind you with them." The prophet's binding goes with the silence God imposes on him before a rebellious house (Ezekiel 3:26).

  • Hosea 10:10 — God will bind Israel for its two transgressions, so the people are held by the consequences of their sin.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2280 (chabash), to bind up, bandage — This is a distinct word and not a relative of ʼâçar. It is paired with ʼâçar in Isaiah 61:1, where the Messiah binds up the brokenhearted and opens the prison to the bound. Psalm 147:3 uses it: "He heals the brokenhearted And binds up their wounds."

  • Greek "bind" in the New Testament — This is a thematic parallel in another language. Examples are the binding of Jesus (Mark 15:1), the believer's authority to bind and loose (Matthew 16:19; 18:18), and the angel who "bound" Satan (Revelation 20:2).
