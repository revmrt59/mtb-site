MOUTH, EDGE, COMMAND - (peh, Strong's H6310)

Summary

The Hebrew word peh means mouth. It is the ordinary word for the mouth a person eats and speaks with. The same word names the opening of a well or a sack, the "edge" of a sword, and the spoken word or command that comes from someone's mouth. When the NKJV says Israel moved "at the command of the LORD," the Hebrew says they moved at the mouth of the LORD. When Joshua strikes a city "with the edge of the sword," the Hebrew speaks of the sword's mouth.

Scripture gives this everyday word great weight. God's mouth makes things happen: "the mouth of the LORD has spoken" is the prophets' guarantee that what He said will come to pass. The God who made man's mouth fills the mouths of His prophets with His own words. The human mouth shows what is in the heart, and it can bring life or ruin. The word reaches its deepest point in Jesus Christ. He is the Prophet in whose mouth God put His words, and the Servant who did not open His mouth when He was led to the slaughter. He will return with a sword coming from His mouth.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Peh is one of the most common body words in the Hebrew Bible. It appears in every major section of the Old Testament. It is especially frequent in the Psalms and Proverbs, where it marks prayer, praise, deceit, and wisdom, and in Numbers, where it marks the Lord's commands. Like English "mouth," it easily stretches from the body to other things. A river has a mouth, and so does a cave. Hebrew extends the word further than English does, applying it to a sword's edge and to speech itself. Many English translations therefore render peh with words like "command," "word," "edge," or "opening." The reader would never know from the English that one Hebrew word sits behind them all.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The mouth of a person or animal. This is the physical organ used for eating, drinking, breathing, and speaking. The dove returned to Noah with an olive leaf "in her mouth" (Genesis 8:11).

2. What the mouth speaks: word, command, instruction, testimony. Acting "at the mouth of" someone means acting on that person's spoken authority. Pharaoh told Joseph that all his people would be ruled "according to your word" (Genesis 41:40). Jeremiah dictated a scroll to Baruch "at the instruction" of his mouth (Jeremiah 36:4).

3. The edge of a sword. The phrase "edge of the sword" is literally the mouth of the sword. It describes a sword that cuts down everything in its path (Genesis 34:26; Joshua 6:21).

4. An opening. The word names the mouth of a well (Genesis 29:2), of a sack (Genesis 42:27), of a cave (Joshua 10:18), and the neck opening of the high priest's robe (Exodus 28:32).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's meaning often depends on whose mouth is in view. When the subject is God, the phrase points to decisive authority, as in "the mouth of the LORD has spoken" (Isaiah 1:20). When the subject is a fool or a liar, the mouth exposes the character behind it.

One construction is worth knowing. The phrase according to the mouth of appears in matters of measure and fairness. The Passover lamb was shared "according to" each person's need (Exodus 12:4). Manna was gathered "according to" each one's eating (Exodus 16:18). Land prices were set "according to" the number of years left until Jubilee (Leviticus 25:16). In these places the "mouth" sets the standard.

The word also gives a mouth to things that have none. The earth "opened its mouth to receive your brother's blood" (Genesis 4:11). It opened its mouth again and swallowed Korah's rebels "with their households" (Numbers 16:32). Sheol, the realm of the dead, "has enlarged itself and opened its mouth beyond measure" to swallow the proud (Isaiah 5:14). The enemies in Psalm 22 "gape at Me with their mouths, like a raging and roaring lion" (Psalm 22:13). In each case the open mouth pictures something that receives or devours, and in Genesis, Numbers, and Isaiah it is tied to God's judgment.

4. Theological Significance

When God speaks, events follow. The prophets close their warnings and their promises with the same seal. Isaiah warns, "You shall be devoured by the sword; for the mouth of the LORD has spoken" (Isaiah 1:20). He also promises, "The glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together; for the mouth of the LORD has spoken" (Isaiah 40:5). Micah 4:4 pictures everyone sitting safely "under his vine and under his fig tree" with the same guarantee. Isaiah 55:11 explains why that guarantee holds: "So shall My word be that goes forth from My mouth; it shall not return to Me void." The psalmist traces this back to creation itself, where the heavens were made "by the breath of His mouth" (Psalm 33:6). The God who spoke the world into being keeps His word.

The human mouth, by contrast, reveals whether the heart is right with God. David prays, "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in Your sight" (Psalm 19:14). God's complaint in Isaiah is that "these people draw near with their mouths and honor Me with their lips, but have removed their hearts far from Me" (Isaiah 29:13). A mouth can say the right words and still be separated from the heart behind them.

In Isaiah's call, cleansing began at the mouth. When Isaiah confessed that he was a man of unclean lips, the seraph touched his mouth with a coal and said, "Your iniquity is taken away, and your sin purged" (Isaiah 6:7). The cleansing reached the whole man, and it began at the place where his sin had been exposed.

Scripture also uses a gesture with the mouth. Laying a hand over the mouth shows that a person has nothing left to say before someone greater. Princes did this before Job in his days of honor (Job 29:9). Later Job does it before God: "I am vile; what shall I answer You? I lay my hand over my mouth" (Job 40:4). Micah foresees the nations doing the same when God acts for His people (Micah 7:16).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 4:10–16 — the God who made the mouth. Moses objects that he is slow of speech. God does not answer by promising Moses better speaking skills. He answers by asking who made the mouth: "Who has made man's mouth? … Have not I, the LORD?" (Exodus 4:11). Then He makes a promise: "I will be with your mouth and teach you what you shall say" (Exodus 4:12). When Moses still hesitates, God gives him Aaron, and the word peh runs through the arrangement. God will be with Moses' mouth and with Aaron's mouth, and Aaron will be a mouth for Moses (Exodus 4:15–16). The pattern carries forward through Scripture. God tells Jeremiah, "Behold, I have put My words in your mouth" (Jeremiah 1:9), and He touches Jeremiah's mouth as He says it. The prophet's ability was never the point. The Creator who made the mouth takes responsibility for what He puts in it.

Numbers 9:15–23 — living at the mouth of the LORD. Numbers 9 repeats one phrase again and again: "At the command of the LORD the children of Israel would journey, and at the command of the LORD they would camp" (Numbers 9:18). The cloud might rest over the tabernacle for one night or for "two days, a month, or a year" (Numbers 9:22), and Israel did not move until the cloud was taken up. The LORD commanded Israel's movements, and the cloud's rising from the tabernacle or resting over it was the signal by which they knew His command (Numbers 9:17–22). The chapter closes with the phrase three times in a single verse (Numbers 9:23). Moses later recorded their journeys "at the command of the LORD" (Numbers 33:2). Every stage of the wilderness journey, the long waits included, happened under the LORD's command.

Numbers 12:6–8 — mouth to mouth. God tells Miriam and Aaron that He speaks to prophets in visions and dreams. With Moses it is different: "I speak with him face to face, even plainly, and not in dark sayings" (Numbers 12:8). The Hebrew behind "face to face" is literally mouth to mouth. God sets His speech with Moses apart from the way He speaks to other prophets. It is direct, clear, and personal.

Isaiah 53:7, 9 — the mouth that stayed shut. Isaiah has shown God's mouth deciding history. Now he shows the Servant of the Lord, oppressed and afflicted, who did not open His mouth. Isaiah says it twice in the same verse and compares the Servant to a lamb led to slaughter and a sheep silent before its shearers. Verse 9 adds that there was no deceit in His mouth. This silence is not weakness. A mouth that could have accused, defended, or condemned stays closed while the LORD lays on Him "the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6). The Gospels show Jesus silent before His accusers (Matthew 27:12–14). Peter quotes Isaiah 53:9 and adds that when Jesus was reviled, He did not revile in return (1 Peter 2:22–23).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophetic pattern of Exodus 4 points to a greater Prophet. God promised Moses, "I will … put My words in His mouth, and He shall speak to them all that I command Him" (Deuteronomy 18:18). Peter applies that promise directly to Jesus (Acts 3:22). Jesus Himself said He spoke only what the Father commanded Him (John 12:49). He is the Prophet like Moses, and He is more than Moses. Moses spoke with God mouth to mouth. Jesus is the Word who was with God from the beginning (John 1:1).

Jesus also chose to live by God's mouth. In the wilderness He answered Satan with Deuteronomy 8:3: man lives by every word that comes from the mouth of the Lord (Matthew 4:4). God had taught Israel that lesson through forty years of hunger and manna in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 8:2–3). Jesus lived it perfectly through forty days of hunger.

The Hebrew way of speaking about a sword offers an image that helps readers see a striking reversal in Christ. Hebrew spoke of a sword's edge as its mouth, as though the blade devoured whatever it struck. That ordinary idiom predicts nothing. The prophetic connection comes from Isaiah, who says of the Messiah that He will strike the earth with the rod of His mouth (Isaiah 11:4), and from the Servant, who says God has made His mouth like a sharp sword (Isaiah 49:2). Revelation takes up these prophecies in two scenes. In John's vision of the Son of Man among the lampstands, "out of His mouth went a sharp two-edged sword" (Revelation 1:12–16). When Christ returns as the rider on the white horse, "out of His mouth goes a sharp sword, that with it He should strike the nations" (Revelation 19:11–15). The sword with a mouth has become a mouth with a sword. The New Testament writes in Greek, so this is not the Hebrew word itself, but the imagery clearly echoes Isaiah.

Taken together, these passages show two comings. The Servant who kept His mouth shut before His accusers will open it in judgment, and His word alone will be enough to judge. Isaiah adds that when the Servant is exalted, "kings shall shut their mouths at Him" (Isaiah 52:15). He was silent before His accusers, and in the end kings will be silent before Him.

Psalm 22 adds one more strand. Jesus prayed its opening words from the cross. The psalm's sufferer is surrounded by gaping, lion-like mouths and cries for rescue from the lion's mouth (Psalm 22:13, 21). Christ entered the devouring mouth that Isaiah gave to Sheol, and He came out again. The grave could not keep Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Numbers 9 describes a way of life that every believer can share. Some encampments were long. The cloud could stay over the tabernacle "many days" (Numbers 9:19) while nothing seemed to happen. Scripture describes those waiting months the same way it describes the marches: both happened "at the command of the LORD." To follow Christ by His Spirit means trusting that God's word governs our standing still as surely as our going.

God's answer to Moses also brings comfort to anyone who feels unequal to what God asks. The Lord did not argue that Moses was gifted enough. He pointed to who made Moses' mouth and promised to be with it. The same God hears a believer's halting prayer or trembling witness and stays with the mouth He has called.

Proverbs treats speech as a matter of life and death. "He who guards his mouth preserves his life, but he who opens wide his lips shall have destruction" (Proverbs 13:3). The mouth of the righteous is a well of life (Proverbs 10:11), while a fool's mouth becomes his own ruin (Proverbs 18:7). In Isaiah 6 the change began with God's cleansing. God did not ask Isaiah to clean up his own lips. He cleansed them first, and then He sent Isaiah to speak.

God has made His word near, "in your mouth and in your heart, that you may do it" (Deuteronomy 30:14). He told Joshua not to let the Book of the Law depart from his mouth (Joshua 1:8). Paul hears in Deuteronomy 30:14 the message of faith: confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead (Romans 10:8–10). The mouth that once revealed a distant heart can now speak from a believing one.

Malachi applies this to spiritual leaders. A faithful priest had the law of truth in his mouth, and the people sought instruction from his mouth (Malachi 2:6–7). When leaders' mouths fail, God's people stumble. Those who teach Scripture carry that same responsibility.

Finally, Job shows the right response when God speaks. Sometimes the most faithful thing a person can do is lay a hand over the mouth and listen.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 29:2 — "A large stone was on the well's mouth." Jacob rolls the stone away and waters Rachel's flock. Some readers have heard in this scene a faint echo of the Shepherd who opens access to living water, though that is a pastoral illustration rather than the passage's own teaching.

  • Exodus 28:32 — The high priest's robe had a woven binding around its opening, its mouth, "so that it does not tear." The Law also forbade the high priest to tear his garments (Leviticus 21:10), which gives a sober backdrop to Caiaphas tearing his robes at Jesus' trial (Matthew 26:65).

  • Psalm 81:10 — God tells His people to open their mouths wide and promises that He will fill them. The God who brought Israel out of Egypt invites them to be hungry for what only He can give.

  • Psalm 141:3 — David asks the Lord to set a guard over his mouth. His prayer places the guarding of his speech in the LORD's hands.

  • Ezekiel 3:2–3 — God gives Ezekiel a scroll to eat, and in his mouth it tastes as sweet as honey. The prophet takes in God's word before he speaks it.

  • Proverbs 31:8–9 — The king is told to open his mouth for those who cannot speak for themselves and to judge righteously. A godly mouth defends the helpless.
