BREATHE, BLOW, PUFF, SPEAK - (pûwach, Strong's H6315)

Summary

The Hebrew verb pûwach means to breathe or to blow. It describes breath or wind moving outward, and Scripture uses that picture in several ways. Wind blows across a garden. The day breathes as the cool breeze rises. An arrogant man puffs at his enemies. A needy person yearns for safety. Scoffers blow on a city until it bursts into flame. Most often, in Proverbs, the word describes a witness who utters lies or, once, truth. The English reads "speaks" or "utter," but the Hebrew pictures words going out of a person like breath.

These fourteen uses show that breath is rarely neutral. What a person breathes out shows what is inside him. The wicked use their breath to deceive, to sneer, and to stir up strife. God, however, hears the sighing of the poor. He answers the arrogant with the blast of His own judgment. He gives a prophetic word that "will speak" at its appointed time and will not lie. The word invites a searching question: what are we breathing out, and whose breath are we waiting for?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pûwach is a vivid, physical Hebrew verb. It belongs mostly to Hebrew poetry: Proverbs, Psalms, and the Song of Solomon, with one use each in Ezekiel and Habakkuk. It is related in idea, though not in form, to the better-known words ruach (wind, breath, spirit) and neshamah (the breath of life). Those words name breath or spirit itself. Pûwach names the action of breath leaving: blowing, puffing, panting, exhaling.

Because Hebrew poets loved concrete pictures, the verb often carries the image of breath into speech and attitude. In Proverbs the expression rendered "speaks lies" or "utter lies" reads literally breathes out lies, a set expression for a lying witness. Some Hebrew scholars think the Proverbs form may be a closely related word meaning simply witness. If the usual reading is right, the picture of breath stands behind the expression.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to send out breath or air. Context determines the nuance:

1. To blow, used of wind or the breeze of the day: "Blow upon my garden" (Song of Solomon 4:16); "Until the day breaks" (Song of Solomon 2:17).

2. To breathe out words, meaning to utter or declare, especially as a witness: "A false witness who speaks lies" (Proverbs 6:19).

3. To puff or pant, expressing contempt or eager longing: the wicked man "sneers" at his enemies (Psalm 10:5), and the needy one longs for safety (Psalm 12:5).

4. To blow upon in order to kindle, fanning a fire: "Scoffers set a city aflame" (Proverbs 29:8); God's blowing "with the fire of My wrath" (Ezekiel 21:31).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Proverbs holds half the uses. Six of them describe a witness and what he utters. Five concern a witness who "speaks lies" (Proverbs 6:19; 14:5, 25; 19:5, 9). Proverbs 6:19 lists him among the things the LORD hates, Proverbs 14:5 contrasts him with a faithful witness, and Proverbs 19 says he "will not escape" (Proverbs 19:5) and "shall perish" (Proverbs 19:9). Proverbs 14:25 sets "a true witness" who "delivers souls" beside the liar, but the verb belongs only to the second line: "a deceitful witness speaks lies." The one positive use is Proverbs 12:17: "He who speaks truth declares righteousness."

The Psalms show breath as an expression of the heart. In Psalm 10 it is the puff of contempt. In Psalm 12 it stands behind the NKJV's "yearns," the longing of the oppressed for safety; the King James Version reads the verse differently, as Section 5 explains. The Song of Solomon uses the word for the moving air of the day and the wind in a garden. In the prophets, the subject becomes God's own word and God Himself. The vision "will speak" (Habakkuk 2:3), and the Lord says, "I will blow against you" (Ezekiel 21:31). The verb stays the same, but the one breathing changes. That change in subject carries most of the word's meaning.

4. Theological Significance

Proverbs treats speech as an outflow of character. The false witness in Proverbs 6:19 stands in a list of things the LORD hates, right beside "a heart that devises wicked plans" (Proverbs 6:18) and the one "who sows discord among brethren." The verb itself simply means to breathe out or utter. The picture it carries, though, fits the setting: words leave a person the way breath does, from the inside. Proverbs places this liar among the settled evils the LORD hates, which suggests that his lies are not a single slip but come from what lives inside him. Jesus taught the same truth when He said the mouth speaks out of the abundance of the heart (Matthew 12:34). Speech is the breath of the soul made audible, and Proverbs holds that breath accountable.

The word also sets human breath against God's breath. Psalm 10 describes a wicked man who "does not seek God; God is in none of his thoughts" (Psalm 10:4). God's judgments are "far above, out of his sight," and "as for all his enemies, he sneers at them" (Psalm 10:5). He sneers at them as though a puff could blow them away. Ezekiel shows how God answers such pride in one proud nation. In His oracle against the Ammonites (Ezekiel 21:28–32), a people who had mocked Israel's ruin (Ezekiel 25:3–6), the Lord declares, "I will blow against you with the fire of My wrath" (Ezekiel 21:31). Set beside Psalm 10, the picture is striking: the one who puffs at his enemies faces a God whose breath is fire. God allows human arrogance a long run, and Psalm 10 opens by asking why He seems to stand far off. But the last breath belongs to Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 12:5: the breath of the needy. "'For the oppression of the poor, for the sighing of the needy, Now I will arise,' says the LORD; 'I will set him in the safety for which he yearns.'" In this verse pûwach stands behind "yearns." The word "sighing" translates a different Hebrew word. The NKJV takes pûwach as the yearning of a person desperate for rescue. The King James Version instead reads, "I will set him in safety from him that puffeth at him." That reading recalls the sneering man of Psalm 10:5. The two readings differ. In the NKJV, the needy person yearns for safety, and the LORD promises to set him in the safety he longs for. In the KJV, the LORD sets the oppressed person safe from the adversary who puffs at him. Both readings carry the same assurance: God hears the needy and rises to protect them. The verse is the turning point of the psalm, where the LORD Himself begins to speak.

Habakkuk 2:3: the vision that will speak. Habakkuk had complained that God seemed silent while violence spread. God's answer was to write the vision plainly and wait: "For the vision is yet for an appointed time; But at the end it will speak, and it will not lie. Though it tarries, wait for it; Because it will surely come, It will not tarry." The verb is the same one Proverbs uses for a witness breathing out testimony. The vision is pictured as a witness, or as someone panting toward a goal. It is not a dead letter waiting to be dusted off. It presses toward its appointed time, and when it speaks, it will tell the truth. The surrounding words explain how delay and certainty fit together. From the prophet's side the vision "tarries." From God's side "it will not tarry," because it arrives exactly when He appointed. Unlike the false witness of Proverbs, this witness cannot lie.

Song of Solomon 4:16: the wind in the garden. The bride calls, "Awake, O north wind, And come, O south! Blow upon my garden, That its spices may flow out. Let my beloved come to his garden And eat its pleasant fruits." The garden is already planted and full of spices (Song of Solomon 4:12–15), but its fragrance stays locked inside until the wind moves through it. She asks for both winds: the cold north wind and the warm south wind. Her stated wish is that her beloved may come and enjoy it, and the closing line changes "my garden" into "his garden."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hebrews takes up Habakkuk 2:3 and applies it to the return of Christ: "For yet a little while, And He who is coming will come and will not tarry" (Hebrews 10:37). The vision that "will speak" at the end finds its final fulfillment in a Person. Every promise God has spoken is moving toward the day Jesus appears. Peter makes the same point when he says the Lord is not slow about His promise but patient, giving time for repentance (2 Peter 3:9).

The two "breaths" in this word also meet in Christ. He is the true and faithful Witness (Revelation 3:14), the opposite of the false witness of Proverbs. He was also the One on whom the sneering of the wicked fell at the cross (Psalm 22:7). At His return, the fiery breath that Ezekiel 21:31 announced against Ammon has its echo: Paul says the Lord will consume the lawless one "with the breath of His mouth" (2 Thessalonians 2:8). The Song's picture of wind and breath also has a canonical companion in Jesus' words to Nicodemus: "The wind blows where it wishes... So is everyone who is born of the Spirit" (John 3:8). These texts use other words for breath and wind, but they continue the same biblical image.

7. Spirit & Life

The Song's prayer for wind is a fitting picture of the believer's dependence on the Holy Spirit. The Song does not name the Spirit, so this is an analogy rather than the verse's direct teaching. Still, Scripture often links wind and Spirit, and John 3:8 makes the link plain. A Christian may have the graces of Christ planted within, yet those graces must be stirred before they give off fragrance. Love, joy, patience, and kindness are the Spirit's fruit (Galatians 5:22–23), not our own production. The bride's courage is worth noticing: she asks for the north wind too. Sometimes the Spirit releases the fragrance of Christ through hard and cold seasons, and sometimes through warm comfort. The aim in both is that the Beloved may come into His garden and enjoy it.

Proverbs 29:8 gives the dark mirror of this picture: "Scoffers set a city aflame, But wise men turn away wrath." A scoffer finds the small embers in a community, such as a grievance, a rumor, or an old offense, and blows on them until the whole city burns. James warns in the same way that the tongue is a small fire that can set a whole forest ablaze (James 3:5–6). Every one of us breathes on something. We fan strife, or we turn wrath away.

Habakkuk's word gives strength to those who are still waiting. A promise that seems quiet has not died. It is moving toward God's appointed time, and the instruction is simple: "wait for it." Those who wait through a long night, sighing like the needy of Psalm 12:5, are not forgotten by the God who hears them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Proverbs 12:17 — "He who speaks truth declares righteousness." This is the one use of the word for honest testimony. The verse sets it against a false witness who offers deceit, so the same act of speaking can carry either one.

Proverbs 14:5 — "A faithful witness does not lie, But a false witness will utter lies." The Hebrew reads literally breathes out lies. Faithfulness or falsehood shows in what a person speaks.

Proverbs 14:25 — "A true witness delivers souls, But a deceitful witness speaks lies." The word belongs to the second line. The verse contrasts a true witness whose honest testimony rescues lives with a liar who offers only deceit.

Proverbs 19:5, 9 — Verse 5 warns that the one who speaks lies "will not escape," and verse 9 that he "shall perish." God does not let the lying breath go unanswered.

Song of Solomon 4:6 — The bridegroom, still praising his bride (Song of Solomon 4:1–7), says he will go to the mountain of myrrh "until the day breaks," using the same phrase as Song of Solomon 2:17. The phrase may describe the cool breeze of morning or of evening. In Song of Solomon 2:17 it introduces the woman's appeal, "Turn, my beloved"; in Song of Solomon 4:6 it sets the time of the bridegroom's going.
