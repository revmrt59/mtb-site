PUT - (Pûwṭ, Strong's H6316)

Summary

Put is the name of the third son of Ham listed in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:6), and of the African people descended from him. The meaning of the name is unknown. Smith's Bible Dictionary proposes a link to an old word for "bow," but the evidence is thin, and Scripture itself never builds anything on the name. English readers can easily miss Put because the NKJV keeps the name "Put" in Genesis, 1 Chronicles, and Nahum but renders the same Hebrew word as "Libya" or "Libyans" in Jeremiah and Ezekiel. Ancient tradition placed Put in Libya, west of Egypt. Others connect it with Punt along the Red Sea coast, and the exact location remains uncertain.

After its place in the genealogies, Put appears only in the prophets, and always in the same role. Its warriors fight for someone else's kingdom and are repeatedly associated with the shield. They stand behind Thebes, march with Pharaoh's army, hang their shields in the city of Tyre, and, in Ezekiel's vision of the latter days, join Gog's assault on God's restored people. Each time, the power they defend falls under God's judgment, and Put falls with it. The pattern shows what borrowed and purchased strength is worth when the Lord acts. Against it stands the Lord's promise to Abram that He Himself would be his shield (Genesis 15:1).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew lemma Pûwṭ refers to two connected realities. The first is a person, Put, the son of Ham and brother of Cush, Mizraim, and Canaan (Genesis 10:6; 1 Chronicles 1:8). The second is the people and land that carry his name. The Table of Nations treats a forefather and the nation that comes from him as one line, so the two belong together. Even so, the prophetic passages speak of a people, not of an individual.

The meaning of the name is unknown. Smith's Bible Dictionary suggests a connection to an ancient word for "bow," but it should be held loosely. In the one verse where Scripture pairs Put with a particular weapon alongside an archer people, the bow belongs to the Lydians and the shield belongs to Put (Jeremiah 46:9). No biblical passage plays on the name or explains it.

Put's location is debated. The Greek Old Testament and the historian Josephus associated Put with Libya, and modern translations, including the NKJV, often follow that tradition. Others identify it with the land of Punt, a trading partner of Egypt along the southern Red Sea. Scripture consistently groups Put with Cush and Egypt, which places it somewhere in Africa near the Egyptian sphere of power. Put should also be kept distinct from two similar names. The Lubim (H3864), also often linked to Libya, appear right beside Put in Nahum 3:9 as a separate people. Lud, or the Lydians, are frequent companions of Put in the lists of hired soldiers (Ezekiel 27:10; 30:5) but are a different nation.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Origins. "The sons of Ham were Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan" (Genesis 10:6). The Chronicler repeats the line word for word (1 Chronicles 1:8). Genesis goes on to list the descendants of Cush, Mizraim, and Canaan, sometimes at length (Genesis 10:7–20), but it names no descendants for Put. He stands in the record as the head of a people whose story the Bible does not trace further. When Put next appears, it is no longer a family but a fighting force.

The helpers of Thebes. Nahum, prophesying against Nineveh, points the Assyrians to the great Egyptian city of No Amon, or Thebes (Nahum 3:8). "Ethiopia and Egypt were her strength, And it was boundless; Put and Lubim were your helpers" (Nahum 3:9). Yet Thebes was carried into exile anyway (Nahum 3:10). Nahum's argument is direct: if a city with that many allies fell, proud Nineveh should not imagine it will stand. Put appears here as part of a defense that looked limitless and failed.

With Pharaoh at Carchemish. Jeremiah 46 concerns Pharaoh Necho's army, which Nebuchadnezzar defeated at Carchemish (Jeremiah 46:2). The prophet sounds a mocking call to battle: "Come up, O horses, and rage, O chariots! And let the mighty men come forth: The Ethiopians and the Libyans who handle the shield, And the Lydians who handle and bend the bow" (Jeremiah 46:9). The "Libyans" here translate Put. The very next verse explains that this is the day of the Lord GOD of hosts, a day of vengeance (Jeremiah 46:10). Egypt brought out its finest hired troops, and the battle belonged to God from the start.

In the army of Tyre. Ezekiel's lament over Tyre pictures the city as a splendid merchant ship (Ezekiel 27). Tyre's wealth came from the sea, and it hired its soldiers from abroad. "Those from Persia, Lydia, and Libya Were in your army as men of war; They hung shield and helmet in you; They gave splendor to you" (Ezekiel 27:10). The detail is striking. Put's soldiers did more than guard Tyre. Their displayed armor added to the city's glory. The same chapter ends with Tyre sinking into the heart of the seas (Ezekiel 27:26–36).

Falling with Egypt. In Ezekiel's oracle against Egypt, a sword comes on the land, and its allies share the blow: "Ethiopia, Libya, Lydia, all the mingled people, Chub, and the men of the lands who are allied, shall fall with them by the sword" (Ezekiel 30:5). The following verse adds that those who uphold Egypt shall fall and the pride of her power shall come down (Ezekiel 30:6).

Among the armies of Gog. Put's last appearance in the prophets moves beyond any single empire. In Ezekiel's vision of the latter days, Gog gathers a vast coalition against Israel, restored and living securely in its land (Ezekiel 38:8–11). "Persia, Ethiopia, and Libya are with them, all of them with shield and helmet" (Ezekiel 38:5). The Lord Himself meets this army with earthquake, sword, pestilence, hail, fire, and brimstone, so that the nations will know that He is the LORD (Ezekiel 38:19–23).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

What Scripture states plainly is consistent. Put is never shown fighting for its own cause. It appears as the hired or allied strength of Thebes, Egypt, Tyre, and Gog, and every one of those powers comes under God's judgment. Ezekiel 30:5 says outright that Egypt's allies "shall fall with them by the sword." Put's soldiers chose a side, and they shared that side's fate.

From this pattern a careful conclusion follows. The prophets use Put to show how empty human security becomes once the Lord sets Himself against a nation. Thebes had boundless strength, Egypt had its chariots, and Tyre had money enough to fill its city with foreign shields. None of it held. Ezekiel 27:10 sharpens the point by tying Put's armor to Tyre's "splendor." Military display had become part of the city's pride, and pride is exactly what Ezekiel's oracles bring down (Ezekiel 28:2–8).

Put's place in Genesis 10 also matters. The Table of Nations sets every people, Put included, inside one human family under one Creator. It comes just before God promises Abram that all the families of the earth will be blessed in him (Genesis 12:3). The nations who later fight against God's purposes are the same nations God intends to bless.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Put is not named in the New Testament. Revelation does take up Ezekiel's language of "Gog and Magog," describing Satan going out "to deceive the nations which are in the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, to gather them together to battle" (Revelation 20:8). The ending is brief and final: "fire came down from God out of heaven and devoured them" (Revelation 20:9). How Ezekiel 38–39 and Revelation 20 relate in timing is debated, but the canonical development is clear. Revelation takes up Ezekiel's picture of a worldwide rebellion, which in Ezekiel includes Put, and uses it for the last gathering of deceived nations against the people of God. Without settling whether the two visions describe the same event, both end the same way, at God's hand. If Put is Libya, then Persia in the east, Cush in the south, and Put in the west mark out the reach of Ezekiel's coalition, and Revelation widens it to the four corners of the earth.

A second line runs through the word "shield." The ordinary Hebrew word Put's soldiers carry in Jeremiah 46:9, Ezekiel 27:10, and Ezekiel 38:5 is the same word God uses when He tells Abram that He is his shield (Genesis 15:1). This is a thematic connection, not a stated type. Even so, it is a fruitful one. The empires hired Put to carry the shield for them. Abram received a shield he could not buy, the Lord Himself, and that promise comes to rest in Christ, through whom the blessing of Abraham reaches the nations (Galatians 3:14).

There is also a hopeful resonance. On the day of Pentecost, the crowd in Jerusalem included people from "the parts of Libya adjoining Cyrene" (Acts 2:10). This is not Put by name, and the identification of Put with Libya is uncertain. Still, the region that the old prophecies remembered for hired soldiers was among the first to hear the gospel in its own language.

5. Spirit & Life

Put's story is mostly a story of judgment, and it is sobering. Strength placed in the service of a proud kingdom falls with that kingdom. Ezekiel does not portray Put as a neutral contractor who simply lost a client. Those who uphold a power the Lord opposes share its ruin (Ezekiel 30:5–6).

The deeper word concerns where security comes from. Tyre could afford the best protection money could buy and hang it on display. Thebes had allies on every side. Scripture shows these defenses collapsing, not because the soldiers were weak, but because no hired shield can stand between a nation and the judgment of God. Isaiah warns those who go down to Egypt for help and trust in its horses and chariots but do not look to the Holy One of Israel (Isaiah 31:1).

The gospel answers the need that Tyre tried to meet with money. God offers Himself as the shield of His people. Abram did not earn or purchase that protection; God gave it by promise. In Christ, believers receive that same promise. They can examine their own sources of confidence, whether money, reputation, or the support of powerful allies, and bring their trust home to the God who does not fail.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:6; 1 Chronicles 1:8 — Put as the son of Ham and head of a people, named without listed descendants.

  • Nahum 3:8–10 — Put as one of the "helpers" of Thebes, a city that fell despite boundless strength, offered as a warning to Nineveh.

  • Jeremiah 46:9–10 — Put (rendered "Libyans") as shield-bearers in Pharaoh's army on the day of the Lord's vengeance.

  • Ezekiel 27:10 — Put (rendered "Libya") in Tyre's hired army, hanging shield and helmet and giving the city splendor.

  • Ezekiel 30:5–6 — Put falls by the sword with Egypt and those who uphold her.

  • Ezekiel 38:5, 19–23 — Put among Gog's latter-day armies, destroyed by the Lord's direct intervention.

  • Revelation 20:7–9 — Later use of Gog and Magog imagery for the final deceived nations consumed by fire from God.

  • Genesis 15:1 — God's promise to be Abram's shield, set against the purchased shields of the empires.
