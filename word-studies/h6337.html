PURE GOLD, FINE GOLD - (pâz, Strong's H6337)

Summary

The Hebrew word pâz means pure or fine gold, gold valued for its purity and worth. It is a rare word, used only nine times, and always in poetry or prophecy. It never appears in a merchant's ledger or a building list. Scripture uses it as the highest measure of earthly value and then sets something above it. The psalmist says God's judgments are "more to be desired" than "much fine gold" (Psalm 19:10), and he confesses, "I love Your commandments More than gold, yes, than fine gold!" (Psalm 119:127). Wisdom says her fruit is better than fine gold (Proverbs 8:19).

The same word crowns a king (Psalm 21:3) and describes the beloved in the Song of Solomon, whose head is "like the finest gold" (Song 5:11). In the prophets it measures loss. In judgment a human being becomes "more rare than fine gold" (Isaiah 13:12), and Zion's sons, once "valuable as fine gold," are treated like cheap clay pots (Lamentations 4:2). Taken together, pâz asks what we treasure most. It points to a King whose glory exceeds every earthly standard, and it shows what sin does to people God made precious.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hebrew has several words for gold. The common one, zahav, is used for jewelry, money, and the gold of the tabernacle and temple. Pâz is different. It belongs to the language of poets and prophets. Each of its nine uses falls in Job, Psalms, Proverbs, the Song of Solomon, Isaiah, or Lamentations.

Gold had to be mined, smelted and refined, so pure gold was scarce and costly. Scripture does not use pâz as a technical grade ranked against the other gold words; it names gold at its most precious. Biblical poets often place pâz beside other words for gold to build toward a climax, as in "than gold, yes, than fine gold." Hebrew poetry often states a thought and then intensifies it in the next line, and this word supplies the strongest term in that progression.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Pâz means pure, refined, fine gold. It has a single sense. The word names the substance itself. Its poetic weight comes from what that substance stood for in people's minds, which was the most precious and desirable thing a person could hold.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses pâz in three ways.

The first is as a standard that is surpassed. In Job, Psalms, and Proverbs, fine gold is named so that God's Word and wisdom can be shown to be worth more. The comparison usually builds by steps: gold, then fine gold, then the greater treasure.

The second is as an image of royal glory and beauty. A crown of pâz marks the king's honor (Psalm 21:3), and the beloved's head and the bases beneath his legs are pictured in fine gold (Song 5:11, 15).

The third is as a measure of loss in judgment. Isaiah uses its rarity to describe how few people will survive God's judgment. Lamentations uses its value to show how far Zion's people have fallen.

4. Theological Significance

Pâz reveals how God orders value. The world naturally treats fine gold as the summit of worth. Scripture accepts that judgment as far as it goes, then shows that it does not go far enough. Gold cannot enlighten the eyes, give wisdom, or restore the soul, and God's Word does all three (Psalm 19:7–8). The purity of fine gold resembles the purity of God's words, which are "pure words, Like silver tried in a furnace of earth, Purified seven times" (Psalm 12:6). Even so, only His words give life.

The prophetic uses show God's other judgment of value. He made His people precious, and sin degrades what He made glorious. The same word that measures the worth of His Word also measures the tragedy of people who turn from it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 19:10 and Psalm 119:127: desiring and loving God's Word. In Psalm 19, David first praises God's voice in the heavens and then turns to His law: the law is perfect, and "the commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes" (19:8). Then he weighs these judgments against "much fine gold" and finds them "more to be desired," and "sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb" (19:10). The comparison concerns what David wants. A person can admit that Scripture is valuable while still longing for wealth. David says his longing has changed.

Psalm 119:127 carries this further: "Therefore I love Your commandments More than gold, yes, than fine gold!" The "therefore" follows the psalmist's cry in verse 126 that it is time for the LORD to act because people have made His law void. While others treat God's law as worthless, his love for it grows stronger. Fine gold becomes the test of a heart God has changed. Gold is what people naturally crave, and the psalmist loves God's commandments more.

Psalm 21:3 and Song of Solomon 5:11, 15: the King in fine gold. Psalm 21 is the people's thanksgiving for their king: "You meet him with the blessings of goodness; You set a crown of pure gold upon his head." God himself sets this crown on the king's head, so the king's honor comes from God as a gift and does not originate with the king.

In the Song, the bride searches for words high enough to describe her beloved. "His head is like the finest gold" (5:11), and "His legs are pillars of marble Set on bases of fine gold" (5:15). The bases in verse 15 are sockets that bear weight. The same kind of term describes the sockets that held up the tabernacle's frames. Fine gold beneath marble pillars pictures strength that does not tarnish and a standing that cannot be shaken. The bride takes the purest substance she knows and uses it to describe the one she loves.

Isaiah 13:12 and Lamentations 4:2: value measured in judgment. In the oracle against Babylon, the LORD announces the Day of the LORD against human pride: "I will make a mortal more rare than fine gold, A man more than the golden wedge of Ophir." Proud people spend their lives gathering rare gold, and in that day they themselves will become rarer than the gold they gathered. The comparison is about scarcity. It does not suggest that the survivors are pure.

Lamentations 4:2 grieves over Jerusalem: "The precious sons of Zion, Valuable as fine gold, How they are regarded as clay pots, The work of the hands of the potter!" The poet measures these sons against fine gold. Now they are regarded as ordinary clay pots. The poet sets the highest possible measure of worth so that the depth of their fall is plain. They were God's people, glorious among the nations, and their rebellion brought them under judgment until they were valued no higher than common pottery.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The poetry of pâz finds its fullest meaning in Jesus Christ. Psalm 21 celebrates the king of David's line whom God crowns and preserves. The psalm does not name Christ, but its royal hope finds its fullest expression in the Son of David, whom the Father exalts. The Gospels add a striking contrast: before His exaltation, the King was mocked with a crown of thorns (Matthew 27:29; John 19:2). The psalm does not predict that crown, but the link is telling. The One worthy of a crown of pure gold took shame first, and His glory came through the cross.

The Song of Solomon's beloved is not labeled a picture of Christ in the New Testament. Still, the Bible's larger pattern of God as Bridegroom to His people makes the connection fruitful. The bride's search for words worthy of her beloved echoes the church's praise of Christ, whose glory never tarnishes.

The story also answers Lamentations. Paul says God has placed the treasure of the knowledge of His glory in "earthen vessels" (2 Corinthians 4:7). Zion's sons were reduced to clay pots, and in Christ God fills clay pots with His own glory. Peter says that tested faith is "much more precious than gold that perishes, though it is tested by fire" (1 Peter 1:7). The risen Christ counsels a lukewarm church "to buy from Me gold refined in the fire, that you may be rich" (Revelation 3:18). The story ends in a city of pure gold, the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:18). There the measure of value the psalmists used becomes the setting of God's people's eternal home.

7. Spirit & Life

Fine gold is a mirror for the heart. Every believer can tell what he or she ought to value most. Psalm 119:127 shows what a heart sounds like after God has changed it, when love for His commandments has grown stronger than the craving for wealth. The psalmist's "therefore" teaches something hopeful. When others make light of God's Word, a believer's love for it can deepen. Opposition does not have to cool that devotion.

Lamentations warns that God's people can lose the honor He gave them. Those who were once valued as fine gold came to be treated like ordinary clay pots. Yet the gospel answers that grief. In Christ, God takes clay vessels and fills them with treasure. He also refines the faith of His people through trials, until it is worth more than the gold the world chases. Trials then serve His purpose to refine what He has already declared precious.

Finally, the bride shows what worship does. She does not stop at "he is good." She reaches for the finest gold she knows and finds even that too small for her beloved. Christians who have seen Christ's glory do the same.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 28:17: "Neither gold nor crystal can equal it, Nor can it be exchanged for jewelry of fine gold." Job's poem on wisdom concludes that no treasure can buy it. Wisdom belongs to God, and the fear of the Lord is where it begins (Job 28:28).

  • Proverbs 8:19: "My fruit is better than gold, yes, than fine gold, And my revenue than choice silver." Wisdom speaks like an investor whose returns beat every market, and what she yields is righteousness and life.

The remaining occurrences are discussed in Section 5.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H2091 (zahav), gold: the common word for gold, paired with pâz in Psalm 19:10 and 119:127 ("gold... fine gold") and in Job 28:17.

  • H2742 (charuts), gold: a poetic word for gold, paired with pâz in Proverbs 8:19.

  • H3800 (kethem), gold, fine gold: joined with pâz in Song 5:11 ("the finest gold") and placed in parallel with it in Isaiah 13:12 ("the golden wedge of Ophir").
