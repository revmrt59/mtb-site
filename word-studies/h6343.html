DREAD, TERROR, FEAR - (pachad, Strong's H6343)

Summary

Pachad is a Hebrew word for dread. It names the shaking, heart-stopping fear that grips a person when danger, judgment, or the presence of God suddenly becomes real. The NKJV usually translates it "fear," "dread," or "terror." Scripture uses it for the panic that falls on enemy nations, the night fears that trouble the vulnerable, the private anguish of a suffering man like Job, and the terror of proud people when the Lord rises to judge the earth. Most surprisingly, Jacob uses the word as a name for God: "the Fear of Isaac" (Genesis 31:42).

The word lets Scripture place two kinds of fear side by side. The first is a dread that hunts the wicked and torments the soul, and no hiding place can stop it. The second is the trembling reverence of a believer who prays, "My flesh trembles for fear of You" (Psalm 119:120). The person who fears God in this way is told, "You shall not be afraid of the terror by night" (Psalm 91:5). Through this word we see God ruling over human hearts, warning of judgment no one can escape, and, in Christ, becoming a safe refuge from the very dread His holiness rightly produces.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pachad is a Hebrew noun. It appears from Genesis through Lamentations, and it is most common in poetry and wisdom writing, especially Job and the Psalms. Its settings vary widely. It appears on battlefields, at night watches, in a suffering man's complaint, in the warnings of wisdom, and in the prophets' oracles of judgment.

The word belongs to a family that includes a verb meaning to tremble in dread. Job 3:25 joins the noun and the verb in one phrase, which reads literally I dreaded a dread. The NKJV conveys this doubled intensity with "the thing I greatly feared." Hebrew has other words for fear, including the common word for reverent "fear of God." Pachad leans toward the physical side of fear: trembling flesh, frozen limbs, and a racing heart.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, pachad means sudden or overwhelming dread. Context shows several distinct senses:

1. The feeling of dread or terror inside a person. In Deuteronomy 28:67, Israel under judgment longs for evening in the morning and for morning in the evening "because of the fear" that fills the heart.

2. Dread that God sends upon people. In 2 Chronicles 14:14, "the fear of the LORD came upon" the cities around Gerar.

3. Reverent awe toward God. By extension, the word can also name God Himself as the One who is feared, as in "the Fear of Isaac" (Genesis 31:42) and "My flesh trembles for fear of You" (Psalm 119:120).

4. The disaster or thing that causes dread. In Proverbs 1:27, "your terror comes like a storm," so the word names the calamity itself. In Psalm 31:11, the psalmist became "repulsive," that is, an object of dread, to those who knew him.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Two patterns in how the word is used help readers understand it. First, dread is often said to "fall on" or "come upon" people (Exodus 15:16; 2 Chronicles 14:14; Esther 8:17). The language pictures fear arriving from outside a person like a weight dropped on them, rather than rising from their own reasoning. Psalm 53:5 captures this: the wicked are "in great fear where no fear was." Nothing visible threatened them, yet terror seized them.

Second, the phrase "fear of ..." can point in different directions. "The fear of the LORD" in 1 Samuel 11:7 means dread that comes from the Lord and falls on people. The phrase "fear of the Jews" in Esther 8:17 means dread that others felt toward the Jewish people. "Fear of You" in Psalm 119:120 is the believer's own reverence directed toward God. The surrounding words show which meaning is intended.

Fear is also frequently paired with night (Psalm 91:5; Song of Solomon 3:8), the hour when danger cannot be seen. One caution: the name Magor-Missabib that Jeremiah gave to Pashhur (Jeremiah 20:3), meaning terror on every side, and the cry "Fear on every side!" (Jeremiah 20:10) use a different Hebrew word.

4. Theological Significance

The passages using pachad reveal that God governs fear. After the Red Sea, Moses sang, "Fear and dread will fall on them; By the greatness of Your arm They will be as still as a stone, Till Your people pass over, O LORD" (Exodus 15:16). Deuteronomy 2:25 promises, "This day I will begin to put the dread and fear of you upon the nations." In Jehoshaphat's day, the fear of God fell on the surrounding kingdoms when they heard that the Lord had fought for Israel, and his realm was quiet (2 Chronicles 20:29–30). God often protected His people this way. He did not always give them stronger armies. Instead, He let a fraction of His reality register in hostile hearts, and the enemy's courage drained away before a sword was drawn.

The same power can work within Israel. When Saul sent out the pieces of the oxen, "the fear of the LORD fell on the people, and they came out with one consent" (1 Samuel 11:7). In Esther, a book that never names God, "fear of the Jews fell upon them," and many became Jews (Esther 8:17). Even where God stays out of sight, He moves human hearts.

Scripture also teaches that dread rightly belongs to God. Bildad says that dominion and fear belong to Him (Job 25:2). Job warns his friends that if they speak falsely on God's behalf, God's dread will fall on them (Job 13:11). Both statements share a conviction: the living God cannot be handled lightly.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 31:42, 53 — "the Fear of Isaac." Jacob, confronting Laban after twenty years of hard treatment, says, "Unless the God of my father, the God of Abraham and the Fear of Isaac, had been with me, surely now you would have sent me away empty-handed" (Genesis 31:42). When the two men make their agreement, Laban swears by a mixed list that includes "the God of Nahor." Jacob answers by swearing by "the Fear of his father Isaac" (31:53). Isaac was still living, and Jacob names God by what God was to his father: the One before whom Isaac trembled. This is not the vocabulary of casual religion. It is startling that a word for visceral dread could become a name for the God who protects and blesses.

Readers naturally think of Moriah, where Isaac lay bound on the altar and the angel declared, "now I know that you fear God" (Genesis 22:12). Those words were spoken to Abraham and use a different Hebrew word, so the link is an inference. Still, it is a fitting one: the man who was nearly sacrificed and then spared would know God as both terrifying and merciful.

Isaiah 2:10–21 — the terror of the Lord. Isaiah commands, "Enter into the rock, and hide in the dust, From the terror of the LORD And the glory of His majesty" (2:10). The closing words of that command become a refrain. "From the terror of the LORD And the glory of His majesty" returns in 2:19 and 2:21, where people flee into "the holes of the rocks" and "the clefts of the rocks." The chapter pictures the Lord rising to shake the earth. Proud people are brought low, the Lord alone is exalted, and people throw their idols "to the moles and bats" (2:20). In this passage, pachad is how unrepentant flesh responds to unshielded holiness. The rocks and caves where people hide are a picture of their futility, because nothing created can shelter anyone from the Creator.

Isaiah 24:17–18; Jeremiah 48:43–44 — dread, pit, and snare. Isaiah announces, "Fear and the pit and the snare Are upon you, O inhabitant of the earth." The next verse shows what this means: "he who flees from the noise of the fear Shall fall into the pit, And he who comes up from the midst of the pit Shall be caught in the snare." In Hebrew the three words sound alike (pachad, pachath, pach), so the sentence itself sounds like a closing trap. The imagery likely comes from hunting. Noise drives frightened animals out of cover into a hidden pit, and anything that escapes the pit is caught in the net. Jeremiah applies the same words to Moab (48:43–44), and Lamentations 3:47 uses the same pairing to describe Jerusalem's own ruin. God's people are not exempt. No one can outrun or outthink God's justice, because each escape route leads into the next trap.

Job — dread inside suffering. Job's first lament confesses, "For the thing I greatly feared has come upon me, And what I dreaded has happened to me" (Job 3:25). Eliphaz describes a night vision in which fear and trembling made his bones shake (4:14). Job admits that destruction from God was a terror to him, and that this dread kept him from wrongdoing (31:23). The book treats dread as a real part of human suffering, and God never rebukes Job for feeling it. When God finally answers from the whirlwind, the word appears again in His own speech: the ostrich is "without concern" for her young (39:16), and the war horse "mocks at fear" (39:22). God does not explain Job's suffering. Instead, He shows Job a creation full of His wisdom and power, and Job's dread of the unknown gives way to awe of the One he now sees (42:5–6).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The terror Isaiah described returns in Revelation. Kings, commanders, and slaves hide "in the caves and in the rocks of the mountains" and cry, "Fall on us and hide us from the face of Him who sits on the throne and from the wrath of the Lamb!" (Revelation 6:15–16). The last line asks, "who is able to stand?" (6:17).

Scripture also tells of a rock that does shelter. God placed Moses in the cleft of the rock and covered him with His hand while His glory passed by (Exodus 33:22). Hiding from glory is useless for the proud, yet God Himself provides a hiding place for the one He chooses to spare. In Christ, the believer finds that refuge.

There is a fitting connection, though not an explicit fulfillment, between the dread of judgment and the cross. In Gethsemane, Jesus "began to be troubled and deeply distressed" (Mark 14:33). Hebrews says He prayed "with vehement cries and tears to Him who was able to save Him from death, and was heard because of His godly fear" (Hebrews 5:7). In Jesus, anguish and reverence stood together. He entered the darkness that belonged to sinners, so that those hidden in Him meet God with "reverence and godly fear" rather than terror, because "our God is a consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:28–29).

The gospel also diagnoses why we need this refuge. Psalm 36:1 says of the wicked that there is no fear of God before his eyes, and Paul quotes this line to close his case that all humanity stands guilty (Romans 3:18).

7. Spirit & Life

The wisdom books describe two destinies for fear. For those who reject wisdom, "your terror comes like a storm" (Proverbs 1:27). The one who listens, however, will dwell safely and "be secure, without fear of evil" (Proverbs 1:33), and need not be afraid of sudden terror (Proverbs 3:25). Psalm 91:5 extends the promise into the dark hours: "You shall not be afraid of the terror by night, Nor of the arrow that flies by day." Even Solomon's sixty mighty men in the Song of Solomon stood with swords on their thighs "because of fear in the night" (3:8). Night fear is ancient and ordinary, and human strength can only guard against it. God offers to remove its power over us.

The same psalter that frees the believer from night terror also says, "My flesh trembles for fear of You, And I am afraid of Your judgments" (Psalm 119:120). The believer is freed from lesser dreads by a greater fear, not by ceasing to fear altogether. When God is truly feared, smaller terrors lose their authority. Jehoshaphat applied this to public life when he told Judah's judges, "let the fear of the LORD be upon you," because with God there is "no partiality, nor taking of bribes" (2 Chronicles 19:6–7). He charged them to judge fairly and refuse bribes out of reverence for the God they served. Chronicles records the charge, not how the judges carried it out, but the implication for anyone who judges or leads today is plain: reverence for God gives courage before powerful people and care for the weak.

Job's story adds tenderness to this teaching. If you have lain awake while "the thing I greatly feared" came true, Scripture does not shame you. God answered Job with Himself, and He still meets His people in their fear. Job's dread gave way to awe when he saw God (Job 42:5), though Scripture does not promise that every believer's dread will lift at once. Even while it lingers, we can bring the dread itself to the God who holds the storm, the night, and the judgment in His hand, and who has opened a refuge in His Son.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 105:38 — Egypt was glad when Israel departed, because dread of Israel had fallen on them. The oppressor ended up afraid of the oppressed.

  • Psalm 64:1 — David asks God to preserve his life "from fear of the enemy." He brings the dread itself to God in prayer, as well as the danger.

  • Psalm 31:11 — The sufferer became "repulsive," an object of dread, to his acquaintances. This prefigures the righteous sufferer whom others avoid.

  • Jeremiah 30:5 — Before Jacob's restoration comes "a voice of trembling, of fear, and not of peace." In this passage, dread comes before deliverance rather than replacing it.

  • Job 15:21 — Eliphaz says that dreadful sounds fill the ears of the wicked. Even in prosperity, the guilty conscience hears danger everywhere.

  • Job 21:9 — Job complains that the houses of the wicked seem safe from fear. Honest faith can admit that judgment does not always look immediate.
