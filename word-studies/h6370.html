CONCUBINE, PARAMOURS - (pîylegesh, Strong's H6370)

Summary

The Hebrew word pîylegesh means a concubine: a woman joined to a man as a secondary wife. She was publicly recognized as belonging to his household and was expected to be faithful to him, but she held lower standing than a primary wife. Scripture does not set out one uniform package of rights for her across every period, and her children did not necessarily share the chief inheritance, as when Abraham gave gifts to his concubines' sons and sent them away from Isaac (Genesis 25:5–6). Patriarchs took concubines to build families, judges and kings kept them, and royal courts gathered them by the hundreds. In one prophetic passage the word is turned to describe the "paramours" whom faithless Jerusalem pursued.

Scripture records these relationships without ever holding them up as God's design. In the narratives where concubines appear, trouble often follows. Households divide, rivals seize thrones, kings drift into idolatry, and in the worst chapter of the Judges a defenseless woman is handed to a mob. Against that background the Song of Solomon sets one beloved apart from "sixty queens and eighty concubines" and calls her "the only one." The word teaches us to notice the vulnerable people whom powerful men used and discarded. It also points ahead to the undivided love of Christ for His bride.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pîylegesh belongs to the household world of the ancient Near East. Large families and royal courts commonly included secondary wives alongside primary wives. These women were sometimes taken to produce heirs and sometimes, in the case of kings, to display wealth and power. The word appears across Genesis, the narratives of Judges, Samuel, and Kings, the genealogies of Chronicles, the court of Persia in Esther, the poetry of the Song of Solomon, and once in Ezekiel's prophecy. More than half of its uses fall in just two places: the Levite's story in Judges 19–20 and the struggles over David's throne in 2 Samuel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A concubine or secondary wife: a woman with a recognized but lower place in a man's household. Her children belonged to his family but did not automatically share the chief inheritance. Example: "He took for himself a concubine from Bethlehem in Judah" (Judges 19:1).

2. A lover or paramour, used in a transferred sense in Ezekiel 23:20, where NKJV renders it "paramours" for the foreign lovers Jerusalem pursued.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Genesis and Chronicles the word usually sits in family records. Nahor's concubine Reumah bears children (Genesis 22:24). Timna, concubine of Esau's son Eliphaz, becomes the mother of Amalek (Genesis 36:12). Caleb's concubines Ephah and Maachah appear in Judah's lines (1 Chronicles 2:46, 48). These notices explain who belongs to whom, and they also mark which branches of a family stand apart from the main line.

Even in these early records, Scripture keeps the line of promise distinct. Abraham gave gifts to the sons of his concubines and, while he was still living, sent them east, away from Isaac (Genesis 25:6; see 1 Chronicles 1:32). That separation honored God's word that Abraham's seed would be called in Isaac (Genesis 21:12). When Reuben lay with Bilhah, his father's concubine (Genesis 35:22), the act cost him the firstborn's birthright, as 1 Chronicles 5:1 later records. God bore with the customs of the age, but He did not let them redirect His promise.

In the monarchy the word becomes political. A king's concubines belonged to his throne, so claiming them amounted to claiming the kingdom. In Ezekiel the sense twists. Applied to Jerusalem's foreign lovers, a term normally used for a lower-ranked woman exposes how degraded her pursuit had become. The exact force of the word in that verse is debated, but the shock of the image is plain.

4. Theological Significance

The concubine stories show what happens when human arrangements depart from God's pattern of one man and one woman becoming one flesh (Genesis 2:24). A household with ranked wives is a household with ranked affections, and Scripture lets the consequences speak. Gideon's concubine in Shechem "bore him a son, whose name he called Abimelech" (Judges 8:31). That son murdered his brothers and brought ruin on Shechem (Judges 9).

Solomon is the clearest warning. Deuteronomy forbade the king to multiply wives: "Neither shall he multiply wives for himself, lest his heart turn away" (Deuteronomy 17:17). Solomon "had seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines; and his wives turned away his heart" (1 Kings 11:3). The verse reads almost like a fulfillment of the warning, and the result was idolatry and a divided kingdom. Nehemiah later held up the same example: "pagan women caused even him to sin" (Nehemiah 13:26). Rehoboam carried the pattern on with eighteen wives and sixty concubines (2 Chronicles 11:21). God's law guarded the king's heart, and the harem was the very thing it guarded against.

Through it all, God notices the people the system overlooks. The concubine is often nameless and usually voiceless, yet Scripture records what was done to her and holds the doers accountable.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Judges 19–20: the Levite's concubine. The story opens "in those days, when there was no king in Israel" (Judges 19:1), and the book closes with the explanation: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). Between those lines comes Israel's darkest night. A Levite's concubine returns to her father's house in Bethlehem, and her husband goes to bring her back. In Gibeah of Benjamin, men of the city surround the house. The Levite pushes her out to them. They abuse her all night, and at dawn she collapses at the door with her hands on the threshold. He cuts her body in pieces and sends them through Israel, which ignites a civil war that nearly wipes out Benjamin. Her lower standing made her the easiest person to sacrifice. When God's people abandon His rule, the most vulnerable pay first. Before the assembly, the Levite tells a version of events that leaves out his own part (Judges 20:4–6).

2 Samuel: concubines and the throne. When Ishbosheth asks Abner, "Why have you gone in to my father's concubine?" (2 Samuel 3:7), the accusation, in that context, implies that Abner is reaching for the crown. The narrative records the charge without independently confirming it. Years later David flees Absalom and leaves ten concubines to keep the house (15:16). Ahithophel advises Absalom to lie with them publicly. A tent is pitched on the roof, and he does so "in the sight of all Israel" (16:21–22). This answered the word Nathan spoke after David's sin with Bathsheba: "I will take your wives before your eyes and give them to your neighbor, and he shall lie with your wives in the sight of this sun" (12:11). David's hidden sin was repaid openly. The women who suffered it had no part in the rebellion that is narrated. When David returned, he shut them away and provided for them, and they lived as widows until they died (20:3).

Song of Solomon 6:8–9: the only one. The Bridegroom grants that royal courts are crowded: "There are sixty queens and eighty concubines, and virgins without number." Then he sets his beloved apart: "My dove, my perfect one, is the only one." The surprise is that the queens and concubines themselves "praised her." The harem, a picture of affection divided many ways, ends up honoring the one who is loved without rival.

Ezekiel 23:20: Jerusalem's paramours. God describes Samaria and Jerusalem as two sisters, Oholah and Oholibah, who "were Mine" (23:4) yet chased foreign powers. Jerusalem "lusted for her paramours," and the coarse imagery that follows is meant to shock. Seeking security from Egypt instead of from the LORD is pictured as degrading lust. Here the word for a secondary, lower-ranked partner is used of the very nations Jerusalem chose over her rightful Husband.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Judges keeps saying Israel had no king, and the reader waits for one. Yet the kings who came multiplied concubines. Their women were seized in rebellion, and their hearts were turned toward idols. David and the merely human kings after him could not be the king Israel needed. That king is Jesus, the promised Son of David, who does not use the weak to secure His throne. He gives Himself for them.

The Song's "only one" finds its fullest answer in Christ and His church. Paul describes Christ loving the church and giving Himself for her, to present her to Himself without spot (Ephesians 5:25–27). The New Testament does not cite Song 6:9, but the resonance is real. Christ has no secondary bride and no second-rank members of His household. All who belong to Him are fully His, with a full share in the inheritance (Romans 8:17). Ezekiel's grief over a wife who chased paramours shows the other side: God's love is jealous because it is exclusive.

7. Spirit & Life

Rizpah shows how God honors those the world ranks low. She first appears as a bargaining point between Abner and Ishbosheth (2 Samuel 3:7). Later, when her two sons were executed for Saul's bloodguilt toward the Gibeonites, she spread sackcloth on a rock. From the beginning of harvest until the rains came, she kept the birds and beasts away from their bodies (21:10). When David was told what she had done (21:11), he gathered the bones of Saul and Jonathan, buried them with the others, and afterward God heeded the prayer for the land (21:14). A concubine's faithful grief moved a king to finish what justice required.

The Levite's concubine has no name and is given no words. Still, the Spirit preserved her story, and all Israel was told to "consider it, confer, and speak up" (Judges 19:30). Scripture does not let us look away from the vulnerable. Believers who know Christ's undivided love should be the first to protect those whom others treat as disposable.

The Song also searches the heart. Solomon's divided household divided his heart, and Deuteronomy's warning still applies to anyone who multiplies competing loves. Christ does not love His people partially, and He asks for an undivided heart in return.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 36:12 — Timna, concubine of Eliphaz, bore Amalek, the ancestor of a people who became Israel's bitter enemy.

  • 2 Samuel 5:13 — David took more concubines and wives after coming to Jerusalem. The notice is brief, but it plants the household troubles that later erupt.

  • 2 Samuel 19:5 — Joab rebukes David for mourning Absalom, reminding him that his servants had saved his concubines' lives along with his family's.

  • 1 Chronicles 3:9 — David's sons are listed apart from "the sons of the concubines," keeping the royal line clear.

  • Esther 2:14 — Women of Ahasuerus's court passed to the custodian of the concubines and never saw the king again unless he delighted in them and called them by name. It shows how easily a person could be forgotten in a king's house.
