DIVIDED, DIVIDE - (pâlag, Strong's H6385)

Summary

The Hebrew verb pâlag means "to divide" or "to split," in the sense of cutting something apart or cutting a channel through it. It appears only four times in the Old Testament, and in each use God is, or is most naturally understood to be, the one who divides. Genesis and 1 Chronicles use it to explain the name of Peleg, "for in his days the earth was divided." The surrounding chapters make it a well-supported reading that this refers to the scattering of the nations after Babel, though the verse does not name the event. In Psalm 55, in words many readers hear as an echo of Babel, David prays, "Destroy, O Lord, and divide their tongues," asking God to break apart the united counsel of those plotting against him. In Job 38, God asks who has "divided a channel for the overflowing water," a picture of the Creator cutting paths for the storm.

These few verses show that division in God's hands works in two directions. He splits apart the proud unity of people set against Him, and He cuts paths for the storm so rain can reach land where no one lives. The same God who scattered Babel and frustrated Ahithophel's counsel also waters the empty wilderness. The word teaches believers not to treat every kind of human unity as good. It also assures them that God sends His provision to the places others overlook.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pâlag is a Hebrew verb. It belongs to a small word family that includes the personal name Peleg and the common noun peleg, which means a stream or channel of water. The noun appears in Psalm 1:3, where the righteous man is like a tree planted by the "rivers of water." The verb never appears in law, prophecy, or narrative action. It shows up in a genealogy (twice), a lament psalm, and God's speech from the whirlwind. Because the word is so rare, each occurrence carries real weight.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Pâlag describes a decisive splitting or channeling. Context determines what is split. Scripture's four uses fall into two senses:

1. To divide a population or its speech into separate parts, breaking up what had been united. Genesis 10:25 says "the earth was divided," and Psalm 55:9 prays, "divide their tongues."

2. To cut or cleave a physical path, especially a channel for water. Job 38:25 asks, "Who has divided a channel for the overflowing water."

The word differs from Hebrew terms that mean sorting or setting apart, such as the separating of holy from common. Pâlag is a word for breaking something open or breaking it up.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the genealogies the verb is passive. The earth "was divided," and no one is named as the one who divided it. Readers look to the surrounding chapters, and the most natural reading connects the division with God's scattering at Babel. In Psalm 55:9 the word becomes a petition. David asks God directly to do the dividing, and its object is "their tongues," meaning the speech and shared plans of his enemies. In Job 38:25, God Himself speaks the word in a question that has only one possible answer. Across the three settings the meaning stays steady while the object changes: people, speech, and ground. In the psalm and in Job the one who divides is explicitly God; in the genealogies that is the well-supported reading.

Genesis 10:25 has drawn speculative readings, including continental drift. The context offers a simpler explanation. Genesis 10 repeatedly groups the nations by their families, languages, lands, and nations, and Genesis 11 tells of a scattering that is widely and reasonably taken to be the division meant, though other historical proposals exist.

4. Theological Significance

These verses show God using division both as judgment and as grace. When people unite in rebellion, God breaks up their unity. Babel's builders wanted to make a name for themselves "lest we be scattered abroad" (Genesis 11:4). David's enemies joined forces to fill the city with "violence and strife" (Psalm 55:9). In both cases God's dividing stops evil before it reaches its full strength. The LORD's own words at Babel explain why: "now nothing that they propose to do will be withheld from them" (Genesis 11:6). United rebellion is dangerous, and God's dividing restrains it.

Yet the same verb describes God splitting the landscape so water can flow. In Job, His dividing gives life. He breaks the proud and He waters the barren. Both acts come from the same sovereign care over His world. God is never passive toward what He has made. He opposes what destroys it and supplies what sustains it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:25 — the earth divided in Peleg's days. The note interrupts a genealogy. Eber's son was named Peleg, meaning "Division," "for in his days the earth was divided." A family name preserves the memory of a turning point in history. The next chapter tells an event that is widely and reasonably taken to be the one meant. "The whole earth had one language and one speech" (Genesis 11:1), and the people gathered in Shinar to build a city and a tower "whose top is in the heavens." The LORD came down, confused their language "that they may not understand one another's speech," and "scattered them abroad from there over the face of all the earth" (Genesis 11:7–8). The very scattering they had tried to prevent became the means by which humanity filled the earth. On that reading, Peleg's name marks that moment in the record.

Psalm 55:9 — "divide their tongues." David writes as a man betrayed. The psalm grieves that the wound came from a close companion, someone who had shared worship with him (Psalm 55:12–14), rather than from an open enemy. Many readers connect the psalm with Absalom's revolt, when David learned that "Ahithophel is among the conspirators with Absalom" and prayed, "O LORD, I pray, turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness!" (2 Samuel 15:31). Whether or not the psalm comes from that exact night, the parallel helps explain the prayer. When David asks God to "divide their tongues," he seems, in a likely echo of Babel, to be asking for that kind of confusion again on a smaller scale. He wants the conspirators unable to agree, unable to coordinate, and unable to carry out what they have planned.

The account in 2 Samuel shows God answering a prayer of this kind. Ahithophel's plan was sound: strike David "while he is weary and weak," kill only the king, and bring the people back (2 Samuel 17:1–3). "The saying pleased Absalom and all the elders of Israel." Then Absalom said, "Now call Hushai the Archite also, and let us hear what he says too" (17:5). Hushai's competing advice won the room, and the narrator explains that the LORD had determined to defeat Ahithophel's good counsel so that disaster would come on Absalom (17:14). One voice was enough to split the council and rescue the king. God took apart the conspiracy from the inside by dividing its counsel.

Job 38:25 — a channel for the overflowing water. God questions Job: "Who has divided a channel for the overflowing water, Or a path for the thunderbolt." To a person caught in a storm, floodwater and lightning look random. God says He has cut their paths. The next verses explain His purpose. He sends rain on land where no one lives, on wilderness without people, to satisfy the desolate waste and make tender grass spring up (Job 38:26–27). Rain on land without inhabitants does not depend on human usefulness or human witness. He waters it anyway, because He cares for His creation where no one lives.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The scattering in Peleg's day does not end the story. Genesis 11 follows Peleg's line forward to Abram (Genesis 11:16–26), and God promises Abram that in him "all the families of the earth" will be blessed (Genesis 12:3). Luke includes Peleg in the genealogy of Jesus (Luke 3:35). The family line named for division leads to the One through whom the divided nations are gathered.

Pentecost shows that gathering. At Babel, God acted on human tongues so that people could not understand one another. In Acts 2, the Spirit acts on tongues again, and people from many nations each hear the apostles speaking of God's mighty works in their own language (Acts 2:6–11). Luke does not call this a reversal of Babel, but the resonance is clear. God answers the scattering by uniting people in Christ, without erasing their languages. Revelation shows the end result: worshipers from every nation, tribe, people, and tongue standing before the Lamb (Revelation 7:9). Jesus also prayed that His followers would be one (John 17:21). He wants a unity built around Himself, which is the opposite of Babel's unity built around making a name for themselves.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 55 and Genesis 11 caution believers against assuming that unity is always good. A crowd that agrees, a coalition that speaks with one voice, or a group that shares one plan is only as good as what it is united around. Babel was united. Absalom's council was united until God divided it. Believers can pray David's prayer against organized evil and ask God to confuse schemes that would harm the innocent or the church. Christ's command to love and pray for our enemies (Matthew 5:44) still governs the heart behind that prayer. Its target is the wicked counsel, and near the psalm's close David casts his burden on the LORD (Psalm 55:22) and concludes, "But I will trust in You" (Psalm 55:23). Prayer did not replace faithful action for David, who also sent Hushai back to the city (2 Samuel 15:34), but it placed the outcome in God's hands.

Job 38 speaks to anyone who feels like a wilderness: unnoticed, unproductive, far from where life seems to be happening. God cuts channels for rain to reach land where no one lives. His attention does not follow human ideas of usefulness. If He waters empty desert so that grass can grow, He has not forgotten the believer whom others have overlooked.

The same verb covers both truths. The God who divides the plots of the proud is the God who opens a path for rain to fall on dry ground. People who trust Him can rest in both.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

1 Chronicles 1:19 — "for in his days the earth was divided." The Chronicler repeats Genesis 10:25 word for word in his genealogy from Adam to Israel. Centuries later, Peleg's name and the event behind it still marked history for God's people. The other three occurrences of pâlag have been discussed above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

(a) Lexical relatives

  • H6389 (Peleg) is the personal name "Division," explained by the verb in Genesis 10:25.

  • H6388 (peleg) is a stream or channel of water, as in the "rivers of water" of Psalm 1:3. It corresponds to the channel-cutting sense in Job 38:25.

(c) Thematic connection

  • The confusing of language at Babel (Genesis 11:7–9) is the well-supported background for both Genesis 10:25 and Psalm 55:9, though neither verse names it. A different Hebrew verb is used there.
