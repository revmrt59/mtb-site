PELEG - (Peleg, Strong's H6389)

Summary

Peleg was a descendant of Shem who lived after the Flood. He was the son of Eber, the brother of Joktan, and the father of Reu, and he stands in the family line that runs from Noah to Abraham. His name means "division." Genesis explains why he was given it: "for in his days the earth was divided" (Genesis 10:25). Scripture records no words or deeds of Peleg. What it does preserve is his name, his family, and his years. Because he is listed beside the scattering of the nations, he marks the point where the Bible's account of all humanity begins to narrow toward the one family God would call.

The division in Peleg's day is best understood as God's scattering of the peoples at Babel (Genesis 11:1–9). Right after that judgment, Genesis follows Peleg's line down to Abram, through whom God promised to bless all the families of the earth (Genesis 12:3). Luke later names Peleg among the ancestors of Jesus (Luke 3:35). The man whose name recalls the breaking apart of the nations belongs to the family of the One who gathers people from every nation back to God.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Peleg (Hebrew פֶּלֶג) is one person, the son of Eber. Genesis 10:21–25 and 11:10–16 trace Eber's descent from Shem through Arphaxad and Shelah, which makes Peleg Shem's great-great-grandson. His brother was Joktan, and his son was Reu (Genesis 11:18). His name appears seven times in the Hebrew Old Testament, in Genesis 10:25, Genesis 11:16–19, 1 Chronicles 1:19, and 1 Chronicles 1:25. In the New Testament the Greek form Phalek (G5317) appears in Luke's genealogy, and the NKJV renders it "Peleg" (Luke 3:35).

The name comes from the Hebrew root palag (H6385), "to divide" or "to split." Genesis 10:25 makes the wordplay explicit. Peleg was named this "for in his days the earth was divided," and the verb for "was divided" comes from the same root. The name therefore means "division." The text does not say who named him or whether the name was given at birth in anticipation or chosen to recall events already under way. It says only that the name and his lifetime belong together. Peleg the man should also be kept distinct from the ordinary noun peleg (H6388), which means a stream or channel, as in the "rivers of water" of Psalm 1:3. The two words share a root, but the psalm has nothing to do with this patriarch.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

By the figures in the Hebrew text, Peleg was born 101 years after the Flood: Arphaxad was born two years after it, and the next three generations add 35, 30, and 34 years (Genesis 11:10–16). Eber had two sons whose lines Genesis follows separately. The Table of Nations traces Joktan first. It lists his thirteen sons, among them Sheba, Ophir, and Havilah, and records that they settled toward "the mountain of the east" (Genesis 10:26–30). Many of these names are later associated with Arabia. Then Genesis 10 moves on and leaves Peleg's descendants untraced.

Genesis 11 picks up the story after Babel. There the line runs through Peleg: "Peleg lived thirty years, and begot Reu. After he begot Reu, Peleg lived two hundred and nine years, and begot sons and daughters" (Genesis 11:18–19). He had other children, but the record follows only the son who leads toward Abram. Reu leads to Serug, Nahor, Terah, and finally Abram (Genesis 11:20–26).

The lifespans in this genealogy carry their own story. Shem lived 600 years, and his descendants down to Eber still lived more than 430 years each. Eber lived 464. Then Peleg lived only 239, about half his father's span, and of the patriarchs after him only Reu matches it, also living 239 years (Genesis 11:20–21); none of the rest reaches it (Genesis 11:10–32). Genesis gives no explanation for the change. The decline had already begun after Noah, but within the Shem-to-Terah genealogy the sharpest single drop falls at the generation linked with the division of the earth.

Centuries later the Chronicler copied Peleg's name and the explanation of it word for word (1 Chronicles 1:19, 25). He was opening Israel's history for a people who had lived through exile, and the full genealogy reminded them that their story began within God's dealings with the whole human race.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states three things plainly: Peleg was Eber's son, his name was tied to the earth being divided in his days, and he stands in the line to Abram. Linking that division to Babel is a contextual reading, but a strong one. Genesis 10 repeatedly describes the nations spreading out by their languages and lands, and Genesis 11:1–9 explains how this came about. There the Lord confused human speech and scattered the people from Babel over the face of the earth. Some modern writers have suggested the verse refers to continents separating, and others think of land being parceled out. Neither view fits the surrounding chapters as naturally as the scattering of the peoples does. Moses later describes the same scattering as the Most High dividing the nations' inheritance and setting their boundaries (Deuteronomy 32:8).

Peleg's name sits at the point where Genesis turns from humanity's united rebellion to God's chosen remedy. The scattering left the nations divided and estranged. Within one generation's record of that judgment, God was preserving a single line that would reach Abram. When God called Abram, He promised that "all the families of the earth" would be blessed through him (Genesis 12:3, paraphrased). The Table of Nations had just counted those families, scattered and separated. The pattern of Genesis is also visible here: the line that does not carry the promise is traced first, and the chosen line follows, as later happens with Ishmael and Isaac and with Esau and Jacob.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament states Peleg's link to Christ explicitly. Luke traces Jesus' ancestry back through "the son of Reu, the son of Peleg, the son of Eber" (Luke 3:35) and on to Adam. Matthew begins his genealogy with Abraham, so only Luke reaches back far enough to include Peleg. In placing him there, Luke shows that the Son of God belongs to humanity before Abraham, joined by descent to the common human family whose scattering Genesis records, and not only to Israel's story.

The larger canonical story develops the theme of Peleg's day further, though without naming him. At Pentecost, Jews and converts "from every nation under heaven" heard the apostles speaking in their own languages the wonderful works of God (Acts 2:5–11, paraphrased). The confusion of tongues that scattered people at Babel gave way to many tongues proclaiming one gospel. Scripture never calls this a reversal of Babel, but the connection fits the direction of the whole Bible. Paul writes that Christ has broken down the dividing wall between peoples (Ephesians 2:14), and John sees a multitude from every nation, tribe, people, and tongue standing before the throne (Revelation 7:9). Peleg himself is not a type of Christ. His name, though, marks the division that Christ came to heal.

5. Spirit & Life

Genesis tells us nothing Peleg did, yet God kept his name in Moses, in Chronicles, and in Luke. The Lord carried His promise through a man whose life left no recorded story, and the line Genesis follows for the promise was not the one that branched into thirteen named clans, though that does not make Joktan's clans of less worth to God. That should steady anyone who measures faithfulness by visibility. God's purposes often move through quiet households long before they become public history.

The division itself shows God's judgment and His mercy working together. At Babel the Lord saw that a united humanity in rebellion would hold back from nothing it planned (Genesis 11:6). Scattering the people restrained that pride and left room for His promise to grow. The shortened years that begin with Peleg also point toward the frailty that Moses later lamented (Psalm 90:10). Into a world marked by scattering and brevity, God sent His Son, who died not for Israel only, "but also that He would gather together in one the children of God who were scattered abroad" (John 11:52). The gospel answer to Peleg's day is a gathered people reconciled to God through Christ, not a rebuilt tower.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:25 – Gives Peleg's parentage and brother and explains his name: "in his days the earth was divided."

  • Genesis 10:26–30 – Traces Joktan's thirteen sons, the branch of Eber's family set beside Peleg's.

  • Genesis 11:1–9 – Records the scattering at Babel, the most natural setting for the division.

  • Genesis 11:16–19 – Records Peleg's birth, fatherhood, and 239-year life, the sharp drop from Eber's 464.

  • Genesis 11:20–26; 12:1–3 – Carries Peleg's line to Abram and God's promise to bless all families of the earth.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:19, 25 – Preserves Peleg's name and its explanation in Israel's post-exilic record.

  • Deuteronomy 32:8 – Describes God dividing the nations' inheritance.

  • Luke 3:35 – Names Peleg in the ancestry of Jesus.

  • Acts 2:5–11 – Shows the nations hearing one gospel in their own languages.
