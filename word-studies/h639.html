ANGER, WRATH, NOSE, NOSTRILS, FACE - (ʼaph, Strong's H0639)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼaph begins with the human nose: the nostrils that draw breath, the face pressed to the ground in a bow. From there it became the Old Testament's most common word for anger. The connection comes from ordinary experience. An angry person breathes hard, the nostrils flare, and the face flushes. Scripture uses this vivid, physical word for human temper and for the holy anger of God. It does not treat anger as a cold verdict. It treats it as a personal and burning response.

The same word also carries one of the Bible's most important descriptions of God. When the LORD proclaimed His name to Moses, He called Himself "longsuffering" (Exodus 34:6). The Hebrew phrase means long of nose, or slow to heat up. God's anger is real, and it burns against idolatry and betrayal. It is also slow to ignite and open to the plea of an intercessor, and toward His restored people His anger gives way to lasting favor (Psalm 30:5). ʼaph also tells the story of breath itself. God breathed life into Adam's nostrils, and the blast of His nostrils both parts the sea and consumes the wicked. Every breath is His gift, and the patience of God holds back the judgment that sin deserves. That patience reaches its fullest expression in Jesus Christ.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼaph is a Hebrew noun found throughout the Old Testament, from Genesis to Zechariah. It is most frequent in Psalms, Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Job. Its basic meaning is nose or nostrils. It is related to the Hebrew verb ʼanaph, to be angry. Hebrew often names emotions through the body, and this word shows how naturally the nose came to stand for anger. Hard breathing and flared nostrils are visible signs that someone is furious.

The word often appears in a dual form, ʼappayim, meaning two nostrils. This form lies behind several idioms, including the phrase for patience discussed below. ʼaph is a different word from the usual Hebrew word for "face" (panim). When the NKJV translates ʼaph as "face," the setting is nearly always a person bowing to the ground, with the nose touching the dust.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Nose, nostrils, snout. This is the literal organ of breathing and smelling. God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Genesis 2:7). A proverb pictures "a ring of gold in a swine's snout" (Proverbs 11:22).

2. Face, especially bowed in reverence. Lot "bowed himself with his face toward the ground" (Genesis 19:1). The word appears in the same sense when Joseph's brothers, David, Abigail, and the worshiping congregation bow.

3. Anger, wrath. This is the dominant sense. It covers human anger, such as Esau's fury against Jacob (Genesis 27:45), and the anger of the LORD (Exodus 32:10).

4. Patience or temper, in idioms. Long of nostrils means slow to anger, longsuffering, or forbearing (Exodus 34:6; Proverbs 25:15). Short of nostrils describes someone who is "quick-tempered" (Proverbs 14:17). A related idiom appears in 1 Samuel 1:5, where Elkanah gives Hannah a portion described with the dual form, two faces, an idiom the NKJV renders "a double portion."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When ʼaph means anger, Scripture usually describes it as burning. "The anger of the LORD was aroused against Israel" (Numbers 25:3) translates a Hebrew expression in which anger grows hot or is kindled. "Fierce wrath" (Exodus 32:12) is literally the burning of the nose. The imagery shows that anger is a rising heat, which can be provoked, held back, or turned away.

The subject of the word changes how it should be read. Human anger appears in Potiphar's rage (Genesis 39:19), Saul's fury at Jonathan (1 Samuel 20:30), and Jacob's curse on the cruel anger of Simeon and Levi (Genesis 49:6–7). A few human uses are righteous. When the Spirit came upon Saul, his anger was "greatly aroused" against the Ammonites' cruelty to Jabesh (1 Samuel 11:6). Still, Wisdom literature treats human anger with deep caution. The anger of God, by contrast, is never petty. It answers sin, and Scripture repeatedly holds it together with His mercy.

The dual form matters for the idiom of patience. Long of nostrils describes a person or God who takes a long time to heat up. The picture comes from breath that stays slow and even when it might have flared.

4. Theological Significance

God's anger has a clear cause. It burns against idolatry and betrayal of the relationship He formed with His people. At Sinai it rose against the golden calf (Exodus 32:10). At Baal of Peor it was aroused when "Israel was joined to Baal of Peor" (Numbers 25:3). It burned again when Achan stole what had been devoted to God (Joshua 7:1). Deuteronomy explains the reason: the LORD is "a jealous God," and turning to other gods kindles His anger (Deuteronomy 6:15). Scripture pictures His anger as the zeal of a faithful husband guarding a marriage that others have invaded. It is part of His love and does not contradict it. A God who felt nothing when His people gave themselves to idols would not be loving them.

Scripture also insists that God's anger is not His final word toward His redeemed people. David calls God's saints to sing, because His anger is but for a moment while His favor is for life (Psalm 30:4–5). Micah marvels that God, pardoning the remnant of His heritage, does not retain His anger forever "because He delights in mercy" (Micah 7:18). The redeemed in Isaiah praise God because, though He was angry, His anger has turned away and He comforts them (Isaiah 12:1). Anger is a real response to sin and serves His justice. Mercy is what He delights in.

The word also shows God's complete rule over the proud. Sennacherib of Assyria raged against the LORD, and God answered, "I will put My hook in your nose And My bridle in your lips" (2 Kings 19:28; Isaiah 37:29). The proud emperor would be led home like a captive animal. Job uses the same image for creatures no human can master. God asks, "Can you put a reed through his nose?" (Job 41:2; see 40:24). What no man can tame, God leads wherever He wishes.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Breath of life and blast of judgment (Genesis 2:7; 7:22; Exodus 15:8). The first use of ʼaph is intimate. God formed man from dust "and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Genesis 2:7). The nostrils are where human life begins. Five chapters later, after humanity filled the earth with corruption, the same word marks the end of that life in the flood: "All in whose nostrils was the breath of the spirit of life... died" (Genesis 7:22).

God's own nostrils then appear in poetry. At the Red Sea, "with the blast of Your nostrils The waters were gathered together" (Exodus 15:8). In Psalm 18, "Smoke went up from His nostrils" as God came down to rescue David (Psalm 18:8; 2 Samuel 22:9). Eliphaz says the wicked perish "by the breath of His anger" (Job 4:9). The God who gives breath also commands breath, both for rescue and for judgment. Isaiah draws the conclusion: stop trusting man, "Whose breath is in his nostrils; For of what account is he?" (Isaiah 2:22). Human life rests on borrowed breath. Job confessed the same thing when he spoke of "the breath of God in my nostrils" (Job 27:3).

The long anger of God (Exodus 32–34). In the golden calf episode, ʼaph appears again and again. God says, "let Me alone, that My wrath may burn hot against them" (Exodus 32:10). Moses refuses to step aside. He pleads, "Turn from Your fierce wrath, and relent from this harm to Your people" (Exodus 32:12), and he appeals to God's promises and His reputation among the nations. Later in the same chapter, Moses' own anger burns and he shatters the tablets (Exodus 32:19). The anger of God and the anger of man appear side by side.

When God renews the tablets, He passes before Moses and proclaims His name: "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). The word "longsuffering" contains ʼaph. God names Himself long of anger in the same story where His anger had blazed, and He does so for the same people who provoked it. His patience is the restraint of a holy God who could judge at once and chooses to wait.

Israel never forgot those words. At Kadesh, after the people refused to enter the land, Moses prayed them back to God: "The LORD is longsuffering and abundant in mercy" (Numbers 14:18). The same verse adds that "He by no means clears the guilty." Jonah quoted it bitterly, knowing God was "slow to anger and abundant in lovingkindness" and would spare Nineveh (Jonah 4:2). Later Nahum announced Nineveh's fall with the same words in a new direction: "The LORD is slow to anger and great in power, And will not at all acquit the wicked" (Nahum 1:3). God's patience gives time to repent, but it does not erase justice. Jeremiah even leaned on that patience for himself: "In Your enduring patience, do not take me away" (Jeremiah 15:15).

Intercession that turns away anger (Exodus 32; Numbers 11). Moses' intercession sets a pattern. When God's anger burned and fire broke out in the camp at Taberah, the people cried to Moses, Moses prayed, and the fire died down (Numbers 11:1–2). Earlier, at Sinai, Moses had pleaded for the people after the golden calf (Exodus 32). The psalmist recalls that intercession at Horeb: Moses "stood before Him in the breach, To turn away His wrath" (Psalm 106:19–23; there the Hebrew uses a different word for wrath). An intercessor does not claim the people are innocent. He appeals to God's own character and promises, and God listens.

Human anger in Proverbs. Wisdom literature holds up God's patience as the pattern for people. "A quick-tempered man acts foolishly" (Proverbs 14:17). One who is slow to anger has great understanding (Proverbs 14:29). A soft answer turns away wrath (Proverbs 15:1), and the one slow to anger is better than the mighty, and ruling one's spirit is better than taking a city (Proverbs 16:32). Proverbs warns not to befriend an angry man (Proverbs 22:24), because an angry man stirs up strife and abounds in transgression (Proverbs 29:22). Patience also has power: "By long forbearance a ruler is persuaded, And a gentle tongue breaks a bone" (Proverbs 25:15). Human anger, left unruled, destroys. Human patience reflects God and wins hearts.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of ʼaph moves from breath given, to anger provoked, to anger restrained, and finally to anger turned away. Each stage points toward Jesus.

Moses stood in the breach and pleaded on the basis of God's promises. Jesus intercedes permanently, for "He always lives to make intercession" (Hebrews 7:25). Paul writes that believers are saved from wrath through Him (Romans 5:9). Unlike Moses, Christ intercedes as the one who has borne the judgment Himself. The longsuffering God proclaimed at Sinai is the same Lord Peter describes, who "is longsuffering toward us, not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). The delay of the final judgment shows the patience proclaimed in Exodus 34:6 still at work in history.

The anger itself does not disappear from the Christ story. Psalm 2 warns the nations to "kiss the Son," lest He be angry and His wrath be kindled (Psalm 2:12). The anointed King carries the ʼaph of God against rebellion. Isaiah's warrior treads the winepress in His anger (Isaiah 63:3, 6), an image Revelation applies to the returning Christ (Revelation 19:15). The Lamb who offers mercy is also the Judge, which is why His patience is so urgent.

There is also a fitting echo of Genesis 2:7. After His resurrection, Jesus breathed on His disciples and said, "Receive the Holy Spirit" (John 20:22). John does not call this a fulfillment of Genesis. Even so, the God who first breathed into human nostrils now breathes new life through His risen Son. In Gethsemane, Jesus fell on His face to pray (Matthew 26:39). He took the posture that ʼaph so often describes, yielding to the Father and going to the cross on our behalf.

7. Spirit & Life

Every breath is a gift. The nostrils God filled in Eden are the same nostrils that draw air today, and Isaiah's question, "of what account is he?", strips away any illusion of self-sufficiency. This is a source of humility and of comfort. The God who holds our breath is the same God who calls Himself slow to anger.

The bowed face expresses the right response. In Hebrew the word for anger and the word for a face pressed to the ground are the same word. When Ezra opened the Book of the Law and blessed the LORD, the people bowed with their faces to the ground and worshiped (Nehemiah 8:6). The proud do the opposite. Psalm 10:4 describes the wicked man who, in his proud countenance (literally the height of his nose), does not seek God. A believer can bow the face before God's majesty or lift it against Him. Scripture invites the first.

God's patience is meant to change how we treat others. The God who could have consumed Israel at Sinai chose to wait for them, and Proverbs commends being long of nose toward others. That means breathing slowly before speaking, giving a soft answer, and ruling one's own spirit. Holding back anger in this way is not weakness. Proverbs says it is better than taking a city (Proverbs 16:32).

There is also a quieter image. Moses' blessing on the Levites says they would put incense before the LORD (Deuteronomy 33:10), literally in Your nostrils. The nostrils that blaze against idolatry also receive the fragrance of worship. Smoke from God's nostrils signals judgment, while incense rising to Him signals communion. Through Christ, the life of a believer can rise to God as a pleasing aroma.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 1:5 — Elkanah gave Hannah "a double portion," an idiom built on the dual form, two faces. Her husband honored her with a generous gift even while she grieved her empty womb.

  • Numbers 11:20 — God promised meat "until it comes out of your nostrils and becomes loathsome to you." Israel's craving would turn to disgust because they had "despised the LORD who is among you."

  • Psalm 115:6 — Of idols: "Noses they have, but they do not smell." The carved nose only exposes how lifeless the idol is, in contrast to the living God who breathes life and receives worship.

  • Ezekiel 8:17 — God shows the prophet idolaters in the temple: "Indeed they put the branch to their nose." The exact practice is uncertain, but the context makes clear that it was idolatrous worship that provoked God's anger.

  • Lamentations 4:20 — The captured king is called "the breath of our nostrils, the anointed of the LORD." Judah's life depended on its anointed king, and his fall felt like losing breath itself.

  • Hosea 11:9 — God declares that He will not carry out the fierceness of His anger, because He is God and not man. His holiness shows itself in mercy toward a wayward people.
