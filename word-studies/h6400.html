MILLSTONE, PIECE - (pelach, Strong's H6400)

Summary

The Hebrew word pelach means a piece or slice of something larger. Scripture uses it for two very different things: the heavy stones of a household hand-mill, and a slice of fruit or fig cake. It appears only six times, yet those few uses move from violent judgment to tender mercy to the language of love.

A piece of millstone dropped from a tower crushes the skull of Abimelech, a king who murdered his brothers to take power, before his armorbearer finishes him at his command. In Job, God uses the "lower millstone" to picture the hard heart of Leviathan, a creature no one can tame. A piece of fig cake, given with bread, water and raisins, is part of the food that revives a sick and starving Egyptian slave, who then leads David to recover everything the Amalekites had stolen. In the Song of Solomon, a slice of pomegranate describes the bride's beauty behind her veil. Ordinary household objects carry heavy meaning here. God brings down the proud with a kitchen tool, restores the discarded with a few bites of food, and, in Ezekiel's words, replaces a heart of stone with a heart of flesh.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pelach is a Hebrew noun usually linked to a verb meaning to cleave or slice. The connection fits how the word is used, because every pelach in Scripture is a portion of something: one stone of a pair, or a cut from a fruit or a pressed cake.

An Israelite hand-mill had two stones. A heavy, flat lower stone stayed fixed. A smaller upper stone was moved back and forth or turned on top of it to grind grain. Grinding was daily work, usually done by women, and families depended on it for bread. Deuteronomy 24:6 reflects how necessary the mill was: "No man shall take the lower or the upper millstone in pledge, for he takes one's living in pledge." Taking a family's millstone left them unable to make bread.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A millstone, one of the two stones of a hand-mill. This can be the upper stone (Judges 9:53) or the fixed lower stone (Job 41:24).

2. A piece or slice of food, such as a portion of a cake of figs (1 Samuel 30:12) or a cut of pomegranate (Song of Solomon 4:3; 6:7).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears in historical narrative, in Job, and in the poetry of the Song of Solomon. Its meaning comes from what it is a piece of. In Judges and 2 Samuel it is a stone heavy enough to kill. In Job it is the bedstone that never moves. In 1 Samuel and the Song it is something soft, sweet, and life-giving. These are among the hardest and the softest things in an Israelite home, and the same small word covers both.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God who works through ordinary things. No angel or army ends Abimelech's reign. Judges credits his death to God: "Thus God repaid the wickedness of Abimelech, which he had done to his father by killing his seventy brothers" (Judges 9:56). Paul describes a similar pattern: "God has chosen the weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty" (1 Corinthians 1:27).

The millstone also helps us think about the human heart. Job uses the lower millstone for Leviathan's heart, not a human one, but the comparison is easy to draw, because Scripture often speaks of hard human hearts. A tool made for daily bread becomes a picture of hardness. The passage that directly teaches God's answer to that hardness is Ezekiel's promise of new hearts: "I will take the heart of stone out of your flesh and give you a heart of flesh" (Ezekiel 36:26).

The two narrative passages also face each other. In Judges a piece of stone is thrown down from a height to crush a proud man. In 1 Samuel a piece of fig cake is among the food given to a starving man found in a field, and his strength comes back. Scripture does not present this as a formal code, but the contrast is real. The same God who brings down the proud also lifts up the abandoned.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Judges 9:53–56: A tyrant brought down by a kitchen stone. Abimelech, son of Gideon, made himself king by killing seventy of his brothers "on one stone" (Judges 9:5). Years later he attacked the tower of Thebez, where the people had fled. As he came near the door, "a certain woman dropped an upper millstone on Abimelech's head and crushed his skull" (Judges 9:53). He had held the high ground of power, and now judgment fell on him from a height. The man who killed his brothers on one stone was struck down by a stone. His dying words show his concern for his reputation: "Draw your sword and kill me, lest men say of me, 'A woman killed him'" (Judges 9:54). So his armorbearer thrust him through, and he died. He could not escape that shame. Generations later Joab expected King David to remember it (2 Samuel 11:21). The woman is never named, and she needed no weapon. Her household tool was enough to carry out God's judgment.

Job 41:24: The heart like a lower millstone. God's description of Leviathan in Job 41 is meant to show Job how far this creature lies beyond human control, and therefore how much greater its Maker is. One line reads, "His heart is as hard as stone, Even as hard as the lower millstone." The lower stone was chosen for hardness and weight because it had to stay still while grain was crushed against it. Leviathan's heart cannot be softened, frightened, or bargained with. Job does not apply the image to people, but it invites a comparison with human hearts, which Scripture elsewhere describes as hard. Jesus was "grieved by the hardness of their hearts" (Mark 3:5) in men who watched Him closely to see whether He would heal a man with a withered hand on the Sabbath, "so that they might accuse Him" (Mark 3:2).

1 Samuel 30:11–19: A piece of fig cake and the captives recovered at Ziklag. David came back to Ziklag and found it burned and every family taken captive. His own men "spoke of stoning him," and "David strengthened himself in the LORD his God" (1 Samuel 30:6). God told him, "Pursue, for you shall surely overtake them and without fail recover all" (1 Samuel 30:8). God fulfilled that promise through a sick and starving Egyptian found in a field. His Amalekite master had left him behind because he fell sick. They gave him bread and water (1 Samuel 30:11). "And they gave him a piece of a cake of figs and two clusters of raisins. So when he had eaten, his strength came back to him; for he had eaten no bread nor drunk water for three days and three nights" (1 Samuel 30:12). Once revived, he led David to the raiders' camp, and David recovered the captives and everything that had been taken: "nothing of theirs was lacking, either small or great, sons or daughters, spoil or anything which they had taken from them; David recovered all" (1 Samuel 30:16–19). The Amalekites threw this man away. Mercy shown to him became the way God kept His promise to David.

Song of Solomon 4:3: The pomegranate behind the veil. The bridegroom tells his bride, "Your temples behind your veil Are like a piece of pomegranate," and he repeats the line in Song of Solomon 6:7. A cut pomegranate opens to bright red, packed with seeds and juice. Love poetry like this works less by exact visual likeness than by the qualities an image calls to mind: color, sweetness, richness, and value. The veil covers her, yet her beloved knows what lies beneath it, and he treasures it. Many Christian readers have heard in the Song a reflection of Christ and His people, and the image carries that reading well. Her hidden life is full, alive, and precious to the one who loves her.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The millstone image continues into the New Testament under different words. Jesus warns that whoever causes "one of these little ones who believe in Me" to sin would be better off "if a millstone were hung around his neck, and he were drowned in the depth of the sea" (Matthew 18:6). At the end of the story, "a mighty angel took up a stone like a great millstone and threw it into the sea, saying, 'Thus with violence the great city Babylon shall be thrown down'" (Revelation 18:21). The kind of stone that fell on Abimelech appears again in these pictures of judgment on those who harm the weak and on proud Babylon.

The fig cake points in the other direction. On the night He was betrayed, the Lord Jesus "took bread; and when He had given thanks, He broke it and said, 'Take, eat; this is My body which is broken for you'" (1 Corinthians 11:23–24). Pelach is never used for the Lord's Supper, so this connection is an analogy rather than a direct link. It is still a fitting one. The abandoned Egyptian revived after David's men fed him, and a piece of fig cake was part of that meal. Sinners live because Christ gave His body, broken for them. He is the one Ezekiel's promise looks toward. Through His Spirit, hearts like the lower millstone become hearts of flesh.

7. Spirit & Life

The Egyptian slave asks something of every believer. David's men were grieving and worn out, and they had every reason to hurry past a sick and starving stranger who belonged to the enemy. They stopped and fed him. God had already promised victory, and He brought it through their kindness. We usually cannot tell which small kindness God will use, and that should make us slower to decide that anyone is not worth our time.

Placed side by side, Job and the Song offer a comparison that helps us think about our own hearts. Job's lower millstone describes the hard heart of Leviathan, and the Song's slice of pomegranate describes the bride's temples behind her veil. Neither passage speaks directly about a person's heart toward God, but together they picture the difference between a heart that is hard and one that is open and full of life. A believer can grow hard slowly, through bitterness, pride, or the long habit of resisting God. The remedy is a gift, and Ezekiel 36:26 states it plainly: God takes out the heart of stone and gives a heart of flesh. The Song adds a picture of being treasured, for the bridegroom sees beneath the veil a life he calls lovely, and believers can hear in that a reflection of how Christ regards His people. Abimelech's end also stands as a warning to anyone who trusts in position and power. One stone from an unnamed woman's hand was enough to bring him down.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

2 Samuel 11:21. Joab expects that King David may be angry about soldiers lost near the wall at Rabbah, and that he may recall Abimelech's death by asking, "Who struck Abimelech the son of Jerubbesheth? Was it not a woman who cast a piece of a millstone on him from the wall, so that he died in Thebez?" Joab has the messenger ready to answer with the news that Uriah is dead. The story of a proud man's downfall is called to mind in the middle of the plot to kill Uriah and hide David's sin, though the text does not say whether Joab saw the irony.

Song of Solomon 6:7. The bridegroom repeats the pomegranate line from Song of Solomon 4:3. His praise has not changed, even after the strain between the lovers in chapter 5.
