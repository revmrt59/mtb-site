PHILISTINE, PHILISTINES, PHILISTIA - (Pᵉlishtîy, Strong's H6430)

Summary

Pᵉlishtîy is the Hebrew word for a Philistine, a member of the people who lived along the southern coast of Canaan in cities such as Ashdod, Ashkelon, Ekron, and Gath. The NKJV usually renders it "Philistines" for the nation, "the Philistine" for an individual such as Goliath, and occasionally "Philistia" for their land. The word names a people. It does not mean "enemy" or "wicked." Still, no nation pressed harder against Israel from the days of the judges to the reign of David, and the word gathers most of its weight in Judges, 1 and 2 Samuel, and the parallel accounts in Chronicles.

Through the Philistines, Scripture shows how God deals with His people and with the nations. He handed unfaithful Israel over to them as discipline. He humbled their god Dagon without the help of a single Israelite soldier. He brought down their champion through a shepherd who came in the LORD's name, and He broke their power through a king who inquired of Him before battle, as David did twice against them in the Valley of Rephaim (2 Samuel 5:19, 23). The prophets announce their judgment. Then Zechariah, in the same breath, promises that a remnant of the Philistines will belong to Israel's God. The people who stood for everything outside God's covenant community are finally drawn inside it, and that turn points directly to the gospel.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pᵉlishtîy is a gentilic noun, a word that identifies someone by people or place, much as "Egyptian" or "Moabite" does. Genesis 10:14 places the Philistines in the table of nations, and Amos 9:7 says God brought them from Caphtor. In the patriarchal stories they live around Gerar (Genesis 21 and 26). By the time of the judges they hold a strong coastal territory and repeatedly push inland into the hill country of Judah and Benjamin.

The word turns up most often where Israel's survival as a nation hangs in the balance. Most occurrences fall in Judges and 1 Samuel, with many more in 2 Samuel and 1 Chronicles, and a smaller group in the prophets.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one meaning, "Philistine," and context determines how it is applied:

1. A member of the Philistine people, most often plural for the nation or its army ("the Philistines encamped in Aphek," 1 Samuel 4:1), and sometimes singular for one person, as with Goliath in 1 Samuel 17.

2. By extension, the Philistine territory, rendered "Philistia" in a few places (Exodus 23:31; Obadiah 19), or appearing in phrases like "land of the Philistines" (Zephaniah 2:5).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Genesis the Philistines are neighbors who make treaties and quarrel over wells. In Judges they rule Israel. In Samuel they are the enemy on every frontier. In the prophets they are one of the nations under God's judgment, and in Zechariah they are among the nations to whom He shows mercy.

Two patterns deserve notice. The first is the pairing with "uncircumcised." Samson's parents ask why he must take a wife "from the uncircumcised Philistines" (Judges 14:3), and David uses the same description of Goliath (1 Samuel 17:26, 36). Circumcision marked Abraham's family as belonging to God, so the word set the Philistines outside Israel's covenant community.

The second is the narrator's way of referring to Goliath. In 1 Samuel 17 he is named only twice, and nearly every other time he is simply "the Philistine." The story is less interested in the man's name than in what he stands for: an outsider to God's covenant people defying the God who made promises to them.

4. Theological Significance

The Philistines first rise to power as an instrument of God's discipline. Judges 10:6 lists the gods Israel turned to, and the list ends with "the gods of the Philistines; and they forsook the LORD and did not serve Him." The next verse gives the consequence: "He sold them into the hands of the Philistines" (Judges 10:7). Israel chose the Philistines' gods and ended up under the Philistines' rule. God gave His people the masters that matched their worship. His purpose was to bring them back. When Israel cried out and put away their foreign gods, Scripture says God could no longer bear Israel's misery (Judges 10:10–16). Samson's story begins under the same discipline (Judges 13:1). Even then God was at work, because Samson's marriage plans came "of the LORD," who was seeking an occasion to move against the Philistines (Judges 14:4, paraphrased). Discipline did not mean God had let His people go.

The same God governs the Philistines' history too. Amos 9:7 sets Israel's exodus from Egypt beside the Philistines' migration from Caphtor. Israel could not treat God as a private possession, and the Philistines could not treat their coastland as beyond His reach.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The Ark among the Philistines (1 Samuel 4–6). After a defeat, Israel's elders decided to bring the ark of the covenant into battle so that "it" might save them (1 Samuel 4:3, paraphrased). They treated God's presence like a charm to carry into war. God let Israel be routed and the ark taken. The Philistines set it beside Dagon in Ashdod as a trophy, and the next morning Dagon lay face down before it. The following morning the idol was broken. Plagues spread from city to city until the Philistine lords sent the ark back with a guilt offering (1 Samuel 5–6). No Israelite fought in any of this. God refused to be used by His own people and refused to be mocked by His enemies. His holiness did not depend on Israel's army.

"This uncircumcised Philistine" (1 Samuel 17). For forty days Goliath defied Israel's ranks, and Saul's army was terrified (17:11, 16). David saw something they had missed. His question was why an uncircumcised Philistine should be allowed to defy the armies of the living God (17:26, paraphrased). Goliath stood outside the covenant community of Abraham's family and was taunting the God who keeps His promises to it. David went out against him in the name of the LORD of hosts (17:45), and the battle turned on whose God was real. Jonathan had already shown the same faith when he said of a Philistine garrison, "nothing restrains the LORD from saving by many or by few" (1 Samuel 14:6).

The LORD breaks through (2 Samuel 5:17–25). As soon as David was anointed king over all Israel, "all the Philistines went up to search for David" (5:17). Saul had faced the Philistines by forcing an offering instead of waiting, and Samuel said he had not kept the LORD's commandment (1 Samuel 13:11–14), and he died confessing that the Philistines were warring against him while God had departed from him (28:15). David instead "inquired of the LORD" (5:19). After the victory he said, "The LORD has broken through my enemies before me, like a breakthrough of water" (5:20). The Philistines also "left their images there, and David and his men carried them away" (5:21). The people who once carried off God's ark now lost their own gods to Israel's king. When the Philistines came back, David asked again, and God gave him a different plan: wait for "the sound of marching in the tops of the mulberry trees," because "then the LORD will go out before you" (5:24). Yesterday's answer was not enough for today's battle.

From doom to belonging (Zechariah 9:6–7). The prophets speak hard words about the Philistines. Amos declares, "the remnant of the Philistines shall perish" (Amos 1:8). Zephaniah says, "I will destroy you; So there shall be no inhabitant" (Zephaniah 2:5). Zechariah begins the same way: "I will cut off the pride of the Philistines" (9:6). Then the oracle changes direction: "I will take away the blood from his mouth, And the abominations from between his teeth. But he who remains, even he shall be for our God, And shall be like a leader in Judah, And Ekron like a Jebusite" (9:7). The blood and abominations refer to food that God's law forbade and to the idolatrous sacrifices that went with it. God Himself removes them. The survivor is given a place of honor in Judah. Ekron becomes like the Jebusites, the former inhabitants of Jerusalem who were absorbed into David's city. The prophecies about the same people move in sharply contrasting directions: Amos announces that the remnant shall perish, and Zechariah announces a survivor who belongs to God. Scripture does not say these are the very same persons or supply the sequence between them, but Zechariah's oracle shows judgment purging and then God claiming what remains.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Philistine story keeps returning to an anointed king. The Philistines move against David the moment he is anointed. Victory comes when God goes before His king, and the enemy's gods are carried away. Jesus, the Son of David and the Anointed One, faces greater enemies than any Philistine army. His triumph over sin and death is the breakthrough that David's battles foreshadowed.

Zechariah ties this together directly. Only two verses after the promise about the Philistine remnant comes the announcement of Zion's King, coming lowly and riding on a donkey (Zechariah 9:9). Matthew 21:5 and John 12:15 apply that announcement to Jesus' entry into Jerusalem. The King and the ingathering of former enemies belong to the same oracle. In Christ, Gentiles who were once strangers to the promises are brought near by His blood (Ephesians 2:12–13), and believers receive a circumcision made without hands (Colossians 2:11). The "uncircumcised Philistine" was the picture of the outsider. In the gospel, outsiders become family.

7. Spirit & Life

Judges 10 gives a sobering mirror. The gods we choose bring the bondage that comes with them. When God lets His people feel that bondage, He is calling them home, and the moment they put away their idols, His heart is already moved toward them.

The ark narrative frees us from a false burden. God does not need us to protect His glory, and He will not be carried into our battles as a guarantee of success. He is looking for obedience. He can deal with Dagon alone.

David's two inquiries in 2 Samuel 5 speak to anyone tempted to repeat what worked last time. The same enemy came back to the same valley, and God gave a different instruction. Walking with God means asking again and listening for the sound in the mulberry trees.

Zechariah 9:7 shows what God wants in the end. He wants more than His enemies' defeat. He wants them cleansed and given a place at His table. Goliath's people, given a seat among the leaders of Judah, are a picture of the grace that reached each of us while we were still outside.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 26:15, 18 — The Philistines stopped up the wells Abraham had dug, and Isaac reopened them and gave them their old names. He reclaimed his inheritance patiently, without going to war.

  • Exodus 13:17 — God did not lead Israel by the shorter road through Philistine land, so that they would not see war and turn back to Egypt. He paced His people's battles to their strength.

  • Psalm 56 (title) — The psalm is set when the Philistines seized David in Gath. The same David who had killed Gath's champion learned to say "I will not fear" while in Philistine hands.

  • Isaiah 2:6 — Isaiah charges Israel with practicing soothsaying "like the Philistines." The danger was becoming like the Philistines more than being conquered by them.

  • Ezekiel 16:27 — Even the daughters of the Philistines are pictured as ashamed of Jerusalem's lewd conduct. When God's people sin, even the nations around them can see it.
