LEST, SO THAT NOT, FOR FEAR THAT - (pên, Strong's H6435)

Summary

Pên introduces an outcome that someone wants to prevent. It usually follows a command, a warning, a decision, or a prayer, and then names the danger that the command, warning, decision, or prayer is meant to avoid. The NKJV most often renders it "lest." After words like "beware" or "swear," it often becomes a simple "not," and in Genesis 31:31 it appears as "Perhaps," voicing Jacob's fear. Wherever it appears, pên tells the reader why something must be done or avoided. Noticing it helps us see that many biblical commands carry their reasons with them. Each warning points to a real harm the speaker can see coming.

Full Study

1. Role & Function

Pên links two parts of a thought. The first part states an action, command, or plea. The second part, introduced by pên, names the result to be avoided.

Exodus 23:33 shows how this works. "They shall not dwell in your land" forbids an action with Hebrew's ordinary negative. Pên then supplies the reason: "lest they make you sin against Me." The first clause draws a boundary, and the pên clause explains what the boundary protects.

Because pên looks ahead to a feared result, it often carries a tone of urgency, caution, or anxiety. The tone depends on who is speaking and what is at stake.

2. Primary Ways It Is Used

Warning attached to a command (usually "lest")

This is the most familiar pattern. A command or prohibition is followed by the consequence it guards against.

  • Genesis 3:3. Eve tells the serpent what God said about the tree: "You shall not eat it, nor shall you touch it, lest you die." The prohibition and its danger stand together. The reason is part of the command itself.

  • Proverbs 5:10. A father warns his son away from the immoral woman. He then names what such a life would cost: "Lest aliens be filled with your wealth, And your labors go to the house of a foreigner." The warning is concrete. A man's hard-earned livelihood ends up in strangers' hands.

Content of a caution or oath (often "not")

After a verb of guarding or swearing, pên states what must not happen. English smooths this into "do not" or "will not."

  • Genesis 24:6. Abraham tells his servant, "Beware that you do not take my son back there." The servant is told to guard against a specific outcome: Isaac returning to the land God called Abraham to leave.

  • Judges 15:12. Samson tells the men of Judah, "Swear to me that you will not kill me yourselves." The oath is aimed at one feared result.

  • A similar pattern appears in 1 Samuel 4:9. There the Philistines urge one another to be strong so that they do not end up serving the Hebrews (paraphrase).

Expressing personal fear (often "lest" or "perhaps")

Pên can voice what a person dreads. It often explains why that person acted as he did.

  • Genesis 31:31. Jacob explains his secret departure to Laban: "Because I was afraid, for I said, 'Perhaps you would take your daughters from me by force.'"

  • Genesis 42:4. Jacob keeps Benjamin home "for he said, 'Lest some calamity befall him.'" Having lost Joseph, Jacob will not risk Rachel's other son.

  • Genesis 26:9 belongs here as well. Isaac explains that he called Rebekah his sister because he feared he might die on her account (paraphrase).

Naming the danger in prayer

Sometimes the person praying sets the feared outcome before God as part of the plea.

  • Psalm 28:1. David asks the LORD not to be silent toward him, for if He is, David will become like those who go down to the pit (paraphrase).

  • Jeremiah 10:24. Jeremiah asks God to correct him with justice rather than in anger, so that he is not brought to nothing (paraphrase).

In both prayers, pên lays the danger honestly before God. The one praying is saying, in effect, "This is what I fear, and only You can prevent it."

3. Practical Reading Insight

Pên shows that God's commands and His servants' warnings are not arbitrary. When Exodus 23:33 forbids the Canaanites from staying in the land, it says plainly what is at risk: Israel's own heart being drawn into sin. When Proverbs 5 warns against adultery, it names the losses. When we notice the "lest," we are reading the reason the speaker himself gives, so we do not have to guess at one.

Isaiah 6:10 is the hardest use of the word. The prophet is told to make the people's heart dull, "Lest they see with their eyes... And return and be healed." The grammar expresses a purpose of prevention. Readers have long wrestled with what that means. Some see God's active judgment on the people. Others stress that the message confirms a hardness the people had already chosen, since in 6:9 they keep hearing without understanding. Jesus applies this passage to those who heard His parables without receiving them (Matthew 13:14–15). The passage should be read soberly rather than softened. A people who persistently refuse God's word can come under a judgment in which hearing it no longer leads them to repentance.

4. Reading Guidance

"Lest" is not a prediction that the feared thing will certainly happen. In Genesis 31:31, the NKJV's "Perhaps" shows that pên can express a dreaded possibility rather than a certainty.

Not every "not" in an English verse comes from pên. In Exodus 23:33, "shall not dwell" and "lest" represent different Hebrew words. Look at the whole sentence to see which clause states the command and which states the danger.

Finally, Isaiah 6:10 should not be lifted out of its chapter to build a full doctrine of hardening from one conjunction.
