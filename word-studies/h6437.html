TURN, LOOK, REGARD, PREPARE - (pânâh, Strong's H6437)

Summary

The Hebrew verb pânâh means to turn: to face a direction, turn toward someone, turn away from something, or turn obstacles aside. The word is closely related to the Hebrew word for "face," so the verb describes directing your face somewhere. For this reason the NKJV can render it "turn," "look," "regard," "respect," "face," and even "prepare." Moses looks this way and that before striking the Egyptian. Ezekiel's temple gate "faces toward the east." Isaiah's herald cries, "Prepare the way of the LORD."

The word carries much of the Old Testament's teaching about God and His people. When God turns toward His people, they receive favor, mercy, and life. When God says Israel "turned to other gods," He also says He will hide His face. Isaiah sums up the human condition with this verb: "We have turned, every one, to his own way" (Isaiah 53:6). Isaiah also uses it for the remedy: "Look to Me, and be saved" (Isaiah 45:22). The same verb describes clearing the road for the Lord's coming, a call the Gospels see fulfilled in John the Baptist's ministry before Jesus. Everyone is facing something. The question this word presses is whether that face is turned toward God.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pânâh is a common Hebrew verb found throughout the Old Testament, from Genesis to Malachi. It belongs to the same word family as panim, the ordinary Hebrew word for "face" and, by extension, "presence." That link explains why one verb can describe both a change of direction and a change of attention. A person who turns toward something sets his face toward it.

The verb appears in very ordinary settings, such as travelers changing route, armies retreating, and the layout of buildings. It also appears in Israel's prayers, in God's warnings about idolatry, and in the prophets' announcements of salvation. Ezekiel uses it repeatedly in his temple vision to describe which way gates and structures face.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To turn or face a direction. This is the physical sense of turning around, setting out, or facing a direction. In Exodus 2:12 Moses looks this way and that before he acts, and in Ezekiel 43:1 the temple gate "faces toward the east." The word can also describe the "turning" of the day, as in "evening" (Genesis 24:63) and "dawning" (Judges 19:26).

2. To turn toward with attention: to look to, regard, or heed. Here turning means paying attention to someone, relying on them, or showing them favor. Israel must not "turn to idols" (Leviticus 19:4). The psalmist prays, "Turn Yourself to me, and have mercy on me" (Psalm 25:16).

3. To turn away or turn back. In this sense the word means departing, or fleeing in battle. Israel "turned their backs before their enemies" (Joshua 7:12).

4. To clear away, empty, or prepare. This sense means turning things out of the way so a space or road is open. Before the priest goes in to examine a house for disease, he commands the people to "empty the house," so that its contents will not be counted unclean along with it (Leviticus 14:36). The herald cries, "Prepare the way of the LORD" (Isaiah 40:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The verb's meaning depends heavily on who is turning and toward what. When ordinary people turn, the word usually just reports movement. When Israel turns toward idols or mediums (Leviticus 19:31; 20:6), the verb describes misplaced trust and allegiance. When God is the subject, His turning means favor and help. In Leviticus 26:9, the NKJV renders the verb "I will look on you favorably," and the promise of fruitfulness follows from that turning.

The direction of the turn also matters. Turning the back pictures retreat and defeat. Turning the face pictures attention and relationship.

The sense "prepare" (sense 4) comes from a particular form of the verb that means to make clear by removing what is in the way. Leviticus 14:36 shows this plainly: the house is cleared before the priest goes in to examine it, "that all that is in the house may not be made unclean." When Isaiah, and later Malachi, tells people to "prepare the way," the picture is road-clearing. Rubble and obstacles are turned aside so a traveler can come through.

4. Theological Significance

This verb shows that the relationship between God and His people can be described in terms of which way each is facing. God's grace appears as God turning toward people. In Leviticus 26:9, His favorable look comes first, and fruitfulness and confirmed promises follow. In Ezekiel 36:9, He speaks to a ruined land: "For indeed I am for you, and I will turn to you, and you shall be tilled and sown." Desolate ground becomes farmland again because God has turned toward it. This is why the psalmists pray for God to turn (Psalm 25:16; 69:16; 86:16). They are not asking Him to notice something He has overlooked. They are asking Him to come near and act in mercy. The priestly blessing expresses the same hope with the noun "face": "The LORD make His face shine upon you" (Numbers 6:25).

Sin is described with the same verb, turned in the wrong direction. God foresaw Israel's unfaithfulness and named it: "I will surely hide My face in that day because of all the evil which they have done, in that they have turned to other gods" (Deuteronomy 31:18). Israel's turning away is answered by God's hidden face. Hosea extends the picture to a broken marriage. The Lord still loves Israel, the children "who look to other gods" (Hosea 3:1).

The consequences are visible in history. After Achan's sin, Israel "turned their backs before their enemies" (Joshua 7:12), and God added, "Neither will I be with you anymore." Not every retreat in Scripture is a judgment; Judges 20:42 simply describes a military rout. In Joshua 7, however, the backs turned toward Israel's enemies reveal that God had withdrawn His protection.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

2 Kings 13:23 — Grace in a dark time. This verse comes from the decline of the northern kingdom under kings who did evil. Even so, "the LORD was gracious to them, had compassion on them, and regarded them, because of His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and would not yet destroy them or cast them from His presence." The verb "regarded" is pânâh. The word translated "presence" is a different but related word, panim, the noun for face. God turned toward a people who had turned from Him, and so they were not yet sent away from His face. The reason given is God's sworn promise to the patriarchs, not Israel's faithfulness. The grace described here is God's attention to His people, and that attention rests on His own faithfulness.

Isaiah 45:22 — The turn that saves. "Look to Me, and be saved, All you ends of the earth! For I am God, and there is no other." The NKJV's "look" translates this verb, so the invitation is to turn toward God. The surrounding chapter mocks idols that cannot save (Isaiah 45:20), which means turning to the Lord also means turning away from them. The offer reaches "all you ends of the earth." It asks for no journey, payment, or achievement. It asks for a face turned toward the only God who saves.

Isaiah 40:3 and Malachi 3:1 — Clearing the road for the King. "Prepare the way of the LORD; Make straight in the desert A highway for our God." Isaiah 57:14 and 62:10 repeat the command to prepare the way. In this sense the verb pictures turning stones and debris aside so the road lies open. Malachi brings the command to a person: "Behold, I send My messenger, And he will prepare the way before Me. And the Lord, whom you seek, Will suddenly come to His temple." The road is cleared because the Lord Himself is coming.

Jeremiah 2:27 and Ezekiel 43:1 — Back or face. Jeremiah reports God's charge that Israel turned its back to Him and not its face, while calling on wooden and stone idols in time of need. The image is pointed: Israel faced its idols and showed God only its back. Ezekiel's vision gives the opposite picture. He is brought to "the gate that faces toward the east," and the glory of God then comes from the east and fills the house (Ezekiel 43:2–5). Later, healing waters flow out by the east-facing gate (Ezekiel 47:1–2). The restored sanctuary is built to face the direction from which God's glory returns. Solomon's bronze Sea, set on twelve oxen "looking" toward all four directions (1 Kings 7:25), is another sacred object whose orientation is deliberate. The exact meaning of that arrangement is not stated, but Ezekiel's temple clearly faces the coming glory.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah 53:6 traces both the human problem and God's answer with this verb: "All we like sheep have gone astray; We have turned, every one, to his own way; And the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all." Each person turning to his own way is the problem of the whole story, from idols in Leviticus to the backs turned in Jeremiah. God's answer is not simply to wait for people to turn around. He lays their turning-away on His Servant. The New Testament identifies that Servant as Jesus.

The other prophetic uses also lead to Christ. Mark opens his Gospel by joining Malachi and Isaiah: "Behold, I send My messenger before Your face, Who will prepare Your way before You," followed by "Prepare the way of the LORD" (Mark 1:2–3). John the Baptist clears the road, and the Lord who comes is Jesus. The road-clearing John called for was repentance: the removal of pride and sin so that people would be ready to receive the King.

Isaiah's "Look to Me, and be saved" finds a parallel in Jesus' own words. Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, and anyone bitten "when he looks at it, shall live" (Numbers 21:8). Jesus says the Son of Man must be lifted up in the same way, "that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have eternal life" (John 3:14–15). The serpent story uses a different Hebrew word for looking, but the pattern is the same: life comes by turning toward what God has provided. Hebrews describes the Christian life in the same terms: "looking unto Jesus, the author and finisher of our faith" (Hebrews 12:2).

7. Spirit & Life

Ecclesiastes shows a man turning from one pursuit to another. "Then I looked on all the works that my hands had done… and indeed all was vanity" (Ecclesiastes 2:11). In the next verse, "Then I turned myself to consider wisdom and madness and folly" (2:12). The Preacher turns from his projects to weigh wisdom against folly, and he does find that "wisdom excels folly As light excels darkness" (2:13). Yet he also sees that the wise man dies just as the fool does (2:14–16), so even wisdom cannot give him lasting rest under the sun. He uses the same verb Isaiah uses in his invitation. The passage does not tell us which way the Preacher's heart was facing, but it invites a comparison with our own lives: a person can keep turning from one pursuit to another without ever turning to the Lord. Restlessness can look like searching while the person is really circling.

The prayers of the Psalms give believers words for the opposite movement: "Turn Yourself to me, and have mercy on me, For I am desolate and afflicted" (Psalm 25:16). A person may ask God to turn toward him because God has already shown, in 2 Kings 13:23, that He turns toward those who do not deserve it. The believer's turn to God and God's turn to the believer belong together.

Ezekiel's east-facing gate offers a fitting analogy for a heart that belongs to God. Scripture calls believers God's temple (1 Corinthians 3:16). A temple is built to face the One who comes to fill it. Keeping that orientation includes regular road-clearing in the sense of Isaiah 40:3: turning aside the rubble of sin, distraction, and self-reliance so that the Lord has an open way in.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 32:15 — After interceding for Israel, Moses turned and went down from the mountain with the tablets. He turned from God's presence toward a rebellious people, carrying God's words to them.

  • Numbers 16:15 — Moses asks the Lord not to respect the offering of Korah's rebels. God's regard is not automatic for those who oppose Him.

  • Malachi 2:13 — God no longer regards the offerings of people who are unfaithful to their wives. Tears at the altar cannot replace faithfulness at home.

  • Psalm 102:17 — The Lord regards the prayer of the destitute. His attention goes to the lowly.

  • 2 Kings 5:12 — Naaman turned and went away in a rage when Elisha's instructions offended his pride. He nearly turned his back on his own healing.

  • Zephaniah 3:15 — The Lord has cast out Israel's enemy. Here the clearing away sense describes God removing what threatened His people.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6440 (panim, face, presence) — A lexical relative. Panim is the noun that stands behind the verb's idea of turning one's face. The two words appear together in 2 Kings 13:23 ("regarded… His presence") and are paired in Deuteronomy 31:18, where Israel turns and God hides His face.

  • H7725 (shuv, to turn back, return) — A distinct verb that is the Old Testament's main word for repentance and returning. It is a thematic companion to pânâh, not a lexical relative. Pânâh describes the turning of the face, while shuv describes the return itself.
