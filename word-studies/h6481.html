WOUND, WOUNDED, EMASCULATED - (pâtsaʻ, Strong's H6481)

Summary

The Hebrew verb pâtsaʻ means to wound, bruise, or crush. It describes a real blow that breaks or injures the body. The word appears only three times in the Old Testament, once in each major part of the Hebrew Bible. In Deuteronomy 23:1 it describes a man injured so that he cannot father children, and that injury keeps him out of "the assembly of the LORD." In 1 Kings 20:37 a prophet asks to be struck so that the wound can carry God's message to King Ahab. In Song of Solomon 5:7 a bride searching the streets at night for her beloved is beaten by the city's watchmen.

Taken together, these three wounds trace a path through much of Scripture. A wound can shut a person out. A wound can be accepted in obedience so that God's word is heard. And a wound can be suffered while seeking the one you love: the bride was beaten as she searched, yet the beating did not end her search and is no measure of her devotion. All three can be set beside the wounds of Jesus Christ. Isaiah foretold that the Servant would be "wounded for our transgressions" (Isaiah 53:5), and Peter applies that passage to the cross (1 Peter 2:24). The New Testament also tells of an Ethiopian eunuch, a foreigner whose circumstances recall Isaiah's promise to welcome eunuchs and foreigners into God's house. Philip preached Jesus to him from Isaiah's description of the suffering Servant (Acts 8).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pâtsaʻ is a Hebrew verb. A closely related noun, petsaʻ (H6482), means wound and appears in passages such as Isaiah 1:6 and Proverbs 27:6. The verb occurs once in the Law, once in the historical books of the Prophets, and once in the poetry of the Writings. Its three settings are very different: a rule about who may join Israel's worshiping assembly, a prophet's acted-out parable on a battlefield road, and a love poem. In each one the wound is physical, and in each one the injury carries meaning well beyond the pain.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one core sense: to wound or bruise by striking or crushing. It refers to bodily injury, not to hurt feelings. Context decides how serious and how lasting the wound is. In Deuteronomy 23:1 it is a permanent mutilation of the reproductive organs, which the NKJV renders "emasculated by crushing." In 1 Kings 20:37 it is a deliberate wound meant to be seen. In Song of Solomon 5:7 it is the result of a beating.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What changes across the three uses is the person who suffers the wound and why. In Deuteronomy the wounded man is identified by his condition, which the law treats as a lasting barrier. In 1 Kings the prophet chooses to be wounded and asks for it "by the word of the LORD" (1 Kings 20:35). In the Song the bride is wounded against her will by men whose duty was to guard the city. So the wound is a condition in the first passage, an obedient choice in the second, and suffering inflicted unjustly in the third.

4. Theological Significance

Deuteronomy 23:1 governed who could enter "the assembly of the LORD," Israel gathered as God's people. A different law, Leviticus 21:17–23, applied only to Aaron's descendants who served as priests. A priest with any "defect" could not "approach to offer the bread of his God" at the altar, yet he could still "eat the bread of his God, both the most holy and the holy" (Leviticus 21:22). The two rules are not the same, and neither one made bodily wholeness the condition for every Israelite to pray to God or belong to Him. Both rules did teach Israel, in visible ways, that the holy God is approached on His terms, and the prophets later named a deeper wound. Isaiah describes the whole nation as a wounded body: "From the sole of the foot even to the head, there is no soundness in it, but wounds and bruises and putrefying sores; they have not been closed or bound up" (Isaiah 1:6). Jeremiah hears the Lord say, "Your affliction is incurable, your wound is severe" (Jeremiah 30:12). The psalmist confesses, "My wounds are foul and festering because of my foolishness" (Psalm 38:5).

These verses use other Hebrew words, but they show where the theme leads. The real barrier between people and God is not a damaged body but the sin that wounds the whole nation. The question running through Scripture is how wounded people can be brought near. God's answer is a Wounded One who heals the wounds of others.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 23:1 — the wound that excluded. "He who is emasculated by crushing or mutilation shall not enter the assembly of the LORD." Such injuries were often associated with pagan religious practice or with forced service in foreign courts. The verses that follow also exclude certain foreigners (Deuteronomy 23:3). Isaiah 56 answers both exclusions directly. It tells the foreigner not to say "The LORD has utterly separated me from His people," and the eunuch not to say "Here I am, a dry tree." To eunuchs who hold fast to Him, God promises "in My house and within My walls a place and a name better than that of sons and daughters... an everlasting name that shall not be cut off" (Isaiah 56:3–5). A man with no descendants receives a name that lasts forever.

Acts 8 shows that promise coming to life. The Ethiopian official is both a foreigner and a eunuch, so his circumstances recall the two people Isaiah 56 addressed. Acts does not say how he became a eunuch or whether he had ever been turned away. It says that he "had come to Jerusalem to worship" (Acts 8:27) and was reading Isaiah, a few chapters before Isaiah 56: "He was led as a sheep to the slaughter" (Acts 8:32). Philip, "beginning at this Scripture, preached Jesus to him" (Acts 8:35), and the man was baptized. Isaiah had told the eunuch not to call himself "a dry tree," and here a eunuch is welcomed among God's people through faith in the Servant who was wounded for others.

1 Kings 20:35–43 — the wound that carried God's word. King Ahab had released Ben-Hadad, the enemy king God had appointed for destruction. A prophet told a companion by the word of the LORD to strike him. The companion refused. Scripture does not say why, but the prophet told him, "Because you have not obeyed the voice of the LORD, surely, as soon as you depart from me, a lion shall kill you," and it happened (1 Kings 20:36). That detail shows how seriously God took the command. A second man obeyed: "So the man struck him, inflicting a wound" (1 Kings 20:37). After he was wounded, the prophet waited for the king by the road and disguised himself with a bandage over his eyes (1 Kings 20:38). As the king passed, he cried out with a story: he had gone into the battle, a prisoner was put in his charge, and the man escaped. Ahab pronounced the sentence himself: "So shall your judgment be; you yourself have decided it" (1 Kings 20:40). Then the prophet took the bandage away from his eyes, and the king recognized him as one of the prophets (1 Kings 20:41). He delivered God's verdict: Ahab's life would go for the life of the man he had let go (1 Kings 20:42, paraphrased).

The wound gave the message its force. It probably helped his story of the battle seem believable, though the text names only the bandage as his disguise. Ahab judged the case first, and only then did the prophet reveal who he was and turn the verdict on the king. The man who obeyed by striking also illustrates a proverb: "Faithful are the wounds of a friend" (Proverbs 27:6).

Song of Solomon 5:7 — the wound suffered in seeking love. The bride had hesitated to open the door, and her beloved withdrew (Song of Solomon 5:2–6). She went out after him into the night: "The watchmen who went about the city found me. They struck me, they wounded me; the keepers of the walls took my veil away from me." Earlier, the watchmen had simply let her pass, and she found him (Song of Solomon 3:3–4). This time the men appointed to protect the city beat her and exposed her by taking her veil. Even so, she keeps searching and tells the daughters of Jerusalem that she is lovesick for him (Song of Solomon 5:8).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The theme of wounding begins in Genesis 3:15. The serpent will bruise the woman's Seed on the heel, and the Seed will bruise the serpent's head. Romans 16:20 carries that promise forward: "the God of peace will crush Satan under your feet shortly." From the first promise, victory over evil is linked with a wound to the Redeemer.

Isaiah 53 tells us what that wound would accomplish: "He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities... and by His stripes we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5). Others saw Him as "smitten by God" (Isaiah 53:4). Peter applies the passage directly to the cross: "by whose stripes you were healed" (1 Peter 2:24).

Each use of pâtsaʻ can be set beside the cross. These are comparisons, not prophecies; the stated fulfillment is Isaiah 53, which Peter applies to Christ. Like the prophet in 1 Kings, Jesus accepted His wounds in obedience to the Father's word. The two cases also differ in an important way. The prophet's wound announced a death sentence on a guilty king. Christ's wounds took the sentence upon Himself so that the guilty might live. The prophet's verdict was "your life shall go for his life," and at the cross Jesus gave His own life in place of ours. The bride was struck by the city's watchmen. Jesus, after He stood before the council, was struck as well: "they spat in His face and beat Him; and others struck Him with the palms of their hands" (Matthew 26:67; compare Mark 14:65). Because He was wounded, people like the man in Deuteronomy 23:1 are no longer kept outside.

After the resurrection, Jesus kept His wounds. He invited Thomas, who had demanded to see "the print of the nails" (John 20:25), to touch His hands and side (John 20:27, paraphrased). John later sees in heaven a Lamb that looked as though it had been slain (Revelation 5:6, paraphrased). The marks of human cruelty have become the lasting evidence that the price was paid.

7. Spirit & Life

These passages speak to anyone who fears that a wound has disqualified him. Isaiah 56:3 puts that fear into the words a eunuch might say and directly forbids it: "Nor let the eunuch say, 'Here I am, a dry tree.'" In Acts 8 a eunuch from Ethiopia hears Jesus preached and is baptized, welcomed through the gospel. Christ does not ask us to hide our wounds before coming to Him. He shows us His own. Isaiah promises that "a bruised reed He will not break" (Isaiah 42:3), and Matthew applies those words to Jesus (Matthew 12:20).

The prophet's story calls for courage. God's word sometimes has to be carried at personal cost. That word may hurt the one who brings it, or it may hurt the one who hears it, as in the faithful wounds of a friend. Scripture does not say why the neighbor in 1 Kings refused to strike. Whatever his reason, he was refusing a command that came "by the word of the LORD" (1 Kings 20:35).

The bride's wounds also speak to people who have been hurt by those who were supposed to protect them. The watchmen were appointed to guard the city, yet they beat her and took her veil. Some believers know that kind of harm from a parent, a leader or even a church. The Song does not treat the bride's wounds as the measure of her love, and Scripture never asks believers to seek out suffering to prove their devotion. What the Song shows is that the beating did not end her search. Her answer is to keep looking and to say that she is lovesick. Paul, who was beaten for preaching Christ, wrote that he bore in his body the marks of the Lord Jesus (Galatians 6:17, paraphrased). A believer who has been wounded, even by supposed protectors, is not shut out from Christ, and the Bridegroom who was Himself struck after standing before the leaders of His own people understands that harm.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All three occurrences of pâtsaʻ (Deuteronomy 23:1; 1 Kings 20:37; Song of Solomon 5:7) are discussed in Section 5. The following passages use related words for wounds and help show the wider biblical picture:

  • Jeremiah 10:19 — "Woe is me for my hurt! My wound is severe." Jeremiah's lament speaks of the nation's coming wound in the first person, as if the injury were already felt.

  • Micah 1:9 — "For her wounds are incurable." Micah mourns that Samaria's incurable wound has come to Judah and reached the gate of Jerusalem.

  • Proverbs 20:30 — "Blows that hurt cleanse away evil." Painful correction can purify the heart.

  • Jeremiah 30:13 — "There is no one to plead your cause, that you may be bound up." The verse names a wound that only God can heal.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H6482 (petsaʻ), the noun wound, which comes from the same root. It appears in Isaiah 1:6 and in Proverbs 27:6, "the wounds of a friend."

  • Canonical pairings: H2490 (chalal), used in "wounded for our transgressions," and H2250 (chabburah), translated "stripes," both in Isaiah 53:5. These passages develop the healing wounds of the Servant.

  • Thematic connections: H7779 (shuwph), "bruise," in Genesis 3:15, and H7533 (ratsats), "bruised reed," in Isaiah 42:3.
