FRUIT, PRODUCE, OFFSPRING - (pᵉrîy, Strong's H6529)

Summary

The Hebrew word pᵉrîy is the Old Testament's ordinary word for fruit: the yield of trees and the produce of fields. It is also used for offspring, both the young of flocks and herds and human children. Scripture speaks of "the fruit of the womb," meaning children, and "the fruit of their thoughts," meaning the outcome of a life. In many uses, fruit is the visible result of something hidden, such as a root, a womb, a heart, or a mouth. Because fruit comes from a source, the word keeps raising a deeper question: what is this life drawing from?

That question runs through the whole Bible. Fruit is first God's good gift at creation. Then it becomes the thing taken in Eden that brought death. In Israel's life under the covenant, the harvest showed whether the nation was walking with God or turning away. The wise and the foolish both end up eating the fruit of their own ways. Through the prophets, God answers Israel's long search for life among idols with the words "Your fruit is found in Me" (Hosea 14:8). God promised David that "the fruit of your body" would sit on his throne, and that promise reaches its fulfillment in Jesus Christ. Ezekiel then sees trees beside a river flowing from God's sanctuary, bearing fruit that never fails. From beginning to end, fruit tells the story of life lost by grasping and given back by grace.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pᵉrîy is a common Hebrew noun that appears across the Law, the historical books, the Psalms, the wisdom books, and the prophets. The heaviest concentration is in Deuteronomy, especially chapter 28, where Moses sets the blessings and curses before Israel. That pattern fits Israel's world. Farming families lived from one harvest to the next, so the fruit of the orchard and field was a daily concern. The NKJV usually translates the word "fruit." Depending on context, it also uses "produce," "increase," "offspring," "reward," and "profits."

The word first appears at creation. God commands the earth to bring forth "the fruit tree that yields fruit according to its kind, whose seed is in itself" (Genesis 1:11). From the beginning, fruit carries the seed of what comes next.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Pᵉrîy means the product or yield that comes from a living source. Scripture uses it in three main senses:

1. Produce of plants and land. This is the fruit of trees and the crops of the ground. In Leviticus 26:4 the word names the trees' yield: "the trees of the field shall yield their fruit." (The land's "produce" earlier in that verse translates a different Hebrew word.) Deuteronomy 28:4 uses pᵉrîy for the crops of the field, translated "the produce of your ground."

2. Offspring. Children are called "the fruit of the womb" (Psalm 127:3) or "the fruit of your body" (Psalm 132:11). The same word is used for the young of animals, as in "the increase of your herds" (Deuteronomy 28:4).

3. The result of conduct, thought, or speech. This is the outcome a person's ways produce, as when the foolish "shall eat the fruit of their own way" (Proverbs 1:31).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word is most often joined to the source of the fruit: fruit of the ground, of the womb, of the mouth, of the hands, of their thoughts, of his doings. This pattern matters for reading the passages. Many passages name where the fruit came from, so judging the fruit also judges its source. In a phrase like "fruit of the ground," the source is attached directly to the word. Psalm 107:37 names its source differently: the fields and vineyards "yield a fruitful harvest," and there pᵉrîy is translated "fruitful" rather than standing in a "fruit of …" phrase. The frequent pattern still draws attention to the bond between source and result.

Leviticus and Deuteronomy tie harvest closely to Israel's walk with God. The wisdom books turn the word toward speech and character. The prophets use it for both judgment and restoration. Jeremiah warns that the coming calamity is "the fruit of their thoughts" (Jeremiah 6:19). Amos and Isaiah pair fruit with roots to describe a people's whole existence. Amos 2:9 says God destroyed the Amorite's "fruit above and his roots beneath," meaning both his visible future and his hidden foundation. Isaiah 37:31 reverses the image for the surviving remnant of Judah, who "shall again take root downward, and bear fruit upward." Ruin and rescue alike reach from the hidden root to the visible fruit.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture presents God as the Lord of all fruitfulness. "The earth is satisfied with the fruit of Your works" (Psalm 104:13), because God waters the hills from above. In the Law, Israel learned to read the harvest as a sign of its relationship with Him under the covenant He made with the nation (Deuteronomy 29:1). Deuteronomy 28 promises blessing on the fruit of the body, the produce of the ground, and the increase of the herds (Deuteronomy 28:4, 11). It warns that disobedience would bring curses on that same fruit (Deuteronomy 28:18). Enemies would eat it (Deuteronomy 28:33). Locusts would consume it (Deuteronomy 28:42). In the worst siege, parents would eat "the fruit of your own body" (Deuteronomy 28:53). Lamentations 2:20 asks in anguish, "Should the women eat their offspring?" and Lamentations 4:10 states that it came true: "The hands of the compassionate women Have cooked their own children." Deuteronomy 30:9 holds out the opposite hope: God would make His repentant people abound in fruit, for He "will again rejoice over you for good." These blessings and curses were spoken to Israel as a nation living in the land God gave it. They do not make every good or poor harvest a verdict on an individual's faithfulness. Under that covenant, the fields and families of Israel showed outwardly the nation's hidden faithfulness or unfaithfulness.

The word also shows that God's judgment often takes the form of a harvest. Jeremiah 6:19 calls the coming disaster "the fruit of their thoughts," because the people rejected His law. Proverbs 1:31 says the rebellious will "eat the fruit of their own way, and be filled to the full with their own fancies." God is not inventing a penalty unrelated to the sin. He brings the sin's own crop to ripeness and sets it on the table. Paul states the same principle in other words: "whatever a man sows, that he will also reap" (Galatians 6:7).

God's ownership of the harvest also shaped Israel's worship. In Deuteronomy 26:2, the worshiper takes "some of the first of all the produce of the ground," places it in a basket, and brings it before the Lord. Giving the first of the produce declared that the whole harvest belonged to God. It can also be read as an expression of trust that the God who gave the first of the crop would keep providing.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 3:2–6 — the fruit that was taken. God had freely given Adam and Eve "the fruit of the trees of the garden" (Genesis 3:2). He withheld only one tree. The serpent's words made that single limit sound like stinginess on God's part. Eve saw that the tree was "good for food, that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree desirable to make one wise," and "she took of its fruit and ate" (Genesis 3:6). The tragedy turns on the word "took." The fruit of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil was the one fruit God had forbidden (Genesis 2:17). Instead of receiving life from the God who had given every other tree, Eve grasped at what He had withheld, drawn by the serpent's promise that they would "be like God" (Genesis 3:5), and her husband ate with her. Death followed. Much of the later story of fruit in Scripture answers this moment.

Psalm 1:3 and Jeremiah 17:8 — the rooted tree. The righteous man of Psalm 1 "shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that brings forth its fruit in its season." His fruit comes from where he is planted, and the verse never pictures him straining to produce it. Jeremiah 17 adds the setting of drought. The person who trusts in the Lord is like a tree that sends its roots out by the river, does not fear when heat comes, and does not stop yielding fruit (Jeremiah 17:7–8). That same chapter warns that God gives each person "according to the fruit of his doings" (Jeremiah 17:10). Both passages make the same point. A life cannot bear lasting fruit apart from a lasting source. The phrase "in its season" also invites patience. God sets the timing of fruit, and human pressure cannot force it.

Hosea 14:8 — "Your fruit is found in Me." Hosea's whole book exposes Israel's habit of crediting Baal, the Canaanite fertility god, with its grain, wine, and oil, when God had given them all along (Hosea 2:8). Hosea 10:1 calls Israel a vine that brought forth fruit for itself and used the increase to build more altars. At the end of the book, God foretells that Ephraim will say, "What have I to do anymore with idols?" Then God declares, "I am like a green cypress tree; Your fruit is found in Me." This is a rare place where the Lord compares Himself to a tree. The life Israel had sought from idols was never in them. It was in God, always green and always living, and repentance meant returning to the true source.

Proverbs 18:20–21 — the fruit of the mouth. "A man's stomach shall be satisfied from the fruit of his mouth; from the produce of his lips he shall be filled" (Proverbs 18:20). The next verse warns that death and life are in the power of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruit (Proverbs 18:21). Words do not simply disappear after they are spoken. They grow into the atmosphere of a home, a friendship, or a church, and the speaker ends up living in that harvest. Proverbs 12:14 makes the same promise of good for the one whose words are wise.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The phrase "fruit of the womb" teaches that children are a gift from God: "children are a heritage from the LORD, the fruit of the womb is a reward" (Psalm 127:3). Jacob's angry answer to Rachel assumes the same truth: he asked whether he stood in God's place, since God had withheld from her the fruit of the womb (Genesis 30:2). Later God attached a particular promise to one family line. "The LORD has sworn in truth to David; He will not turn from it: 'I will set upon your throne the fruit of your body'" (Psalm 132:11), repeating His promise in 2 Samuel 7:12. The New Testament states the fulfillment directly. At Pentecost, Peter cites this oath and says that from the fruit of David's body God raised up the Christ (Acts 2:30). The angel likewise tells Mary that her Son will receive the throne of His father David (Luke 1:32). Isaiah had already joined the coming "Branch of the LORD" with excellent "fruit of the earth" (Isaiah 4:2).

Firstfruits also find their meaning in Christ. Paul uses a different Greek word, but the image is the same as Deuteronomy 26. "Christ is risen from the dead, and has become the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). His resurrection is the first sheaf, and it guarantees the full harvest. Believers "have the firstfruits of the Spirit" while they wait for "the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23).

The arc from Eden reaches its end in the prophets and Revelation. Ezekiel sees water flowing from the temple into the barren places. Beside it grow trees whose "fruit will not fail. They will bear fruit every month, because their water flows from the sanctuary. Their fruit will be for food, and their leaves for medicine" (Ezekiel 47:12). John sees the same scene completed: a river "proceeding from the throne of God and of the Lamb," and "the tree of life, which bore twelve fruits," with leaves "for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:1–2). The two trees frame the story. In Eden, fruit from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil was taken against God's command, and death followed. At the end, the tree of life stands in God's city bearing its fruit every month, and its leaves bring healing to the nations. The connection that follows is a theological resonance rather than an explicit statement in these passages: the turning point between Eden and Revelation is the cross, where Christ hung on a tree (1 Peter 2:24) so that the curse that followed Eden's forbidden fruit could be undone.

7. Spirit & Life

Hosea 14:8 and John 15 belong together. "I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing" (John 15:5). God had already told Israel that their fruit was found in Him. Jesus now tells His disciples that their fruit is found in staying joined to Him. "The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness" (Galatians 5:22). Paul does not call these the works of the believer. Christians cannot manufacture this fruit by anxious effort. A branch bears fruit by remaining in the vine, and a tree bears fruit by staying near the river. When we feel spiritually barren, the first question is not "How can I produce more?" The better question is "Where have my roots gone?"

Psalm 1 also gives patience to the believer. Fruit comes "in its season," and even Israel's young fruit trees were left for years before their fruit could be eaten (Leviticus 19:23–25). The law itself built years of waiting into an orchard's life.

Fruit is also honest. A person can display leaves of religious profession, but the fruit reveals the source. Amos accused Israel of turning "the fruit of righteousness" into wormwood (Amos 6:12). Isaiah promised that the righteous would eat the fruit of their doings (Isaiah 3:10). This is a sober reason to watch our thoughts and our words, because both are planting a crop we will one day eat.

Finally, the harvest of the lips can return to God as worship. The Israelite carried the first fruits of the ground to the altar. The believer now offers "the sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of our lips, giving thanks to His name" (Hebrews 13:15). The bride in the Song of Solomon sat in her beloved's shade and found "his fruit was sweet to my taste" (Song of Solomon 2:3). Read alongside Eden, the image suggests that the life and delight Adam and Eve tried to seize by grasping are now received in communion with the One who gives them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 4:3 — Cain brought an offering "of the fruit of the ground." Bringing fruit to God did not by itself make an offering acceptable. The LORD respected Abel and his offering but not Cain and his (Genesis 4:4–5), and Hebrews 11:4 traces the difference to faith.

  • Leviticus 19:23–25 — Fruit from newly planted trees was forbidden for three years, holy to the Lord in the fourth, and the law permitted the people to eat it in the fifth. The law built patience and consecration into the orchard itself.

  • Numbers 13:26–27 — The spies brought back the fruit of Canaan as visible proof that God's promise of a good land was true.

  • Proverbs 31:31 — The godly woman is to be given "the fruit of her hands." Faithful labor receives its reward.

  • Micah 6:7 — Micah asks whether a person should give "the fruit of my body for the sin of my soul." The question exposes the horror of child sacrifice and the futility of trying to buy forgiveness.

  • Malachi 3:11 — God promises to rebuke the devourer so that the fruit of the ground will not be destroyed when His people return to faithful giving.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The well-known "basket of summer fruit" in Amos 8:1–2 uses a different Hebrew word, chosen for its wordplay with the Hebrew word for end. That passage should not be read as a use of pᵉrîy.
