STRIP, TAKE OFF, RAID - (pâshaṭ, Strong's H6584)

Summary

The Hebrew verb pâshaṭ means to strip something of its covering. It can describe taking off a garment, skinning an animal, stripping armor from a fallen soldier, or soldiers rushing upon a city or a territory in attack, a raid that could end with goods and people carried off. The word itself is morally neutral. Its significance depends on who is doing the stripping, who or what is being stripped, and why. Jonathan takes off his royal robe and gives it to David. Joseph's brothers tear the favored tunic off his back. Moses removes Aaron's priestly garments on Mount Hor and dresses Aaron's son in them. The worshiper who brings a burnt offering skins it (Leviticus 1:6), and priests take off their holy linen garments before carrying the ashes out or going out to the people (Leviticus 6:11; Ezekiel 44:19).

Taken together, these passages show God as the One who clothes and covers His people, and they show what it means when that covering is surrendered, torn away, or removed in judgment. The word points toward Jesus Christ in several ways. Like Joseph, He was rejected and stripped of His garments. Jonathan gave his royal robe to David and later acknowledged that David would be king. In a far greater way, Christ, though He was in the form of God, willingly humbled Himself and took the form of a servant. Unlike Aaron, He holds His priesthood permanently and will never be stripped of it. For the believer, the word raises a searching question: what are we holding onto that God may be asking us to lay down?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pâshaṭ is an ordinary Hebrew action verb. It appears in law, narrative, poetry, and prophecy. In Leviticus and Chronicles it belongs to the altar, where it describes skinning sacrifices. In Leviticus and Ezekiel it also describes priests changing out of their holy garments. In Judges, Samuel, and Chronicles it is most often a soldier's word, used for raids, invasions, and the stripping of the dead after battle. The prophets and Job use it for the stripping away of honor, wealth, and security. Ancient readers understood clothing as a sign of rank and identity. A prince's robe, a priest's linen, and a warrior's armor told everyone who a person was, so removing a garment could mean far more than undressing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to remove a covering. Scripture uses the verb in three closely related senses:

1. To take off or strip off clothing or armor, whether a person does this to himself or someone does it to another. Jonathan "took off the robe that was on him" (1 Samuel 18:4); Joseph's brothers "stripped Joseph of his tunic" (Genesis 37:23).

2. To skin or flay an animal, removing its hide. The person who brings the burnt offering kills it, the priests sprinkle its blood, and then "he shall skin the burnt offering and cut it into its pieces" (Leviticus 1:5–6). In Hezekiah's day the Levites helped the priests with the skinning (2 Chronicles 29:34).

3. To rush upon, attack, or raid a place or people. Sometimes the attack itself is the whole point: Abimelech's companies "rushed upon all who were in the fields and killed them" (Judges 9:44). Sometimes plunder follows the raid: the Chaldeans "raided the camels and took them away" (Job 1:17).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The military sense carries the largest share of the narrative uses. Zebul tells Abimelech to "rush upon the city" of Shechem at sunrise (Judges 9:33), and Abimelech's companies later rush forward to the city gate and upon the people in the fields (Judges 9:44). The Amalekites "invaded" Ziklag while David was away and carried off every wife and child (1 Samuel 30:1–3). The Philistines came up to "raid" the Valley of Rephaim twice in 1 Chronicles 14:9 and 1 Chronicles 14:13. When a raid ends in plunder, as at Ziklag, it strips the land the way a thief strips a coat from a traveler. In each of these stories the attack is sudden, and it leaves people exposed. The same pattern appears in Job's opening losses.

The verb works differently depending on its object. When a person strips himself, the act can express surrender (Jonathan), mourning (the princes of the sea in Ezekiel 26:16), or simple rest (Song of Songs 5:3). When others do the stripping, it usually means violence, plunder, or humiliation. When God is the One behind the stripping, as in Job 19:9 or Hosea 2:3, it becomes judgment or testing. Context decides the meaning.

4. Theological Significance

Several of these passages present God as the One who clothes. He dressed the priests in holy garments. In Ezekiel 16:10–14 He describes clothing Israel in embroidered cloth, fine linen, silk, and jewelry until she was beautiful. Because the covering is His gift, He has the right to set its boundaries and to remove it.

Those boundaries appear clearly in the laws about priestly garments. Dressed in his linen garments, the priest first takes up the ashes from the altar and puts them beside the altar (Leviticus 6:10). Only then does he take off those garments and put on others, and in the ordinary clothes he carries the ashes outside the camp to a clean place (Leviticus 6:11). On the Day of Atonement the high priest takes off the linen garments inside the tabernacle and leaves them there (Leviticus 16:23). Ezekiel gives the reason: the priests "shall take off their garments in which they have ministered, leave them in the holy chambers, and put on other garments; and in their holy garments they shall not sanctify the people" (Ezekiel 44:19). What had been set apart for God's presence was not to be carried casually into common life. Taking off the holy clothes protected the difference between the holy and the ordinary, and it taught Israel that God's holiness is not something to handle carelessly.

The word also exposes how deeply God hates the abuse of power. Micah accuses Israel's rulers of treating their own people as prey: "You pull off the robe with the garment from those who trust you, as they pass by" (Micah 2:8). He then uses the same verb in a terrible image. The leaders "eat the flesh of My people, flay their skin from them, break their bones, and chop them in pieces like meat for the pot" (Micah 3:3). Elsewhere this verb describes the skinning of sacrifices for the altar. Israel's leaders were doing the same work to the flock God had given them to protect, only to feed their own appetites. God calls these people "My people," and He takes their abuse personally.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 37:23 — Joseph stripped of his tunic. As soon as Joseph reaches his brothers at Dothan, "they stripped Joseph of his tunic, the tunic of many colors that was on him." The tunic was Jacob's sign of special love (Genesis 37:3–4), and the brothers hated Joseph for it. Their first act of violence is to remove the sign of the father's favor. Then they throw him into the pit (Genesis 37:24). The text does not say what the brothers meant by taking the tunic, but it shows what the tunic then does in the story. They dip it in a goat's blood and send it to Jacob, and the robe that once announced his love becomes the false evidence that breaks his heart (Genesis 37:31–33). Yet the stripping began a road that ended with Joseph clothed in fine linen and set over all the land of Egypt (Genesis 41:42–43), saving the very brothers who had rejected him.

1 Samuel 18:4, 1 Samuel 19:24 and 1 Samuel 31:8–9 — three strippings in Saul's house. First Samuel uses this verb in three episodes at turning points in the story of Saul's family. In the first, after making a covenant with David, "Jonathan took off the robe that was on him and gave it to David, with his armor, even to his sword and his bow and his belt" (1 Samuel 18:4). Jonathan was the heir to the throne, and these were the clothes and weapons of a crown prince. He gave them freely to the man God had chosen. Later he said it plainly: David would be king, and Jonathan would stand next to him (1 Samuel 23:17).

In the second, Saul comes to Naioth hunting David. The Spirit of God comes upon him, and he prophesies all the way to Naioth (1 Samuel 19:23). There Saul takes off his own clothes: "he also stripped off his clothes and prophesied before Samuel in like manner, and lay down naked all that day and all that night" (1 Samuel 19:24). The king who came with murderous intent lay helpless and exposed before God's prophet. God stopped him without a sword. This was a unique event, not a pattern for how the Spirit normally works, but it shows how quickly human power collapses before the presence of God.

In the third episode, where the verb appears twice, after the battle on Mount Gilboa, "the Philistines came to strip the slain," and they found Saul and his three sons (1 Samuel 31:8). They cut off his head, "stripped off his armor," and placed it in the temple of the Ashtoreths as a trophy (1 Samuel 31:9–10). In the ancient world, a defeated warrior's armor meant shame for the loser and glory for the winner. So in one family, the son who stripped himself in humility honored God's anointed, while the father who refused to yield lay stripped and helpless before God's prophet and was finally stripped of his armor by his enemies in shame.

Numbers 20:23–29 — Aaron stripped on Mount Hor. God tells Moses to strip Aaron of his garments and put them on Eleazar his son, because Aaron will die there. Moses does so, and Aaron dies on the mountaintop. No shame is involved here. God is teaching that the priesthood continues even though the priest does not. The office passed to the next generation because death had brought Aaron's service to an end.

Ezekiel 16:39, Ezekiel 23:26, Hosea 2:3 and Isaiah 32:11 — the covering removed. God warns unfaithful Israel that her lovers will strip her of her clothes and take her beautiful jewelry, leaving her naked and bare (Ezekiel 16:39; see Ezekiel 23:26). Hosea speaks the same warning: God will strip her and expose her as on the day she was born (Hosea 2:3). The force of these warnings comes from Ezekiel 16:10–14. The clothing being removed was God's own gift, and Israel had used it to pursue idols. Judgment reverses grace, returning her to the exposed condition in which God first found her. In Isaiah the call comes before the judgment falls. To the women "at ease," he says: "Strip yourselves, make yourselves bare, and gird sackcloth on your waists" (Isaiah 32:11). The call is to lay aside false security in humble mourning before it is torn away in judgment.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The stripping of Joseph points toward the passion of Jesus. Joseph's brothers stripped him of his tunic and threw him into the pit, and only afterward sold him to the traders (Genesis 37:23–28). With Jesus the order is different. Pilate first handed Him over to be crucified, and then the soldiers stripped Him and put a scarlet robe on Him to mock His kingship (Matthew 27:26–28). After the crucifixion they "took His garments and made four parts, to each soldier a part, and also the tunic" (John 19:23). The parallel lies in rejection and stripping: in each story a beloved son is rejected by his own and stripped of his garment. The endings differ in scale. Joseph's road led from the pit to authority in Egypt, where God used him to save his brothers' lives (Genesis 45:7). Jesus' road led through death to resurrection, and God exalted Him "to be Prince and Savior, to give repentance to Israel and forgiveness of sins" (Acts 5:31). The New Testament does not formally name Joseph as a type of Christ, but the parallel runs deep through the story.

Jonathan's gift of his robe points to a greater laying aside. The gift did not formally hand David the throne. Its force comes from what Jonathan later said openly: "You shall be king over Israel, and I shall be next to you" (1 Samuel 23:17). The heir to the throne freely honored the one God had chosen. Paul describes a far greater humbling. Christ, "being in the form of God, did not consider it robbery to be equal with God, but made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant" (Philippians 2:6–7). Paul adds that "He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross" (Philippians 2:8). The Son did not stop being God. He chose the lowly place of a servant, and He went all the way to the cross. In Christ the two kinds of stripping meet. He freely humbled Himself, and He allowed His enemies to strip Him by force.

Aaron's death on Mount Hor reveals a weakness that every earthly priesthood shared. "There were many priests, because they were prevented by death from continuing. But He, because He continues forever, has an unchangeable priesthood" (Hebrews 7:23–24). No one will ever lead Jesus up a mountain to remove His priestly garments. And where the Philistines stripped Saul to display their triumph, Paul says that at the cross God disarmed the rulers and authorities and made a public spectacle of them (Colossians 2:15). Paul uses a different Greek word, but the battlefield image is the same. The One who was stripped became the One who strips His enemies.

7. Spirit & Life

Job's lament gives voice to something many believers eventually experience: "He has stripped me of my glory, and taken the crown from my head" (Job 19:9). Job's words treat his honor and standing as coverings that God had given and that God could remove. His earlier words, "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away" (Job 1:21), were tested through long darkness. The stripping did not end Job's relationship with God. In the end Job could say, "I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees You" (Job 42:5). When God removes reputation, comfort, or position, He may be clearing away what stood between us and a real knowledge of Him. Paul speaks of loss in the same direction. What had been gain to him he counted loss for Christ, and he counted all he had lost "as rubbish, that I may gain Christ" (Philippians 3:7–8).

The burnt offering points in the same direction. Before it went up in smoke to God, the animal had to be skinned (Leviticus 1:6). In Hezekiah's day there was so much of this work that the priests "could not skin all the burnt offerings," and the Levites had to help them (2 Chronicles 29:34). The law does not explain the skinning as a symbol, so this application is drawn from the larger teaching of Scripture. Even so, the picture fits. Scripture says "all things are naked and open to the eyes of Him to whom we must give account" (Hebrews 4:13), and Paul urges believers to present their bodies "a living sacrifice" (Romans 12:1). We come to God without our outward pretenses. He already sees through them, and Christ was laid completely bare for us.

The Song of Songs gives a gentler warning. The beloved knocks in the night, and the bride answers, "I have taken off my robe; how can I put it on again?" (Song of Songs 5:3). Her words sound less like rebellion than like reluctance to leave her comfort. By the time she rises to open the door, he has gone (Song of Songs 5:6). The Song is first a picture of love between a husband and wife, but many readers have heard in it a caution about spiritual ease. Rest is good, but rest that will not get up when the Lord calls can cost us fellowship with Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 30:1 — The Amalekites "invaded" Ziklag. David "strengthened himself in the LORD his God" (1 Samuel 30:6) and recovered everything the raiders had taken.

  • Job 22:6 — Eliphaz falsely accuses Job of having "stripped the naked of their clothing," which shows that stripping the poor was counted a serious wrong.

  • Nehemiah 4:23 — While enemies threatened Jerusalem, Nehemiah and his men did not take off their clothes, staying ready day and night.

  • Ezekiel 26:16 — The princes of the sea take off their embroidered garments in mourning over Tyre's fall.

  • Hosea 7:1 — "A band of robbers takes spoil outside," a picture of a nation where fraud and theft had become common.

  • Nahum 3:16 — Nineveh's merchants are like the locust that "plunders and flies away," stripping the land and leaving nothing.
