PATHRUSIM - (Pathruçîy, Strong's H6625)

Summary

Pathrusim is the name of a people. It refers to the descendants of Mizraim (Egypt) who settled in Pathros, the southern stretch of the Nile valley usually called Upper Egypt. The word appears only twice in the Bible. Both times it sits in a list of nations descended from Ham: Genesis 10:14 and its parallel in 1 Chronicles 1:12. Neither verse tells a story about the Pathrusim. They are named, placed in their family line, and left there.

That brief mention still carries weight. In Genesis 10 God's Word traces the nations that spread over the earth after the Flood, and a people from the far south of Egypt receives a name in that record. Their homeland, Pathros, later appears in the prophets. Judeans fled there in disobedience, Ezekiel promised that Egypt's scattered people would one day return there, and Isaiah included Pathros among the places from which the Lord would gather His remnant in the days of the Root of Jesse. The people God named at the beginning were still within His sight when He spoke of judgment, mercy, and the gathering of the nations.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Pathruçîy is a Hebrew gentilic noun, meaning a word that names a people by their place or ancestry. In both verses it takes the plural form, "Pathrusim," as do the other peoples in the list. It is closely tied to Pathros (H6624), the name of the region itself. The prophets use the place name. The people name belongs only to the genealogies.

Both occurrences sit in the "Table of Nations," the record of how Noah's sons became the peoples of the ancient world (Genesis 10). Ham's son Mizraim is the ancestor figure for Egypt (Genesis 10:6), and his "sons" in Genesis 10:13–14 are peoples connected with Egypt and its neighbors. The Chronicler repeats the list almost word for word in 1 Chronicles 1:11–12. He was writing for Israelites after the exile, and he opens Israel's story with all humanity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one sense. "Pathrusim" means the people of Pathros, the inhabitants of southern (Upper) Egypt, presented in Scripture as descendants of Mizraim. It is an ethnic name and carries no symbolic meaning in its own right. Jeremiah's list of Judean settlements in Egypt names Migdol, Tahpanhes, and Noph (Memphis), and then "the country of Pathros" (Jeremiah 44:1). The geographical identification of these sites, not the order of the verse alone, places the first three in the north and Pathros in the south.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the word in only one way: as an entry in a genealogy of nations. Genesis 10:13 says Mizraim "begot" the Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, and Naphtuhim, and verse 14 continues with "Pathrusim, and Casluhim (from whom came the Philistines and Caphtorim)." Each name carries the plural -im ending, which marks these as peoples rather than individual sons. That one grammatical detail matters for reading the passage. Here "begot" describes family relationships among nations: who is related to whom, and how the peoples of Egypt and its borderlands trace back to a common ancestor.

The Chronicles parallel reads: "Pathrusim, Casluhim (from whom came the Philistines and the Caphtorim)" (1 Chronicles 1:12). Its only differences from Genesis are small ones of wording. The term never appears in narrative, law, poetry, or prophecy. When the prophets speak of this area, they use the related place name Pathros.

4. Theological Significance

The Table of Nations shows a God whose concern reaches beyond Israel from the very beginning. Before Abraham is introduced in Genesis 12, Scripture pauses to record the peoples of the earth. That includes a people of southern Egypt whom no later biblical narrative individually names. Genesis 10 does not claim to name every clan on earth. It does claim that the spreading of nations after the Flood happened within God's knowledge and under His hand. Verse 32 sums up the chapter: the nations were divided on the earth after the Flood.

This matters for the way the Bible's story moves. God chose Abraham's family as His means of blessing "all the families of the earth" (Genesis 12:3). Genesis 10 shows who those families are. The Pathrusim are among them. They were not chosen as Israel was, but they were never outside the reach of the God who governs all peoples. Paul said the same in Athens: God made every nation from one man and determined their times and boundaries, so that they would seek Him (Acts 17:26–27, paraphrased).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:13–14 and 1 Chronicles 1:11–12 — Named at the start. In Genesis, the Pathrusim appear among the descendants of Mizraim between the Naphtuhim and the Casluhim. Nothing more is said about them. Although the Chronicler's history moves toward David and the temple, he copies these names again. Israel after the exile needed to remember that its story began with all of humanity. The nations around them, Egypt included, were relatives within one human family under one Creator, and the God of Israel had numbered and named them.

The prophets and Pathros — The homeland stays in view. The people name does not appear after the genealogies of Genesis 10 and 1 Chronicles 1, but its homeland returns at serious moments. In Jeremiah 44, Judeans who fled to Egypt against the Lord's word were living in Pathros, among other places (44:1). When Jeremiah confronted them, "all the people who dwelt in the land of Egypt, in Pathros," answered him (44:15), and they declared that they would keep burning incense to the queen of heaven (44:17). The land the Pathrusim settled became the setting for one of the darkest refusals in Judah's history.

Ezekiel 29:14 speaks to Egypt itself. After forty years of scattering, God says He will bring back Egypt's captives to Pathros, the land of their origin (paraphrased). He adds that Egypt would become a lowly kingdom. Pathros is called Egypt's place of origin, which fits the picture in Genesis 10 of an ancient people rooted in the south. Judgment falls on Egypt, but it does not end in annihilation. God restores a humbled people to their ancestral land.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest step forward comes in Isaiah 11. The chapter opens with the Rod from the stem of Jesse, on whom the Spirit of the Lord rests. Then verse 11 says the Lord will set His hand "again the second time" to recover the remnant of His people from Assyria, Egypt, Pathros, Cush, and the coastlands (paraphrased). Pathros is named in a messianic promise. The far south of Egypt, where the Pathrusim settled and where Judeans later hardened their hearts, appears on the map of the coming King's gathering.

Isaiah 19 goes further and speaks of a day when the Lord will call Egypt "My people" (19:25, paraphrased). At Pentecost, people from Egypt heard the apostles speak the wonderful works of God (Acts 2:10–11, paraphrased). The New Testament does not mention the Pathrusim, and no verse calls them a type of anything. The line of the story is still clear. The nations listed in Genesis 10 become the "all nations" of the Great Commission, and finally the countless multitude from every nation standing before the Lamb (Revelation 7:9).

7. Spirit & Life

A name with no story attached can teach something by its presence. The Pathrusim did nothing in Scripture that would make them famous, yet God's Word records them. The Lord does not lose track of peoples whom history forgets. He knew the clans of the upper Nile before Israel existed, and He knows the neighborhood, town, and family a believer comes from today.

Pathros also carries a warning. The Judeans of Jeremiah 42–43 sought safety in Egypt from war, hunger, and the Chaldeans, against the Lord's word, and the word followed them there. Distance from Jerusalem did not put them out of God's reach, and moving away from obedience did not silence Him.

Pathros carries hope as well. The region linked with stubborn refusal in Jeremiah is named in Isaiah's promise of gathering. God's purposes for a place are not exhausted by the worst things that happened there. Under the Root of Jesse, a land associated with rebellion becomes a place from which the Lord recovers His people.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word occurs only in Genesis 10:14 and 1 Chronicles 1:12, and both have been discussed above. The prophetic passages in Isaiah 11:11, Jeremiah 44:1, 15, and Ezekiel 29:14 use the related place name Pathros (H6624). Ezekiel 30:14 also names Pathros among the Egyptian regions under God's judgment.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6624, Pathros (lexical relative): the region of southern Egypt that gives the Pathrusim their name; Isaiah 11:11; Jeremiah 44:1; Ezekiel 29:14.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The parenthesis in Genesis 10:14, "(from whom came the Philistines and Caphtorim)," follows the Casluhim rather than the Pathrusim. It should not be read as tracing the Philistines to Pathros. Elsewhere Scripture connects the Philistines with Caphtor (Amos 9:7; Jeremiah 47:4), which is why some interpreters discuss how the Casluhim and Caphtorim relate. That question does not affect the Pathrusim, who appear in both verses simply as one people descended from Mizraim.
