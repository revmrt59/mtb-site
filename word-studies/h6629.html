FLOCK, SHEEP - (tson, Strong's H6629)

Summary

The Hebrew word tson names the flock: the sheep and goats that grazed together across the hills of Israel. Abraham's wealth was counted in flocks, and Jacob's family lived by them. Abel offered from his flock, and the Passover lamb came from it. Much of the tabernacle's worship drew on it. The flock was a family's living, its measure of God's blessing, and the animals it brought to worship.

Scripture also takes this ordinary farm word and makes it one of the Bible's clearest pictures of God's relationship with His people. Israel confessed, "We are His people and the sheep of His pasture" (Psalm 100:3). The prophets used the same word to condemn kings who fleeced and scattered the flock. They also used it to promise that God Himself would come to gather His sheep. Isaiah's confession that "all we like sheep have gone astray" (Isaiah 53:6) and Zechariah's word, "Strike the Shepherd, And the sheep will be scattered" (Zechariah 13:7), prepare the way for Jesus. He is the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep, and He is also the Lamb who takes away the sin of the world.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tson is a collective noun. It is a single word for a whole group, like the English word "flock." It covered sheep and goats together. When Rebekah sends Jacob "to the flock" in Genesis 27:9, he comes back with young goats. Ezekiel 34:17 speaks of God's flock as containing "rams and goats." Small livestock gave Israel wool, milk, meat, and animals for sacrifice. Because the patriarchs lived as herdsmen, the word appears constantly in Genesis.

Shepherding also formed Israel's leaders. Moses was keeping Jethro's flock when God met him at the burning bush (Exodus 3:1). David was out "keeping the sheep" when Samuel came to anoint a king (1 Samuel 16:11). Amos says that the LORD took him while he was following the flock and sent him to prophesy (Amos 7:15).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A flock of sheep and goats, often counted as property and wealth. Job owned "seven thousand sheep" (Job 1:3). The same literal flock supplied animals for food and sacrifice. Leviticus 1:10 speaks of a burnt offering "of the flocks—of the sheep or of the goats," and in Exodus 12:21 the NKJV renders this collective word as "lambs" when Moses tells each family to choose animals for the Passover.

2. Figuratively, a people under a leader's care, especially Israel as God's flock (Psalm 100:3; Ezekiel 34:31).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the historical books the flock is usually literal. Blessing is often measured by its growth. Deuteronomy promises "the offspring of your flocks" to an obedient people (28:4), and it warns that disobedience will bring a cursed flock and stolen sheep (Deuteronomy 28:18, 31). Jacob's flocks increased even under Laban's mistreatment (Genesis 30:43), which showed that God's hand was on him.

Two patterns change the word's force. The first is possession. When God says "My flock," as He does repeatedly in Ezekiel 34, He is claiming ownership of the people and taking responsibility for them. The second is comparison. "Like sheep" describes people who are helpless, lost, or without a leader (Numbers 27:17; Isaiah 53:6). Because tson is collective, it usually pictures the people as one body whose welfare rises or falls with its leadership.

4. Theological Significance

The Psalms ground Israel's identity in God's ownership. "It is He who has made us, and not we ourselves; We are His people and the sheep of His pasture" (Psalm 100:3). A flock does not make itself, feed itself, or defend itself. Psalm 95:7 calls Israel "the sheep of His hand" and then adds, "Today, if you will hear His voice." Belonging to God's flock means depending on Him and listening to Him. Israel's history proved this. "You led Your people like a flock By the hand of Moses and Aaron" (Psalm 77:20). Even the bitter complaint of Psalm 74:1, "Why does Your anger smoke against the sheep of Your pasture?", appeals to God on the basis that the people still belong to Him.

The flock also teaches a sober lesson about what God gives. Job lost thousands of sheep in a single day (Job 1:16). He worshiped anyway, and God later gave him fourteen thousand (Job 42:12). God gives flocks as blessing, yet He Himself is to be worshiped above them.

From the beginning, the flock also supplied the cost of worship. The word first appears when Abel is named a keeper of sheep (Genesis 4:2), and two verses later Abel brings the firstborn of his flock to the Lord (Genesis 4:4). At the Passover, Moses told the elders to take lambs from the flock "according to your families" (Exodus 12:21), and a household too small for a whole lamb was to share one with its neighbor (12:4). The lamb died so that the firstborn might live. Under the law, a worshiper could bring a burnt offering from the flock, "of the sheep or of the goats" (Leviticus 1:10), and the priests offered two lambs on the altar "day by day continually" (Exodus 29:38). God tied that daily offering to His presence: "I will dwell among the children of Israel and will be their God" (Exodus 29:45). In this way a holy God could live among a sinful people. As Hebrews 9:22 says, "without shedding of blood there is no remission." Israel offered from its flock long before the prophets told Israel that it was the flock.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Ezekiel 34: false shepherds and the true Shepherd. Ezekiel opens with a woe: "Woe to the shepherds of Israel who feed themselves! Should not the shepherds feed the flocks?" (34:2). Israel's kings and leaders took everything the flock could give. "You eat the fat and clothe yourselves with the wool... but you do not feed the flock" (34:3). As a result, "My flock became a prey, and My flock became food for every beast of the field" (34:8). The wild beasts picture the nations that devoured Israel; Ezekiel 34:28 sets "the nations" and "beasts of the land" side by side. Moses had once prayed that the people would not be like sheep without a shepherd (Numbers 27:17), and that fear had now come true.

God's answer is to come Himself. He says He will search for His sheep and seek them out (34:11–12). Then the chapter takes a surprising turn. God says, "I shall judge between sheep and sheep, between rams and goats" (34:17). Some of the flock had become oppressors too. They trampled the pasture and fouled the water (34:18–19). They "pushed with side and shoulder, butted all the weak ones with your horns" (34:21). God's rescue therefore includes judgment inside the community: "I will save My flock, and they shall no longer be a prey" (34:22). The chapter ends with the relationship stated plainly: "You are My flock, the flock of My pasture; you are men, and I am your God" (Ezekiel 34:31).

Isaiah 53:6 and Zechariah 13:7: the wandering flock and the struck Shepherd. Isaiah gives the problem in sheep's language: "All we like sheep have gone astray; We have turned, every one, to his own way." He names everyone, not only the bad leaders. Sheep drift into danger and cannot easily find their own way back. Isaiah's remedy is a substitute: "the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all."

Zechariah adds something startling. God Himself calls for a sword against His own Shepherd: "Awake, O sword, against My Shepherd, Against the Man who is My Companion." The one struck is no hireling. He is God's own companion. "Strike the Shepherd, And the sheep will be scattered." Read together, these two passages show that the flock's wandering and the Shepherd's wounding belong to the same story. The sheep go astray, and the Shepherd is struck in their place.

From the sheepfold to the throne. When Samuel looked over Jesse's sons, the boy God had chosen was in the field "keeping the sheep" (1 Samuel 16:11). David later told Saul he had rescued his father's sheep from a lion and a bear (1 Samuel 17:34–35). God remembered where He found him: "I took you from the sheepfold, from following the sheep, to be ruler over My people, over Israel" (2 Samuel 7:8). Scripture presents David's faithful care of vulnerable animals as the background for his care of a vulnerable nation. That pattern stands in direct contrast to the shepherds Ezekiel condemned. Ezekiel himself joins the two threads. In the same chapter where the LORD promises to search for His sheep Himself, He also says, "I will establish one shepherd over them, and he shall feed them— My servant David. He shall feed them and be their shepherd" (Ezekiel 34:23). David had been dead for centuries when Ezekiel spoke, so the promise looks ahead to a king from David's line who would shepherd God's flock as David had. Verse 24 holds both promises together: "I, the LORD, will be their God, and My servant David a prince among them." God will shepherd His flock Himself, and He will do it through His chosen Shepherd from David's house.

Ezekiel 36:37–38 and Micah 2:12: the flock restored. Ezekiel draws on the crowded flocks brought to Jerusalem for the feasts. He says the ruined cities will be filled with "flocks of men," as full as the flocks of holy offerings at the festivals. The image joins the two great uses of tson. The flock once brought for sacrifice now pictures the people themselves, gathered and set apart for God. Micah promises that God will gather the remnant like sheep in a fold, so many that they make a loud noise because of the crowd (Micah 2:12).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Each thread of this word leads to Jesus. Moses prayed that the people would not be sheep without a shepherd. Ezekiel promised that God Himself would shepherd them, and that He would set over them one shepherd, His servant David (Ezekiel 34:23–24). Jesus, the Son of David, stands in both promises when He says, "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). He contrasts Himself with the hireling, "one who does not own the sheep," who "sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees" (John 10:12). Jesus owns the sheep, so He stays. "I know My sheep, and am known by My own" (John 10:14). This is the language of Psalm 100 and Ezekiel 34:31 spoken by the Shepherd in person.

On the night of His arrest, Jesus quoted Zechariah 13:7 about Himself: "All of you will be made to stumble because of Me this night, for it is written: 'I will strike the Shepherd, And the sheep will be scattered'" (Mark 14:27). The disciples scattered as Zechariah foretold. The sword fell on God's Companion, and Isaiah's word was fulfilled as the iniquity of the wandering flock was laid on Him.

The Bible's two uses of the flock meet in Christ. For centuries Israel brought animals from its flock to the altar. John the Baptist pointed to Jesus and said, "Behold! The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). The Shepherd of the flock became the offering for the flock. Even the Sheep Gate in Jerusalem points in the same direction. The priests rebuilt and consecrated it, and Nehemiah 3 lists their work first (Nehemiah 3:1). The gate’s name recalls the flocks Israel brought for sacrifice. In a chapter about rebuilding, it offers a fitting picture of a people returning to worship.

7. Spirit & Life

Calling ourselves sheep is not flattering, and Scripture means it that way. Sheep wander, cannot defend themselves, and need to be led to water. Psalm 100 turns that weakness into rest. We did not make ourselves, and we are not left to keep ourselves. The One who made us also owns us and feeds us. Psalm 95 adds the other side of that belonging: "Today, if you will hear His voice." A flock that refuses to listen will scatter.

Ezekiel 34 also speaks to the church. The flock contains strong sheep who push the weak aside, take the best pasture, and muddy the water for everyone who comes after them. God notices how His people treat one another, and He takes the side of the lean and the bruised. Anyone who leads, teaches, or simply has influence among believers should hear Ezekiel's question: are we feeding the flock or feeding ourselves?

Jesus sent His disciples out "as lambs among wolves" (Luke 10:3). His people remain vulnerable in the world. Their safety rests in a Shepherd who has already faced the sword for them. When the beloved in the Song of Solomon asks where the shepherd feeds his flock, she is told to follow the footsteps of the flock (Song of Solomon 1:8). Applied carefully to believers, the line suggests that we find the Shepherd's pasture by walking where His faithful sheep have walked before us.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 27:23: "Be diligent to know the state of your flocks, And attend to your herds." Faithful care of what is entrusted to us begins with knowing its condition.

  • Psalm 80:1: The psalmist appeals to the Shepherd of Israel as the One who leads Joseph like a flock. In a time of national distress, the people call on God to act as their Shepherd again.

  • Jeremiah 50:6: "My people have been lost sheep. Their shepherds have led them astray... They have forgotten their resting place." Israel's exile is traced to leaders who led the flock away from God.

  • 2 Samuel 12:2–4: In Nathan's parable, a rich man with very many flocks takes a poor man's only lamb. The flock's abundance exposes the rich man's greed, and through him David's sin against Uriah.

  • Isaiah 63:11: Isaiah remembers Moses as the shepherd of God's flock, recalling the Exodus as God's own shepherding of His people.

  • Zechariah 11:4–7: The prophet is told to shepherd "the flock for slaughter," a flock betrayed by its own leaders. The scene exposes shepherds who "do not pity" the flock (Zechariah 11:5).
