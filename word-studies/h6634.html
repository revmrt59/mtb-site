WILL, CHOOSE, WISH - (tsᵉbâʼ, Strong's H6634)

Summary

Tsᵉbâʼ is an Aramaic verb that means to will, choose, or wish. It describes someone wanting an outcome and deciding on it. The word appears only in the Aramaic chapters of Daniel, and nearly every use sits inside one argument. That argument concerns whose will actually governs the world. The NKJV translates it "will," "chooses," and "wished," so English readers easily miss that one word ties these passages together.

The word tells Daniel's story by changing who does the willing. Daniel 4 describes the Most High, who "does according to His will" in heaven and on earth and gives the kingdom "to whomever He will." Daniel 5:19 uses the same verb four times for Nebuchadnezzar, who executed, spared, promoted, and demoted whomever he wished. Daniel 7:19 uses it again when Daniel himself wished to know the truth about a terrifying vision. A king's unchecked will is placed beside God's will, which no one can restrain. The book then shows that God's choosing ends with one like a son of man receiving an everlasting kingdom. For believers who live under powers they cannot control, this small word answers a large question about who is in charge.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Most of the Old Testament is written in Hebrew, but parts of Ezra and Daniel are written in Aramaic. Aramaic is a closely related language that was used for international business and government in the Babylonian and Persian empires. Daniel signals the change of language himself: the Chaldeans "spoke to the king in Aramaic" (Daniel 2:4). The book stays in Aramaic until the end of chapter 7. Tsᵉbâʼ belongs to that Aramaic section. Nine of its ten uses come from the court narratives of Daniel 4–5, the stories set in the palaces of Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar. The tenth comes from a different kind of passage: the vision Daniel himself received "in the first year of Belshazzar king of Babylon" (Daniel 7:1). In the court narratives, the question of whose will governs events is never abstract. It decides who lives and who dies.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tsᵉbâʼ is an ordinary word for wanting or choosing. The word itself carries no special theological meaning. Its weight in Daniel comes from the people who exercise the will it describes. Two senses can be distinguished:

1. To will or choose an outcome and decide on it, especially by someone with authority to act. Examples are God giving the kingdom "to whomever He will" (Daniel 4:17) and Nebuchadnezzar acting on whomever he wished (Daniel 5:19).

2. To wish or desire something personally. Daniel uses it of his longing to understand a vision: "Then I wished to know the truth about the fourth beast" (Daniel 7:19).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word has three kinds of subject in Daniel. Five uses describe God's sovereign choice over kingdoms (Daniel 4:17, 25, 32, 35; 5:21). Four describe Nebuchadnezzar's unrestrained royal power, and all four appear in a single verse (Daniel 5:19). One describes Daniel's personal desire (Daniel 7:19).

The NKJV varies its English wording, using "will" in Daniel 4:17 and "chooses" in 4:25. That variation can hide a deliberate repetition. In Daniel 4–5, the clause about God giving the kingdom to whomever He wills comes back almost like a refrain, and the same verb then describes a human king's whims. Readers who know that one Aramaic word stands behind "will," "chooses," and "wished" can see how directly Daniel sets the two wills against each other.

4. Theological Significance

God's will in these passages has a goal. Daniel 4:17 says that the sentence on Nebuchadnezzar is passed "in order that the living may know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men." God does not exercise His sovereignty in secret. He intends people to recognize it, and He humbles the proud so that the world can see who truly rules.

God's will also reaches higher and lower than any earthly power. Daniel 4:35 says He acts "in the army of heaven and among the inhabitants of the earth." Neither angelic powers nor human rulers stand outside His purpose. Earlier in the chapter, Daniel 4:17 shows that His choosing also reverses human ranking: the Most High gives the kingdom "to whomever He will, And sets over it the lowest of men." The Most High is not bound by human greatness. He can raise up the lowly and bring down the mighty because the kingdom belongs to Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Daniel 4:17 and 4:35 — the confession passes to the king's own lips. The refrain about God's will moves through four speakers in Daniel 4. It begins as the decree of the watchers in the king's dream (4:17). Daniel repeats it in his interpretation (4:25). A voice from heaven pronounces it as judgment falls (4:32). Finally Nebuchadnezzar confesses it himself after his sanity returns (4:35). The man who had boasted of the Babylon he built now says of God, "No one can restrain His hand or say to Him, 'What have You done?'" In the same breath he admits that "all the inhabitants of the earth are reputed as nothing," and he includes himself in that number. God's will was not simply announced to Nebuchadnezzar. By the end of the chapter, Nebuchadnezzar himself acknowledged it.

Daniel 5:19–21 — the king who did whatever he wished. Years later Daniel stands before Belshazzar and recalls Nebuchadnezzar's power: "Whomever he wished, he executed; whomever he wished, he kept alive; whomever he wished, he set up; and whomever he wished, he put down." Daniel uses the verb four times, so the sentence reads like a description of total control. The verse also begins by saying this majesty was something "He gave him." Even this unchecked human will had been delegated by God. Two verses later, Daniel tells how Nebuchadnezzar was driven from men until he knew that the Most High appoints over the kingdom whomever He chooses (5:21). The verb that described the king's whims now describes God's decision. Daniel is warning Belshazzar, who is drinking from the temple vessels that very night, that God overrules a king's will and outlasts it.

Daniel 7:19 — a will turned toward truth. The last Aramaic use moves from the court narratives to Daniel's own vision, which he dates to the first year of Belshazzar (7:1). Here the one doing the willing is neither God nor a tyrant. Daniel has seen a fourth beast "exceedingly dreadful, with its teeth of iron and its nails of bronze" (7:19). The visions troubled him (7:15). Even so, he did not try to look away. He "came near to one of those who stood by, and asked him the truth of all this" (7:16), and then he wished to know the truth about the fourth beast in particular. His desire to understand turned his fear into a question. The explanation he was given ends with God's promise that the kingdom will be given to "the saints of the Most High" (7:27).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The refrain that God gives the kingdom to whomever He wills raises a question: whom will He finally choose? In Daniel 4–5, the kingdom passes for a time to proud men, and one of them is given the mind of a beast. Daniel 7 then shows a series of beasts rising from the sea. Their rule ends when the Ancient of Days gives dominion, glory, and an everlasting kingdom to "One like the Son of Man" (Daniel 7:13–14). Later in the chapter, that kingdom is shared with the saints of the Most High (7:27). Daniel 7:13–14 does not use tsᵉbâʼ, but it completes the story the word has been telling. God's last choice is not another beastly empire but a truly human King from heaven.

Jesus took "Son of Man" as His own title. After His resurrection He declared that all authority in heaven and on earth had been given to Him (Matthew 28:18). This matches the reach of the will Nebuchadnezzar confessed in Daniel 4:35. The detail that God "sets over it the lowest of men" (4:17) also finds a fitting answer in Christ, who humbled Himself to the cross and was highly exalted by the Father (Philippians 2:8–9). Daniel does not present the phrase as a direct prophecy of Jesus, but it shows the same pattern of God raising the lowly. In Gethsemane, the Son also submitted His own will to the Father's (Luke 22:42). In Jesus, a human will finally agreed completely with God's will, which is the opposite of Nebuchadnezzar's pride.

7. Spirit & Life

Jesus taught His disciples to pray that the Father's will be done on earth as it is in heaven (Matthew 6:10). Nebuchadnezzar had to lose his mind before he learned the same truth: God does according to His will in heaven and among the inhabitants of the earth (Daniel 4:35). When believers pray the Lord's Prayer, they are agreeing in advance with what Babylon's king learned through humiliation.

For exiles in Babylon, and for believers under hostile governments today, Daniel 5:19 describes a familiar reality. Some people can do whatever they wish, and others suffer the consequences. Daniel does not dismiss that suffering. He shows that such power is given, limited, and temporary, and that the One who gave it can take it away in a single night. A believer's peace does not depend on the restraint of rulers. It depends on a will that no one can hold back.

Daniel's own use of the word adds another lesson. In the court narratives, Nebuchadnezzar's will was set on his own glory and led him into the madness of a beast. In the vision of chapter 7, Daniel's will was set on knowing God's truth, even about something terrifying. He brought his fear to God's messenger as a question and was given an explanation that led him to hope. What a person wants matters. God does not promise to explain every terrifying event His people face, but a heart that wants to understand His purposes can bring its fears to Him and hold on to what He has revealed: the kingdom belongs to the saints of the Most High.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word occurs only in the passages discussed above. Two uses in the chapter 4 refrain add to that picture:

  • Daniel 4:25 — Daniel repeats the watchers' decree to Nebuchadnezzar, telling him he will be driven out until he knows that the Most High gives the kingdom to whomever He chooses. The prophet repeats heaven's message without softening it for the king.

  • Daniel 4:32 — A voice from heaven repeats the same words as the sentence begins. Heaven's own voice confirms what Daniel had spoken, and the warning becomes judgment.
