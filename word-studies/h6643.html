GAZELLE, GLORY, BEAUTY - (tsᵉbîy, Strong's H6643)

Summary

The Hebrew word tsᵉbîy has two faces in the Old Testament. In some passages it means the gazelle, the swift, graceful animal of Israel's hills. In others it means splendor or glory: beauty that stands out and draws the eye. The gazelle appears in Israel's food laws, in descriptions of fast warriors, in a warning to escape a trap, and in the Song of Solomon, where the beloved comes "leaping upon the mountains." The splendor sense belongs mostly to the prophets. They use it for the proud glory of Babylon, Tyre, and Ephraim, for the Promised Land as "the glory of all lands," and for the coming Branch of the LORD.

The word's most important use comes in Isaiah 28. The prophet calls Ephraim's "glorious beauty" "a fading flower," and a few verses later promises that "the LORD of hosts will be For a crown of glory" to the remnant of His people. The same Hebrew word describes both kinds of glory. The difference lies in where the glory comes from. Human splendor wilts, and God humbles it. The glory that lasts is the Lord Himself, given to His people and finally revealed in Christ. Isaiah's contrast still asks the same question of every reader: whose glory are you wearing?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsᵉbîy is a Hebrew noun. Strong's places the gazelle and splendor under one number. Many Hebrew dictionaries treat them as two words that happen to sound alike, so any link between the animal and the idea of beauty is uncertain. Readers should not build lessons on a supposed connection between them. Each sense should be understood from its own passages.

The animal sense dominates the earlier books: Deuteronomy, Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Proverbs, and the Song of Solomon. The splendor sense is concentrated in Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel, along with David's lament in 2 Samuel 1:19.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Gazelle. This is the wild animal, known for its speed and grace and counted clean for eating (Deuteronomy 14:5; Proverbs 6:5).

2. Splendor, glory, beauty. This is something conspicuously beautiful or honorable, the pride and ornament of a people or place. NKJV renders it "glory," "glorious," "beauty," or "beautiful" (Isaiah 13:19; Ezekiel 20:6).

Other Hebrew words carry the idea of glory. Kābôd suggests weight and honor. Tif'ārâ suggests adornment or beauty. Tsᵉbîy leans toward the splendor that catches the eye and makes something prized.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Ordinary provision. In Deuteronomy the gazelle sets the standard for everyday meat. These laws distinguish ordinary meat, slaughtered and eaten "within all your gates," from the holy offerings, which had to be brought to the altar at the place the LORD would choose (Deuteronomy 12:15, 26–27). Meat eaten at home was like game: the unclean and the clean could eat it, "of the gazelle and the deer alike" (Deuteronomy 12:15). Deuteronomy 12:22 makes the same comparison: "Just as the gazelle and the deer are eaten, so you may eat them." A blemished firstborn animal was treated the same way (Deuteronomy 15:22). The gazelle was clean food but never a sacrifice. It belonged to the family table. Solomon's daily provisions included gazelles (1 Kings 4:23).

Swiftness. Asahel was as fast on his feet as a wild gazelle (2 Samuel 2:18). David's Gadite warriors were as swift as gazelles on the mountains (1 Chronicles 12:8).

The hunted animal. The gazelle is also prey. Isaiah 13:14 pictures those in Babylon scattering "as the hunted gazelle."

Conspicuous splendor. In the prophets, the word names whatever a nation holds up as its glory. That includes Babylon, "the glory of kingdoms" (Isaiah 13:19), Tyre's honor (Isaiah 23:9), and Moab's frontier cities (Ezekiel 25:9). It can also describe God's gifts: the land (Ezekiel 20:6) and Israel's ornaments (Ezekiel 7:20).

Isaiah 13 places both senses side by side. Babylon, called the "glory" of kingdoms in Isaiah 13:19, sees those within it fleeing like a hunted gazelle in Isaiah 13:14.

4. Theological Significance

The prophets use tsᵉbîy to show that God rules over every kind of human splendor. Babylon's glory "Will be as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah" (Isaiah 13:19). Isaiah says the Lord of hosts purposed to bring into contempt Tyre's pride and honor (Isaiah 23:9). Moab's prized cities are handed over (Ezekiel 25:9). The nations' glory is real, but it is borrowed and temporary, and God takes it away when it becomes a rival to Him.

The same word shows that God gives true splendor freely. The Promised Land was "the glory of all lands" because God "searched out" that land for His people (Ezekiel 20:6). Its glory rested on His choice and His oath, more than on its soil. Jeremiah 3:19 lets us hear the Father's longing behind the gift. God wanted to give "A beautiful heritage" and said, "You shall call Me, 'My Father,' And not turn away from Me." Jeremiah joins the gift of the beautiful land to God's desire that His people call Him Father and not turn away.

Daniel simply calls the land "the Glorious Land" (Daniel 8:9; 11:16, 41). In his visions, the place God set His affection on becomes the place hostile powers march against. The little horn grows "toward the Glorious Land." Daniel 11:45 locates the invading king's camp near the glorious holy mountain. Many readers see these last verses reaching beyond Antiochus IV, the Greek king who persecuted Judea in the second century BC, to the end of the age. Either way, Daniel's visions show a pattern: the land God marks as glorious draws the hostility of His enemies.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 28:1–5: two crowns. Isaiah speaks to Ephraim, the northern kingdom. Its capital, Samaria, sat on a hill above fertile valleys like a garland on a drinker's head. The prophet says: "Woe to the crown of pride, to the drunkards of Ephraim, Whose glorious beauty is a fading flower" (28:1). Verse 4 makes the image sharper. Ephraim's glory is "Like the first fruit before the summer," an early fig a passerby sees and "eats it up while it is still in his hand." The nation's glory would not merely fade slowly. The Lord's "mighty and strong one" (28:2), commonly identified with Assyria, would swallow it in a moment, and the proud crown would be "trampled underfoot" (28:3).

Then Isaiah turns: "In that day the LORD of hosts will be For a crown of glory and a diadem of beauty To the remnant of His people" (28:5). The "crown of pride" in verse 1 is answered by a "crown of glory." The fading flower is answered by a diadem of beauty. The same word, tsᵉbîy, describes both. God does not repair Ephraim's wilting garland. He offers Himself in its place. The remnant's glory is a Person, the Lord of hosts.

Isaiah 4:2 and Isaiah 53:2: the Branch. After describing Jerusalem's judgment, Isaiah promises: "In that day the Branch of the LORD shall be beautiful and glorious." Some readers take "Branch" as the land's renewed growth, since the verse goes on to mention "the fruit of the earth." Later prophets, however, use "Branch" for the coming Davidic King (Jeremiah 23:5; Zechariah 6:12). That usage supports reading Isaiah 4:2 as a promise of the Messiah.

The context matters. The Branch's splendor appears for "those of Israel who have escaped." It appears "When the Lord has washed away the filth of the daughters of Zion" (4:4). Those who are left "will be called holy" (4:3). The Branch's beauty and the people's cleansing come together.

Isaiah's plant imagery returns in Isaiah 53:2, where the Servant grows up "as a tender plant, And as a root out of dry ground." There, "There is no beauty that we should desire Him." Isaiah uses different Hebrew words in that verse. Still, the contrast is striking. The Branch who will be "beautiful and glorious" first appears as a root in dry ground that no one finds attractive.

Ezekiel 7:20: splendor turned into idols. God says of Israel, "As for the beauty of his ornaments, He set it in majesty." The beauty was God's gift, and He gave it honor. Ezekiel continues: "But they made from it The images of their abominations." So God declares, "I have made it Like refuse to them." The silver and gold God had given became the material for idols. Ezekiel 20 shows a related consequence of idolatry. God had sworn to bring Israel into "the glory of all lands" (Ezekiel 20:6), but because "their heart went after their idols," He swore in the wilderness that He would not bring that generation into it (Ezekiel 20:15–16). In Ezekiel 7 idolatry corrupts gifts already received; in Ezekiel 20 it keeps a generation from entering the gift at all.

Song of Solomon 2:8–9; 8:14: the gazelle who comes. The bride hears "The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes Leaping upon the mountains, Skipping upon the hills" (2:8). Next she says, "My beloved is like a gazelle," and he stands "behind our wall," "Gazing through the lattice" (2:9). The gazelle image shows his eagerness. Mountains do not slow him, and he arrives at the very wall of her house. The book closes with her plea: "Make haste, my beloved, And be like a gazelle" (8:14). The Song ends with love still longing for the beloved to come.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The two crowns of Isaiah 28 run through the whole Bible. Peter quotes Isaiah 40 to say that the glory of man is like the flower of the grass, which falls away (1 Peter 1:24). He then promises faithful shepherds "the crown of glory that does not fade away" when the Chief Shepherd appears (1 Peter 5:4). Isaiah's fading flower and unfading crown come to rest in Christ.

Christ also fulfills the Branch paradox. The One promised to be "beautiful and glorious" wore a crown of thorns. Hebrews 2:9 says that Jesus, who suffered death, is now crowned with glory and honor. John testifies that the disciples beheld His glory, full of grace and truth (John 1:14). The glory of the Branch was hidden in the root out of dry ground.

The Song's gazelle has long been read by Christians as a picture of Christ's love for His people. That reading is an analogy rather than a stated fulfillment, but the parallel is strong. The Song's last words, "Make haste," sound much like the Bible's own closing prayer: "Come, Lord Jesus" (Revelation 22:20).

The "Glorious Land" belongs to this same story. It is where God's promises took root, where the Messiah came, and where the nations' hostility was focused. It points ahead to the inheritance God will finally give His people.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 28 gives us a searching question: whose glory are you wearing? Ephraim's crown looked impressive from the valley, yet it was already wilting. Reputation, beauty, success, and influence can all become a "crown of pride." God offers something better than a stronger garland. He offers to be our crown Himself. To accept that offer, a believer has to stop guarding a fading glory.

Ezekiel 7:20 warns those whom God has blessed. Israel's idols were made from God's own gifts. Our gifts can become our idols too: talents, possessions, families, even ministry. When a gift is valued above the Giver, God may strip it of the very splendor we prized.

The gazelle teaches how to respond to a trap. Proverbs 6:5 tells the one who has rashly guaranteed another person's debt, "Deliver yourself like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter." A gazelle has no claws or armor. It survives by running immediately. Some snares, like foolish entanglement and temptation, are meant to be fled rather than fought (compare 2 Timothy 2:22).

The Song shows the other side of the gazelle's speed. Christ's love for His people is eager, not reluctant. He comes over the mountains and stands at the lattice. A believer can answer that love the way the bride does, with longing: "Make haste."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Samuel 1:19 — David opens his lament for Saul and Jonathan by calling them the beauty of Israel, now slain on the high places. Israel's glory lay dead on the mountains of Gilboa.

  • Song of Solomon 2:7; 3:5 — The bride charges the daughters of Jerusalem "by the gazelles" not to awaken love before its time. The swift, shy gazelle pictures love that cannot be forced.

  • Isaiah 24:16 — In the middle of worldwide judgment, songs are heard from the ends of the earth: glory to the righteous.

  • Ezekiel 26:20 — While Tyre goes down to the pit, God says He will establish glory in the land of the living. Tyre's splendor falls, while the glory God establishes endures.

  • Daniel 11:41 — The invading king enters the Glorious Land, showing again that the land God honors is the land His enemies attack.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3519 (kābôd, glory) — Canonical pairing in the splendor passages; a different Hebrew word, not a relative. Isaiah 4:2 joins this word with tsᵉbîy: the Branch shall be "beautiful and glorious."

  • H8597 (tif'ārâ, beauty, glory) — Canonical pairing in the splendor passages; a different Hebrew word, not a relative. In Isaiah 28:1, Ephraim's "glorious beauty" combines the two words. In 28:5, the Lord is a "crown of glory" (tsᵉbîy) and a "diadem of beauty" (tif'ārâ). Isaiah 13:19 pairs the words again for Babylon.

  • H6646 (tsᵉbiyyâ, female gazelle) — Lexical relative, belonging to the gazelle sense only. This is the feminine form of the animal word, used in the Song's "twins of a gazelle" (Song of Solomon 4:5).
