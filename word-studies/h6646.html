GAZELLE - (tsᵉbîyâh, Strong's H6646)

Summary

The Hebrew word tsᵉbîyâh means gazelle, and specifically a female gazelle. The gazelle is a slender, swift wild animal of Israel's hill country. This word appears only twice in the Bible, both times in the Song of Solomon and both times in the same phrase. The bridegroom tells his bride that her breasts are "like two fawns, twins of a gazelle" (Song of Solomon 4:5; 7:3). The picture is gentle. Twin fawns are soft, perfectly matched, full of life, and quick to shy away from a stranger. The image suggests beauty that is tender, reserved, and offered only to the beloved.

The word's footprint is small, but it carries real weight. Scripture speaks about married delight with warmth and without embarrassment. Later in the Song, after a scene in which the bride searches the city for her absent beloved, the bridegroom's praise returns in these same words and with the same wonder. That faithful delight belongs within the Bible's larger picture of marriage, which Paul says speaks "concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). Read in that light, the gazelle can help believers see that the God who created married love also delights in His people with a love that does not wear thin.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsᵉbîyâh is the feminine form of tsᵉbîy (H6643), the more common Hebrew word for gazelle. Gazelles were familiar to Israel. They were clean animals that could be eaten (Deuteronomy 12:15). They were also proverbial for speed: Asahel "was as fleet of foot as a wild gazelle" (2 Samuel 2:18). Above all, they were wild creatures of the open field (Song of Solomon 2:7), more often admired from a distance than seen up close.

Both occurrences come in a particular kind of love poem. In these poems the lover praises his beloved one feature at a time, using pictures drawn from nature. The pictures do not work like photographs. A woman does not look like two fawns. The comparisons convey qualities instead: softness, grace, liveliness, and a modest shyness. Even a passerby knows how quickly young fawns shy away from a stranger.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tsᵉbîyâh has one plain meaning: a female gazelle, the wild animal itself. The word carries no hidden spiritual definition. Its significance comes from how the poet uses the animal as a picture. In both verses, the young of the gazelle, "twins of a gazelle," describe the bride's beauty as gentle, matched, and alive.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Each time the word appears, it sits inside a comparison: "Your two breasts are like two fawns, twins of a gazelle." The word "twins" adds the idea of perfect balance, two that belong together and match each other. The word "fawns" adds youth and softness.

The two settings differ in instructive ways. In chapter 4 the bridegroom's praise moves from her eyes downward (4:1–5), and the gazelle image ends with the fawns "which feed among the lilies." In chapter 7 his praise begins at her feet and moves upward (7:1–5), and the phrase about the lilies drops away.

The imagery also runs in both directions. The bride uses the masculine form of the word for her beloved: "My beloved is like a gazelle or a young stag" (Song of Solomon 2:9; see also 2:17; 8:14). Each sees the other through the same picture of wild grace.

4. Theological Significance

These verses show that God is not embarrassed by the love He designed. In Eden, "they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25). The Song speaks out of that same unashamed goodness, and it does so with restraint. The gazelle image honors the body instead of cheapening it. It praises the wife's softness and modesty without crude description. Proverbs gives the same blessing in plain counsel: "Let your fountain be blessed, and rejoice with the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18). It even uses parallel animal imagery: "As a loving deer and a graceful doe… always be enraptured with her love" (Proverbs 5:19).

The gazelle family also teaches something about the nature of love. The bride charges the daughters of Jerusalem "by the gazelles or by the does of the field, do not stir up nor awaken love until it pleases" (Song of Solomon 2:7; 3:5). Love, like a gazelle, cannot be forced or manufactured. When it comes, it comes freely and alive. The bride's beauty carries that same unforced vitality.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 4:5 — the fawn comparison in chapter 4. This verse comes soon after the poem speaks of King Solomon "on the day of his wedding" (3:11). The bridegroom speaks with fresh wonder, and his description in 4:1–5 closes with the fawns "which feed among the lilies." Elsewhere the Song links lilies with the bride herself, an image that suggests her purity and set-apart place: "Like a lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters" (2:2). Soon after, he uses a separate image for her, calling her "a garden enclosed" (4:12), kept for him alone. Read alongside these images, the fawns grazing among lilies can be seen as a picture of beauty nourished in a pure and protected setting.

Song of Solomon 7:3 — the same words, after the search. Between chapters 4 and 7 the poem places its most painful scene. The bride is slow to open the door. Her beloved withdraws, and she searches the city for him and is wounded by the watchmen (5:2–8). After that search scene, the bridegroom's voice returns, and he does something telling. He repeats, almost word for word, the praise he spoke in chapter 4 (compare 6:5–7 with 4:1–3). Then in chapter 7 he speaks again of "two fawns, twins of a gazelle."

The poem does not say how much time passes between these scenes, and its chapters may not follow a strict order of events. What the reader can see is that the praise recurs on purpose. The scene of searching and loss has not dulled the bridegroom's wonder. He repeats the fawn comparison of chapter 4 with the same delight, and he speaks as tenderly as he did at first.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never quotes the Song of Solomon. It does, however, give marriage a meaning that reaches beyond itself. After citing Genesis, "the two shall become one flesh," Paul adds, "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32). Marriage, as God designed it, was always meant to point to a greater union.

The Song's picture of a bridegroom's undimmed delight therefore rightly resonates with Christ's love for His people. Paul says Christ gave Himself for the church "that He might sanctify and cleanse her… that He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle" (Ephesians 5:26–27). The prophets had already spoken this way of God: as a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so the Lord rejoices over His people (Isaiah 62:5). The return to the gazelle image in Song of Solomon 7, after the scene of searching and separation, fits the pattern of God's dealings with Israel and with the church. His love outlasts our failures and greets His people again with joy.

One further echo is worth noticing, though it is suggestive rather than stated. The bride says of her beloved, "He feeds his flock among the lilies" (Song of Solomon 2:16; compare 6:3). The fawns of 4:5 are pictured with the same words, "which feed among the lilies," so the image recurs in 2:16, 4:5 and 6:3. The repeated picture quietly links the bride's beauty with her beloved.

7. Spirit & Life

The fawn comparison carries a quiet lesson. Fawns are gentle and shy, and in the Song this comparison is spoken by her beloved, to whom she belongs. The bride can say, "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (Song of Solomon 2:16). That exclusive affection is a fitting picture of a believer's devotion. By way of comparison, the heart's deepest intimacy is reserved for Christ, held back from the passing world and offered freely to Him.

The lilies suggest another lesson. As noted above, the image of feeding "among the lilies" is used both for the fawns of 4:5 and for the beloved who "feeds his flock" (2:16; 6:3). The Song does not explain that repeated image, but applied to the Christian life it is suggestive. A believer's spiritual life does not keep itself fresh. It is sustained by staying near the Bridegroom, in His Word and in prayer. Grace comes from Christ Himself, and a clean life grows out of nearness to Him rather than producing grace on its own. The common pastures of the world cannot give what is found only with Him.

Some readers have also seen in the matched "twins" a picture of balance in the Christian life. Christ came "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14), and faith works "together with" its works (James 2:22). This is an analogy rather than the passage's meaning, but it is a fitting one. Healthy spiritual life keeps together what God has paired.

Finally, Song of Solomon 7:3 offers a direct application for married believers. Years and hurts tempt a husband or wife to stop saying what they once said freely. This bridegroom says it again. Spouses who renew their words of delight imitate the Lord, whose love for His own does not grow stale.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Both occurrences of tsᵉbîyâh (Song of Solomon 4:5; 7:3) are discussed above. The following verses use other Hebrew words from the same family of images. They illuminate the picture but are not occurrences of this word.

  • Song of Solomon 2:9 — "My beloved is like a gazelle or a young stag." The bride gives her beloved the gazelle image she later receives from him.

  • Song of Solomon 2:7 — The charge "by the gazelles or by the does of the field" ties these animals to love that must awaken freely.

  • 2 Samuel 2:18 — Asahel, "fleet of foot as a wild gazelle," shows the speed for which gazelles were known.

  • Habakkuk 3:19 — "He will make my feet like deer's feet." A related animal pictures the sure footing God gives on high and dangerous ground.

  • Proverbs 5:19 — "As a loving deer and a graceful doe." Wisdom uses similar imagery to bless faithful married love.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6643 (tsᵉbîy), gazelle — the lexical relative of this word. It is the masculine and more common form, used by the bride of her beloved (Song of Solomon 2:9).

  • H0355, doe or hind — a distinct word from the same family of images. It appears alongside the gazelles in the Song's repeated charge about awakening love (Song of Solomon 2:7).
