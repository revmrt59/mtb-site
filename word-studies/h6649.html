ZIBEON - (Tsibʻôwn, Strong's H6649)

Summary

Zibeon was a Horite chief who lived in Mount Seir, the rugged highlands south of the Dead Sea that later became the land of Edom. He was one of the sons of Seir the Horite, the clan leaders who held the land before Esau's descendants took it over (Genesis 36:20, 29). His family joined Esau's when Esau married Aholibamah, a descendant of Zibeon usually traced through his son Anah (Genesis 36:2, 14, 24–25), though which Anah is meant is debated. The meaning of his name is uncertain. The most commonly proposed meaning is "hyena," and some connect it with a Hebrew root meaning "to dye" or "to color." Animal names were common among ancient clans, so "hyena" is plausible, but Scripture itself does not explain the name.

Zibeon appears only in the family records of Genesis 36 and their retelling in 1 Chronicles 1. One small detail stands out in those lists. His son Anah "found the water in the wilderness as he pastured the donkeys of his father Zibeon" (Genesis 36:24). The record has no worship, prophecy, or encounter with God in it. Even so, the care with which Genesis preserves this chief's family shows that the God of Abraham sees and governs the peoples outside Israel's story. Moses later recalls that Esau's descendants dispossessed the Horites, and he presents this as evidence that the LORD gives lands to nations as He wills (Deuteronomy 2:12, 22).

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

Zibeon (Hebrew צִבְעוֹן, Tsibʻôwn) is usually identified as one individual. He was a son of Seir the Horite and a chief of the Horites in Mount Seir. He was the father of Ajah and Anah (Genesis 36:24; 1 Chronicles 1:40) and an ancestor of Aholibamah, one of Esau's wives. He belongs to the patriarchal era, the generation in which Esau settled in Seir. He appears in several overlapping genealogical lists; Barnes distinguishes a Hivite namesake in Genesis 36:2, but the references are usually taken as one man.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. Two possibilities are commonly proposed. Brown-Driver-Briggs proposes "hyena," which fits a known pattern of Semitic clan names taken from animals. Strong's instead links the name to the root tsaba (צָבַע), "to dye," giving a sense like "colored" or "variegated." Neither meaning is confirmed by the biblical text, and Genesis makes no wordplay on the name. It would be a mistake to read either meaning as a comment on Zibeon's character.

The family lists need to be read carefully, because names repeat. One Anah is Zibeon's brother, a son of Seir (Genesis 36:20). Another Anah is Zibeon's son, and Genesis distinguishes him by saying, "This was the Anah who found the water" (36:24). Aholibamah is the daughter of this second Anah (36:25). So when Genesis 36:2 calls her "the daughter of Anah, the daughter of Zibeon," it most naturally means she was Zibeon's granddaughter. Hebrew can use "daughter" for a female descendant, and Anah himself is male.

Genesis 36:2 also calls Zibeon "the Hivite," while verses 20 and 29 call his family Horites. In Hebrew the two words, חִוִּי (Hivite) and חֹרִי (Horite), differ by letters that look very similar (waw and resh). One proposed explanation, represented in Ellicott's commentary, treats "Hivite" as an early copying variation, which would make Zibeon a Horite as the rest of the chapter does. That remains a proposal, not an established textual fact.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

The Horites were the established inhabitants of Mount Seir. Genesis 14:6 already places them there in Abraham's day. Genesis 36 introduces Zibeon among "the sons of Seir the Horite who inhabited the land" (36:20) and lists him among the "chiefs of the Horites" (36:29). The word translated "chief" marks a clan leader, a man with standing, people, and property.

Zibeon's household enters the biblical story through marriage. Esau "took his wives from the daughters of Canaan," and among them was "Aholibamah the daughter of Anah, the daughter of Zibeon" (Genesis 36:2). She bore Esau sons who themselves became chiefs in Edom (Genesis 36:5, 14, 18). The marriage tied Esau's family to the land of Seir. Esau eventually settled in Mount Seir apart from Jacob, because the land could not support both of their herds (Genesis 36:6–8).

One line in the genealogy gives a glimpse of daily life. Zibeon owned donkeys, and his son Anah was pasturing them when he "found the water in the wilderness" (Genesis 36:24). The Hebrew word behind "water" appears only here in the Bible. The Latin Vulgate understood it as warm springs, and older English tradition rendered it "mules." Many modern interpreters favor hot springs, which suit the geology of the Dead Sea rift. The meaning cannot be settled with certainty. Either way, the family remembered the discovery well enough that it was written into the record.

The Horites did not keep their land. Moses notes that "the descendants of Esau dispossessed them and destroyed them from before them, and dwelt in their place" (Deuteronomy 2:12). He adds that the LORD Himself did this for Esau's descendants (Deuteronomy 2:22). Centuries later the Chronicler copied Zibeon's family into Israel's great opening genealogy (1 Chronicles 1:38, 40), preserving the record of Seir's chiefs for a people returning from exile.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states three things plainly. Zibeon was a real Horite chief. His descendant became Esau's wife. His people were later displaced by Edom under God's hand (Deuteronomy 2:12, 22). His main role in the Bible is genealogical. He helps explain how Esau's line became rooted in Seir while Jacob's line continued toward Canaan and the promise given to Abraham.

From this record we can draw a careful conclusion. Genesis 36 deliberately traces the nations that branched away from the chosen family, and it does so with surprising detail. God did not ignore Esau or the peoples around him. Deuteronomy 2:5 says the LORD gave Mount Seir to Esau as a possession, and the Horites' fate falls under that same sovereign rule. Moses uses this history to steady Israel. The God who had settled Esau in Seir could certainly bring Israel into the land He had promised them (Deuteronomy 2:12).

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

Scripture establishes no direct connection between Zibeon and Christ, and none should be invented. His importance for the larger story is indirect. He belongs to the record of Esau's line, and that record shows God's purposes moving forward through Jacob even as other nations are named and governed.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Genesis 36 can look like a page to skim, but Genesis 36:24 slows the reader down. Zibeon's son Anah is watching his father's donkeys and comes upon water in the wilderness, and God's Word keeps the memory. The Bible's history happened among real families doing ordinary work in real places. The God who rules nations also takes notice of a herdsman's day.

Zibeon's story also shows something about how God governs. The Horites were the established power in Seir, yet Moses says the LORD removed them and gave their land to others (Deuteronomy 2:22). Earthly standing is held only for a time. Esau's marriage into this powerful family helped anchor him in Seir. Earlier, Esau's foreign marriages had already brought grief to Isaac and Rebekah (Genesis 26:34–35). God's promise ran through Jacob's line, and no alliance with a chief of Seir could change that.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 36:2, 14 — Aholibamah, Esau's wife, is traced to Anah and Zibeon, and Zibeon is called "the Hivite."

  • Genesis 36:20–21, 29 — Zibeon is listed among the sons of Seir and the chiefs of the Horites.

  • Genesis 36:24–25 — Zibeon's sons, Anah's discovery of water while tending Zibeon's donkeys, and Aholibamah as Anah's daughter.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:38, 40 — The Chronicler preserves Zibeon's place in the genealogy of Seir.

  • Deuteronomy 2:12, 22 — The Horites are dispossessed by Esau's descendants, and Moses credits the LORD with doing it.
