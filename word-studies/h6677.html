NECK, NECKS - (tsavvâʼr, Strong's H6677)

Summary

The Hebrew word tsavvâʼr means the neck, especially the back of the neck where a yoke sits, a chain hangs, or a weeping brother lays his face. It is an ordinary body word, but Scripture places some of its most important scenes there. Esau embraces Jacob and falls on his neck, Joseph and Benjamin weep on one another's necks, and Joseph falls on the neck of his father Jacob. Jeremiah walks through Jerusalem wearing a wooden yoke on his neck. Joshua's captains set their feet on the necks of defeated kings. Pharaoh hangs a gold chain around Joseph's neck, and the bridegroom in the Song of Solomon praises his bride's neck as a tower.

These scenes show that the neck is where a person is most exposed and where allegiance is shown. It can be stiffened against God, bowed under the yoke of sin's consequences, pinned beneath a conqueror's foot, or embraced in tears of reunion. God rules over the yokes nations bear. He disciplines with yokes, and He breaks them. In Jesus' parable the father runs and falls on his son's neck, and Jesus Himself invites weary people to take His yoke. The place that might have borne a sword or a chain becomes the place where grace lands.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsavvâʼr is a Hebrew noun for the neck. It occurs mostly in Genesis, the Prophets, and the poetic books. In an agricultural world the neck was the part of an ox that carried the wooden yoke, and that everyday picture supplies much of the word's figurative use. Hebrew has another common word for the back of the neck, ʽoreph, and that is the word behind Israel's familiar description as "stiff-necked." Tsavvâʼr appears more often with burdens, embraces, and ornaments.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The physical neck of a person or animal, as when Joseph "fell on his neck and wept" (Genesis 46:29), or when God describes the war horse's neck in Job 39:19.

2. The neck as the place where a yoke, bond, or burden rests, used for servitude and subjection, as in the "yoke of iron on your neck" of Deuteronomy 28:48.

3. The neck as an expression of bearing or attitude, especially pride. Psalm 75:5 warns, "Do not speak with a stiff neck," and in Job 15:26 the NKJV renders the word "stubbornly."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning depends on what touches the neck. When another person's arms reach it, the scene is reunion (Genesis 33:4). When a yoke is placed on it, the subject is servitude, whether imposed by enemies or ordered by God (Jeremiah 27:8). When a chain is hung on it, the subject is honor (Genesis 41:42) or love (Song of Solomon 1:10). When a foot is set on it, the subject is defeat (Joshua 10:24).

The prophets also use the neck as a measure of depth. Assyria floods Judah until "he will reach up to the neck" (Isaiah 8:8), and God's breath is "like an overflowing stream, which reaches up to the neck" (Isaiah 30:28). The water rises almost to the point of drowning.

The NKJV occasionally translates the word by its sense rather than literally. In Lamentations 5:5, "They pursue at our heels" reads in Hebrew more like upon our necks we are pursued. Enemies were close enough to breathe on the survivors, who "labor and have no rest."

4. Theological Significance

The God of Israel is Lord over the yokes His people bear. Deuteronomy 28:48 warns that if Israel turns from Him, "He will put a yoke of iron on your neck." When Babylon later rules Judah, the reason is not Babylon's strength. The LORD has sent the yoke. Lamentations 1:14 confesses this from inside the disaster: "The yoke of my transgressions was bound; they were woven together by His hands, and thrust upon my neck." Jerusalem's sins became the very cords of her bondage, and God's hand tied them.

Hosea 10:11 shows what that bondage does to a people. Ephraim was "a trained heifer that loves to thresh grain," which was light work, since a threshing ox was allowed to eat as it walked. God says, "I harnessed her fair neck, I will make Ephraim pull a plow." A neck made for honor was fitted with farm harness. Idolatry promised independence and delivered drudgery.

The posture of the neck also reveals the heart. The wicked man in Job 15:26 goes "running stubbornly against Him," charging at God neck-first. Psalm 75:5 forbids speaking "with a stiff neck." Pride holds the neck rigid before God, while the reunions of Genesis show necks bent toward one another in tears.

God's judgment is also measured. The flood in Isaiah 8:8 reaches the neck and stops short of the head, and the next line names the land "Immanuel," God with us. Assyria overran Judah but did not take Jerusalem. Judgment can come terrifyingly close, yet God still sets its limit.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The reunions in Genesis 33, 45, and 46. The neck first carries a burden in Genesis when Isaac tells Esau, "you shall serve your brother; And it shall come to pass, when you become restless, That you shall break his yoke from your neck" (Genesis 27:40). The yoke is Esau's subjection to Jacob, which Esau would one day throw off. The same chapter has already shown Jacob wearing goatskins on "the smooth part of his neck" to deceive their father (Genesis 27:16). Twenty years later Jacob prays, "I fear him, lest he come and attack me and the mother with the children" (Genesis 32:11). Instead, "Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept" (Genesis 33:4). The neck where deception had been staged became the neck where a brother wept.

Joseph's story repeats the gesture twice. First Joseph "fell on his brother Benjamin's neck and wept, and Benjamin wept on his neck" (Genesis 45:14). Then, after more than twenty years, Joseph meets his father Jacob and "fell on his neck and wept on his neck a good while" (Genesis 46:29). These embraces are not polite greetings. They are reunions after long separation, and the tears fall on the exposed neck.

Jeremiah's yokes in Jeremiah 27–28. God tells Jeremiah, "Make for yourselves bonds and yokes, and put them on your neck" (Jeremiah 27:2), as a sign that the nations must serve Nebuchadnezzar. Those who bow their necks will stay in their land (Jeremiah 27:11–12). The prophet Hananiah then breaks the wooden yoke off Jeremiah's neck and promises freedom within two years (Jeremiah 28:10–11). God answers that Hananiah has broken yokes of wood but made yokes of iron in their place, and He will put an iron yoke on the necks of the nations (Jeremiah 28:13–14). Refusing God's discipline did not remove it. It made it heavier. Yet the same book promises that in God's time He will break the yoke from Israel's neck and burst her bonds (Jeremiah 30:8). Freedom would come from God's hand, not from a comfortable prophecy.

Feet on the necks of kings in Joshua 10:24. After the defeat of five Amorite kings, Joshua tells his captains, "Come near, put your feet on the necks of these kings." Joshua did not keep the gesture for himself. He called the captains forward to share in the sign of victory, and then he explained what it meant: "Do not be afraid, nor be dismayed; be strong and of good courage, for thus the LORD will do to all your enemies against whom you fight" (Joshua 10:25). The victory belonged to the LORD, and the captains who fought were given a share in its sign.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus' parable closely echoes the Genesis reunion. In His parable the son is "still a great way off" when "his father saw him and had compassion, and ran and fell on his neck and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). Luke writes in Greek and does not quote Genesis, but the narrative resemblance to Esau's run toward Jacob is close: a run, an embrace on the neck, and a kiss. The resemblance invites the comparison without showing direct borrowing. Esau welcomed a brother who had wronged him. Jesus shows the Father running to welcome a son who had wronged Him.

The yoke-breaking promises also point forward. Isaiah 10:27 says the oppressor's yoke will be taken "from your neck, and the yoke will be destroyed because of the anointing oil." Habakkuk 3:13 celebrates God going out "for salvation with Your Anointed," striking the house of the wicked "from foundation to neck." Jesus is the Anointed One, and He announced liberty to captives. Isaiah 52:2 calls the captive daughter of Zion to "loose yourself from the bonds of your neck," a summons the good news of redemption answers.

Joshua's captains also anticipate Christ's victory. The LORD tells David's Lord, "Sit at My right hand, till I make Your enemies Your footstool" (Psalm 110:1), and Paul tells ordinary believers, "the God of peace will crush Satan under your feet shortly" (Romans 16:20). As with Joshua, the Captain wins the battle and calls His people near to share in it.

Jesus also offers a different yoke. He invites the weary to take His yoke and learn from Him, because His yoke is easy and His burden light (Matthew 11:29–30).

7. Spirit & Life

Every neck bears something. Israel threw off the LORD's gentle rule and ended up under Babylon's iron. Lamentations shows that sin weaves its own yoke, so the real question is whose yoke a person will bear. Nehemiah 3:5 gives a quiet warning: while others rebuilt Jerusalem's wall, "their nobles did not put their shoulders to the work of their Lord." The NKJV says "shoulders," but the Hebrew word is the one for necks. These men would not bend their necks to the work. Christ's yoke is a yoke of service, and pride is what keeps a neck from accepting it.

Proverbs adds a contrast with a different Hebrew word for the neck or throat. There wisdom's instruction becomes "chains about your neck" (Proverbs 1:9), and "mercy and truth" are to be bound "around your neck" (Proverbs 3:3). Wisdom welcomed becomes an ornament. In the light of Lamentations 1:14, wisdom refused can end as a yoke.

Grace turns the neck into a place of honor. Joseph went from prison to wearing Pharaoh's gold chain (Genesis 41:42). The bridegroom in the Song praises the neck itself, not only its jewelry: "Your neck is like the tower of David, built for an armory, on which hang a thousand bucklers" (Song of Solomon 4:4). One link of her necklace has "ravished my heart" (Song of Solomon 4:9). Applied to Christ and His church, this suggests a people who stand upright with the secure confidence of the beloved, defended by Him, rather than with the rigid pride of Psalm 75:5. And when a sinner comes home, the Father does not strike the exposed neck. He runs, falls on it, and kisses His child.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 41:22 — Strength dwells in Leviathan's neck, and fear goes before him. The creature's terrifying strength displays the power of the God who made it.

  • Judges 8:26–27 — Gideon's plunder included the chains around the necks of the Midianite kings' camels, besides the gold earrings he had requested from his men. Gideon made the earrings into an ephod, and that ephod "became a snare to Gideon and to his house" (Judges 8:27).

  • Job 39:19 — God asks Job whether he clothed the horse's neck with thunder, pointing to the Creator's strength displayed in His creatures.

  • Song of Solomon 7:4 — "Your neck is like an ivory tower," praise of the beloved's dignity and grace.

  • Micah 2:3 — God plans a disaster "from which you cannot remove your necks," a yoke the proud cannot shake off.

  • Ezekiel 21:29 — The sword falls on "the necks of the wicked," the neck as the place where judgment strikes.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6203 (ʽoreph), back of the neck — A distinct Hebrew synonym rather than a word from the same family. It is the word behind the idiom "stiff-necked" (for example, Exodus 32:9), describing Israel's resistance to God. It overlaps in meaning with tsavvâʼr, which leans more toward burdens, embraces, and adornment.

  • H1621 (gargerot), neck imagery in Proverbs — A different Hebrew word for the neck or throat is used where wisdom, mercy, and truth are bound as ornaments (Proverbs 1:9; 3:3). This is a thematic pairing, not lexical evidence for tsavvâʼr.
