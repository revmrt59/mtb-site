FLOURISH, BLOSSOM, GAZE - (tsûwts, Strong's H6692)

Summary

The Hebrew verb tsûwts means to blossom or to flourish. In one verse it is translated "gazing." Most of its uses picture a plant bursting into flower. Scripture uses that moment of bloom to tell two very different stories. Grass flourishes in the morning and withers by evening, and so does human life (Psalm 90:6; Psalm 103:15). The wicked flourish too, but only "that they may be destroyed forever" (Psalm 92:7). As doom comes on the land of Israel, "The rod has blossomed, Pride has budded" (Ezekiel 7:10).

Against that fading bloom, God causes things to flourish that no one else could. A dead almond staff blossomed overnight to show which priest He had chosen (Numbers 17:8). God promised that the crown of His Anointed King would flourish, and that Israel would "blossom and bud, and fill the face of the world with fruit" (Isaiah 27:6). The word asks every reader what is blossoming in their life, and whether it is the passing flower of human pride or the life God Himself brings out of death.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsûwts appears nine times in the Old Testament: once in the story of Aaron's rod, five times in the Psalms, once in the Song of Solomon, and once each in Isaiah and Ezekiel. It closely resembles the Hebrew noun tsiyts, which can mean a flower and also names the gold plate the high priest wore on his forehead (Exodus 28:36).

Strong's dictionary links the verb's two English meanings, to glance and to blossom, treating flowering as a kind of shining forth, and it explains the noun tsiyts as something glistening, whether a burnished plate or a bright flower. Some Hebrew dictionaries instead list two separate verbs spelled alike. If Strong's connection holds, flowering and gleaming belong together, which adds depth to Psalm 132.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To blossom or flourish: a plant breaking into flower, or a person, people, or thing compared to one. Numbers 17:8 uses it literally of Aaron's rod. The Psalms and the prophets use it figuratively of human life, the wicked, a crown, and Israel.

2. To gaze or peer: a quick, eager look through an opening. This sense appears only in Song of Solomon 2:9, where the beloved is "gazing through the lattice."

The word itself does not say whether the flourishing is good or bad. The context decides that.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The biggest difference between uses is who or what flourishes and what follows. When grass, mortal man, or "the workers of iniquity" flourish, the next line brings withering or destruction (Psalm 90:6; 92:7; 103:15). In two passages where God is the One causing the flourishing, fruit follows: the rod "yielded ripe almonds" (Numbers 17:8), and God promises that Israel will "fill the face of the world with fruit" (Isaiah 27:6).

In three passages tsûwts stands beside another Hebrew growth verb, pârach, meaning to sprout or bud, and the pairing works differently in each. In Numbers 17:8 both verbs describe a single rod, which "sprouted" and "produced blossoms" and also yielded ripe almonds. In Isaiah 27:6 both describe Israel's future fruitfulness: Israel will "blossom and bud." In Ezekiel 7:10 the verbs stand in parallel lines with different subjects: the rod "has blossomed" while pride "has budded."

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God measures life by its end, not by its first bright appearance. Morning flowers, prosperous evildoers, and a proud city all show real vitality. Psalm 92 does not deny that the wicked flourish. It denies that their flourishing lasts. God, who sees evening from morning, is not impressed by rootless blossoms.

The same word also shows God's power to bring flowering out of death. Aaron's staff was a cut stick with no root, and God made it bloom. That miracle reveals a God who gives life where nature cannot. God used that life to confirm His chosen priest, and He had the rod kept as a warning against rebellion so that the people would not die (Numbers 17:10). Scripture moves from the flesh that fades to the life only God can give.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 17:8 and Ezekiel 7:10 — Two Rods in Bloom. Korah's rebellion in Numbers 16 challenged Aaron's priesthood and brought judgment, then a plague when the people complained (Numbers 16:41–48). Aaron stood between the dead and the living with incense, and the plague stopped. God then settled the question of His chosen priest. Twelve tribal staffs were laid before Him in the tabernacle overnight. The next day Aaron's rod "had sprouted and put forth buds, had produced blossoms and yielded ripe almonds."

Buds, flowers, and ripe fruit all appeared at once on dead wood, in a single night. No natural growing season could explain it. God had the rod kept before the Testimony "as a sign against the rebels," so that they would not die (Numbers 17:10). Hebrews 9:4 remembers "Aaron's rod that budded" among the treasures of the ark. God's flowering life marked His chosen priest, and the rod stood as His warning against further rebellion.

Ezekiel 7:10 uses the same picture in reverse: "The rod has blossomed, Pride has budded." Verse 11 explains, "Violence has risen up into a rod of wickedness." In Ezekiel's images the sin of the land has come into full flower, and the harvest is the sword (Ezekiel 7:15). In Numbers, the blossoming rod, kept after the plague had stopped, warned against further rebellion and death. In Ezekiel, a blossoming rod announced death. In that picture, rebellion does not stay hidden in the ground forever. It grows until it shows itself, and then God judges it.

Psalm 92:7 — The Flourishing Wicked. "When the wicked spring up like grass, And when all the workers of iniquity flourish, It is that they may be destroyed forever." The psalmist faces the old question of why evil people prosper, and he answers with a picture. Their prosperity is like grass: fast, green, and shallow. A few verses later, "the righteous shall flourish like a palm tree, He shall grow like a cedar in Lebanon" (Psalm 92:12). The psalm goes on to say the righteous are planted in the house of the LORD and still bear fruit in old age. Both the wicked and the righteous flourish. The difference lies in the root and in how long the life lasts.

Psalm 132:18 — The Crown That Flourishes. Psalm 132 recalls God's sworn promise to David: a son on his throne, Zion as God's dwelling, and a lamp for His anointed. It closes with this line: "His enemies I will clothe with shame, But upon Himself His crown shall flourish." Crowns are made of metal and do not grow. Several English versions translate the verb "shine," which fits the possible link between flowering and gleaming.

The Hebrew word Psalm 132 uses for "crown" also appears in "the plate of the holy crown" (Exodus 39:30). The plate itself has a different Hebrew name, tsiyts, and was engraved "HOLINESS TO THE LORD" (Exodus 28:36). Tsiyts sounds much like the verb tsûwts, to flourish. The psalm does not name the plate, and the word history is uncertain, but a Hebrew reader could hear the echo: the king's flourishing crown recalls the priest's crown of holiness. Isaiah 28:1 pronounces woe on "the crown of pride" whose beauty "is a fading flower." The crown God gives His Anointed blooms and stays.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament does not call Aaron's rod a picture of Christ. Still, the pattern fits closely. God confirmed His chosen priest by bringing life out of dead wood. In the same way, God declared Jesus to be the Son of God with power by raising Him from the dead (Romans 1:4). Hebrews says Jesus became a priest "according to the power of an endless life" (Hebrews 7:16). Like Aaron's rod, His resurrection ends the argument over who may stand before God for sinners, and like Aaron with his censer, He stands between His people and death.

Psalm 132 belongs to the promise to David, which the New Testament sees fulfilled in Jesus, David's Son. The crown that flourishes on the Anointed's head points to the risen King whose rule never fades. The psalm's echo of the priest's holy crown fits Christ, who is both King and Priest, as Hebrews presents Him after the order of Melchizedek.

Isaiah 27:6 completes the story: "Those who come He shall cause to take root in Jacob; Israel shall blossom and bud, And fill the face of the world with fruit." The people whose pride once budded into judgment will blossom again, rooted this time by God's hand. Their fruit will fill the world. The bloom of the flesh fades, but the flourishing God brings about in His King and His people will cover the earth.

7. Spirit & Life

Every life produces a bloom. That is the uncomfortable lesson of this word. Pride buds. Prosperity without God blossoms. So does our own strength in the morning of life. Psalm 92 frees the believer from envying people whose success looks bright today, because it teaches us to ask where their roots go. It also searches our own hearts. In Ezekiel the rod "has blossomed," pride "has budded," and violence "has risen up into a rod of wickedness" (Ezekiel 7:10–11). Sin we tolerate in small forms keeps growing.

The deeper comfort lies with Aaron's rod. God does His finest work on dead wood. A heart that seems barren is not beyond Him. The life He gives is His own, and it bears real fruit. Our hope does not rest on how well we flourish. It rests on a Priest whose appointment God confirmed by raising Him from the dead.

Song of Solomon 2:9 adds a tender note. The beloved stands "behind our wall… Gazing through the lattice," eager for the one he loves. Many Christians have read the Song as an echo of Christ's love for His people. If we read it that way, the picture is moving. Walls and lattices do not keep His attention away. He is already near, looking in, and He calls His beloved to rise and come away (Song of Solomon 2:10).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 90:6 — "In the morning it flourishes and grows up; In the evening it is cut down and withers." Moses sets the brief day of grass against the everlasting God of Psalm 90:2.

  • Psalm 103:15 — "As a flower of the field, so he flourishes." David admits human frailty in order to magnify the mercy of the LORD, which is "from everlasting to everlasting" (Psalm 103:17).

  • Psalm 72:16 — "Those of the city shall flourish like grass of the earth." Under the righteous King, grass becomes a picture of abundance rather than frailty.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6731 (tsiyts) — Probable lexical relative. The noun means flower or blossom, as in "a flower of the field" (Psalm 103:15). It also names the high priest's gold plate (Exodus 28:36).

  • H6524 (pârach) — Canonical pairing. This distinct verb means to sprout or bud, and it appears beside tsûwts in Numbers 17:8, Ezekiel 7:10, and Isaiah 27:6. In Psalm 92 it describes both the wicked springing up (Psalm 92:7) and the righteous flourishing (Psalm 92:12).
