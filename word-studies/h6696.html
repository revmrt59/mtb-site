BESIEGE, BIND, FASHION - (tsûwr, Strong's H6696)

Summary

The Hebrew verb tsûwr is used for several actions that close something in or give it form: surrounding a city, tying something up, and shaping metal. Most often it is a word of war. Armies "besiege" cities, surrounding them until food runs out and the gates open. The same word also describes tying silver into bags, casting bronze in a mold, and fashioning gold into an idol. Everything depends on who is doing the enclosing and why.

That range makes the word spiritually revealing. Through Israel's history tsûwr traces the siege that fell on Samaria and Jerusalem when God's people turned from Him, and the prophets say plainly that the Lord Himself was the One laying siege. Yet David uses this hard word of God's nearness: "You have hedged me behind and before, And laid Your hand upon me" (Psalm 139:5). The pressure that brings down proud cities becomes, for the person who belongs to God, the security of being surrounded by Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsûwr is a Hebrew verb used chiefly in the historical books and the prophets, especially 2 Kings and Jeremiah. Those books record the wars of Israel and Judah, including the Syrian siege of Samaria (2 Kings 6:24) and Nebuchadnezzar's siege of Jerusalem (Jeremiah 39:1; Daniel 1:1). A smaller group of uses comes from everyday life: tying up money, binding hair into a hem, and casting metal.

Ancient warfare explains why the word carried so much weight. A siege did not need to break the walls. It surrounded a city, cut off its supplies, and waited. The people inside slowly lost everything they depended on.

Some Hebrew dictionaries treat the show hostility and fashion uses as separate words that happen to share the same spelling. Strong's groups them under one entry. Either way, context determines the meaning in each passage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Strong's summarizes the word as to cramp or to confine, but in actual use the same Hebrew spelling covers several distinct actions, and context decides which one is meant. The main senses are:

1. To besiege or surround a city with hostile intent, as when Ben-Hadad "went up and besieged Samaria" (2 Kings 6:24).

2. To show hostility or oppose, as in God's promise to be "an adversary to your adversaries" (Exodus 23:22), or His command that Israel not "harass" Moab (Deuteronomy 2:9).

3. To bind or tie up, as when Gehazi's silver was "bound… in two bags" (2 Kings 5:23).

4. To form or cast metal. Aaron "fashioned" the people's gold "with an engraving tool, and made a molded calf" (Exodus 32:4), and Huram "cast two pillars of bronze" for the temple (1 Kings 7:15). These are different crafts with different metals.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes everything. When an enemy king surrounds a city, tsûwr means danger and starvation. When the Lord does the surrounding, the meaning depends on whether He comes in judgment (Isaiah 29:3) or in care (Psalm 139:5).

Even the siege law shows restraint. Deuteronomy 20:19 forbids cutting down fruit trees while besieging a city, because people would need them for food. God limited the destruction even inside an act of war.

The object of the verb matters as well. Silver tied into bags (2 Kings 5:23; 12:10) is being packed or kept safe. Gold fashioned into a calf (Exodus 32:4) is being turned into an idol, something it was never meant to be. The same Hebrew spelling covers both, but the material, the action, and the purpose are different, and the purpose decides whether the result is faithful or rebellious.

4. Theological Significance

The word shows that God is not neutral toward His people's safety. Before Israel entered the land, He promised that if they obeyed His voice He would oppose their enemies Himself (Exodus 23:22). The confining pressure would fall on those who attacked Israel.

When Israel persisted in idolatry, that pressure turned. Deuteronomy 28:52–53 had warned of enemies shutting the people in at every gate, and 2 Kings 6:25 records famine in besieged Samaria so severe that a donkey's head sold for silver. In that light, the siege became a visible sign that God's protection had been withdrawn.

The prophets then say something sharper still. God did not merely step aside. He took up the siege Himself: "I will encamp against you all around, I will lay siege against you with a mound" (Isaiah 29:3). Through Jeremiah, He promised to "turn back the weapons of war that are in your hands" while the Chaldeans "besiege you outside the walls" (Jeremiah 21:4). Human pride had no hiding place, because the One surrounding the city was the Lord. Daniel opens his book with Jerusalem besieged and explains that the Lord gave Judah's king into Nebuchadnezzar's hand (Daniel 1:1–2).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 139:5 — the siege of grace. David begins by saying, "O LORD, You have searched me and known me" (Psalm 139:1). He then describes God's knowledge of his sitting and rising, his thoughts, and even the words he has not yet spoken (Psalm 139:2–4). Then he reaches for a soldier's word: "You have hedged me behind and before." David is a city with God encamped on every side. The next line, "And laid Your hand upon me," shows the purpose of that enclosure. The hand that surrounds him is the same hand that "shall lead me" and "hold me" (Psalm 139:10). God cuts off every escape route, so that neither heaven, the grave, the far sea, nor the darkness can hide David from Him (Psalm 139:7–12). Yet God cuts off no source of help. David's response is wonder, not terror: "Such knowledge is too wonderful for me" (Psalm 139:6). Whether "behind and before" is read as space or as past and future, nothing can come at David without first meeting the God who surrounds him.

Exodus 32:4 and 1 Kings 7:15 — two kinds of shaping. Aaron took the people's earrings and "fashioned it with an engraving tool, and made a molded calf." The people then credited that shaped gold with bringing them out of Egypt. Idolatry takes what God has given and forces it into a god of our own design. The same Hebrew spelling describes Huram casting the bronze pillars for the temple (1 Kings 7:15), work done by a man "filled with wisdom and understanding and skill in working with all kinds of bronze work" (1 Kings 7:14) for the worship God Himself appointed. The two stories involve different metals and different work: Aaron fashioned the gold with an engraving tool and made a molded calf, while Huram cast bronze pillars for the house of God. What sets them side by side is that both shape metal for worship, and what separates them is whose design governs the work.

Ezekiel 4:3 and 5:3 — judgment and a bound remnant. God told Ezekiel to set an iron plate "as an iron wall between you and the city" and to "lay siege against it," calling this "a sign to the house of Israel." The iron wall may picture more than an army: a barrier between the Lord and Jerusalem, since the prophet was to set his face against the city. One chapter later the same verb appears in a different act. After Ezekiel shaved his head and divided the hair for burning, scattering, and the sword, he was to "take a small number of them and bind them in the edge of your garment" (Ezekiel 5:3). Elsewhere tsûwr describes tying up silver for safekeeping (2 Kings 12:10). Here a few fragile hairs receive the same care. Yet even some of the bound hairs are taken out again and burned in the fire, and from there "a fire will go out into all the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 5:4). Being bound in the prophet's garment did not place them beyond the judgment that was falling on the city.

Song of Solomon 8:9 — protective enclosure. Brothers consider their young sister: "If she is a wall, We will build upon her A battlement of silver; And if she is a door, We will enclose her With boards of cedar." A door opens easily, so the brothers enclose it with valuable cedar boards. The enclosing appears meant to guard her honor until the right time. Love sometimes surrounds what it treasures.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The siege story did not end with Babylon. As Jesus approached Jerusalem, He wept over the city and foretold that its enemies would build an embankment around it and close it in on every side, because it had not recognized the time of God's visitation (Luke 19:41–44). These words are Greek and do not use tsûwr, but they echo the prophetic sieges. Jesus stood where Isaiah and Jeremiah had stood and grieved over the city He came to save.

The cross reveals the deeper resolution of the word's two faces. Scripture says Christ became a curse for us (Galatians 3:13) and bore our sins in His body (1 Peter 2:24). The Ezekiel sign acts are not stated prophecies of the cross, but they offer a fitting comparison. Because Christ bore the curse and our sins, He entered the full pressure of judgment and was surrounded, abandoned, and cut off. The hairs in Ezekiel's hem were a small, fragile remnant, and even some of them were burned. Those joined to Christ are kept far more securely, because He has already borne the judgment Himself. Jesus says no one can snatch His sheep out of His hand or His Father's hand (John 10:28–29). John 10 does not quote Psalm 139, but it shows the same security David described: the hand that leads and holds David in the darkness (Psalm 139:10–12) is seen in the hands of the Good Shepherd who gives His life for the sheep (John 10:11).

7. Spirit & Life

A believer can read Psalm 139:5 when life feels closed in on every side and ask who is doing the surrounding. The pressures may be real, but for those in Christ the outermost ring belongs to God. Applied this way, David's hedge "behind and before" means regret cannot ambush us from the past, and fear cannot meet us in the future, without passing through the hand that leads and holds us.

The word also exposes a quieter danger. Aaron did not invent new gold. He reshaped what God had given. We do the same whenever we press God's gifts, or our idea of God Himself, into a shape that serves our wishes. The cure is to let God hold the mold. As an analogy, not as the meaning of the word, the limits He places on us can work like a casting mold: they confine so that something useful for His house can take shape.

The cedar boards of Song of Solomon 8:9 carry a similar lesson. God's boundaries around purity and intimacy are not a besieging army. They are the protection of family who love what they guard. The difference between a siege and a hedge is the heart of the One who encloses, and in Christ that heart has been fully shown.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 2:9, 19 — God forbids Israel to harass Moab and Ammon, because He had given those lands to Lot's descendants. He restrains even His own people's hostility.

  • Deuteronomy 14:25 — Worshipers who lived far away could take their tithe money in hand to the place God chose, binding it up for the journey to worship.

  • 2 Samuel 11:1 — Joab's army "besieged Rabbah. But David remained at Jerusalem." The siege is the setting: while the army was at Rabbah, David's sin with Bathsheba took place in Jerusalem (2 Samuel 11:1–2).

  • 2 Kings 17:5; 18:9 — Assyria besieged Samaria for three years, ending the northern kingdom.

  • Jeremiah 32:2 — Babylon besieged Jerusalem while Jeremiah sat confined in prison, and there God told him to buy a field as a pledge of future restoration.

  • Esther 8:11 — The king's decree allowed the Jews to defend themselves against any force that would assault them, reversing the threat against God's people.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H4692 (mâtsôwr), lexical relative: a noun drawn from this verb, meaning siege or siege works. It appears in the siege warnings of Deuteronomy 28:53.
