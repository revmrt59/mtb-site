DAZZLING, WHITE, CLEAR, PLAINLY, DRY - (tsach, Strong's H6703)

Summary

The Hebrew word tsach describes something glaring, bright, and unclouded. It names the kind of intensity nothing softens: a complexion that shines, a summer heat with no cloud to filter it, a wilderness wind with no moisture in it, and speech that comes out clear instead of stumbling. The NKJV renders it four different ways in its only four occurrences: "white," "clear," "plainly," and "dry." Behind each English word is the same picture of brightness or force with nothing in the way.

These few verses reach a long way. The bride in the Song of Solomon sees her beloved as radiant and "chief among ten thousand." Isaiah hears the Lord describe Himself watching the nations, still and unhurried, "like clear heat in sunshine." Jeremiah warns of a scorching wind of judgment that comes "not to fan or to cleanse." And Isaiah promises that under a righteous King, "the tongue of the stammerers will be ready to speak plainly." Taken together, these passages show the Lord watching quietly before He acts (Isaiah 18), sending a wind too strong for winnowing (Jeremiah 4), and promising clear understanding and plain speech under His King (Isaiah 32), while the Song celebrates a beloved whose radiance sets him apart from every other.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsach is a Hebrew descriptive word found only in poetry and prophecy: once in the Song of Solomon, twice in Isaiah, and once in Jeremiah. Standard Hebrew lexicons give its range as dazzling, glowing, clear. The idea is visual brightness, and it extends naturally to heat and wind, since in the land of Israel the glare of an unclouded sky and the burning heat came together.

The word is rare, so each use carries real weight. English readers seldom notice that "white," "clear," "plainly," and "dry" in these four verses go back to a single Hebrew word. Recognizing the common thread opens a surprising set of connections.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, tsach means glaring or dazzling, bright and unobscured. Context decides how that brightness shows itself:

1. Radiant or dazzling in appearance, describing a person whose look shines with health and beauty (Song of Solomon 5:10, "white").

2. Glaring and scorching, describing heat or wind with nothing to soften it (Isaiah 18:4, "clear heat"; Jeremiah 4:11, "a dry wind").

3. Clear and distinct, describing speech that comes out plainly, without stammering (Isaiah 32:4, "plainly").

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject governs the nuance. When tsach describes a person, it speaks of shining beauty. In the Song of Solomon, "white" refers to that brightness of appearance, not to skin color as modern readers might think of it. When it describes weather, the brightness becomes heat or wind: in Isaiah 18:4 clear heat is an image for the LORD's quiet watchfulness, while in Jeremiah 4:11 the dry wind depicts approaching judgment. When it describes speech, the clarity belongs to mind and tongue together: Isaiah pairs a rash heart that comes to understand with a stammering tongue that speaks plainly.

The word carries no fixed tone. The same glare can delight, quiet, or terrify, depending on whether it describes a beloved, pictures a watching God, or depicts a coming judgment.

4. Theological Significance

Across these passages, tsach helps show how God acts. In Isaiah 18 He watches quietly from His dwelling place before He moves. In Jeremiah 4 His judgment comes as a wind too strong for winnowing, one that no longer separates grain from chaff. In Isaiah 32 His restoring work gives understanding to the rash and plain speech to the stammerer.

This also corrects two opposite mistakes. People sometimes take God's stillness for absence, and sometimes take His judgment for something mild and corrective in every case. Isaiah 18 shows that His quiet is attention. Jeremiah 4 shows that a day can come when the wind is too strong to winnow and announces judgment by invasion and plunder (Jeremiah 4:12–13). Isaiah 32 shows that the same God also promises a day of clear sight, understanding, and plain speech.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 32:4 — Stammering turned to clear speech. Isaiah 32 opens with the promise of a king who will reign in righteousness, and of a man who will be "a hiding place from the wind" (Isaiah 32:1–2). Under His reign, "the eyes of those who see will not be dim, And the ears of those who hear will listen" (Isaiah 32:3). Then "the heart of the rash will understand knowledge, And the tongue of the stammerers will be ready to speak plainly" (Isaiah 32:4). Isaiah 28 provides a sharp contrast. There God answered the mockers of Jerusalem, who would not hear His plain words of rest, by warning that "with stammering lips and another tongue He will speak to this people" (Isaiah 28:11–12). Isaiah 32 promises the opposite kind of day. The rash, who acted in haste, will understand. The stammerers, whose words tripped and failed, will speak with tsach clarity. Isaiah does not say why they stammered; he promises only that under the righteous King their tongues will be set free. The brightness of the word now belongs to the restored mind and tongue.

Isaiah 18:4 — The stillness of the watching Lord. Isaiah 18 pictures a distant nation sending envoys by sea, and the whole world on alert for war. Against that anxious motion, the Lord says, "I will take My rest, And I will look from My dwelling place Like clear heat in sunshine, Like a cloud of dew in the heat of harvest." Anyone who has stood in a still, hot afternoon knows the feeling of this simile: nothing moves, yet nothing escapes the sun. God is neither panicked nor absent. Then, "before the harvest," He cuts off the sprigs with pruning hooks (Isaiah 18:5). His patience has a harvest time, and His quiet is the calm of One who already knows when He will act.

Jeremiah 4:11 — The wind that does not winnow. Jeremiah announces "a dry wind of the desolate heights" blowing toward Jerusalem, "not to fan or to cleanse." A farmer winnowed grain in a gentle breeze that carried away chaff and left the wheat. This wind does no such sorting. It is the scorching blast off the wilderness, "a wind too strong for these" (Jeremiah 4:12), and it comes with an invader rising "like clouds" with chariots "like a whirlwind" (Jeremiah 4:13). The image belongs to a wider biblical picture. Hosea describes the east wind as "the wind of the LORD" that dries up springs and fountains (Hosea 13:15). Jonah faints under "a vehement east wind" that God prepared (Jonah 4:8). Isaiah says the grass withers "because the breath of the LORD blows upon it" (Isaiah 40:7). The burning wind dries up springs, withers grass, and, with the beating sun, leaves even a prophet faint.

Song of Solomon 5:10 — The radiant beloved. When asked what makes her beloved different, the bride answers, "My beloved is white and ruddy, Chief among ten thousand." Tsach gives "white" its glow: he is bright with life and beauty, and that radiance sets him apart from every rival. Her description of him follows, and it comes from love, not from a list.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Isaiah's righteous King points to Jesus. In Mark 7, Jesus heals a deaf man with a speech impediment: "the impediment of his tongue was loosed, and he spoke plainly" (Mark 7:35). The crowd's verdict, "He makes both the deaf to hear and the mute to speak" (Mark 7:37), echoes most closely Isaiah 35:5–6, where "the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped" and "the tongue of the dumb sing." Mark writes in Greek, so the Hebrew word tsach does not appear there, but the healing gives a concrete picture of the restoration Isaiah 32 promises: a tongue set free to speak plainly. In a complementary passage about clarity and boldness, Paul writes, "since we have such hope, we use great boldness of speech" (2 Corinthians 3:12), and "when one turns to the Lord, the veil is taken away" (2 Corinthians 3:16). His boldness rests on hope, and the lifted veil pictures clear sight. Paul does not cite Isaiah 32, but together the passages show the Lord giving understanding and freedom to speak.

The radiance of the beloved in the Song also finds its fullest echo in Christ. Christians have long read the Song as a picture of the Bridegroom and His people. On that reading, the dazzling one "chief among ten thousand" points ahead to the mountain where Jesus' "face shone like the sun, and His clothes became as white as the light" (Matthew 17:2). It also points to the risen Lord whose "countenance was like the sun shining in its strength" (Revelation 1:16), and to the Ancient of Days whose garment was "white as snow" (Daniel 7:9). The Song does not state this fulfillment, but the line of radiance running through Scripture makes the connection a fruitful one.

The heat and the wind also invite a comparison with the gospel. Jeremiah's dry wind was a particular judgment against Jerusalem, while Isaiah 32 pictures "a hiding place from the wind" (Isaiah 32:2). In Christ that shelter is from condemnation: "There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1).

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 18 speaks directly to anxious hearts. When nations rage and events seem to spin out of control, God is not pacing. He rests and He watches, "like clear heat in sunshine." His stillness is not slackness. Peter makes a similar point: "The Lord is not slack concerning His promise... but is longsuffering toward us" (2 Peter 3:9). A believer can rest because the Lord is not distracted, and a careless person should not mistake His quiet for permission.

Jeremiah's wind teaches sober honesty. Not every hardship is a gentle sifting. There is such a thing as persistent refusal meeting a judgment that no longer cleanses. This should make the call to repent today urgent, while the breeze still winnows.

Isaiah 32 holds the deepest encouragement. Many believers know what it is to stammer spiritually: to half-understand, to fumble for words about God, to lose nerve when the moment to speak comes. Isaiah's promise of a righteous King speaks to exactly those places. Paul says that "we all, with unveiled face, beholding as in a mirror the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from glory to glory, just as by the Spirit of the Lord" (2 Corinthians 3:18). The clarity is real now and still growing. We see "in a mirror, dimly" today, but "then face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12). The more a person looks at the radiant Bridegroom of the Song, the more clearly that person sees, and the more plainly that person can speak of Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Tsach occurs only four times, and each occurrence has been developed above. Hosea 13:15, Mark 7:35, and 2 Corinthians 3:18 use different words; they are canonical companions, not additional occurrences of tsach.
