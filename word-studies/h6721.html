SIDON - (Tsîydôwn, Strong's H6721)

Summary

Sidon was an ancient Phoenician port city on the Mediterranean coast, north of Israel. Genesis also gives the name to a person: "Canaan begot Sidon his firstborn" (Genesis 10:15). The firstborn of Canaan and the old coastal city stand at the head of the Canaanite peoples. The name most likely means "hunting" or "fishing." It is usually traced to a Hebrew root meaning to hunt or catch, which suits a seafaring town, though the derivation is probable rather than certain. In the Old Testament, Sidon marks the northern edge of Canaan. The tribe of Asher never drove out its inhabitants, and its gods drew Israel away from the LORD. Through Jezebel, daughter of a Sidonian king, Baal worship reached the throne of Israel. The prophets repeatedly name Sidon with Tyre as proud trading powers that God would judge.

Sidon's story does not end in judgment. While Jezebel's Baal worship covered Israel, God sent Elijah to Zarephath, "which belongs to Sidon," where a Gentile widow fed the prophet (1 Kings 17:9). Centuries later Jesus told the Galilean towns that Tyre and Sidon would have repented had they seen His mighty works. He then went into "the region of Tyre and Sidon," where a woman Matthew calls a "Canaanite" received mercy for her daughter. Joshua's armies never took this territory, and the gospel reached into it. By the time Paul sailed for Rome, he had friends in Sidon who cared for him (Acts 27:3). The firstborn city of Canaan shows both God's judgment on human pride and the reach of His mercy to the nations.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name Tsîydôwn (Strong's H6721) names Canaan's son and also the coastal city and its surrounding territory. The first is a person: Sidon, the firstborn son of Canaan and great-grandson of Noah through Ham (Genesis 10:15; 1 Chronicles 1:13). The second, and by far the more common, is the city and its surrounding territory on the Phoenician coast. In the Table of Nations, the person and the city belong together. Sidon heads the list of Canaan's descendants, and the city of Sidon marks where "the border of the Canaanites" began (Genesis 10:19). The text does not explain exactly how the ancestor relates to the founding of the city, and it is best not to press beyond what Genesis says. Still, Genesis plainly names Sidon as Canaan's firstborn and places the city at an early boundary of Canaanite territory. That the city itself was the oldest of all Canaanite cities is a separate historical inference.

The city lay on the Mediterranean shore north of Israel, about twenty miles north of Tyre. Joshua twice calls it "Greater Sidon" (Joshua 11:8; 19:28), which points to its regional importance in the conquest era. Sidon appears to be the older of the two great Phoenician cities. Isaiah's phrase "virgin daughter of Sidon" (Isaiah 23:12) comes in an oracle against Tyre and may present Tyre as Sidon's offspring, although the phrase could also address Sidon herself. In later Scripture the two cities are named together so often that "Tyre and Sidon" becomes a single way of speaking about the whole Phoenician coast.

The name is generally understood to mean "hunting" or "fishing." It is linked to the Hebrew root tsud, meaning to hunt, catch, or take prey. The meaning fits a harbor people who drew their living from the sea, but it is a probable derivation, and Scripture makes no wordplay on it. The name should not be treated as a key to the city's spiritual character.

Several related names are indexed separately and help fill out the picture. The people of the city, the Sidonians, appear under H6722, including Ethbaal, "king of the Sidonians" and father of Jezebel (1 Kings 16:31). Zarephath, the town "which belongs to Sidon," has its own entry (H6886). In the New Testament the Greek form of the name appears under G4605. These are not additional occurrences of H6721, but they are essential to Sidon's full biblical story.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Firstborn of Canaan

Sidon enters Scripture in the genealogy of Noah's sons. Canaan's line begins with "Sidon his firstborn, and Heth" (Genesis 10:15; 1 Chronicles 1:13), and Canaanite territory stretched from Sidon southward toward Gerar and Gaza (Genesis 10:19). Before Israel exists as a nation, Sidon already stands as a fixed landmark at the northern edge of the land God would later promise to Abraham's descendants.

Jacob's blessing on his sons also mentions the city: "Zebulun shall dwell by the haven of the sea; He shall become a haven for ships, And his border shall adjoin Sidon" (Genesis 49:13). Long before Israel entered Canaan, the patriarch pictured one of the tribes living within reach of this famous harbor.

The Boundary Israel Never Crossed

In the conquest, Joshua defeated the northern coalition at the waters of Merom and pursued its armies as far as Greater Sidon (Joshua 11:8). When the land was divided, Asher's boundary ran toward Greater Sidon (Joshua 19:28). Judges then records the failure: Asher did not drive out the inhabitants of Acco or Sidon and lived among the Canaanites instead (Judges 1:31–32).

The consequence came later. "Then the children of Israel again did evil in the sight of the LORD, and served the Baals and the Ashtoreths, the gods of Syria, the gods of Sidon, the gods of Moab, the gods of the people of Ammon, and the gods of the Philistines; and they forsook the LORD and did not serve Him" (Judges 10:6). Israel failed to remove Sidon from the land and then took Sidon's gods into its own heart. In the same chapter the LORD reminds Israel that He had rescued them when the Sidonians oppressed them (Judges 10:12).

Sidon also serves as a reference point in the story of Dan's migration. The city of Laish was "far from Sidon," with no one to help it, which made it easy prey for the Danites (Judges 18:7, 28). The detail suggests that Sidon was the natural protector of the region and that a town beyond its reach stood exposed.

Kingdom Years: Cooperation and Corruption

David's census route went north around to Sidon (2 Samuel 24:6), so Sidon still marked the outer reach of Israel's northern sphere. In Solomon's day, the Sidonians' skill at cutting timber made them partners in building the temple (1 Kings 5:6, H6722). The same connection led to harm. Solomon followed "Ashtoreth the goddess of the Sidonians" (1 Kings 11:5, 33), and the shrine he built for her remained until Josiah defiled it (2 Kings 23:13).

The low point came when Ahab king of Israel married Jezebel, daughter of Ethbaal, king of the Sidonians, and began to serve Baal (1 Kings 16:31–33). Through the marriage, the religion of Sidon reached the royal court of Israel.

Elijah in Sidon's Territory

God answered with drought. Elijah declared that no dew or rain would fall except at his word (1 Kings 17:1). This was a direct challenge to Baal, who was honored as the giver of storm and harvest. After the brook Cherith dried up, God gave Elijah a surprising command: "Arise, go to Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon, and dwell there. See, I have commanded a widow there to provide for you" (1 Kings 17:9). The prophet hunted by Jezebel was sent into her father's territory, and the LORD, not Baal, kept the widow's flour and oil from running out. When her son died, the LORD raised him through Elijah, and she confessed that the word of the LORD in Elijah's mouth was truth (1 Kings 17:14–16, 24).

Jesus later pointed to this episode in His Nazareth sermon. There were many widows in Israel in Elijah's day, He said, yet the prophet was sent to a widow in Zarephath in the region of Sidon (Luke 4:25–26, paraphrased).

The Prophets Against Sidon

In the prophetic books Sidon appears as a proud merchant power. Isaiah's burden against Tyre speaks of the merchants of Sidon who crossed the sea (Isaiah 23:2) and calls Sidon to be ashamed because the sea itself mourns its lost children (Isaiah 23:4). Then he declares: "You will rejoice no more, O you oppressed virgin daughter of Sidon. Arise, cross over to Cyprus; There also you will have no rest" (Isaiah 23:12).

Jeremiah lists the kings of Tyre and Sidon among those who must drink the cup of the LORD's wrath (Jeremiah 25:22). When envoys from Sidon and its neighbors came to King Zedekiah in Jerusalem, likely to plan resistance, Jeremiah sent them home with yokes and a message: submit to Nebuchadnezzar, because God had given these lands into his hand (Jeremiah 27:3–11). In his oracle against the Philistines, Jeremiah adds that every helper would be cut off from Tyre and Sidon (Jeremiah 47:4).

Ezekiel describes the men of Sidon as rowers for Tyre's great merchant fleet (Ezekiel 27:8). He then gives Sidon its own short oracle: "Son of man, set your face toward Sidon, and prophesy against her, and say, 'Thus says the Lord GOD: "Behold, I am against you, O Sidon; I will be glorified in your midst; And they shall know that I am the LORD, When I execute judgments in her and am hallowed in her"'" (Ezekiel 28:21–22). Pestilence and the sword would come into her streets (28:23). Sidon would no longer be a pricking brier to Israel, and the oracle moves straight on to God gathering His scattered people to live safely in their land (28:24–26).

Joel adds a charge of injustice. Tyre, Sidon, and Philistia had taken God's silver and gold and sold the people of Judah to the Greeks (Joel 3:5–6). God answers: "Indeed, what have you to do with Me, O Tyre and Sidon, and all the coasts of Philistia? Will you retaliate against Me? But if you retaliate against Me, Swiftly and speedily I will return your retaliation upon your own head" (Joel 3:4). Zechariah mentions Sidon alongside Tyre, noting her wisdom (Zechariah 9:2), before describing how the LORD would strip Tyre of the wealth she had piled up "like the dust" (9:3–4).

Sidon in the New Testament

In the Gospels, the old enemy appears in a different light. Crowds from the coasts of Tyre and Sidon came to hear Jesus and be healed (Mark 3:8; Luke 6:17). Jesus used the two cities as a standard against which to measure the towns of Galilee: "Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if the mighty works which were done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago, sitting in sackcloth and ashes. But it will be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon at the judgment than for you" (Luke 10:13–14; compare Matthew 11:21–22).

Then He went there Himself. "From there He arose and went to the region of Tyre and Sidon. And He entered a house and wanted no one to know it, but He could not be hidden" (Mark 7:24). A woman came begging for her demon-tormented daughter. Mark calls her a Greek, a Syro-Phoenician by birth (Mark 7:26). Matthew calls her "a woman of Canaan" (Matthew 15:22). She persisted through silence and refusal, and Jesus praised her great faith and healed her daughter (Matthew 15:28). Mark then traces His route back through the region toward the Sea of Galilee (Mark 7:31).

Acts gives two final glimpses. The people of Tyre and Sidon depended on Herod's territory for food, so they sought peace when he was angry with them (Acts 12:20). On Paul's voyage to Rome as a prisoner, "the next day we landed at Sidon. And Julius treated Paul kindly and gave him liberty to go to his friends and receive care" (Acts 27:3). Earlier, believers scattered by persecution had carried the word through Phoenicia (Acts 11:19). These "friends" in Sidon were probably fellow believers, though Acts does not say so.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Sidon was Canaan's firstborn, that Asher failed to drive out its people, that Israel served its gods, and that the prophets announced God's judgment on it. From these facts a clear pattern emerges. Sidon shows what happens when God's people leave unfinished what He told them to do. The city Asher allowed to remain became, over generations, the source of Israel's worst idolatry. The path runs from Judges 1:31 to Judges 10:6 to 1 Kings 16:31. Ashtoreth of the Sidonians drew away Solomon's heart, and Baal of the Sidonians came to Israel's throne through Jezebel.

Sidon also shows that God is not a local deity confined to Israel's borders. He sent His prophet into Baal's homeland and provided bread there during a drought He Himself had sent. Through Jeremiah He told Sidon's envoys whose yoke they would wear (Jeremiah 27:3–6). Through Ezekiel He announced that He would be "hallowed" in Sidon (Ezekiel 28:22). Sidon's wealth, harbors, and skill at sea gave it no shelter from the One who rules the nations. Joel's charge adds that God takes personal offense when His people are mistreated and sold. What was done to Judah, He treats as done to Him (Joel 3:4–6).

A further reading is an inference, though a reasonable one. Ezekiel's oracle against Sidon is short. It speaks of judgment that makes God known and holy, and it does not use the language of permanent ruin found in some other oracles. Ezekiel does not say that Sidon would one day turn to the LORD. Yet the New Testament shows Sidon's region coming to Jesus, a Canaanite mother receiving mercy, and friends in Sidon, probably believers, caring for an apostle. Seen across the whole canon, God was glorified in Sidon both by His judgments and, later, by His grace. Ezekiel 28:22 does not predict this second outcome, but it fits the God whom Ezekiel proclaims.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament itself establishes several connections. Jesus explicitly interpreted the Elijah episode as a sign that God's mercy had always reached beyond Israel. In Luke 4:25–26 He chose the widow of Zarephath in Sidon as His example, and the synagogue in Nazareth was furious. Jesus also explicitly turned Sidon into a measure of accountability. Sidon, the old pattern of idolatrous pride in the prophets, would have repented if it had seen what Galilee saw. In His teaching, the question at judgment is how a people responded to the light God gave them. Sidon's long record of guilt was real, and Galilee's guilt was greater (Luke 10:13–14).

A strongly supported canonical development links Genesis 10:15 with Matthew 15:22. Mark describes the woman by her culture and region, as a Greek and a Syro-Phoenician. Matthew uses an older word, "Canaanite," which takes the reader back to the Table of Nations, where Sidon heads Canaan's descendants. In Sidon's own region, a daughter of Canaan comes to Israel's Messiah, calls Him "Son of David," and receives what she asks. Joshua's sword reached Sidon's borders but never took it, and Asher lived among its people without overcoming them. Jesus entered the same territory with a word of healing. The woman's faith did not come from Israel's conquest. It came through the grace of the King. Matthew does not call this a fulfillment, but his deliberate word choice invites the reader to see the whole history.

Genesis 49:13 offers a lighter thematic resonance. Jacob spoke of Zebulun's border adjoining Sidon. Matthew notes that Jesus made His home in Capernaum "in the regions of Zebulun and Naphtali," citing Isaiah's promise of light for "Galilee of the Gentiles" (Matthew 4:13–16). Later He went on to the borders of Sidon. This is a geographical echo rather than an announced fulfillment, but it fits Isaiah's picture of a great light dawning from the northern regions toward the nations.

5. Spirit & Life

Sidon's history speaks plainly to God's people about what we tolerate. Asher's failure to drive out the people of Sidon probably looked like a practical compromise at the time. Judges does not give Asher's reasons, but a fortified coastal city with valuable trade may well have seemed difficult to take and easy to live alongside. Generations later, Israel was serving Sidon's gods. Scripture traces that line clearly. What we leave in place because confronting it seems too costly rarely stays harmless. Sin we allow to remain among us often becomes the sin that rules us.

The same history shows a God who is not afraid of hostile ground. When Jezebel was hunting Elijah and Baal worship filled Israel, God sent His servant into Sidon's territory and fed him there through a poor widow down to her last handful of flour. She had nothing to give, yet the LORD had already "commanded" her to provide (1 Kings 17:9). God's provision does not depend on friendly surroundings, and His mercy reached a Gentile household while Israel was refusing Him.

The Canaanite woman in Sidon's region shows what kind of faith receives grace. She had no claim on Israel's promises, and she did not pretend otherwise. She took Jesus at His word, accepted the humbling comparison He made, and still asked for crumbs from the Master's table (Matthew 15:27). Jesus called that great faith. No one stands too far outside God's people to come to Christ in honest need.

Jesus' words about Tyre and Sidon also carry a sober warning for anyone who has heard the gospel often. Sidon's people would have repented in sackcloth if they had seen what Galilee saw. Those who live near the light, in homes, churches, and communities where Christ is preached, will answer for what they have heard. Familiarity with the truth is no protection if it never leads to repentance.

Sidon's last scene in Scripture is a quiet act of kindness. A prisoner on his way to Rome was given liberty to visit friends who cared for him (Acts 27:3). The city whose gods once corrupted Israel now held friends, probably fellow believers, ready to care for an apostle. God's dealings with Sidon move from judgment toward mercy, and the gospel opened that harbor to Christ.
