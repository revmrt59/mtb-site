VEIL - (tsammâh, Strong's H6777)

Summary

The Hebrew word tsammâh means a woman's veil, a covering over the face or head. It appears only four times in the Old Testament, and those few uses fall on opposite sides of a sharp line. Three times in the Song of Solomon, the bridegroom praises his bride's eyes and temples as he sees them "behind your veil." Her veil is part of her beauty. It keeps her loveliness reserved, while the one who loves her still sees it clearly. The fourth use, Isaiah 47:2, is a command from God to proud Babylon: "Remove your veil." There the loss of the veil marks a queen being brought down to a slave grinding grain.

Taken together, the four verses set two women side by side. The bride's honor is given and guarded by the one who loves her. Babylon's honor is self-made, and God strips it away. Scripture carries this contrast through to its last pages: Christ presents a Bride He has made "without blemish," and Babylon, who "glorified herself," is exposed and judged. The question this small word raises is where a person's dignity comes from and whether it will last.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsammâh is a Hebrew noun found only in poetry and prophecy. In the ancient Near East, a woman's veil said something about who she was. One Assyrian law code required respectable married women to go veiled in public and forbade slave women and prostitutes to wear a veil. That law came from Israel's neighbors and was not Israel's law. Even so, it shows how strongly a veil could signal honor and protected status in that world, and Isaiah's imagery appears to draw on the same understanding.

Several other Hebrew words are also translated "veil" in English Bibles, and they should not be confused with this one. The great curtain before the Most Holy Place is a different word, and so is the covering Moses wore over his shining face. Tsammâh belongs to the world of personal dignity, modesty, and the bond between a bride and her husband. It does not belong to the tabernacle or temple. For this reason, the tearing of the temple veil at Christ's death (Matthew 27:51) is a separate subject and is not part of this word's story.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tsammâh means a woman's veil, a covering worn over the face or head. The King James Version rendered it "locks," as in hair. Modern translations follow the context, which describes something the eyes are seen "behind" and something a woman can "remove." The NKJV translates the word "veil" in all four places.

The word itself names the garment. The ideas of honor, modesty, and status come from how the veil functioned in that culture and in these passages, not from the dictionary meaning.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Song of Solomon (4:1, 4:3, 6:7), the veil is always "your veil." It belongs to the bride, and her beloved speaks of what he sees "behind" it. The veil is hers, and it does not keep her beloved from seeing her beauty. He speaks of her eyes and her temples behind it and delights in them.

In Isaiah 47:2, the veil is the subject of a command. The Lord tells Babylon to take it off, along with her skirt, and to uncover her thigh. The same garment that adorns the bride is removed by force from the proud city. The difference between the two settings lies in who is speaking and what relationship stands behind the words. The bridegroom speaks in love. In Isaiah, the Judge speaks in justice.

4. Theological Significance

Read together, these four verses point to God as the One who grants and removes honor. The bride does not earn her beauty or defend it. Her beloved declares it: "Behold, you are fair, my love! Behold, you are fair!" (Song of Solomon 4:1). He finishes his praise with "You are all fair, my love, and there is no spot in you" (Song of Solomon 4:7). Her dignity is something she receives.

Babylon assigned honor to herself. The Lord declares that she "shall no longer be called The Lady of Kingdoms" (Isaiah 47:5), and in her own words she had boasted, "I shall be a lady forever" (47:7). God does not argue with that boast. He removes the veil that displayed it. Human pride can wear the outward signs of dignity for a long time, but those signs last only as long as the Lord allows. What God gives, He protects. What people seize for themselves, He exposes.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 4:1, 3; 6:7 — The cherished bride. The bridegroom begins his praise with her eyes: "You have dove's eyes behind your veil." He then compares her temples behind the veil to a piece of pomegranate, and he repeats that comparison in 6:7. The poem does not describe the veil itself. It tells what the bridegroom sees and delights in behind it, and the veil does not hide her beauty from him. It frames her beauty, keeps it modest, and reserves it. Brides in Israel could be veiled when they came to the man they would marry, as Rebekah was when she met Isaac (Genesis 24:65, though a different Hebrew word is used there). The beauty the veil keeps reserved is the beauty her beloved sees and treasures. The veil is a boundary that protects intimacy, not one that prevents it. The bridegroom repeats the same words of praise in chapter 6, after the couple's estrangement and longing in chapter 5, so the veiled beauty he admires has not been lessened by that trouble.

Isaiah 47:1–3 — The queen brought down. The chapter opens with a reversal: "Come down and sit in the dust, O virgin daughter of Babylon; sit on the ground without a throne." Babylon will no longer be called "tender and delicate." Then comes the work of a slave: "Take the millstones and grind meal. Remove your veil, take off the skirt, uncover the thigh, pass through the rivers" (47:2). The order matters. The veil, the mark of a free and honored woman, comes off first. Then her body is exposed as she wades rivers like a captive led into exile. God names His purpose plainly: "Your nakedness shall be uncovered, yes, your shame will be seen; I will take vengeance" (47:3). Babylon had trusted her wickedness and her sorceries, and she told herself she would never sit as a widow (47:8–10). Losing the veil shows that her glory was never truly hers.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song of Solomon is first of all a love poem between a bridegroom and his bride. Scripture's wider use of marriage imagery, however, invites readers to see in it the love of Christ for His people. Paul writes that Christ "loved the church and gave Himself for her," so that He might present her to Himself "a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing" (Ephesians 5:25, 27). The bridegroom's words in the Song, "there is no spot in you" (Song of Solomon 4:7), find their fullest answer in what Christ accomplishes for His Bride. Her purity and honor are His gift, bought by His self-giving.

Babylon's story also continues to the end of the Bible. In Revelation 18 the great city boasts in her heart, "I sit as queen, and am no widow, and will not see sorrow" (Revelation 18:7). These words echo Isaiah 47:8 almost exactly. She "glorified herself," and her judgment comes "in one day" (Revelation 18:8). Merchants mourn the city "clothed in fine linen, purple, and scarlet" (Revelation 18:16). In the next chapter, the Bride of the Lamb is also dressed in fine linen: "to her it was granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright, for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints" (Revelation 19:8). Both women wear fine linen, but the two passages say different things about it. Babylon's splendor belongs to a city that glorified herself, and it perishes with her in one day. The Bride's linen was granted to her, and John explains that it is the righteous acts of the saints. Her clothing is a gift, and it is also worn in lives of real righteousness. None of it comes from self-glory. Isaiah's tiny veil scene stands near the beginning of a contrast that reaches its conclusion at the marriage supper of the Lamb.

7. Spirit & Life

Believers do not have to manufacture their own dignity. In these verses the bride does nothing to win her beloved's praise. She simply hears it. This is the believer's position before Christ: "your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:3). Hidden does not mean lost or overlooked. The bride behind her veil offers a picture of this by way of comparison: the believer's deepest life is hidden from the world and fully known by the One who loves her. Much of a faithful life goes unseen by others, including prayer, quiet obedience, and the inward fruit the Spirit produces. The bridegroom compares her temples behind the veil to a piece of pomegranate. Taken as a comparison, that image is a fitting picture of hidden richness, though the Song itself does not teach this.

The "dove's eyes behind your veil" offer a similar picture. In the Song, the bridegroom delights in the eyes he sees behind her veil and calls them dove's eyes. The poem does not say what the veil did for her own gaze. Still, the image of a cherished bride, reserved for the one who loves her, makes a fitting comparison for a heart set apart for Christ. Such a heart looks to Him with undivided devotion, and it does so with confidence, not timid hiding.

Babylon is a sober warning. Any honor built on wealth, cleverness, or self-promotion is a veil God can remove with a single command. Isaiah's picture of a proud queen at the millstone calls believers to hold their reputations, accomplishments, and positions loosely. Honor that belongs to us because we belong to Christ is safe. Honor we have claimed for ourselves will one day be exposed.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word occurs only four times, and all of them are developed in Section 5. Song of Solomon 4:1 opens the bridegroom's praise with the bride's eyes behind her veil. Song of Solomon 4:3 and 6:7 repeat the image of her temples behind her veil, the second time confirming his unchanged delight. Isaiah 47:2 reverses the picture by commanding Babylon to remove her veil.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6532 (parokhet) — a distinct word for the curtain before the Most Holy Place (compare Matthew 27:51). It is a thematic contrast only and is not a lexical relative of tsammâh.

  • H4533 (masveh) — a distinct word for the covering Moses wore over his face (Exodus 34). It is also a thematic contrast and not a lexical relative. The "unveiled face" of 2 Corinthians 3:18 builds on this Moses passage, not on the bride's veil.
