ZEMARITE - (Tsᵉmârîy, Strong's H6786)

Summary

"Zemarite" is the name of an ancient people descended from Canaan, the son of Ham. It appears only twice in Scripture, both times in the same short line of a genealogy: "the Arvadite, the Zemarite, and the Hamathite" (Genesis 10:18; 1 Chronicles 1:16). The word carries no abstract meaning. It names a real tribe, usually linked with the coastal city of Sumur (Simyra) in what is now coastal Syria, near the far northern edge of the Canaanite world.

Even a name this obscure belongs to the biblical record for a reason. The Zemarites are part of the Table of Nations in Genesis 10, where Scripture maps the peoples of the earth before God calls Abraham. Listing them shows that God knows every family and where it settled, including the descendants of Canaan, the son under Noah's curse (Genesis 9:25). When the Chronicler copied the same line centuries later for Israelites home from exile, the old map still mattered. The God who knew where the Zemarites lived in antiquity was the same God bringing His scattered people back to the land He had promised them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsᵉmârîy is a Hebrew proper noun of the type called an ethnonym, a name for a people group. The ending works like the English "-ite," so "the Zemarite" means a member of that people or the people as a whole. The NKJV translates it "Zemarite" both times it occurs.

Both uses come in genealogies. Genesis 10, often called the Table of Nations, traces the families descended from Noah's three sons after the flood. First Chronicles 1 opens the Chronicler's long history of Israel by repeating that same material in condensed form.

Historical geographers commonly connect the Zemarites with Sumur, also called Simyra or Zumur, generally identified with Tell Kazel near the Mediterranean coast north of modern Tripoli. A city by that name appears in the Amarna Letters of the fourteenth century BC as an administrative and trading center. This identification is widely held, though still probable rather than proven, and it would place the Zemarites among the northernmost Canaanite peoples. It also grounds the name in a real coastal city of the ancient world.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tsᵉmârîy means "Zemarite," a member of the Canaanite people descended from Canaan and settled in the region of Sumur on the northern Phoenician coast. It has a single sense, and the word does nothing beyond naming. The importance of the Zemarites lies in where Scripture places them, not in any hidden meaning inside the name.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears in two settings that mirror each other. In Genesis 10:15–18 the Zemarite is the tenth of eleven peoples descended from Canaan. The list starts with Sidon, Canaan's firstborn, and Heth. It continues with the Jebusite, Amorite, Girgashite, Hivite, Arkite, and Sinite, and it ends with "the Arvadite, the Zemarite, and the Hamathite" (Genesis 10:18).

The order makes geographical sense. The early names include peoples Israel would later meet in the heart of the land, such as the Jebusites of Jerusalem and the Amorites. The last group, Arkite, Sinite, Arvadite, Zemarite, and Hamathite, belongs mainly to the north: the Phoenician coast and the Orontes valley around Hamath. So the Zemarite stands near the outer edge of the Canaanite family.

First Chronicles 1:16 repeats the line word for word, but the Chronicler leaves out the comment Genesis attaches to it. In Genesis the list moves straight into a statement about dispersion and borders, while Chronicles simply records the name and moves on toward Abraham, David, and the people of Judah.

4. Theological Significance

The Zemarites stand within the line of Canaan. Shortly before the Table of Nations, Noah declared, "Cursed be Canaan; A servant of servants He shall be to his brethren" (Genesis 9:25). Genesis 10 then lists Canaan's descendants by name, the Zemarites among them. This places them among the peoples whose land, and whose long moral story, would later intersect with Israel's. Scripture does not present Genesis 10 as a list of random ancient names. It is the world into which God's promise to Abraham would come.

The genealogy also shows God's detailed knowledge of the nations. Genesis 10:20 describes Ham's descendants "according to their families, according to their languages, in their lands and in their nations." A people as obscure in the biblical record as the Zemarites has its place in that record. The Lord does not deal with humanity in vague generalities. He knows who came from whom, where they settled, and how their histories would unfold.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:18 — Scattered to the edges. Right after naming the Zemarite, Genesis adds, "Afterward the families of the Canaanites were dispersed." The next verse defines the Canaanite border "from Sidon as you go toward Gerar, as far as Gaza," and then eastward toward Sodom and Gomorrah (Genesis 10:19). Notice that the stated border begins at Sidon, while the Zemarites, Arvadites, and Hamathites lived north of it. The sentence about dispersion may help explain this. A plausible reading is that Canaan's families spread beyond that border and that these northern peoples mark how far the spread went, though Genesis 10:18 does not say they moved out from a Sidon core.

This shows that the Canaanite presence was large and well established long before Israel arrived. When the Lord later gave Moses the boundaries of the land, He drew the northern border "from the Great Sea… to Mount Hor… to the entrance of Hamath" (Numbers 34:7–8). That line reaches toward the region of the Hamathites, the Zemarites' neighbors in the same verse. If the Sumur identification is correct, the site lay at or near that border, and the exact Zemarite territory in relation to the promised boundary cannot be fixed. Either way, the promise reached toward the northern edge of the old Canaanite world described in Genesis 10. The inheritance God named for Israel was set within the real map of the peoples Genesis had already recorded.

1 Chronicles 1:16 — The old map retold. The Chronicler wrote for Israelites who had returned from exile in Babylon to a small, struggling province. He began their history with Adam and the nations, copying the line "the Arvadite, the Zemarite, and the Hamathite." By then empires had come and gone over that northern coast, and Scripture says nothing more of the Zemarites. Repeating their name reminded the returning community that their story did not begin with Persian permission or Babylonian defeat. It began with the God who ordered the nations in the beginning and then chose Abraham from among them. The same God who knew the old map was keeping His word to bring them home.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Genesis 10 sets the scene for Genesis 12. Right after the nations are named and scattered, God calls Abraham and promises that in him "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3, paraphrased as referenced). The Zemarites are among those families. Before Israel existed, Scripture had already named the peoples God intended His blessing to reach.

The Gospels show that reach becoming concrete. In the region of Tyre and Sidon, the old Canaanite coast, a woman whom Matthew calls "a woman of Canaan" begged Jesus to deliver her daughter, and He praised her great faith (Matthew 15:21–28, paraphrased). A descendant of Canaan's line received mercy from Abraham's promised Seed. Paul later told the Athenians that God made every nation from one man and determined their boundaries so that they would seek Him (Acts 17:26–27, paraphrased). The Table of Nations, Zemarites included, belongs to that larger purpose. The map of scattered peoples is the setting in which the gospel goes out to gather them.

7. Spirit & Life

God's promises are rooted in real places and real history. When He spoke to Abraham about the land, He was talking about territory held by established peoples, including a people probably tied to a coastal city that appears in Egyptian diplomatic records. Faith in God's word has never meant believing in a mythological landscape. The Lord acts in the same world of cities, borders, and empires where His people live, and He shows His rule over the powers that are already there.

The Chronicler's use of this name also gives steady comfort. For people coming home from exile, a list of ancient tribes might have seemed irrelevant. But it told them that the Lord had been in charge of history long before their troubles and would remain in charge after them. Believers who feel small within large events can take that same footing. The God who did not overlook the Zemarites does not lose track of His people, and His promises outlast the empires around them.
