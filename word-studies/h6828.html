NORTH, NORTHWARD, NORTHERN - (tsâphôwn, Strong's H6828)

Summary

The Hebrew word tsâphôwn simply means north. Most often it marks a direction: a border running northward, the table set on the north side of the tabernacle (Exodus 26:35), a gate facing north in Ezekiel's temple. Because Israel's great enemies, Assyria and Babylon, usually came down on the land from the north, the prophets turned this ordinary compass word into a word heavy with warning. Jeremiah saw calamity breaking out of the north, and Ezekiel saw God's executioners come from the direction of the temple's upper gate, which faces north.

The same word also lifts our eyes to God's majesty. Job says God "stretches out the north over empty space," and Psalm 48 calls Mount Zion the true height "on the sides of the north." The proud king of Babylon tried to climb to that height and was thrown down. The prophets then reverse the direction of dread. The God who sent His people north into exile promises to bring them back from the north in a rescue that will overshadow the memory of the exodus from Egypt. The direction that judgment came from becomes the direction mercy returns from.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsâphôwn is a common Old Testament noun. It appears in land boundaries (Joshua 15–19), in the layout of the tabernacle and Ezekiel's visionary temple, in poetry about creation and the winds, and in prophecies of invasion and return. Many lexicons connect it with a verb meaning to hide or to store up, perhaps because the far north was distant and unknown. That link is uncertain enough that it should not be read into individual verses.

Two facts of the ancient world help. First, a Hebrew speaker oriented himself facing east, which put the north on his left hand. Second, the desert east of Israel meant that armies from Mesopotamia marched up the Euphrates and entered the land from the north. Neighboring Canaanite texts also spoke of Mount Zaphon, a northern peak where Baal was said to dwell. Psalm 48 and Isaiah 14 may well draw on that background, and reading them with it in mind is illuminating.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The direction north, or the northern side of something, such as "northward" in Genesis 13:14 or the "north side" of the tabernacle in Exodus 26:35.

2. A northern region or the peoples who live there, such as "the land of the north" from which God regathers Israel (Jeremiah 16:15) and "the king of the North" in Daniel 11.

In poetry the word can also name the north wind (Song of Solomon 4:16; Proverbs 25:23) or the northern heavens (Job 26:7). These uses still mean the direction. The surrounding words supply the wind or the sky.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most of the uses are plain description. Joshua's tribal borders and Ezekiel's temple measurements (Ezekiel 40–48) use the word again and again, and these passages show a God who cares for orderly inheritance and ordered worship.

In the prophets the word gathers weight. "Out of the north" becomes a set phrase for approaching judgment (Jeremiah 1:14; 4:6; 6:1, 22). A related term, "the far north" or "the farthest sides of the north," describes the remote limits of the earth, whether for Gog's armies (Ezekiel 38:15) or for the height of divine rule (Isaiah 14:13). In Daniel 11, "the king of the North" works like a title. It begins with a series of historical rulers north of Israel who fight "the king of the South," Egypt. It then carries forward to "the time of the end" (Daniel 11:40).

4. Theological Significance

The north belongs to God. Elihu says, "He comes from the north as golden splendor; With God is awesome majesty" (Job 37:22), and Job declares, "He stretches out the north over empty space; He hangs the earth on nothing" (Job 26:7). The northern sky is something God Himself stretched out. That truth stands behind what the prophets say about armies from the north. When disaster comes from the north, it comes from a region God made and holds.

Judgment from the north is therefore never random. In Jeremiah's call vision, God shows him a boiling pot facing away from the north and explains that He Himself is calling the families of the kingdoms of the north against Jerusalem because of its idolatry (Jeremiah 1:13–16). Babylon was building an empire, but God was using it to discipline His people. Even the final enemy is under His hand. God says He will turn Gog around and bring him up "from the far north" to the mountains of Israel (Ezekiel 39:2), only to defeat him there and make His holy name known. Joel speaks the same way of "the northern army" that God will "remove far from you" (Joel 2:20). When the instruments of God's discipline overreach in their own pride, God judges them in turn.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 48:1–2 and Isaiah 14:13 — the true height. The psalm praises "the LORD... In His holy mountain" and then makes a bold claim: "Beautiful in elevation, The joy of the whole earth, Is Mount Zion on the sides of the north, The city of the great King." Zion was not literally in the far north, and it was not the highest hill around. If the psalmist is borrowing the language the nations used for the home of their gods, his point is that the real seat of divine rule is the hill where the LORD has placed His name. Isaiah 14 shows the opposite movement. The king of Babylon says in his heart, "I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God; I will also sit on the mount of the congregation On the farthest sides of the north." The next verses bring him down to Sheol. Many readers have seen behind this proud king the older rebellion of Satan. Whether or not one follows that reading, the point is clear. The height of the north belongs to God, and whoever tries to take it falls.

Ezekiel 8–9 — idolatry at the north gate. The Spirit brings Ezekiel "to the door of the north gate of the inner court, where the seat of the image of jealousy was" (Ezekiel 8:3). He looks north and sees the idol at the entrance (8:5). At the door of the north gate of the LORD's house, women sit weeping for the pagan god Tammuz (8:14). Then judgment arrives from the same direction: "six men came from the direction of the upper gate, which faces north, each with his battle-ax in his hand" (9:2). Judah had planted its rebellion at gates facing the very direction from which its discipline came. Yet a man clothed in linen with a writer's inkhorn walks among the executioners, and he marks those who grieve over the abominations so that they are spared (9:4). Mercy goes in with judgment.

Jeremiah 16:14–15 and 23:7–8 — the greater exodus. Israel's familiar oath recalled the LORD who brought them up from the land of Egypt. Jeremiah says days are coming when people will no longer swear that way. Instead they will swear by the LORD who brought the children of Israel up "from the land of the north" and from all the lands where He had driven them. The changed oath shows that the promised return from the north and from all the other lands would overshadow even the remembrance of the exodus from Egypt. Isaiah pictures God speaking to the regions of captivity and telling the north to give up His sons and daughters (Isaiah 43:6), and he sees exiles coming from the north and the west (Isaiah 49:12). Jeremiah adds the blind, the lame, and mothers with children among those gathered "from the north country" (Jeremiah 31:8), and Zechariah calls the scattered to flee "from the land of the north" (Zechariah 2:6). God brings His people back from the very place where His discipline sent them.

Ezekiel 40–42 — the north side of worship. Ezekiel's temple places tables for slaughtering the burnt, sin, and trespass offerings at the northern gate (Ezekiel 40:39–40), and Ezekiel 42:13 names "the north chambers and the south chambers" as the holy chambers where the priests eat the most holy offerings, with the north chambers named first. This echoes Leviticus 1:10–11, which directs that a burnt offering from the flock, a sheep or a goat, be killed on the north side of the altar before the LORD. Scripture gives no stated reason for that placement. Still, it is worth noticing that in a Bible where judgment so often comes from the north, the substitute is slain on the north side of the altar, as if standing between the worshiper and the direction of judgment. That is a suggestive comparison drawn by the reader, not a meaning the law itself assigns to the location.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pattern of the north moves toward Christ along several lines. The proud king of Isaiah 14 says, "I will ascend." The Son of God, who was "in the form of God," did not grasp at equality with God but humbled Himself to the cross, and God exalted Him (Philippians 2:6–9). The Babylonian king grasped at a throne above the stars and was brought down to Sheol. The exaltation he reached for belongs to the One who came down.

The greater exodus that Jeremiah promised widens in the gospel. Jesus says people will come from east and west, north and south, and sit down in the kingdom of God (Luke 13:29). Hebrews tells believers they have come to Mount Zion, the city of the living God (Hebrews 12:22), the heavenly city that answers to the Zion Psalm 48 celebrated. The names of Ezekiel's final enemy also reappear, though the direction changes. Ezekiel's Gog comes "out of the far north" (Ezekiel 38:15), but Revelation 20:8 uses "Gog and Magog" for the nations "in the four corners of the earth," gathered together to battle. The whole world, not one northern power, surrounds "the camp of the saints and the beloved city," and fire from God out of heaven ends the rebellion (Revelation 20:9). The final conflict echoes Ezekiel's vision, and so does God's victory (Ezekiel 39:6).

The north-side sacrifices also lead to Christ. Read in the light of the gospel, the offerings slain before the LORD look ahead to the One on whom "the LORD has laid... the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6), who bore the judgment His people deserved. Read alongside the prophets' north of judgment, the altar's north side becomes a fitting picture of that truth: for those who trust Him, the judgment they deserved has been met by a substitute.

7. Spirit & Life

Jeremiah leaves no doubt that the trouble Judah met from the north was God's discipline for its idolatry. Judah feared the north, and rightly so, but the boiling pot was tilted by God's hand. Not every hardship in a believer's life can be identified as a direct correction like that; Jesus refused to trace a man's blindness to anyone's sin (John 9:2–3). Yet no trouble comes from outside the rule of the God who holds the north, and when He does discipline His children, it is never a stray blow (Hebrews 12:6). The same God who sent the armies also wrote the promise to bring His people home from the land of their captivity. The place of our hardest correction can become the place where we learn His greater rescue.

Ezekiel 8 holds a searching lesson. Judah set up its idolatry at the temple's north gates (Ezekiel 8:3, 14), and the executioners came from the direction of the upper gate, which faces north (Ezekiel 9:2). Yet God marked those who sighed over the sin around them (Ezekiel 9:4). Believers are called to grieve over what dishonors God rather than grow comfortable with it.

The prayer in the Song of Solomon, usually read as the bride's, gives a gentler picture: "Awake, O north wind, And come, O south! Blow upon my garden, That its spices may flow out" (4:16). She asks for the cold north wind as well as the warm south wind, because both draw out fragrance. Read as a picture of the Christian life, her prayer suggests that God sends bracing seasons as well as tender ones, and both can serve His purpose to make our lives fragrant for Christ.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 13:14 — The LORD tells Abram to look northward, southward, eastward, and westward, because all the land he sees will be his. The compass becomes a map of promise.

  • Genesis 28:14 — Jacob's descendants will spread abroad to the west, east, north, and south, and all the families of the earth will be blessed in him.

  • Psalm 89:12 — The psalmist says God created the north and the south. Every direction belongs to Him.

  • Psalm 107:3 — The redeemed are gathered from the lands, from the east and west, from the north and south.

  • Proverbs 25:23 — "The north wind brings forth rain, And a backbiting tongue an angry countenance." Slander produces anger as surely as the weather follows the wind.

  • Amos 8:12 — In a famine of hearing God's words, people wander from north to east seeking the word of the LORD and do not find it.

  • Zechariah 14:4 — When the LORD stands on the Mount of Olives, half the mountain moves toward the north and half toward the south.
