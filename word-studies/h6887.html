DISTRESS, BIND, ENEMY, HARASS - (tsârar, Strong's H6887)

Summary

The Hebrew verb tsârar means to bind, wrap, cramp, or hem in. It can describe something physical: kneading bowls bundled in clothing, a stone tied into a sling, or a place too small to live in. It can also describe a person pressed in by circumstances until no way out remains. That person is in "distress" or "trouble." And it can describe someone who presses others in, the "enemy" or "adversary" who harasses and besieges. The same idea of narrowing runs through all three uses.

This picture of being hemmed in gives biblical distress its shape. When David says, "I am in great distress," and when the people in Psalm 107 cry to the LORD "in their trouble," they are describing walls closing around them. God's rescue answers that picture. He hears from inside the narrow place and brings His people out into room to breathe. The same word also shows God binding things up on purpose. He secures His testimony among His disciples, and He stores up the unrepented sin of Ephraim. Nothing entrusted to Him, and nothing hidden from Him, slips loose.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tsârar is a Hebrew verb that appears 72 times, from Exodus to Zephaniah. More than a third of those uses are in the Psalms, where it describes the believer's trouble and the enemies who cause it. In the historical books it describes armies, sieges, and kings in crisis. In wisdom literature and the prophets it often keeps its plain physical sense of tying or wrapping. Isaiah 28:20 shows that sense well. A covering is so narrow that a man cannot wrap himself in it. The word's basic picture is a space too tight for comfort.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To bind, wrap, or tie up something physical. Israel's kneading bowls were "bound up" in their clothes at the Exodus (Exodus 12:34). The Gibeonites carried old wineskins that had been "mended," or tied together (Joshua 9:4). God binds the waters in His clouds (Job 26:8).

2. To be cramped, hemmed in, or in distress. In this sense the word describes a person under pressure with no way of escape (2 Samuel 24:14; Psalm 18:6). The prophets also use it for the sudden "pangs" of a woman in labor (Jeremiah 48:41).

3. To press in on someone with hostility, as an adversary does. The word can mean to harass or besiege, and it can name the person who does so as an "enemy" (Numbers 33:55; Esther 3:10). Leviticus 18:18 uses it for a sister taken as a "rival" wife, someone who would crowd the first wife's life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning shifts with the direction of the pressure. When the word is used without an object, the subject is the one squeezed: "In my distress I called upon the LORD" (Psalm 18:6). When it takes an object, the subject is the one doing the squeezing. Midian "harassed" Israel (Numbers 25:18), and invaders "besiege you at all your gates" (Deuteronomy 28:52). When it names a person, that person's identity is defined by hostile pressure. Haman is "the enemy of the Jews" (Esther 3:10).

The physical sense never disappears entirely. The prophet's company in 2 Kings 6:1 says the place where they live is "too small" for them. That literal cramping helps explain why trouble and enemies are described with the same verb. Both leave a person no room.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats distress as a narrowing of space, and so it describes salvation as a widening. Psalm 118:5 states this plainly: "I called on the LORD in distress; The LORD answered me and set me in a broad place." Psalm 4:1 gives thanks in the same way: "You have relieved me in my distress." God does more than take away pain. He opens up room for life.

The word also shows that enmity in Scripture is active. In Numbers 33:55, the Canaanites whom Israel failed to drive out would become "thorns in your sides," and "they shall harass you in the land where you dwell." In Deuteronomy 28:52, an enemy besieges a disobedient people "until your high and fortified walls, in which you trust, come down." Haman did not merely dislike the Jews. He obtained the king's signet ring to destroy them. The adversary's aim is to choke off the life and inheritance God has given. Paul describes this same kind of conflict when he warns against "the wiles of the devil" (Ephesians 6:11).

A third truth is that God binds things deliberately. What He secures stays secure, whether it is His own word kept for His people or sin kept for judgment.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 107: four cries from the narrows. The verb appears four times in this psalm, once in each of its four stanzas. Wanderers are lost in the desert. Prisoners are brought low by hard labor. Fools are drawing near to death because of their sin. Sailors are tossed by a storm until they are at their wits' end. The groups differ, but each reaches the same point: "Then they cried out to the LORD in their trouble, And He delivered them out of their distresses" (107:6; see also vv. 13, 19, 28). Each group cries out at the moment its own strength is gone. The squeeze strips away self-reliance and leaves only the cry, and each time the LORD answers it.

Isaiah 8:16 and Hosea 13:12: two kinds of binding. Judah had turned away, and Isaiah's message was being refused. God told him, "Bind up the testimony, Seal the law among my disciples." When a nation would not listen, God's word was tied up like a precious bundle and entrusted to a faithful few. Hosea uses the same verb for the opposite purpose: "The iniquity of Ephraim is bound up; His sin is stored up." Ephraim's guilt had not faded with time. It was tied and stored, waiting. Paul uses this same storing language when he warns that the impenitent are "treasuring up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath" (Romans 2:5). John later sees books opened and the dead "judged according to their works" (Revelation 20:12). God loses neither His testimony nor unrepented sin.

Distress that turns a heart either way. Chronicles places two kings in the same narrow place. Ahaz was pressed by Assyria, and "in the time of his distress" he became more unfaithful (2 Chronicles 28:22). Manasseh, bound and carried to Babylon, "in affliction" humbled himself and prayed, and God heard him (2 Chronicles 33:12). Saul, in his last crisis, confessed that he was "deeply distressed," yet he turned to a medium rather than to the LORD (1 Samuel 28:15). David, by contrast, said, "I am in great distress. Please let us fall into the hand of the LORD, for His mercies are great" (2 Samuel 24:14). The pressure did not decide how these men ended. What decided it was where each one turned while under pressure.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The testimony bound up in Isaiah 8:16 belongs to a passage that the New Testament takes up again. The next verses, where Isaiah speaks of waiting on the LORD with the children God has given him, are placed on the lips of Christ in Hebrews 2:13. The faithful remnant holding God's sealed word looks forward to the Son, who gathers His own around that word.

Psalm 69, which the Gospels and Paul repeatedly apply to Jesus' sufferings, uses tsârar twice. The sufferer prays, "I am in trouble; hear me speedily" (69:17), and tells God, "My adversaries are all before You" (69:19). Psalm 8:2 speaks of strength ordained "because of Your enemies," and Jesus quoted it when children praised Him in the temple (Matthew 21:16). Christ entered the narrowest place of all, surrounded by enemies and pressed down into death. He came out of the grave, and that is the broad place opened for everyone who belongs to Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Abigail blessed David with a beautiful use of this word. If anyone pursued his life, she said, it would be "bound in the bundle of the living" with the LORD his God (1 Samuel 25:29, paraphrased). The verb that describes being hemmed in also describes being held safe. A believer may feel tied up by trouble while being tied, far more securely, into God's keeping.

The distress itself does not decide the outcome, as Ahaz and Manasseh show. The walls of the narrow place can drive a person deeper into unbelief, or they can become the place where self-reliance finally gives out and the cry of faith begins. Psalm 107 shows that God does not wait for His people to climb out before He listens. David says the LORD "heard my voice from His temple" (Psalm 18:6) while he was still in distress. So we may "come boldly to the throne of grace" for help "in time of need" (Hebrews 4:16), even from inside the tight places.

Psalm 23:5 completes the picture. The table is spread "in the presence of my enemies." The enemies are still there, yet the LORD makes room for a feast.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 12:34 — Israel left Egypt with their kneading bowls "bound up" in their clothes. Even the dough of the Exodus was wrapped for a journey toward freedom.

  • Psalm 129:1–2 — Israel had been "afflicted" from its youth, many times over, yet its enemies did not prevail. The pressure was long, but it did not end in defeat.

  • Proverbs 4:12 — Those who walk in wisdom will not have their steps "hindered." God's ways keep the path from closing in.

  • Lamentations 1:20 — Jerusalem cries out that she is in "distress." Even in judgment, the narrowed city still turns to the LORD.

  • Proverbs 26:8 — Honoring a fool is like one who "binds" a stone in a sling. A stone tied into a sling cannot be thrown, and honor given to a fool is just as useless.

  • Amos 5:12 — The prophet condemns those "afflicting the just." When God's people press in on the powerless, they take on the role of the enemy.
