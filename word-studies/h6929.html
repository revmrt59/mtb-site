KEDEMAH - (Qêdᵉmâh, Strong's H6929)

Summary

Kedemah is the last-named of the twelve sons of Ishmael, Abraham's son by Hagar. His name appears only twice in Scripture, both times in a list: once in the family record of Ishmael (Genesis 25:15) and again in Chronicles' retelling of the same line (1 Chronicles 1:31). His name is traditionally understood to mean "eastward" or "toward the east." It is related to the Hebrew word for "east," which can also carry the sense of "ancient" or "before." Scripture records no deeds, words, or personal story for him. Like his brothers, he headed a clan, and Genesis calls these sons "twelve princes according to their nations" (Genesis 25:16).

Kedemah matters because of where he stands in the list. Years earlier God had told Abraham that Ishmael "shall beget twelve princes" (Genesis 17:20). Kedemah is the twelfth name in a list that records all twelve princes, exactly as God had promised. His name also fits the setting of his chapter. In Genesis 25 Abraham sends his other sons "eastward, away from Isaac his son," and Ishmael's descendants settle in the regions east of Egypt and south of Canaan. Kedemah reminds the reader that God keeps His word in full, even to the families who live outside the line of promise.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

Kedemah is a single individual: the twelfth and final son of Ishmael named in Genesis 25:13–15 and repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:29–31. Because Genesis presents these sons as heads of peoples "by their towns and their settlements" (Genesis 25:16), the name also stands for the clan that descended from him. No other person in Scripture bears this name.

The name is traditionally understood to mean "eastward" or "toward the east"; Strong's also allows a sense of "precedence," and Brown-Driver-Briggs simply identifies the individual. It is built from the Hebrew noun qedem, "east" (Strong's H6924), a word that can also point backward in time to what is "ancient" or "of old." The ending on the name works the way the Hebrew directional ending does, giving the sense of motion "toward." The name therefore sounds almost exactly like the ordinary Hebrew word for "eastward."

That likeness produces a striking echo in Genesis 25. Only a few verses before Kedemah is named, Abraham sends the sons of his concubines "eastward, away from Isaac his son, to the country of the east" (Genesis 25:6). Moses never comments on this connection, so we should treat it as an observation drawn from the text rather than a stated wordplay. Still, the reader who hears both words in the same chapter can hardly miss it.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Kedemah's story consists of his place in his family's record. Genesis 25:12 opens "the genealogy of Ishmael, Abraham's son, whom Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah's maidservant, bore to Abraham." The sons are listed "according to their generations," starting with "the firstborn of Ishmael, Nebajoth" and closing with "Hadar, Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah" (Genesis 25:13–15). Genesis then notes that they lived "from Havilah as far as Shur, which is east of Egypt as you go toward Assyria" (Genesis 25:18). This is the desert country bordering Canaan to the south and east.

Centuries later the Chronicler copied the same names into the opening genealogy of his book, ending: "Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah. These were the sons of Ishmael" (1 Chronicles 1:31). Israel had come home from exile and was relearning its own identity, and it still kept the memory of the peoples descended from Abraham who had not received the land. Some of Kedemah's brothers, such as Kedar, Nebaioth, and Tema, appear again in later Scripture. Kedemah does not.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states the key fact plainly. God promised Abraham, "Behold, I have blessed him, and will make him fruitful, and will multiply him exceedingly. He shall beget twelve princes, and I will make him a great nation" (Genesis 17:20). Genesis 25:16 reports the outcome: "twelve princes according to their nations." Kedemah's name closes that count. God had made His central promise to Isaac's line, yet He did not neglect the smaller promise He made about Ishmael. He fulfilled it to the exact number.

Two further points come from reading the text carefully rather than from direct statement. First, the twelve princes of Ishmael anticipate the twelve sons of Jacob (Genesis 35:22–26). God builds both families into peoples, but only Jacob's twelve become the tribes of Israel who carry the promise of the land and the blessing for all nations. Second, Kedemah's name, "eastward," fits the direction of his family's life. They live away from the land of promise, out toward the desert and the east.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

No passage connects Kedemah personally to Christ. The New Testament does, however, interpret his father's line. Paul reads Ishmael and Isaac as two kinds of sons, one "born according to the flesh" and the other "through promise" (Galatians 4:22–23, paraphrased). Paul's point concerns two covenants, bondage and freedom, and he counts believers as "children of promise" (Galatians 4:28). He is not classifying every physical descendant of Ishmael or of Jacob. With that limit in view, a literary comparison is still possible: Ishmael's twelve princes, listed through Kedemah, show God's providence in natural descent, while the promise ran through Isaac and Jacob's line to Christ.

Genesis also has a wider pattern of eastward movement away from God's presence. Adam and Eve are driven out with cherubim stationed "at the east of the garden of Eden" (Genesis 3:24), and Cain settles "on the east of Eden" (Genesis 4:16). Lot chooses the Jordan plain and journeys east, away from Abraham (Genesis 13:11). Kedemah's name sounds within that pattern as an echo, though Scripture never makes him a symbol of it, and eastward movement is not always away from God; the land promised to Abram stretched eastward too (Genesis 13:14). The prophets, though, look for a day when Ishmael's eastern peoples will come back toward God. "All the flocks of Kedar shall be gathered together to you, the rams of Nebaioth shall minister to you; they shall ascend with acceptance on My altar" (Isaiah 60:7). Isaiah names Kedemah's brothers, not Kedemah, yet the hope reaches his whole family. In Christ, those who were sent away can be brought near.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Most readers pass over a name like Kedemah without a second look. God does not. He promised Abraham twelve princes from Ishmael (Genesis 17:20) before Abraham was asked to send him away, renewed His assurance for Ishmael at the sending away (Genesis 21:13, 18), and the recorded list contains all twelve. That tells us something about the character of the One who makes promises. He keeps the small ones as carefully as the great ones, and He keeps them for people who stand outside the main story. Anyone who feels they live "eastward," at a distance from the center of God's people, can take heart that God's faithfulness reached Ishmael's sons in the desert. Isaiah's vision shows that God intended more for them than survival. He intended to welcome them home.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 17:20 — God's promise that Ishmael would father twelve princes.

  • Genesis 25:6 — Abraham sends his other sons "eastward," the word Kedemah's name echoes.

  • Genesis 25:12–18 — Ishmael's genealogy. Kedemah is the twelfth son, and the family's territory is described.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:29–31 — The later record that preserves Kedemah's name.

  • Isaiah 60:7 — The prophetic hope that Ishmael's descendants, Kedar and Nebaioth, will worship at God's altar.

  • Galatians 4:22–31 — Paul's interpretation of Ishmael and Isaac as sons of flesh and of promise.
