KEDAR - (Qêdâr, Strong's H6938)

Summary

Kedar was the second-listed son of Ishmael and a grandson of Abraham through Hagar (Genesis 25:13; 1 Chronicles 1:29). His descendants became a powerful nomadic Arabian tribe east of Israel. They were known for their archers, their large flocks, and their black tents, and the Bible later uses the name for the people and their desert region. The name is generally traced to a Hebrew root meaning "to be dark" or "dusky." Its exact derivation is probable rather than certain, and Scripture itself never comments on what the name means.

Kedar appears only twelve times, in eleven verses, yet those few verses trace a striking arc. Ishmael's son becomes a byword for living among hostile people ("Woe is me... that I dwell among the tents of Kedar!" Psalm 120:5). He becomes a rebuke to Israel's unfaithfulness (Jeremiah 2:10–11) and a target of God's judgment (Isaiah 21:16–17; Jeremiah 49:28–29). Then Isaiah sees something no one expected. Kedar's villages lift their voices in praise to the LORD (Isaiah 42:11), and Kedar's flocks "ascend with acceptance on My altar" (Isaiah 60:7). The New Testament never names Kedar, but the gospel brings Arabs to hear "the wonderful works of God" (Acts 2:11), and people from every nation stand before the Lamb (Revelation 7:9). Those events show what Isaiah's promise was pointing toward: God intends to bring the far-off family of Ishmael home to worship Him.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The name Kedar refers to one man and to the people and region that came from him. The man is Ishmael's son listed second, after the firstborn Nebajoth (Genesis 25:13; 1 Chronicles 1:29). That places him in the patriarchal period as a great-grandson of Terah and a nephew of Isaac. In the poets and prophets, "Kedar" almost always means his descendants: an Arabian tribe living in the Syro-Arabian desert east of Israel. Sometimes the name stands for the eastern desert world as a whole. The Bible pairs Kedar with Nebaioth (Isaiah 60:7), with Arabia (Ezekiel 27:21), and with "the men of the East" (Jeremiah 49:28). It is not always clear whether later prophets mean strictly Kedar's bloodline or the wider nomadic Arab population that went by his name.

The name is commonly connected with the Hebrew verb qadar (Strong's H6937), which means "to be dark," "dusky," or, in a related sense, "to mourn." The meaning "dark" is the usual and probable explanation, but it remains a lexical derivation. The Bible does not explain it. Readers have often noticed that "the tents of Kedar" in Song of Solomon 1:5 were dark, since nomads wove their tents from black goat hair. That link is an apt observation. The text itself does not present it as a wordplay on the name, and the name's meaning should not be read as a verdict on the people's spiritual condition.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Origins in God's promise to Ishmael. Kedar's existence is itself a promise kept. God told Abraham that Ishmael would be fruitful, would multiply, and would father twelve princes (Genesis 17:20). When Hagar and her son were sent away, Scripture records, "So God was with the lad; and he grew and dwelt in the wilderness, and became an archer" (Genesis 21:20). Kedar appears among those twelve sons (Genesis 25:13–16), and the Chronicler keeps his name in Israel's genealogies centuries later (1 Chronicles 1:29).

A byword in Israel's poetry. By the time of Israel's psalms and songs, Kedar had become a familiar picture of the desert people beyond Israel's borders. In Psalm 120 a pilgrim cries, "Woe is me, that I dwell in Meshech, that I dwell among the tents of Kedar!" (120:5). He goes on to complain that he has lived too long with those who hate peace, and that when he speaks for peace, they are for war (120:6–7). Meshech lay far to the north, while Kedar lay in the eastern desert. Since no one lived in both places at once, the psalmist is almost certainly using the two names as figures for hostile, foreign surroundings. Song of Solomon uses Kedar in a different way. The bride says, "I am dark, but lovely, O daughters of Jerusalem, like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon" (1:5). She has been darkened by the sun while working in the vineyards (1:6). The dark tents of the desert give her an image for her weathered appearance, and Solomon's fine curtains give her an image for her loveliness.

A measuring stick for loyalty. Through Jeremiah, God tells Israel to look west across the coasts of Cyprus and east to Kedar: "Send to Kedar and consider diligently, and see if there has been such a thing" (Jeremiah 2:10). He then asks whether any nation has ever changed its gods, even though those gods are not gods at all. Yet His own people have exchanged their Glory for what does not profit (2:11). Kedar's stubborn loyalty to worthless gods exposes Israel's unfaithfulness to the living God.

Judgment on Kedar's glory. Isaiah's oracle against Arabia announces that within a year "all the glory of Kedar will fail" (Isaiah 21:16, paraphrased). It adds that "the remainder of the number of archers, the mighty men of the people of Kedar, will be diminished; for the LORD God of Israel has spoken it" (21:17). Isaiah names no invader, and classic commentators often place his oracle in the Assyrian period. Jeremiah later gives a separate warning that names the instrument: Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon would strike Kedar, devastate the men of the East, and carry off their tents, curtains, flocks, and camels (Jeremiah 49:28–29). Ezekiel shows what made Kedar prosperous: the princes of Kedar traded lambs, rams, and goats with the great port of Tyre (Ezekiel 27:21).

Praise and offering. Isaiah does not leave Kedar under judgment. In the chapter that opens with the Servant of the LORD, the whole earth is called to sing a new song. The desert joins in: "Let the wilderness and its cities lift up their voice, the villages that Kedar inhabits" (Isaiah 42:11). In Isaiah's vision of Zion's coming glory, the wealth of the East streams to Jerusalem: "All the flocks of Kedar shall be gathered together to you, the rams of Nebaioth shall minister to you; they shall ascend with acceptance on My altar, and I will glorify the house of My glory" (Isaiah 60:7).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Kedar came from God's promise to bless Ishmael (Genesis 17:20; 21:20). The son who was sent away from Abraham's tent was not forgotten, and his line grew into a people. Scripture also states that Kedar would be judged like every proud nation (Isaiah 21:16–17; Jeremiah 49:28). Finally, it states that Kedar would one day praise the LORD and bring offerings He would accept (Isaiah 42:11; 60:7).

From these statements a careful reader can draw out the larger shape. Ishmael's descendants lived for centuries outside Israel's worship, and to Israel they often seemed foreign or hostile. Isaiah's vision does not erase the difference between Isaac's line and Ishmael's. Instead, it brings Ishmael's son to Jerusalem as a worshiper. The family that was divided in Abraham's tent is pictured gathering at one altar.

The judgment on Kedar's archers deserves attention. Isaiah singles out "the mighty men" who handled the bow (21:17), and archery was the very skill that marked Ishmael in the wilderness (Genesis 21:20). Isaiah does not draw this connection himself. Still, it is fitting that God's judgment was announced against the strength Kedar had inherited and trusted, and that in Isaiah's book this warning appears before the later passages that summon Kedar to praise.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never mentions Kedar, so any Christ-centered connection rests on the passages that carry Kedar's promises forward. Those connections are solid. Matthew quotes the Servant song at the start of Isaiah 42 as fulfilled in Jesus and closes with the words that in His name the Gentiles will trust (Matthew 12:18–21; Isaiah 42:1–4). Kedar's call to sing (42:11) belongs to that same chapter. The praise rising from Kedar's villages is part of the world's response to the Servant whom Matthew identifies as Christ.

Isaiah 60 also reaches into the New Testament. Its picture of nations bringing their wealth to the city of God's glory is echoed in Revelation's vision of the nations bringing their glory into the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:24–26). The verse just before Kedar's flocks speaks of gold and incense from the East (Isaiah 60:6). It is hard to read the visit of the wise men from the East, with their gold and frankincense (Matthew 2:1–11), without hearing that echo. Matthew does not cite Isaiah 60 there, so this is a strong thematic resonance rather than a declared fulfillment.

The story of Pentecost brings Kedar's region directly into the gospel's reach. Among the crowd in Jerusalem were "Cretans and Arabs" who declared, "we hear them speaking in our own tongues the wonderful works of God" (Acts 2:11). Paul himself went into Arabia after his conversion (Galatians 1:17). The sacrifice that Isaiah saw accepted on God's altar finds its deepest meaning in the one sacrifice of Christ. Through Him people from every nation, Ishmael's children included, are brought near. John saw "a great multitude which no one could number, of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9).

5. Spirit & Life

Psalm 120 gives words to anyone who has felt surrounded by people who want conflict. The pilgrim does not pretend things are fine: "Woe is me... that I dwell among the tents of Kedar!" (120:5). That psalm opens the Songs of Ascents, the songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem. The grief of living in Kedar becomes the starting point for the climb toward God's house. Isaiah then shows that God's purposes reach farther than the pilgrim could see. The same Kedar that "hates peace" is summoned to lift its voice in praise (Isaiah 42:11). The people we find hardest to live among are not beyond God's reach, and He is able to make enemies into worshipers.

Jeremiah's challenge still searches the heart. God pointed His people to a pagan tribe that would not abandon gods who were no gods (Jeremiah 2:10–11). Kedar's loyalty was misplaced, yet it put Israel to shame. Believers who know the living God should ask whether people devoted to empty things show more constancy than we do.

The bride in the Song speaks honestly about being weathered and darkened, yet she calls herself lovely (Song of Solomon 1:5–6). The passage is first about a young woman marked by hard labor in the sun. Many readers have also heard in it the experience of God's people, who carry the marks of a hard world and of their own failures and yet are loved and made beautiful. Above all, Isaiah 60:7 reveals God's welcome. The flocks of a people once far off are said to "ascend with acceptance on My altar." The animals themselves were ordinary sheep and rams. What makes the offering remarkable is that it comes from Ishmael's distant family and that God receives it. Peter later heard the same heart of God in the words, "What God has cleansed you must not call common" (Acts 10:15). Our acceptance rests on what God has done, not on where we came from.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:20; 21:20; 25:13 — God's promise to bless Ishmael, Ishmael as an archer in the wilderness, and Kedar as Ishmael's second-listed son.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:29 — Kedar preserved in Israel's genealogy.

  • Psalm 120:5–7 — Kedar as a figure for living among people who hate peace.

  • Song of Solomon 1:5 — the dark "tents of Kedar" as an image of a weathered appearance that is still lovely.

  • Jeremiah 2:10–11 — Kedar's loyalty to false gods used to rebuke Israel's faithlessness.

  • Isaiah 21:16–17; Jeremiah 49:28–29 — Isaiah's warning of judgment on Kedar's glory and archers, and Jeremiah's separate warning naming Nebuchadnezzar.

  • Ezekiel 27:21 — the princes of Kedar trading flocks with Tyre.

  • Isaiah 42:11 — Kedar's villages called to praise the LORD in the context of the Servant song (compare Matthew 12:18–21).

  • Isaiah 60:7 — Kedar's flocks accepted on God's altar (compare Revelation 21:24–26).

  • Acts 2:11; Revelation 7:9 — Arabs hearing the gospel, and every nation gathered before the Lamb.
