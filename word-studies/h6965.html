RISE, ARISE, STAND, ESTABLISH - (qûwm, Strong's H6965)

Summary

This Hebrew verb means to rise, stand up, or arise. Most of the time it describes ordinary movement: getting out of bed, standing to greet a guest, or rising to set out on a journey. It can also mean to stand firm or hold good, as when God declares that His counsel "shall stand," that is, it will endure and not be overturned. When the verb is used to mean make something stand, it can mean to raise up, set up, confirm, establish, or carry out. In that sense God "establishes" His covenant, "raises up" judges and kings, and "performs" what He has spoken.

Because of this range, the verb carries some of the Old Testament's central hopes. The psalmists cry, "Arise, O LORD," when they need God to step in. The prophets promise that God will raise up a Prophet, a son of David, and a righteous Branch. They also announce that the dead "shall arise." What God makes to stand cannot be knocked down by human failure or by an enemy. The word points toward the morning when Jesus rose from the dead and stood alive. On that morning the simple meaning of the word, getting up from lying down, and its deepest meaning, God keeping every promise, came together.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

This is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Old Testament. It appears in nearly every kind of writing: stories, laws, psalms, wisdom sayings, and prophecy. Its home is everyday life. When the angels reached Sodom, Lot "rose to meet them" (Genesis 19:1). The capable wife rises while it is still night (Proverbs 31:15), and armies rise for battle.

The same everyday verb is used for God's greatest acts. Hebrew has another common verb for standing still or remaining in place. This verb puts more weight on the movement upward and on the result: something brought into an upright, lasting position.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To rise, get up, or stand up. This is the physical sense, as when the angel woke Elijah and told him to arise and eat (1 Kings 19:5).

2. To rise up against. Enemies, rebels, and false witnesses "rise up" against someone (Psalm 3:1; Deuteronomy 19:16).

3. To stand, hold good, or endure. A vow, plan, or word "stands" when it stays valid and is not overturned (Numbers 30:4; Proverbs 19:21).

4. To cause to stand. This covers raising up, setting up, confirming, establishing, or carrying out a promise or command. The tabernacle was "raised up" (Exodus 40:17–18), and God "raised up judges" (Judges 2:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In stories the verb often marks the first step of a decision. Frequently people do not simply "go"; they "arise and go." Abraham "arose and went to the place of which God had told him" (Genesis 22:3). The Book of Jonah uses this pattern with pointed irony. God tells Jonah to arise and go to Nineveh, and Jonah does arise, but only to run toward Tarshish (Jonah 1:2–3). When the command comes a second time, he arises and goes where God sent him (Jonah 3:2–3). The same small word measures both his disobedience and his return.

In law the verb has a binding sense. A vow "stands" unless it is lawfully cancelled (Numbers 30). Boaz acts "to perpetuate the name of the dead" (Ruth 4:5). The phrase means keeping a dead man's name standing in Israel. Esther's decree "confirmed these matters of Purim" (Esther 9:32).

The grammar makes a real difference here. In one of its forms the verb means to cause something to stand: to raise it up, set it in place, or make it hold good. That form tells the reader who brings something into its upright, lasting position, but it does not by itself settle what part people play. The surrounding passages do that, and when God is the one acting they show that the result rests on His power and faithfulness, as His covenant promises to Abraham and to unfaithful Jerusalem make plain. Scripture also sets this verb against falling. "When I fall, I will arise," Micah says (Micah 7:8). Amos grieves that Israel has fallen and will not rise again (Amos 5:2).

4. Theological Significance

One of the clearest truths these passages teach is that God's purposes do not fall over. Proverbs 19:21 sets the many plans in a person's heart beside the LORD's counsel, which will stand. Human plans fall over through weakness, changing circumstances, and death. God speaks of Himself as "declaring the end from the beginning... Saying, 'My counsel shall stand, And I will do all My pleasure'" (Isaiah 46:10). Isaiah puts the contrast plainly: "The grass withers, the flower fades, But the word of our God stands forever" (Isaiah 40:8).

Balaam makes the same point when he asks whether God would speak and then fail to make His word good (Numbers 23:19). The phrase "make it good" translates this verb. Carrying out a promise means making the spoken word stand up as a fact in history. Jeremiah's promise that God would perform His good word and bring the exiles home after seventy years (Jeremiah 29:10) is a clear example.

The word also asks who can stand before this God. "Who may stand in His holy place?" asks Psalm 24:3. Psalm 1 adds a warning: the ungodly "are like the chaff which the wind drives away. Therefore the ungodly shall not stand in the judgment" (Psalm 1:4–5). Chaff has no weight, so it cannot stay upright in the wind of God's judgment. Standing before God is a matter of having righteousness that holds. That question waits for an answer the Psalms alone cannot fully give.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

God establishes His covenant (Genesis 6:18; 9:9–17; 17:7–21; Ezekiel 16:60–62). When God first makes a covenant with Noah, He says He will "establish" it. The covenant after the flood is made with Noah, his descendants, and every living creature, and the animals bargained for nothing (Genesis 9:9–11). God sets the terms, gives the sign of the rainbow, and holds the covenant up Himself.

With Abraham, God says He will establish His covenant with Isaac, a son not yet born to a ninety-year-old woman, though Abraham himself had just laughed at the idea (Genesis 17:17, 19, 21). The covenant rests on God's power, not on what Abraham and Sarah could produce.

Ezekiel 16 tests this as hard as it can be tested. The chapter describes Jerusalem's unfaithfulness in painful detail, and the people had despised God's oath and broken the covenant. Then God says: "Nevertheless I will remember My covenant with you in the days of your youth, and I will establish an everlasting covenant with you" (Ezekiel 16:60). The covenant stands because God makes it stand. In the following verses God also promises to provide atonement for all they had done.

"Arise, O LORD" (Numbers 10:35; Psalms 3, 7, 9, 10, 12, 68). Whenever the ark set out in the wilderness, Moses called on the LORD to rise up and scatter His enemies (Numbers 10:35). David took up those words: "Let God arise, Let His enemies be scattered" (Psalm 68:1). The cry became a regular prayer of the suffering: "Arise, O LORD; Save me, O my God!" (Psalm 3:7). "Rise up for me to the judgment You have commanded!" (Psalm 7:6). "Arise, O LORD!... Do not forget the humble" (Psalm 10:12).

These prayers ask God to end what feels like His stillness and to act on behalf of the oppressed. Psalm 12:5 records His answer: "For the oppression of the poor, for the sighing of the needy, Now I will arise," says the LORD. God's own rising is the moment the situation turns. The sighing of the needy is what moves Him to act.

God raises up deliverers (Judges 2:10–18; Deuteronomy 18:15; 2 Samuel 7:12; Jeremiah 23:5). Judges 2 sets a generation that arose beside the judges the LORD raised up. After Joshua's death, another generation "arose" that did not know the LORD (Judges 2:10). Exodus 1:8 has the same pattern, when a new king arose who did not know Joseph. In these verses the godless generation and the hostile king simply arise. Then the text says, "Nevertheless, the LORD raised up judges who delivered them" (Judges 2:16), and it repeats that the LORD raised up judges for them in Judges 2:18.

The same verb carries Israel's highest hopes about leadership. God promises to raise up a Prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15, 18). He promises to "set up" David's seed after him (2 Samuel 7:12). Through Jeremiah He promises to raise to David a Branch of righteousness, a King who will reign and prosper (Jeremiah 23:5; compare 30:9). In these promises, rescuing leadership comes from God, not from ambition or force.

The dead shall arise (Job 14:12; Isaiah 26:19). Job states the natural order without comfort: "So man lies down and does not rise." Isaiah 26:14 says the same of Judah's oppressors: they are dead and will not rise. A few lines later the prophet announces something new: "Your dead shall live; Together with my dead body they shall arise. Awake and sing, you who dwell in dust" (Isaiah 26:19). The ordinary verb for getting up becomes a promise that God will reverse death itself. Isaiah's promise answers the psalmist's question, "Shall the dead arise and praise You?" (Psalm 88:10), with a yes.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament gathers these strands in Jesus. Peter and Stephen both identify Him as the Prophet like Moses whom God promised to raise up (Acts 3:22; 7:37). Peter preaches that God swore to David to raise up the Christ to sit on his throne (Acts 2:30). James cites Amos as agreeing with God's gathering of the Gentiles (Acts 15:14–17). In Amos 9:11 God promises, "I will raise up The tabernacle of David, which has fallen down." James's quotation in Acts 15:16 words the promise differently: "will rebuild the tabernacle of David... And I will set it up." The Branch of Jeremiah 23:5 and "David their king," whom God promised to raise up for His people (Jeremiah 30:9), point to the same King.

Hosea 6:2 adds a hint of timing: "After two days He will revive us; On the third day He will raise us up, That we may live in His sight." In context the verse speaks of Israel's restoration. Paul writes that Christ rose "the third day according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:4), though he does not say which passages he has in mind. Hosea's promise of a third-day raising is a fitting echo to hear behind his words. Heard that way, what Hosea hoped for the nation, God did first in its Messiah.

At the empty tomb, the meanings of this verb meet. A dead body lay down, and the Son stood up. In raising Him, God declared Him to be the Son of God with power (Romans 1:4), made His spoken word stand, rose up against death, and answered Isaiah's promise that the dead would arise. Jesus had already inaugurated the new covenant in His blood, poured out in His death (Luke 22:20; Hebrews 9:15). The resurrection is God's confirmation that this covenant, and every promise gathered into it, will stand. The cry "Let God arise, Let His enemies be scattered" receives its fullest answer when the last enemy, death, is broken.

Even during His ministry Jesus used this word's Aramaic relative. Taking a dead girl by the hand, He said, "Talitha, cumi," which Mark translates as a command for the little girl to arise (Mark 5:41). Mark does not connect the scene to Isaiah, but it gives a concrete picture of Jesus' power to raise the dead: at His word, "Immediately the girl arose and walked" (Mark 5:42).

7. Spirit & Life

Some of the most tender uses of this word are God's commands to people who are lying down in defeat. Joshua lay on his face after Israel's loss at Ai until the LORD told him to get up and deal with the hidden sin in the camp (Joshua 7:10, 13). The angel woke Elijah twice beneath the broom tree. Each time he told the exhausted prophet, "Arise and eat," and the second time he added, "because the journey is too great for you" (1 Kings 19:5, 7). There beneath the broom tree Elijah received no rebuke. The episode begins with provision: Elijah found a cake baked on coals and a jar of water by his head, and after eating and drinking he went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb (1 Kings 19:6, 8).

Isaiah calls a ruined Jerusalem, who has drunk the cup of God's fury, to stand up (Isaiah 51:17) and to arise and shake off the dust (Isaiah 52:2). Then he calls her to arise and shine because her light has come (Isaiah 60:1). The order matters. The command to rise follows God's gift: Zion rises because the light has already come. In the same way, believers do not lift themselves out of the dust by effort. We get up because God has already acted.

This is why the righteous can say with Micah, "When I fall, I will arise" (Micah 7:8). Proverbs 24:16 says the same: a righteous man falls seven times and rises again. Getting up after a fall is not self-confidence. It is trust that God's word about us still stands.

The same confidence gives our prayers their strength. A believer who prays "Arise, O LORD" is praying in a way God Himself has answered, and Psalm 12:5 shows that He listens for the sighing of the needy. Psalm 1 warns that the ungodly will not stand in the judgment, and Psalm 24 asks who may stand in God's holy place. Those joined to the One whom God raised up will stand. Our standing before God rests on what He has established, not on how steady we feel.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 25:7 — While a plague was spreading, Phinehas "rose from among the congregation and took a javelin in his hand." Psalm 106:30 remembers that the plague stopped when he stood up and intervened, though that psalm uses a different Hebrew verb. One man's rising turned away wrath. It can be read as a small picture of the Intercessor to come.

  • 2 Kings 13:21 — A dead man thrown into Elisha's tomb touched the prophet's bones, revived, and stood on his feet. It is an early sign that God can make the dead rise.

  • 1 Samuel 2:8; Psalm 113:7 — God raises the poor out of the dust and seats them with princes. Hannah and the psalmist praise a God who lifts the lowly.

  • Psalm 40:2 — God brought the psalmist up out of the pit and set his feet on a rock. For the psalmist, the place where he stands is God's gift.

  • Ruth 4:5, 10 — Boaz redeems so that the name of Ruth's dead husband will stand in Israel, and through that line comes the family of David.

  • Esther 9:31–32 — The days of Purim are confirmed so that the memory of the Jews' deliverance would stay standing for every generation.
