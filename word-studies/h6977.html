LOCKS - (qᵉvutstsâh, Strong's H6977)

Summary

The Hebrew word qᵉvutstsâh means locks of hair: the curls or tresses of a person's head. It appears only twice in the Bible, both times in Song of Solomon 5, and both times it describes the hair of the bride's beloved. That rarity matters. The same small, physical detail appears at two very different moments in the bride's story, and the contrast between those moments is where the word's significance lies.

In Song 5:2 the beloved stands outside her door at night with his locks wet "with the drops of the night," asking her to open. She hesitates, and when she finally rises, he is gone. In Song 5:11, after searching for him, she describes him to the daughters of Jerusalem and praises those same locks as "wavy, And black as a raven." She had left him standing outside in the dew, and now his hair becomes part of her glad testimony to his beauty. Christians have long read this Song as a picture of Christ and His people. Read that way, the order of the poem (her delay, his withdrawal, her search, and then her praise) suggests a heart moving from comfortable delay to wholehearted adoration of the One who waited outside in the night.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Qᵉvutstsâh is a Hebrew noun used only in the Song of Solomon. The Old Testament has a common, everyday word for hair, but this one is more specific. It refers to locks or tresses, hair noticed for its shape and fullness. Scholars are not certain where the word comes from, so its origin adds little to our understanding. Its two uses in the poem tell us far more.

Both occurrences fall in one chapter of love poetry, and both come to us through the bride's telling. In 5:2 she recounts the night scene, and the words about the dew-covered locks are her beloved's own description of himself. In 5:11 she speaks about him directly. The setting is intimate and the language deliberately sensory: dew, night, gold, ravens, spices, myrrh. The poet asks the reader to see this man, and his hair is one of the first things the bride describes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Locks or tresses of hair, the curls of the head. The word names a physical feature and carries no symbolic meaning by itself. In Song 5:2 the locks are wet with night dew; in 5:11 they are dark and wavy.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears in two verses, and both pair "locks" with "head." In 5:2 the beloved speaks of himself: "my head is covered with dew, My locks with the drops of the night." In 5:11 the bride speaks about him: "His head is like the finest gold; His locks are wavy." The shift from his voice to hers matters. First he mentions his dew-covered head and locks as a reason for her to open the door. Later she describes him as an admirer would, celebrating him. The word stays the same, but the speaker's purpose changes from a plea to praise.

4. Theological Significance

Taken as a portrait of divine love, these verses reveal two things about the One who loves His people. He pursues patiently, and He is worth pursuing.

The beloved in 5:2 does not force the door. He knocks, speaks tender names ("my sister, my love, My dove, my perfect one"), and explains that he has been out in the night. Love that has come through the night and still asks rather than demands shows something of God's character. He seeks a willing response, not a coerced one.

The beloved in 5:11 is pictured at full strength and splendor. Proverbs 20:29 says, "The glory of young men is their strength, And the splendor of old men is their gray head." The proverb speaks of youthful strength and the gray hair of age; it does not mention dark hair. In the Song, the raven-black locks under a head "like the finest gold" belong to the bride's picture of the beloved's beauty, and many readers see in them a man in his prime. The One who waited at the door is not diminished by His patience. He remains glorious, and the bride's later description recognizes that glory.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:2–6: Locks wet with the night. The bride is half asleep: "I sleep, but my heart is awake." She hears her beloved's voice and his knocking. His appeal is personal and specific. He tells her that his head is covered with dew and his locks with the drops of the night. The poem does not say how long he has been there. Still, the wet hair is a telling detail: he has come to her through the night and stands waiting at her door.

Her answer, in verse 3, is an excuse. She has already undressed and washed her feet, and she does not want to get up and soil them again. Her reasons are not wicked, only comfortable, and that is the point. When she finally rises and opens (5:4–6), he has withdrawn. She seeks him but cannot find him, and calls but receives no answer (5:6). Then she goes out into the city to look for him.

Song of Solomon 5:10–16: The same locks, now praised. When the daughters of Jerusalem ask what makes her beloved better than any other (5:9), she answers with a full portrait, beginning at his head. "My beloved is white and ruddy, Chief among ten thousand. His head is like the finest gold; His locks are wavy, And black as a raven." She goes on to his eyes, cheeks, lips, hands, body, legs, and countenance, and ends: "Yes, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, And this is my friend."

The word qᵉvutstsâh links these two scenes. Only a few verses earlier, the beloved mentioned his dew-wet locks in his appeal at the door, and she answered with an excuse (5:3). Now, after he has withdrawn and she has searched for him, she praises those same locks in her answer to the daughters of Jerusalem, and they are among the first features she names. The poem does not say she forgot her delay. It simply sets the scenes in order: the appeal, the delay, the search, and then the praise. Nothing in the text explains what the search taught her, but many readers see in that order a love that has been awakened. What the poem does show is that the woman who delayed when he knocked now tells others he is "chief among ten thousand."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song of Solomon is first a celebration of love between a man and a woman. The church has also long heard in it the love between Christ and His people. The New Testament does not quote these verses, so the connection is a resonance rather than a stated fulfillment, but it is a strong one.

Jesus speaks to the church at Laodicea, a church He calls lukewarm, in words that echo Song 5:2: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20). The parallel goes beyond the knocking. Like the bride, Laodicea is comfortable, and it does not know its own need (Revelation 3:17), and Jesus' knocking comes with a loving rebuke: "As many as I love, I rebuke and chasten. Therefore be zealous and repent" (Revelation 3:19). The Lord stands outside His own people's door and asks to be let in.

The bride's portrait in 5:10–16 also sits alongside Psalm 45, where the royal bridegroom is addressed: "You are fairer than the sons of men; Grace is poured upon Your lips." The Bible's picture of the Bridegroom King moves toward Christ, the One whose beauty and strength never fade. Read in that light, the night He endured for His people reached its depth at the cross. He rose in undiminished glory, and the church's worship tells the world what He is like.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride's excuse in 5:3 sounds a lot like ours. Her words are not a rejection of her beloved, only a reluctance to be disturbed. Many believers know that state well: Christ's voice is heard, His nearness is real, yet the response is put off because the moment is inconvenient. Read as a picture of Christ, the wet locks in 5:2 show what that delay looks like from His side of the door. He has been standing outside in the night.

The poem does not end in shame. The same detail returns as praise. Scripture does not describe what changed inside her, but the sequence offers a picture of awakened love: after loss and searching, the bride answers with a full portrait of her beloved, and among its first details are the locks he had mentioned at the door. Peter describes this kind of love in believers who have never seen Christ: "whom having not seen you love. Though now you do not see Him, yet believing, you rejoice with joy inexpressible and full of glory" (1 Peter 1:8). Devotion like this does not stay private. It answers the question "What is your beloved more than another?" by describing Him.

The clear foundation for applying these verses is Jesus' own word to Laodicea, and the Song illustrates that word with both a warning and a hope. The warning is that comfort can keep us from opening to the One who stands at the door and knocks (Revelation 3:20). The hope is that He does not stop being lovely while we hesitate. His call is to "be zealous and repent" (Revelation 3:19), and a heart that turns to seek Him will find more in Him to praise than it had noticed before.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Qᵉvutstsâh occurs only in Song of Solomon 5:2 and 5:11, and both verses have been discussed above. For the fuller picture in which 5:11 belongs, read the bride's entire description of her beloved in Song of Solomon 5:10–16. There "locks" comes near the start of a portrait that ends with "he is altogether lovely."
