BEAM, ROOF - (qôwrâh, Strong's H6982)

Summary

The Hebrew word qôwrâh means a beam: a heavy timber cut and set in place to span the walls of a building and hold up its roof. It appears only five times in the Old Testament. Because there are so few uses, each one stands out. The sons of the prophets haul beams from the Jordan to build a place to live. Solomon covers the beams of the Temple with gold. The Song of Solomon describes the lovers' shelter: "The beams of our houses are cedar." In Lot's mouth the word stands for the whole roof. He pleads that his guests have "come under the shadow of my roof."

Taken together, these passages show what a beam provides: a covered place where people can dwell, rest, love, and be kept safe. God is present at both ends of the scale. He cares about a borrowed ax head lost while the prophets cut their timber, and He fills the gold-covered house that bears His name. Lot's roof could not hold back the mob. His own guests had to rescue him, and that reversal points toward the only covering that never fails. That covering is the shelter of the Almighty, finally given in Christ, who stood between His own people and danger and laid down His life for them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Qôwrâh is a Hebrew building word. It names a beam, a rafter, or a log destined to become one. In the dry lands of Israel, good timber was scarce. Most houses were built of stone and mud brick, and wood was used where it mattered most: across the top of the walls, carrying the roof. Fine wood such as cedar had to be brought from Lebanon, so a house with cedar beams was a sign of wealth and permanence. That background explains why a rare word for roof-timber shows up in a royal Temple, a love poem, and a hard day's work by the Jordan. In each of these passages, beams belong to a place where people live or where God is worshiped.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one basic meaning with one natural extension:

1. A structural beam or log used in building, including timber still being cut for that purpose. The prophets go to "take a beam" (2 Kings 6:2), and Solomon overlays "the beams" of the Temple (2 Chronicles 3:7).

2. By extension, the roof or covered house that the beams hold up. Lot speaks of guests who have come "under the shadow of my roof" (Genesis 19:8).

The NKJV's "tree" in 2 Kings 6:5 is the same word. The man was felling a tree in order to get a beam.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines what the beam represents. In Genesis 19 the focus is shelter, since the roof marks the line between the people inside and the crowd outside. In 2 Kings 6 the focus is labor, with beams cut by hand to enlarge a crowded dwelling. In 2 Chronicles 3 the focus is glory, as the framework of God's house disappears under gold. In the Song of Solomon the focus is delight. The poem praises the cedar and fir over the lovers' heads, and the "house" may well be the forest canopy where they meet, since the surrounding verses picture them outdoors among green growth.

One distinction matters in 2 Kings 6. The stick Elisha throws into the water in 2 Kings 6:6 is described with a different, ordinary Hebrew word for wood. The beam is what the men were working to obtain. The stick is how God restored what they lost.

4. Theological Significance

The beam passages show that God cares about where His people live. Scripture does not treat buildings as unspiritual. The God who gave detailed instructions for His own house also stood by while the prophets cut logs for theirs.

The passages also show that true shelter comes from God, not from timber. A roof gives real shelter and can mark a boundary, but it cannot enforce that boundary against people determined to break in. That lesson begins in Sodom and runs through the whole Bible. The blood on the lintel and doorposts at Passover kept the destroyer from entering Israel's houses (Exodus 12:22–23). The protection came from God, who saw the blood and "will pass over the door." Isaiah looks ahead to a day when the LORD Himself will spread "a covering" over every dwelling on Mount Zion, "a tabernacle for shade in the daytime from the heat, for a place of refuge" (Isaiah 4:5–6). The Bible's movement is from human roofs that fail toward God as the dwelling place of His people.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 19:8 — the roof that could not protect. Two angels come to Sodom, and Lot insists they stay with him. When the men of the city surround the house, Lot steps outside and appeals to the duty every host owed a guest: "only do nothing to these men, since this is the reason they have come under the shadow of my roof." In that world, a guest under your beams was in your care, and a host was expected to defend him.

Lot's appeal is correct, but his solution is monstrous. In the same breath he offers his own daughters to the mob. It is hard not to see Sodom's influence even in his sense of protection. The roof he invokes means nothing to the crowd, and they press to break down the door. Then the scene turns. The guests reach out, pull Lot inside, shut the door, and strike the attackers blind (Genesis 19:9–11). The visitors Lot claimed to shelter are the ones who shelter him. The preceding scene in Genesis 18 offers a plain contrast. There Abraham welcomed three visitors who appeared in the heat of the day, and he served them "under the tree" with water, bread, and a choice calf (Genesis 18:1–8). In that scene he received the promise of a son. Genesis 19 then opens with two angels arriving at Sodom in the evening (19:1). Lot's house did become a place of rescue that night, but the rescue came from his guests, not from the roof. The city around it was already doomed.

2 Kings 6:1–7 and 2 Chronicles 3:7 — rough timber and golden beams. The company of prophets has outgrown its quarters. They ask Elisha, "let every man take a beam from there, and let us make there a place where we may dwell" (2 Kings 6:2). They cut the timber by hand on the riverbank. Then trouble strikes: "the iron ax head fell into the water; and he cried out and said, 'Alas, master! For it was borrowed.'" The man's distress is plain: the tool was not his own, and iron tools were valuable. Elisha asks where it fell. Then he cuts off a stick and throws it into the water, and the iron floats (2 Kings 6:6). God stepped into a work site to answer an honest man's distress over a borrowed tool.

Set beside this the Temple. Solomon "overlaid the house—the beams and doorposts, its walls and doors—with gold" (2 Chronicles 3:7). The inner structure was cedar, and 1 Kings 6:18 says, "All was cedar; there was no stone to be seen." Gold then covered the wood. One set of beams is rough logs cut beside the Jordan, and the other is a framework glowing with gold beneath carved cherubim. The same God is present at both sites. He filled Solomon's house with His glory, and He made iron swim for men building a plain shelter. No dwelling is too humble for His care.

Song of Solomon 1:17 — the house where love lives. The poem describes the lovers' shelter in these words: "The beams of our houses are cedar, And our rafters of fir." Cedar was the finest building wood in the ancient world. It was strong, slow to decay, and fragrant. The Song describes the place where they meet with the language of royal architecture. Whether they mean a real house or the trees arching above them, the Song pictures a sheltered place where the two delight in one another, a covering worthy of their love, strong and lasting and beautiful. The beam image also suggests an application, though the Song does not state it: love that endures needs a structure that will bear its weight.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Lot stood at the door and offered others to save his guests. When soldiers came for Jesus in the garden, He stepped forward, identified Himself, and said of His disciples, "let these go their way" (John 18:8). He is the Host whose protection does not fail, because He puts Himself between His people and the danger. Psalm 91:1 describes the life of everyone who comes under that care: "He who dwells in the secret place of the Most High Shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty."

The Temple's golden beams invite a different comparison. Jesus spoke of His own body as the temple that would be destroyed and raised (John 2:19–21). Through Him, believers are now being "built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:22). The Bible's story of dwelling places moves from Lot's failing roof, through prophets' timber and Solomon's gold, to a living house God builds out of His people.

Christians have long felt the resonance between the load-bearing beam and the wood of the cross, which Scripture calls a "tree" (1 Peter 2:24). This is a connection of imagery, not of vocabulary, since those passages use different words. Still, the picture fits. On rough timber Christ bore the full weight of our sin, and everything God now builds rests on that work. A separate comparison can be drawn from the Song. Paul does not cite the Song, but he calls the union of husband and wife "a great mystery" that speaks "concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). For that reason Christian readers have long seen in the lovers' cedar-beamed shelter a picture of the love between Christ and His people.

7. Spirit & Life

The prophets' story offers an example of inviting God into the work of building. One of them asked Elisha to come with them, and he said, "I will go" (2 Kings 6:3). When the borrowed iron sank, the man cried out to his master at once. The same freedom belongs to believers who are building a marriage, a household, or a ministry with limited means and borrowed tools. A sinking ax head is not too small to bring to God, and an honest worry over something entrusted to you is not beneath His notice.

Lot's story is a warning. He knew the right words about shelter, but he had settled under a roof in Sodom, and the city's values seem to have worked their way under his beams. A home is protected less by its walls than by the presence of God within it. Lot was saved because his guests pulled him inside. Every believer is safe on the same terms: we are drawn into the Lord's keeping.

Applied to our own lives, the Song's cedar beams invite us to build love on something that lasts. Strong, fragrant, durable timber is a fitting picture of a faithful marriage. In the Temple, gold covered the wood. The labor and cost of what we build for God need not be seen once His glory rests on it.
