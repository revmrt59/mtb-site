KETURAH - (Qᵉṭûwrâh, Strong's H6989)

Summary

Keturah was a woman Abraham married. Genesis introduces her after the narrative of Sarah's death and Isaac's marriage, and on the traditional sequential reading the marriage came late in Abraham's life. Genesis introduces her in one plain line: "Abraham again took a wife, and her name was Keturah" (Genesis 25:1). Her name is widely understood to come from the Hebrew root associated with incense and the rising smoke of sacrifice, so it probably means something like "incense" or "fragrance." That meaning is probable rather than certain. Keturah bore Abraham six sons: Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak, and Shuah. Through them came several peoples of the eastern desert, most notably Midian. Genesis calls her a wife. First Chronicles calls her "Abraham's concubine," which marks her place as secondary to Sarah.

Keturah's story is short, but it carries a good deal of weight. Her sons show that God's promise to make Abraham a father of many nations reached beyond the single line of Isaac. They also show that being Abraham's child by birth did not make anyone the heir of the promise. Abraham gave these sons gifts and sent them "eastward, away from Isaac his son" (Genesis 25:6). Centuries later, Isaiah pictured a procession coming to Zion, with the camels of Midian and Ephah, Keturah's son and grandson, and those from Sheba who "bring gold and incense" and "proclaim the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). The sons who were sent away appear again in the prophets as worshipers, a hope that finds its answer in the gathering of the nations to Christ.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Keturah is one individual woman. No other person in Scripture shares this name. She was Abraham's wife, introduced near the close of his story, and the mother of six sons (Genesis 25:1–2; 1 Chronicles 1:32). Scripture keeps her separate from Sarah, the mother of Isaac, and from Hagar, the Egyptian mother of Ishmael. Some later Jewish tradition identified Keturah with Hagar. The biblical text does not explicitly identify Keturah as Hagar, and the most straightforward reading distinguishes them. It names different sons for each woman, and Genesis 25:6 speaks of "the concubines" in the plural.

The name Qᵉṭûwrâh is generally linked to the Hebrew root qāṭar (Strong's H6999), which describes making sacrifices or incense go up in smoke. The same family of words produces the common Hebrew term for incense. On that basis, Keturah is usually understood to mean "incense," "fragrance," or "perfumed." The connection is reasonable and widely accepted, but it rests on the sound and root of the word. Genesis does not explain the name or build any wordplay on it, so the meaning should be treated as probable and not as the key to her life. The fitting link between her name and the later story of incense, discussed below, is a canonical resonance. The text does not claim it as the reason she was named.

Scripture uses two terms for her. Genesis 25:1 calls her a "wife." First Chronicles 1:32 calls her a "concubine" (Hebrew pilegesh), and Genesis 25:6 groups her sons among "the sons of the concubines." Hagar is described the same way. She is called Abram's wife in Genesis 16:3 but is clearly secondary to Sarah. A concubine in the patriarchal world was a lawful but lesser wife. In this household, Scripture explicitly gives Isaac Abraham's estate and gives the concubines' sons gifts (Genesis 25:5–6). That was not a uniform rule for every concubine's children: the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah became tribal heirs in Israel.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Keturah's account sits between two endings: the death and burial of Sarah (Genesis 23) and the death of Abraham (Genesis 25:7–11). Genesis 24 has just told how Isaac received Rebekah, and the promised line looks secure. Then the narrator adds that Abraham "again took a wife." Genesis lists events in this order, but Hebrew narrative does not always follow strict chronology. If the marriage came after Isaac's wedding, Abraham was about 140 years old (Genesis 17:17; 23:1; 25:20). The marriage may have come earlier, but the narrative places it here, near the close of Abraham's life.

Keturah bore six sons (Genesis 25:2). Genesis follows two of the lines a little further. Jokshan fathered Sheba and Dedan. Dedan's descendants were Asshurim, Letushim, and Leummim. Midian fathered "Ephah, Epher, Hanoch, Abidah, and Eldaah." The record then sums up: "All these were the children of Keturah" (Genesis 25:3–4). First Chronicles 1:32–33 repeats the list almost word for word. That verse is how Keturah enters the genealogy of Israel's whole history, which runs from Adam to the return from exile.

The key moment comes in Genesis 25:5–6: "And Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac. But Abraham gave gifts to the sons of the concubines which Abraham had; and while he was still living he sent them eastward, away from Isaac his son, to the country of the east." Abraham did not leave the question of inheritance to be settled after his death. While he still lived, he provided for these sons and moved them away, apparently so that Isaac's place would not be contested.

Keturah's descendants appear again later in Scripture, under names that have their own Strong's entries (Midian is H4080). Midianite traders appear in the Joseph story (Genesis 37:28, 36). Moses fled to Midian and married Zipporah, daughter of Jethro, "the priest of Midian" (Exodus 2:15–21; 3:1). Jethro later heard of the exodus, blessed the LORD, and offered sacrifices to God (Exodus 18:1–12). Midian also joined Moab against Israel, and a Midianite woman was involved in the sin at Peor (Numbers 22:4–7; 25:6–18; 31). In the days of the judges, Midian and "the people of the East" overran Israel until God delivered His people through Gideon (Judges 6–8). Later Scripture remembers that deliverance (Psalm 83:9; Isaiah 9:4). Sheba, Dedan, and Ephah also appear in the prophets as trading peoples. Keturah's family became part of the life around Israel for centuries, sometimes as kin and sometimes as enemies.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states that God promised Abraham, "You shall be a father of many nations" (Genesis 17:4–5, paraphrased). Keturah's sons are part of how that promise came true in history. Abraham's body had once been "as good as dead" for having children (Romans 4:19; Hebrews 11:12, paraphrased). Isaac's birth showed God's power, and, on the traditional sequential reading, six more sons in Abraham's old age show that God's blessing on him had not run dry. Even if the marriage came earlier, the six sons and their peoples show the many-nations blessing at work.

Scripture is just as clear that the promise of the chosen line ran through Isaac alone. God had said, "In Isaac your seed shall be called" (Genesis 21:12; see Romans 9:7, paraphrased). Genesis 25:5–6 sets the two realities side by side. Isaac received "all that he had." Keturah's sons received gifts and a home in the east. Abraham did not reject them. He gave them real provision. What he did not do was let them stand alongside Isaac as heirs of the land and the promise.

From this, a careful implication follows. Paul does not name Keturah, but his reasoning in Romans 9:6–8 applies directly to her sons: not all who come from Abraham are counted as the children of promise. Abraham's sending them away was also an act of obedience. He arranged his household around what God had said, even when it meant separating sons he loved.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest later development belongs to the prophets. Isaiah 60 describes the glory of the LORD rising over Zion and the nations streaming to its light. In verse 6 Isaiah names Keturah's family: "The multitude of camels shall cover your land, The dromedaries of Midian and Ephah; All those from Sheba shall come; They shall bring gold and incense, And they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD." Midian was Keturah's son and Ephah her grandson (Genesis 25:2, 4). The Sheba named here may be Keturah's grandson or another people of the same name (compare Genesis 10:7, 28), so that identification should be held loosely. Midian and Ephah, though, are her line by name. The sons sent "away from Isaac" toward the east are seen coming back toward God's people, and they come as worshipers.

Here the meaning of her name becomes fitting, though the text never draws the link itself. A woman whose name probably means "incense" became the mother of desert peoples who carried goods along the trade routes. In Isaiah they bring incense to honor the LORD. Malachi 1:11 likewise foresees incense offered to God's name among the nations. This is a thematic resonance and not a stated type, but it is a striking one.

The New Testament carries the hope of the nations forward in Christ. Gentiles who were once "far off," strangers to the promises, have been "brought near by the blood of Christ" (Ephesians 2:12–13, paraphrased). Paul teaches that Abraham is the father of all who believe, Jew and Gentile alike (Romans 4:16–17). Keturah's descendants have no special path to God because of their ancestry. Like everyone else, they are brought into the promise through faith in Abraham's true Seed (Galatians 3:16, 29).

Many readers have also noticed that wise men "from the East" brought gold and frankincense to the child Jesus (Matthew 2:1, 11, paraphrased). Matthew does not identify the Magi with Keturah's people, so no link of descent should be claimed. Still, the scene matches Isaiah's picture of eastern travelers bringing gold and incense to honor the LORD's light, and it is fair to let the one passage shed light on the other.

5. Spirit & Life

Keturah's account shows God being generous and exact at the same time. He was generous in giving Abraham six more sons and in making those sons into peoples. He was exact in holding to His word that the promise would run through Isaac. God's kindness to Keturah's sons did not blur His purpose, and His purpose did not erase His kindness. Abraham reflected both when he gave gifts and then sent his sons away.

That combination speaks to anyone who has had to order family and affection around God's call. Abraham did not pretend every son had the same role, and he did not leave the hard decision for others to argue over after he died. He acted "while he was still living." Faith sometimes means arranging the things we love around what God has said, instead of bending God's word around the things we love.

Genesis also points in another direction. Leaving the promised land was not the end of the story for Keturah's sons. The narrative leaves them in "the country of the east," a direction Genesis, as a literary observation, connects in several places with distance from God's presence (Genesis 3:24; 4:16; 13:11). Isaiah, however, sees their descendants traveling back with praise. God's purpose for the nations was larger than their separation. The far-off can be brought near, and the gospel of Christ does exactly that.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 25:1–4 — Keturah's marriage to Abraham, her six sons, and the lines of Jokshan and Midian.

  • Genesis 25:5–6 — Isaac receives the inheritance, while Keturah's sons receive gifts and are sent east.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:32–33 — Keturah's place in Israel's genealogy and her status as Abraham's concubine.

  • Genesis 17:4–5 — The promise that Abraham would be a father of many nations.

  • Genesis 21:12; Romans 9:6–8 — The promised line runs through Isaac; physical descent alone does not make one a child of promise.

  • Exodus 2–3; 18; Judges 6–8 — Keturah's Midianite descendants as kin to Moses and later as oppressors of Israel.

  • Isaiah 60:6 — Midian and Ephah bring gold and incense and proclaim the LORD's praises.

  • Ephesians 2:12–13; Romans 4:16–17 — Gentiles brought near in Christ; Abraham as father of all who believe.
