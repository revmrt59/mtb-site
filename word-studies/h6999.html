BURN, BURN INCENSE, OFFER - (qâṭar, Strong's H6999)

Summary

The Hebrew verb qâṭar means to make something rise as smoke. Usually the smoke is a fragrant offering burned before God. The priests used the word for burning whole burnt offerings, and the fat of other sacrifices, on the bronze altar, and for burning sweet incense on the golden altar every morning and evening. In these settings the fire does more than destroy. It sends the offering upward to God as something He receives, a sweet aroma to the LORD. The word stands at the center of Israel's approach to God: a substitute given in their place, a priest appointed to stand between, and fragrance rising from the sanctuary at its appointed times, day after day.

The same verb also tells a darker story. In Kings, Chronicles, Jeremiah, and Hosea, Israel "burns incense" to Baal, to the queen of heaven, and on high places across the land. The act that was meant to express Israel's closest communion with God became a repeated sign of their unfaithfulness. Even so, the Old Testament does not end with Israel's failure. Through Malachi, God promises that "in every place incense shall be offered to My name." The New Testament does not quote Malachi's promise, but its picture of worship fits that promise closely: Christ's fragrant sacrifice, His unceasing intercession, and the prayers of His people from every nation rising like incense before the throne.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Qâṭar is a Hebrew verb tied to smoke and fragrance. The ordinary Hebrew noun for incense comes from the same word family. The verb belongs mainly to worship: the tabernacle and temple, the priests' daily duties, and the rival altars of idolatry.

Hebrew has a different, ordinary verb for burning something up. Leviticus 4 shows the difference clearly. The fat of the sin offering is burned (qâṭar) on the altar (Leviticus 4:10, 19). The rest of the bull is carried outside the camp and burned with another verb, one that simply means to destroy by fire (Leviticus 4:12). One fire sends an offering up to God. The other disposes of what remains.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, qâṭar means to turn something into rising smoke by fire, especially as an offering. Context determines what is burned and to whom it is offered.

1. To burn sacrificial portions on the altar so that they rise to God, as with the burnt offering in Leviticus 1:9.

2. To burn incense, whether at the golden altar (Exodus 30:7) or before false gods (Jeremiah 11:13).

3. In one poetic use, to be perfumed with fragrant smoke, describing the procession that comes up from the wilderness "perfumed with myrrh and frankincense" (Song of Songs 3:6).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers, the subject of the verb is almost always a priest, and the setting is God's altar. The priest burns the prepared offering, or its prescribed portions, on the altar (Leviticus 1:9; 3:5). In these books the NKJV usually renders the word simply as "burn."

The historical books and prophets use the word in a different way. The subject becomes kings, cities, and households. The setting moves to high places, rooftops, valleys, and streets. The object of the worship becomes Baal or "other gods" (2 Kings 22:17). The NKJV most often renders these uses as "burned incense." Habakkuk 1:16 stretches the word further, picturing a conquering nation burning incense to its own fishing net.

4. Theological Significance

The passages that use qâṭar show that God desires to receive His people's worship and that He Himself sets the terms on which He receives it. The altar was His provision. The fire, the priest, and the fragrance were all given by God, and when worship followed His design, the smoke rose to Him as something pleasing.

Because burning incense was such an intimate expression of devotion, offering it elsewhere meant giving one's deepest loyalty away. The prophets do not treat false incense as a mistake in ritual. They treat it as betrayal of the God who had cared for His people.

The setting of the act also matters. God placed the incense altar inside the Holy Place, an enclosed room served by appointed priests. Apostate Judah set up altars "according to the number of the streets of Jerusalem... altars to burn incense to Baal" (Jeremiah 11:13). Elsewhere, "they burned incense on all the high places, like the nations" (2 Kings 17:11). Incense that God had appointed for a sheltered, holy place was now burned in the streets and on the high places.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 1:9, 13; 3:5; 4:31 — the accepted substitute. In each of these offerings the worshiper brings an animal, lays a hand on it, and it dies. What the priest then burns on the altar depends on the offering. In the burnt offering the animal is first skinned and cut into its pieces (1:6), and the priest then burns "all on the altar" (1:9; also 1:13), that is, the whole prepared burnt offering rather than selected portions. In the peace offering only the fat and certain inner parts are burned (3:3–5), and the remaining meat is shared according to the offering rules, with the breast and right thigh given to the priests (7:31–32) and the thanksgiving meat eaten the same day (7:15). In the sin offering of Leviticus 4:31, the priest removes "all its fat" and burns that portion on the altar. Leviticus calls the burnt offering a sweet aroma to the LORD (1:9), and even the fat of the sin offering is burned "for a sweet aroma" with the result that "the priest shall make atonement for him, and it shall be forgiven him" (4:31). The death of the substitute produces something God receives with pleasure. When that substitute is accepted, the worshiper is accepted along with it.

Exodus 30:7–8 — fragrance morning and evening. "Aaron shall burn on it sweet incense every morning; when he tends the lamps, he shall burn incense on it" (Exodus 30:7). He burned it again at twilight when he lit the lamps, "a perpetual incense before the LORD throughout your generations" (Exodus 30:8). The incense was not one cloud that never stopped. It was a fixed rhythm, kept every morning and every evening, day after day and generation after generation. So the fragrance rose before the veil at its appointed times even when no individual worshiper had come with an offering.

A related passage shows what this incense did on the Day of Atonement. That passage uses the noun for incense rather than this verb. The high priest took burning coals "from the altar before the LORD" (Leviticus 16:12) and put incense on them "that the cloud of incense may cover the mercy seat that is on the Testimony, lest he die" (16:13). The cloud shielded the mediator in the presence of God's holiness. Leviticus 16 opens by recalling the earlier deaths of Aaron's two sons (Leviticus 16:1). Nadab and Abihu had "offered profane fire before the LORD, which He had not commanded them" (Leviticus 10:1), and they died. Leviticus does not describe every detail of what they did wrong. It says their fire was unauthorized, something the LORD "had not commanded them." Incense was accepted only when it was offered in the way God appointed.

2 Chronicles 26:16–19 — Uzziah's presumption. Uzziah was a capable king whom God "marvelously helped" (26:15). When he became strong, however, his heart was lifted up, and he entered the temple to burn incense. Azariah and eighty priests followed him in and told him plainly that burning incense belonged to the consecrated sons of Aaron. Uzziah grew furious with the censer in his hand, and leprosy broke out on his forehead before their eyes. Numbers 16:40 had already established the boundary after Korah's rebellion: no one outside Aaron's line was to come near to offer incense. Uzziah's success had come from God, yet that success gave him no right to approach God on his own terms.

Jeremiah 44:15–23 — betrayal defended. After Jerusalem fell, the Judeans in Egypt told Jeremiah they would keep burning incense to the queen of heaven. When they had done so before, they said, they had plenty of food. Since they stopped, they had lacked everything (Jeremiah 44:17–18, paraphrased). The LORD answered that He had remembered the incense they burned in Judah's cities, and that this was why disaster had come upon them (Jeremiah 44:21–23). Hosea, speaking to the northern kingdom, describes a similar blindness in the language of marriage. Israel "did not know" that God gave her grain, wine, and oil (Hosea 2:8). She burned incense to the Baals and went after her lovers, "but Me she forgot" (2:13). Hosea's picture helps explain why such worship drew so heavy a judgment: a wife was giving her husband's gifts to other lovers.

Malachi 1:11 — incense in every place. Malachi spoke to priests who offered blind and lame animals and found God's table contemptible. God said He wished someone would shut the temple doors rather than keep lighting His altar for nothing (1:10, paraphrased). Then He made a promise: "For from the rising of the sun, even to its going down, My name shall be great among the Gentiles; In every place incense shall be offered to My name, And a pure offering." Malachi sets the incense God had appointed at His sanctuary (Exodus 30:7–8) beside a promise of worship offered to His name "in every place" among the Gentiles, with a pure offering. Israel's failure would not defeat God's purpose to be worshiped.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Paul describes the cross in the language of the altar: Christ "has loved us and given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma" (Ephesians 5:2). The animals burned on Israel's altar pointed toward this one offering, which God fully accepts and through which He accepts those who belong to Christ. Hebrews also notes that Jesus suffered "outside the gate," as the sin-offering carcasses were burned outside the camp (Hebrews 13:11–12). Read together, these passages show both sides of the sin offering coming together in Christ: He bore the shame of what was carried outside the camp, and His sacrifice rose to God as a sweet-smelling aroma.

The morning and evening incense also gives a picture of what Jesus is doing now. Aaron renewed the fragrance at its two appointed times every day, and Exodus calls it a perpetual incense "throughout your generations." Of Christ, Hebrews says, "He always lives to make intercession for them" (Hebrews 7:25). Aaron's service at its set times pictured what Christ carries out without ceasing.

Uzziah's story points toward Jesus as well. "No man takes this honor to himself, but he who is called by God, just as Aaron was" (Hebrews 5:4). Hebrews goes on to say that Christ did not exalt Himself to become High Priest; God appointed Him (5:5–6). What continues from Uzziah's story is that God appoints the priest. Jesus does not come from Aaron's line: He "arose from Judah," and He serves "according to the order of Melchizedek" (Hebrews 7:14, 17). His priesthood rests on God's appointment, not on the Aaronic descent that barred Uzziah (Hebrews 7:11–17).

Revelation does not quote Malachi, yet the two books can fruitfully be read together. Before the Lamb, the elders hold "golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints" (Revelation 5:8), and they praise the Lamb for redeeming people "out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation" (5:9). An angel at the golden altar "was given much incense, that he should offer it with the prayers of all the saints" (8:3), and "the smoke of the incense, with the prayers of the saints, ascended before God" (8:4). The saints' prayers do not rise alone. Incense given from heaven rises with them. Malachi promised that incense would be offered to God's name "in every place." The New Testament never says that Revelation fulfills those words, but the scene fits them well: prayer gathered from every nation rises before God as incense.

7. Spirit & Life

David prayed, "Let my prayer be set before You as incense, The lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice" (Psalm 141:2). Read alongside David's prayer, the golden altar gives a picture of what prayer is: something offered up, kindled by God's fire, and received by Him. A believer who prays morning and evening echoes a rhythm God set long ago at the golden altar. Such a believer can pray with confidence, knowing that Christ's intercession is already rising when we begin to pray.

These passages also press an honest question: where is my incense going? The Chaldeans "sacrifice to their net, And burn incense to their dragnet; Because by them their share is sumptuous And their food plentiful" (Habakkuk 1:16). They worshiped the tools that made them rich. Hezekiah broke in pieces the bronze serpent that God once used to heal Israel, because until those days the people had been burning incense to it (2 Kings 18:4). Whatever we credit for our security, whether skill, income, or a blessing God once gave, can become the altar where our trust goes up in smoke.

The answer to misplaced worship is to come God's way. The remedy is not to try harder at devotion. The three warnings in this study are different. Uzziah entered the LORD's temple to burn incense, a duty given only to the consecrated sons of Aaron (2 Chronicles 26:16–18). Nadab and Abihu offered fire before the LORD "which He had not commanded them" (Leviticus 10:1). The Judeans in Egypt burned incense to the queen of heaven and insisted on continuing (Jeremiah 44:17–23). Uzziah, Nadab and Abihu approached the true God in a way He had not appointed. The Judeans in Egypt gave their worship to another god altogether. Christ's sacrifice and intercession open the true way, and worship offered through Him is acceptable even when it is weak.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 2:15–17 — Before the fat was burned, the priest's servant demanded raw meat from the worshiper, and the sin of Eli's sons was very great before the LORD, for men abhorred the LORD's offering.

  • 1 Kings 3:3 — Solomon loved the LORD, walking in the statutes of his father David, except that he sacrificed and burned incense at the high places. The narrator explains that the people still sacrificed at high places because no house had yet been built for the LORD's name (1 Kings 3:2), and at Gibeon, the great high place, the LORD appeared to Solomon (1 Kings 3:4–5).

  • 1 Kings 13:1–2 — While Jeroboam stood at his rival altar to burn incense, a man of God foretold that Josiah would one day defile that very altar.

  • 2 Chronicles 29:11 — Hezekiah gathered the priests and the Levites (2 Chronicles 29:4) and told them the LORD had chosen them to stand before Him, serve Him and burn incense, calling them back to their appointed temple service. Burning incense before the LORD belonged to the consecrated sons of Aaron (2 Chronicles 26:18), and the duties stayed distinct: the priests cleansed the inner house, and the Levites carried the debris out (2 Chronicles 29:16).

  • Jeremiah 33:18 — Even as judgment approached, God promised that the Levitical priests would not lack a man to kindle offerings before Him.

  • Amos 4:5 — Amos mockingly tells Israel to offer their sacrifices, exposing worship performed for show while their lives remained unchanged.
