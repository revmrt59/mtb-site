KENAN - (Qêynân, Strong's H7018)

Summary

Kenan was one of the early patriarchs before the flood. He was the son of Enosh, the father of Mahalalel, and the fourth generation from Adam through Seth. Readers of the NKJV will find him under the spelling "Cainan" in Genesis 5:9–14 and 1 Chronicles 1:2, and again in Luke's genealogy of Jesus (Luke 3:37). The meaning of his name is uncertain. Scholars have connected it with a Hebrew root meaning "to acquire" or "possess," with a word for "nest" (as Strong's has it), and with other proposals such as "possessor" (Smith's Bible Dictionary) or "smith" (Easton's). None of these can be settled, so none should carry spiritual weight on its own.

Scripture tells us very little about Kenan. He was born, fathered a son at seventy, had other sons and daughters, lived 910 years, and died. His importance lies in where God placed him. He stands in the line God preserved from Adam to Noah, the line that Chronicles later traces toward Israel and that Luke traces all the way to Jesus Christ. His name in that chain shows that God kept His promise of a Redeemer alive through generations whose lives were mostly unremarkable. It also shows that the same death that ended Kenan's 910 years is what Christ came to overcome.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Kenan is a single individual: the son of Enosh and grandson of Seth, born when Enosh was ninety (Genesis 5:9). His line runs Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel. The NKJV spells his name "Cainan" in both Hebrew Bible passages, and the same spelling appears in Luke 3:37, where Luke's Greek uses a separately indexed name (G2536) for this same ancestor.

That Greek spelling causes a common confusion. Luke 3:36 names a second Cainan, "the son of Arphaxad," who lived after the flood. The Masoretic Hebrew text of Genesis 11 does not include this post-flood Cainan, though the Septuagint does. He is a different person from the Kenan of Genesis 5. He falls outside Strong's H7018 and should not be merged with the patriarch studied here.

The meaning of Qêynân is uncertain. Proposed links include the root qanah ("to acquire, possess, create"), the noun qen ("nest"), and suggested meanings such as "possession" or "possessor" (Smith's Bible Dictionary) and "smith" (Easton's). Some of these overlap with ideas associated with the name Cain. Since the derivation is unresolved, it is safest to note the proposals and let the biblical record, not the etymology, describe the man.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Genesis records Kenan's life in three verses: "Cainan lived seventy years, and begot Mahalalel. After he begot Mahalalel, Cainan lived eight hundred and forty years, and had sons and daughters. So all the days of Cainan were nine hundred and ten years; and he died" (Genesis 5:12–14). No place, deed, or saying is attached to him.

The setting around those verses still matters. Kenan was born into the generation of Enosh, and Genesis 4:26 says that in Enosh's days people began to call on the name of the LORD. Alongside this line, Genesis 4 traces Cain's descendants. They built a city and developed herding, music, and metalwork, and their line ended with Lamech's boast of killing a man and his claim of vengeance seventy-sevenfold (Genesis 4:17–24). Kenan lived through centuries of a world moving toward the corruption and violence that would bring the flood (Genesis 6:5, 11–13).

Using the Hebrew figures, Kenan was born when Adam was 325 years old, so his life overlapped Adam's by about six centuries. He also lived into the early years of Noah. People in that world were not separated from the beginning by long ages. Adam's own memory of Eden was within living reach. Scripture does not describe how knowledge of God was handed down, but the chronology suggests it passed through very few hands.

Kenan appears again in 1 Chronicles 1:2 ("Cainan, Mahalalel, Jared,"), where the post-exilic community opened its history of Israel with Adam. Luke 3:37 lists him once more among the ancestors of Jesus.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Genesis 5 repeats a steady rhythm: a man lived, begot a son, had other children, and died. Two truths run through it at once. God was keeping a line alive toward the promised offspring of the woman (Genesis 3:15) and toward Noah, through whom humanity would be preserved. At the same time, the sentence of Genesis 2:17 was being carried out. The refrain "and he died" closes entry after entry, with Enoch the explicit exception (Genesis 5:24) and Noah's death recorded later (Genesis 9:29), and Kenan's 910 years do not change that ending.

Genesis does not tell us whether Kenan personally called on the LORD. We can say that he belonged to the line through which worship began and through which God carried His purpose forward. The deliberate parallel between Cain's line and Seth's line points the same way. Kenan and Cain, Mahalalel and Mehujael, Jared and Irad, Methuselah and Methushael, and the two Lamechs sound strikingly alike, and both lines include an Enoch. The similarity invites comparison. Cain's line ends with a man boasting of revenge. Seth's line produces an Enoch who "walked with God" (Genesis 5:24) and a Noah who "found grace" (Genesis 6:8). The contrast is drawn from the arrangement of the text and is a careful inference rather than a statement Scripture makes about Kenan himself.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Luke's genealogy is explicit. It runs backward from Jesus "the son of Mahalalel, the son of Cainan" (Luke 3:37), then "the son of Enosh, the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38). Matthew begins with Abraham and presents Jesus as the son of David and heir of Israel's promises. Luke goes further back, past Abraham and past the flood, to the first man. By naming Kenan, Luke ties Jesus to the whole human family, not only to Israel.

That connection gives weight to the refrain of Genesis 5. Paul teaches that death reigned from Adam onward and that in Adam all die, while in Christ all will be made alive (Romans 5:12–17; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22). Kenan's name belongs both to the record of those who died and to the family line of the One who rose. Reading Genesis 5 next to Luke 3 is reading Scripture's own development, not imposing a pattern on it.

5. Spirit & Life

Scripture records no city built by Kenan, as Cain built one, and no work in bronze and iron, as Tubal-Cain did. Scripture remembers him for living, raising children, and dying, and God wove that ordinary life into the line that led to His Son. Most believers will not have their deeds recorded either. Kenan's place in the genealogy shows that God's purposes move through generations of quiet continuance as surely as through famous moments.

His 910 years also say something sobering. Scripture records no longer lifespans than those of the patriarchs before the flood, and still the record closes with "and he died." Length of life did not undo the curse. Only the descendant at the end of Luke's list could do that. The hope Kenan's line carried was never in the line itself. It was in the God who kept His word through it.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 4:26 – Enosh's generation, into which Kenan was born, began to call on the name of the LORD.

  • Genesis 4:17–24 – Cain's line, the literary counterpart to Seth's line.

  • Genesis 5:9–14 – Kenan's birth, his son Mahalalel, his 910 years, and his death.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:2 – Kenan in Israel's genealogy from Adam.

  • Luke 3:36 – A separate, post-flood Cainan, not to be confused with Kenan.

  • Luke 3:37–38 – Kenan in the ancestry of Jesus, traced back to Adam and to God.

  • Romans 5:12–17; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 – Death through Adam and life through Christ, the theological frame for Genesis 5.
