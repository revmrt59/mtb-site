JEALOUSY, ZEAL, ENVY - (qinʼâh, Strong's H7068)

Summary

The Hebrew word qinʼâh names a burning, consuming passion that will not rest while something it cares about is threatened. The NKJV translates it as "jealousy," "zeal," or "envy," and the English word changes because the heart behind the passion changes. In a husband who fears his marriage is being betrayed, it is jealousy. In the LORD, it is the fierce love that will not share His people with idols and the tireless energy that keeps His promises. In a selfish heart, it is envy that resents a neighbor's good and, as Proverbs 14:30 puts it, becomes "rottenness to the bones."

The word's richest uses reveal God Himself. Isaiah's great promise of the Messiah's endless kingdom ends with the guarantee, "The zeal of the LORD of hosts will perform this" (Isaiah 9:7). The same zeal that burns against Israel's unfaithfulness also burns for Jerusalem when enemies trample her. The word reaches its human summit in the Psalmist's cry, "Zeal for Your house has eaten me up" (Psalm 69:9), which the disciples remembered when Jesus cleansed the temple. God is never indifferent toward the people He loves, and our hope rests on His zeal more than on our own.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Qinʼâh is a Hebrew noun. It belongs to a family of words about jealousy and zeal, but it has its own wide range. It appears in the law (Numbers 5; 25; Deuteronomy 29), in the history of Judah's deliverance from Assyria (2 Kings 19), in the Psalms and wisdom books, in the Song of Solomon, and most heavily in the prophets, especially Isaiah and Ezekiel.

Many of its settings involve marriage, whether a real marriage, as in Numbers 5, or the prophets' picture of the LORD as Israel's Husband. That setting matters. Biblical jealousy is the passion that rises when an exclusive relationship is threatened. Elsewhere Scripture declares that the LORD's very name is Jealous (Exodus 34:14, using a different Hebrew form), and qinʼâh shows what that name looks like in action.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Qinʼâh means an intense, ardent passion aroused by a rival claim or a threatened bond. Context decides its moral quality and the best English word.

1. Jealousy for an exclusive relationship: a husband's or God's burning response when faithfulness is betrayed or doubted (Numbers 5:14; Ezekiel 16:38).

2. Zeal: ardent devotion that acts to defend or accomplish what it loves, whether God's zeal for His purposes or a servant's zeal for God (Isaiah 9:7; Psalm 69:9).

3. Envy or rivalry: resentful passion toward another person's possession or success (Proverbs 14:30; Ecclesiastes 4:4).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The most important variable is who feels qinʼâh and for what. When the LORD is the subject, the word describes either His burning response to idolatry (Deuteronomy 29:20; Ezekiel 8:3) or His fierce commitment to rescue His people (Zechariah 1:14). When a faithful person is the subject, zeal for God's house or word takes hold of the whole person (Psalm 119:139). When the subject is a self-centered heart, the same intensity becomes envy that destroys its owner (Job 5:2).

The word often appears with fire language. Psalm 79:5 asks whether God's jealousy will burn like fire, and Zephaniah 1:18 speaks of the land devoured by the fire of His jealousy. The pairing captures the word's character as heat and consuming energy. The difference between "for" and "against" matters as well. God is zealous for Zion (Zechariah 8:2) and jealous against those who betray or plunder her.

Human zeal can also be counterfeit. Jehu boasted, "see my zeal for the LORD" (2 Kings 10:16), and he did destroy Baal worship from Israel. Yet he "did not turn away from the sins of Jeroboam," the golden calves at Bethel and Dan, and he "took no heed to walk in the law of the LORD God of Israel with all his heart" (2 Kings 10:29, 31). Claiming zeal for God does not guarantee wholehearted obedience.

4. Theological Significance

God's jealousy tells us something about His love. A husband who shrugged at his wife's adultery would not be generous; he would be indifferent. The LORD refuses to be one god among many to His people, because He is the only true God and because idols ruin the people who serve them. Deuteronomy 29:20 warns that when a man turns to other gods, "the anger of the LORD and His jealousy would burn against that man." Ezekiel 16 and 23 picture Jerusalem and Samaria as faithless wives, and God's jealousy brings judgment on their unfaithfulness (Ezekiel 16:38, 42; 23:25).

The same jealousy turns outward in protection. When Edom and the nations seized His land "with wholehearted joy and spiteful minds," the LORD declared, "Surely I have spoken in My burning jealousy against the rest of the nations" (Ezekiel 36:5). The very next verse explains why: "because you have borne the shame of the nations" (36:6). The fire that disciplines God's people is also the fire that defends them. Zephaniah shows the order. After the fire of His jealousy falls on the earth (Zephaniah 3:8), God promises to give the peoples a pure language so that all may call on His name (3:9). His jealousy clears the ground for restored worship.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 9:7; 37:32; 2 Kings 19:31 — The zeal that guarantees God's promises. Isaiah 9 describes a Child born, a Son given, whose government and peace will have "no end, Upon the throne of David." The prophecy closes by naming who will make it happen: "The zeal of the LORD of hosts will perform this." The verse does not rest the promise on Israel's faithfulness or on a strong king. It rests the promise on God's own burning determination. The same phrase returns when Assyria had swallowed the cities of Judah and Jerusalem stood alone. Isaiah promised that "out of Jerusalem shall go a remnant" and added, "The zeal of the LORD of hosts will do this" (Isaiah 37:32; 2 Kings 19:31). That night the Assyrian army was struck down. In Isaiah 59, when God saw that there was no one to intercede, He armed Himself with righteousness and salvation and "was clad with zeal as a cloak" (59:17). In each case, the outcome rests on God's zeal, not on human strength.

Numbers 5 and Song of Solomon 8:6 — Jealousy in marriage. Seven of the word's uses fall in the law of jealousy, where a husband suspects his wife of unfaithfulness, whether or not she is guilty (Numbers 5:14). The law did not let him take matters into his own hands. He brought a grain offering of jealousy to the priest, and the case was placed before the LORD. The law treats such jealousy as a real force that a marriage must reckon with, and then it puts that force under God's judgment. The Song of Solomon gives the same passion its noblest voice: "For love is as strong as death, Jealousy as cruel as the grave; Its flames are flames of fire, A most vehement flame" (8:6). Love that sets itself like a seal on the beloved will not let go. This human language gives readers the vocabulary for the LORD's jealousy as Israel's Husband.

Numbers 25:11 and Psalm 69:9 — Zeal that shares God's heart. When Israel joined itself to Baal of Peor, an Israelite man brought a Midianite woman into the camp in full view of Moses and the weeping congregation. Phinehas took a javelin and killed them both, and the plague stopped (Numbers 25:6–8). God said Phinehas "has turned back My wrath from the children of Israel, because he was zealous with My zeal among them." Phinehas's passion matched God's own, and his act was a judgment carried out on open rebellion. The Psalmist goes further: "Because zeal for Your house has eaten me up, And the reproaches of those who reproach You have fallen on me" (Psalm 69:9). Zeal for God here is costly. It consumes the one who carries it and draws to him the insults aimed at God.

Proverbs 14:30; 27:4; Ecclesiastes 4:4 — Envy that rots. Cut off from love for God, the same passion destroys the person who harbors it. "A sound heart is life to the body, But envy is rottenness to the bones" (Proverbs 14:30). Proverbs 27:4 ranks it above rage: "Wrath is cruel and anger a torrent, But who is able to stand before jealousy?" Ecclesiastes notes that even skilled work becomes an occasion for a neighbor's envy, and calls this rivalry "vanity and grasping for the wind" (4:4).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The zeal of the LORD that Isaiah promised would establish David's throne forever finds its goal in Jesus, the Son given (Isaiah 9:6–7). The kingdom He brings stands on God's resolve to finish what He began.

When Jesus drove the traders out of the temple, "His disciples remembered that it was written, 'Zeal for Your house has eaten Me up'" (John 2:17). John does not use a formal fulfillment formula here. He says the disciples remembered Psalm 69:9 when they saw what Jesus did, and they recognized in Him the zeal the psalm describes. Paul applies the second half of the same verse to Christ, who bore the reproaches aimed at God (Romans 15:3). Jesus was consumed by zeal for His Father's house, and that zeal took Him to the cross, where He carried the shame of others. Phinehas turned back God's wrath by executing judgment on two people caught in open sin. Christ turned back wrath in a very different way. He did not strike down sinners; He bore their sin in His own body and gave Himself for them. Both show God's zeal, but the judgment at Peor and the self-giving sacrifice of the cross are different kinds of acts.

Isaiah 59's warrior God, clothed with zeal, comes as Redeemer to Zion (59:20), a promise Paul cites in Romans 11:26. Isaiah also foresaw that under the coming Branch of Jesse, "the envy of Ephraim" toward Judah would depart (Isaiah 11:13). The Messiah heals the rivalry that divided God's people. The marriage theme continues as well. Paul tells the Corinthians that he is jealous for them with godly jealousy, having betrothed them to one Husband, Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2).

7. Spirit & Life

Many people today hear "jealous God" and imagine insecurity. Scripture presents a God who loves too deeply to be shared with idols that would destroy His people. When He confronts the rivals in a believer's heart, He is acting as the faithful Husband of Ezekiel 16 and the Song's unyielding love, refusing to give up what He has claimed.

Isaiah's repeated line, "The zeal of the LORD of hosts will do this," gives a believer solid ground. Jerusalem was threatened by the Assyrian army, and its deliverance rested on the LORD, who said, "I will defend this city" (Isaiah 37:35). The promise of the Messiah's kingdom did not depend on Israel's record. A Christian's assurance rests the same way on God's determination to finish His work.

The word also tests our hearts. The same intensity can become Psalm 69's zeal for God's house or Proverbs 14's envy that rots the bones. Envy fixes on what a neighbor has; holy zeal fixes on God's honor and His Word, as when the Psalmist was consumed "because my enemies have forgotten Your words" (Psalm 119:139). Jehu's boast warns that loud zeal in one area can sit beside a heart that never fully turns to God. He tore down Baal but kept Jeroboam's calves. True zeal looks like Christ, who was eaten up for His Father's house and bore the reproach that came with it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 6:34 — A wronged husband's jealousy is his fury, and he will not spare on the day of vengeance; adultery provokes a passion no payment can quiet.

  • Psalm 79:5 — After Jerusalem's ruin, Asaph asks how long the LORD's jealousy will burn like fire, turning the word into a lament and a prayer for mercy.

  • Isaiah 42:13 — The LORD goes out like a mighty man and stirs up His zeal like a man of war, pictured as a warrior rising to rescue.

  • Isaiah 63:15 — The prophet asks where God's zeal and strength have gone, a bold prayer that appeals to God's passion when it seems hidden.

  • Ezekiel 5:13 — The LORD says He has spoken in His zeal when His fury against Jerusalem is spent, showing that His jealousy is the reason behind His judgment.

  • Ecclesiastes 9:6 — The love, hatred, and envy of the dead have perished; every earthly passion ends at the grave.
