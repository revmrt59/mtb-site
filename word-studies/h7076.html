CINNAMON - (qinnâmôwn, Strong's H7076)

Summary

The Hebrew word qinnâmôwn means cinnamon, the fragrant inner bark of a tree that did not grow in Israel. It had to come by long trade routes, which made it rare and very expensive. The Old Testament names it only three times, and each use places this costly fragrance in a very different setting. In Exodus 30 God commands that cinnamon be blended into the holy anointing oil that set apart the tabernacle and its priests. In Proverbs 7 an adulteress perfumes her bed with it to lure a naive young man toward death. In the Song of Solomon it grows among the choice plants of the bride's "garden enclosed," reserved for her beloved.

Together these three passages show what people do with something precious. God claims a holy oil made with this fine spice for His own presence and forbids anyone to copy its recipe. Sin borrows some of the same spices for a different perfume to dress up a trap. In faithful love it grows in a living garden, guarded for one beloved. The theme carries into the New Testament, where cinnamon appears among the luxuries in doomed Babylon's cargo, listed alongside human beings, and where Paul calls believers "the fragrance of Christ." Babylon could buy and sell spices, but the fragrance of God's holiness is never for sale. It belongs to Him, and He gives it to those who belong to Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Cinnamon is not a fruit or a flower. It comes from the inner bark of a tree, which is peeled, dried, and curled into quills. The trees probably grew in South Asia, so cinnamon reached Israel only after a long journey through traders' hands. That cost explains its biblical company. Cinnamon appears beside myrrh, frankincense, spikenard, and aloes, the luxury spices of priests, kings, and the wealthy. The word belongs to the language of worship, of royal courts, and of love poetry, and it does not appear in the vocabulary of ordinary household life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Qinnâmôwn has one plain meaning: cinnamon, the aromatic spice. Exodus 30:23 calls it "sweet-smelling cinnamon," which points to its value as fragrance. The word itself does not mean holiness, seduction, or love. Those associations come from the settings in which Scripture places this costly spice.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The three uses fall into three settings. In Exodus 30:23 cinnamon is an ingredient God prescribes for sacred use. In Proverbs 7:17 it is perfume on a bed of adultery. In Song of Solomon 4:14 it is a plant growing in a garden that pictures the bride. What changes from passage to passage is who uses the cinnamon and for whom. In the tabernacle it is set apart for God. On the adulteress's bed it is spent on deception. In the garden it is grown for one beloved. In each passage the spice keeps the same high value, and the setting shows whether that value is honored or abused.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God values what is precious and claims it for Himself. He did not ask Israel to perfume His dwelling with cheap substitutes. He named measured amounts of the costliest spices, which tells us something about His worth. Worship that costs nothing says little about the One worshiped.

The passages also show God's jealous holiness. The fragrance of the anointing oil marked what was His, and He guarded that mark. Scent spreads through the air and cannot be walled in, yet God reserved the holy oil, with its particular blend, for His house and His priests. Holiness in Scripture is attractive, and it is also fiercely protected.

Finally, the uses reveal how sin works. Evil rarely invents its own pleasures. It takes good things God made, even spices that went into the holy oil of His sanctuary, and turns them toward death. Readers who compare Exodus 30 can see part of the danger in Proverbs 7: the adulteress's bed carries some of the same scents as the holy oil, even though her perfume is not the holy oil.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 30:22–33 — the fragrance reserved for God. God tells Moses, "take for yourself quality spices—five hundred shekels of liquid myrrh, half as much sweet-smelling cinnamon (two hundred and fifty shekels)" (Exodus 30:23), blended with cane, cassia, and olive oil into "a holy anointing oil" (30:25). With it Moses was to anoint the tabernacle, the ark, the table, the lampstand, the altars, and the laver, "that they may be most holy; whatever touches them must be holy" (30:29). Aaron and his sons were to be anointed "that they may minister to Me as priests" (30:30). The fragrance of the holy oil, with its cinnamon, was therefore the smell of things and people set apart for God's presence.

Then come the boundaries: "It shall not be poured on man's flesh; nor shall you make any other like it, according to its composition. It is holy, and it shall be holy to you" (Exodus 30:32). Anyone who made a copy or put it on an outsider "shall be cut off from his people" (30:33). God forbade anyone to treat the holy oil as a private luxury. Anyone who smelled it was smelling God's claim on that place or that priest. Psalm 133:2 later remembers this oil "running down on the beard, the beard of Aaron," a picture of blessing flowing from God's anointed.

Proverbs 7:6–27 — the counterfeit fragrance. The adulteress tells the simple young man, "I have perfumed my bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon" (Proverbs 7:17). Myrrh and cinnamon were two of the spices in the holy oil, and she has just used religious language: "I have peace offerings with me; today I have paid my vows" (7:14). She surrounds her invitation with the vocabulary of worship and with scents that a reader of Exodus will recognize from the sanctuary. Her perfume is a different blend, and Proverbs never says she used the holy oil. Still, the comparison is striking. God had forbidden the holy oil to be "poured on man's flesh," and she uses two of its spices to perfume a bed of adultery. The perfume hides where her house leads: "the way to hell, descending to the chambers of death" (Proverbs 7:27). A costly fragrance proves nothing about a holy reality.

Song of Solomon 4:12–16 — the fragrance that grows. The bridegroom calls his bride "a garden enclosed... a spring shut up, a fountain sealed" (Song of Solomon 4:12). Her "plants" include "calamus and cinnamon, with all trees of frankincense, myrrh and aloes, with all the chief spices" (4:13–14). Here cinnamon is a living plant growing within the garden. The adulteress has perfumed a bed (Proverbs 7:17), applying fragrance from outside to create an impression. The bride's fragrance comes from what is alive in her. The garden is locked, open to one beloved, and she invites him in to enjoy its fruits (4:16). The rare spices also give her a royal dignity, because these were the treasures of kings. The exclusiveness of Exodus finds an echo here in the exclusiveness of faithful love.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The anointing oil set apart priests to serve God, and the title Messiah, or Christ, means "Anointed One." Jesus applied Isaiah 61 to Himself: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon Me, because He has anointed Me" (Luke 4:18). Peter preached that God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power (Acts 10:38). The fragrance that once marked Aaron points to the One who is the true Priest, set apart for God in full.

Paul says Christ gave Himself for us as a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God (Ephesians 5:2). That verse is the scriptural ground for calling His sacrifice fragrant. Some readers also see an illustration in how cinnamon is harvested, since the spice is the inner bark, removed from the tree. Exodus does not draw that lesson; it is only an illustration. Still, it can remind us that the sweet aroma Paul describes came through Christ's suffering: "He was bruised for our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:5).

The story also ends with a warning. In Revelation 18 the merchants mourn Babylon's fall and list her cargo: "cinnamon and incense, fragrant oil and frankincense... and bodies and souls of men" (Revelation 18:13). The Greek word there is a different word with the same meaning, so it is not an occurrence of the Hebrew word, but the contrast is worth noticing. In Babylon's trade, cinnamon is one luxury among many, listed in the same cargo as human beings. Revelation itself sets the harlot city against the bride of the Lamb, introducing each with an angel's "Come, I will show you" (Revelation 17:1; 21:9). Proverbs and the Song each present their own scene, but readers can set the adulteress's perfumed bed in Proverbs 7 beside the bride's fragrant garden in Song of Solomon 4 and see a contrast much like Revelation's. Babylon will buy and sell anything, even human lives, and she falls in a single hour (Revelation 18:10).

7. Spirit & Life

The contrast between the adulteress and the bride touches every believer. A person can wear the scent of godliness the way she perfumed her bed. Religious words, vows, and spiritual vocabulary can be applied from the outside to cover a heart that leads elsewhere. The bride's fragrance is grown. It comes from a guarded life given to one Beloved. Paul describes the fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22–23 in a related way, as fruit produced by the Spirit within a person. Love, joy, and peace that God grows in a person cannot be bought or faked for long.

Paul also writes, "We are to God the fragrance of Christ among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing. To the one we are the aroma of death leading to death, and to the other the aroma of life leading to life" (2 Corinthians 2:15–16). Paul's picture works differently from the cinnamon passages. In 2 Corinthians there is one fragrance, Christ's, and it does not change. What differs is how people receive it: to those being saved it is the aroma of life, and to those perishing it is the aroma of death. In Exodus and Proverbs the difference lies in the use. The same spice goes into the holy oil of God's house in one passage and into an adulteress's perfume in the other. Together these passages give a sober lesson: a good spice can be misused, and even the true fragrance of Christ can be refused. Paul asks, "who is sufficient for these things?" (2:16), and he answers that "our sufficiency is from God" (3:5).

Exodus 30 also asks us what we hold back for God alone. He refused to let His holy oil become a common perfume. The best of our love and worship is His, and, to borrow the Song's picture, when we give it to Him we find that He has already been at work in His garden, growing a fragrance He delights in.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All three occurrences of qinnâmôwn (Exodus 30:23; Proverbs 7:17; Song of Solomon 4:14) are developed in Section 5. Two passages supply useful context, though neither uses this Hebrew word.

  • Psalm 133:2 — "It is like the precious oil upon the head, running down on the beard, the beard of Aaron." The holy oil that contained cinnamon becomes a picture of the blessing of God's people living together in unity.

  • Revelation 18:13 — "and cinnamon and incense, fragrant oil and frankincense." The Greek word for cinnamon appears among the luxuries of Babylon's cargo, listed in the same trade as "bodies and souls of men."
