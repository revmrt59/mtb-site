SHUT, STOP, SKIP - (qâphats, Strong's H7092)

Summary

The Hebrew verb qâphats describes drawing something together or closing it tight. A hand can close around what it holds, and a mouth can clamp shut. In one poetic verse the word means to leap or skip instead. The NKJV translates the word as "shut," "stops," "taken out of the way," and once as "skipping." The Old Testament uses it only seven times, so each use carries weight. Deuteronomy forbids God's people to shut their hands against a poor brother. Asaph, in his grief, asks whether God has shut up His tender mercies. Job and the Psalms show injustice shutting its mouth when God acts for the poor. Isaiah lifts the image to its peak when kings shut their mouths before the exalted Servant of the LORD.

Taken together, these verses trace two kinds of closing. Human beings close their hands in self-protection and keep their mouths open in pride. God reverses this. He silences the boast of the proud, and He keeps His own mercy open. That pattern reaches its fullest expression in Jesus Christ. Before Him every mouth is stopped, and through Him God's compassion is poured out, so His people are called to open their hands to one another. One unexpected use completes the picture. In the Song of Solomon the same word, translated "skipping," describes the beloved bounding over the hills toward his bride, a vivid picture of love that comes eagerly.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Qâphats is a Hebrew verb whose basic idea is drawing together or contracting. It appears once in the Law (Deuteronomy 15:7) and then in poetry and prophecy: twice in Job, twice in Psalms, once in the Song of Solomon, and once in Isaiah. Most of these settings deal with justice, suffering, or God's dealings with the proud and the poor. The Song of Solomon is the exception: there the word describes leaping, not closing.

English readers cannot see that "shut," "stops," "skipping," and "taken out of the way" all translate the same Hebrew word. Six of the seven uses share the picture of something being drawn in or closed tight, and that shared picture connects those passages. The "skipping" of Song of Solomon 2:8 is a separate use of the word, describing quick, bounding movement.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In most of its uses the word describes an active closing or contracting. It does not describe something that simply sits closed. Context determines what is being drawn together.

1. To shut or withhold, closing the hand or holding back compassion. Deuteronomy 15:7 forbids Israelites to "shut your hand from your poor brother," and Psalm 77:9 asks whether God has "shut up His tender mercies."

2. To shut the mouth, falling silent because of defeat or awe. "Injustice shuts her mouth" (Job 5:16), and kings "shall shut their mouths at Him" (Isaiah 52:15).

3. To be gathered up or taken away in death. The wicked are "taken out of the way" (Job 24:24).

4. To leap or skip. The beloved comes "skipping upon the hills" (Song of Solomon 2:8). Hebrew dictionaries often explain this sense by picturing someone drawing in the legs before a jump, which helps show how leaping could be related to drawing together. That picture is an illustration, though. The verse itself simply shows the beloved bounding eagerly toward his bride.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Who does the shutting changes the meaning of each passage. When people close their hands, the closing is selfish withholding, and Scripture condemns it. When injustice or kings close their mouths, they are being silenced. They have nothing left to say in the face of what God has done. Asaph's question about God shutting His mercies is a fear voiced in prayer. It is not a description of what God actually does.

In Job 24:24 the NKJV translates this word as "taken out of the way," and the verb's basic idea of drawing together suggests the wicked being gathered up and removed. The NKJV line reads, "They are taken out of the way like all others." The comparison there is with other people: the wicked are taken away just as everyone else is. The verse then adds a separate picture: "They dry out like the heads of grain." Job's point is that the prosperity of the wicked is short. They are "exalted for a little while," and then they are taken away and wither like grain that has had its brief season in the field.

4. Theological Significance

The Bible often presents shutting as an exercise of power, and qâphats shows God and human beings using that power in opposite directions. People shut what should be open. The hardened heart in Deuteronomy 15:7 shows itself in a closed hand, and the selfish person keeps both resources and affection to himself. Meanwhile the proud mouth stays open, boasting and accusing.

God overturns this arrangement. He closes the mouths that ought to be silent: injustice in Job 5:16, iniquity in Psalm 107:42, and finally the kings of the earth in Isaiah 52:15. He also commands His people to open their hands. Psalm 107 shows Him pouring contempt on princes while He lifts the needy out of affliction. The proud are silenced and the poor receive hope. That reversal reveals what God is like. He refuses to let arrogance have the last word, and He binds the welfare of the weak to His own honor.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 15:7 and Psalm 77:9: the shut hand and the shut mercies. Deuteronomy 15 sets out the year of release, when debts were cancelled, and anticipates the temptation to refuse a loan as that year approached. God names the root of that refusal: "you shall not harden your heart nor shut your hand from your poor brother." The surrounding verses tell Israel instead to open the hand wide. The hand reveals what is in the heart.

That command makes Psalm 77:9 painful to read. Asaph lies awake in trouble and asks, "Has God forgotten to be gracious? Has He in anger shut up His tender mercies?" He fears that God has done to him exactly what God forbade Israel to do to the poor. The psalm does not leave him in that fear. Asaph turns to remember the works of the LORD, especially the night God led His people through the sea like a flock. God's past faithfulness answers the question. Lamentations later declares that His compassions do not fail and are new every morning (Lamentations 3:22–23). The God who forbids a closed hand does not close His own.

Job 5:16, Psalm 107:42, and Isaiah 52:15: the shut mouth. In Job 5:16 Eliphaz states a true principle, even though he wrongly applies it to Job: when God rescues the needy, "the poor have hope, and injustice shuts her mouth." Psalm 107:42 repeats the picture: "The righteous see it and rejoice, and all iniquity stops its mouth." In both verses wrongdoing loses its voice once God acts.

Isaiah carries the image much higher. In the Servant passage of Isaiah 52:13–53:12, the One whose appearance was marred beyond that of any man is exalted and lifted up. Then "Kings shall shut their mouths at Him; for what had not been told them they shall see." The silenced ones are now the most powerful people on earth. They fall silent in stunned awe at something no one had ever told them: the despised Servant is the One who sprinkles many nations. God's justice silences the boast of the wicked, and the revelation of His Servant silences even the rulers of the world.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament identifies the Servant of Isaiah 52–53 as Jesus. Paul cites Isaiah 52:15 to describe the gospel reaching people who had never heard of Christ (Romans 15:21). The kings' silence in Isaiah is the response of the nations to the crucified and risen Lord.

Paul also uses the image of the stopped mouth to describe how God brings people to grace. The law speaks "that every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become guilty before God" (Romans 3:19). Paul uses Greek, not this Hebrew word, but the picture is the same, and it opens his explanation of righteousness given freely through faith in Christ. The mouth must stop boasting before the hand can receive. When human self-defense falls silent, God's Word can be heard.

Christ also answers Asaph's question in Psalm 77. At the cross God did not shut up His tender mercies. He gave His own Son. The Song of Solomon adds another layer. Many Christian readers have long seen the beloved of the Song as a picture of Christ and His people. If that reading is right, the One who silences kings is also the One who comes eagerly for His bride, "leaping upon the mountains, skipping upon the hills" (Song of Solomon 2:8). Scripture moves toward openness, and its story ends in a city whose gates are never shut (Revelation 21:25).

7. Spirit & Life

The same word that describes a hand closing also describes a lover leaping. In Job 24:24 it describes the wicked being taken away. In Song of Solomon 2:8 it describes the beloved bounding over mountains and hills toward his bride, and the next verse compares him to "a gazelle or a young stag" (2:9). A reader can picture him gathering his strength to spring as a gazelle does, though the verse itself simply shows him leaping and skipping. The bride hears his voice before she sees him: "Behold, he comes." The mountains are no barrier to him. Love like this does not hold back, wait to be persuaded, or come reluctantly. It comes in strength, eager to arrive.

This picture speaks to anyone who has prayed Asaph's prayer at night and wondered whether God has closed His heart. The answer comes in what God has already done for His people in the past and in the way He comes toward them now. A believer can bring that fear to Him honestly, as Asaph did, and then remember His works.

The command of Deuteronomy follows directly. John asks the hard question: "whoever has this world's goods, and sees his brother in need, and shuts up his heart from him, how does the love of God abide in him?" (1 John 3:17). Someone who has received open mercy from God cannot keep a closed fist toward a brother in need. The gospel reverses both habits of the fallen heart. It quiets the mouth that defends itself and boasts, and it opens the hand that grasps.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

With only seven occurrences, every use of qâphats has been discussed above. Job 24:24 deserves a second look for the way it brings the word's physical sense to bear on human pride. The wicked "are exalted for a little while, Then they are gone." They are "taken out of the way like all others," and then "They dry out like the heads of grain." Their prosperity lasts only a short time, and they cannot escape being taken away.
