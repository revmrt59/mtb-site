CUT, SHORN - (qâtsab, Strong's H7094)

Summary

The Hebrew verb qâtsab means to cut off or to shear. It names a plain, physical action: cutting or clipping something off. It appears only twice in the Old Testament, and both uses are memorable. In 2 Kings 6:6, the prophet Elisha "cut off a stick" and threw it into the Jordan River, and the lost iron ax head floated to the surface. In Song of Solomon 4:2, the bridegroom compares his bride's teeth to "a flock of shorn sheep" that have come up from the washing. The picture is white, clean, and perfectly matched.

Because the word is so rare, its significance comes from these two scenes. In the first, God cares about a borrowed iron ax head and the distressed man who lost it, and He uses a stick Elisha cut off to bring the iron back up from the river. That cut wood cast into the water gives Christians an image that helps them understand the cross, where Christ lifts up what had sunk beyond human reach. In the second, the bridegroom delights in his bride's smile and pictures her teeth as a well-tended flock of shorn sheep that have come up from the washing, with none missing. In both scenes the cutting serves a good end: rescue in one and beauty in the other.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Qâtsab is a Hebrew verb for cutting something off or shearing it, whether a piece of wood or wool from a sheep. Its two appearances come from very different parts of the Bible. One is a short story in the life of Elisha, and the other is a love poem. Both settings come from everyday life in ancient Israel, where wood was cut for building and sheep were sheared for wool. The Bible uses this ordinary farm and workshop word to describe a miracle in one place and a bride's beauty in the other.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one basic meaning: to cut off or to shear. Its two uses apply that meaning to different materials.

1. To cut off a piece of wood. "So he cut off a stick" (2 Kings 6:6).

2. To shear wool from sheep. "A flock of shorn sheep" (Song of Solomon 4:2).

The word describes the physical act only. Any further meaning comes from the stories and poems in which it appears.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In 2 Kings 6, the sons of the prophets cut down trees by the Jordan, but the verbs for felling trees in 2 Kings 6:4–5 are different Hebrew words. Qâtsab appears only when Elisha cuts off the stick that God uses for the miracle (6:6).

In Song of Solomon 4:2, the word describes the sheep as already shorn. It tells what has been done to them: they have been sheared. The detail adds to the picture of smooth, bright whiteness. When the bridegroom repeats the comparison in Song of Solomon 6:6, he keeps the flock, the washing, and the twins, but he leaves out "shorn." The comparison stands on its own in 6:6; in 4:2 the shearing is one more detail in the picture.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, the two passages show God working through simple, physical means. In the Elisha story, God does not need the stick. Iron does not float because wood touches the water. Still, God chooses to act through a prophet's ordinary act of cutting a stick and throwing it into the water. He often brings His power into human life through humble things that look powerless in themselves.

The two passages also show that God pays attention to small matters. A lost ax head and a bride's smile are not grand national events. Yet Scripture records the one as a work of God's power and the other as an expression of delight. The God who rules history notices a distressed man at a riverbank, and His Word makes room for a bridegroom's delight in the specific features of the one he loves.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

2 Kings 6:1–7 — The cut stick and the floating iron. The sons of the prophets tell Elisha that the place where they dwell with him is too small for them, so they go to the Jordan to take beams and build a place where they may dwell (6:1–2). Elisha agrees to go with them. Then a crisis strikes: "the iron ax head fell into the water; and he cried out and said, 'Alas, master! For it was borrowed'" (6:5). Iron tools were valuable, and this man had lost something that did not belong to him. He had no way to recover it from the river.

Elisha's first question is practical and personal: "Where did it fall?" (6:6). The man shows him the exact place. Then Elisha "cut off a stick, and threw it in there; and he made the iron float." The prophet cuts the stick and casts it at the very spot of the loss, and God reverses what had seemed final. The story ends with another detail. Elisha tells him, "Pick it up for yourself," and "he reached out his hand and took it" (6:7). God restores what was lost, and the man takes hold of it with his own hand.

Song of Solomon 4:1–2 — The shorn and washed flock. The bridegroom praises his bride feature by feature. Her teeth "are like a flock of shorn sheep Which have come up from the washing, Every one of which bears twins, And none is barren among them." Sheep that had been shorn and had come up from the washing would look clean, white, and uniform. The sheep that bear twins match the paired, even rows of her teeth, and "none is barren" pictures a smile with no gaps. The shepherd's careful work of shearing and washing becomes the language of love. A few verses later, the bridegroom sums up his view of her: she is entirely beautiful, with no spot in her (4:7).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never mentions the floating ax head, and 2 Kings does not present the stick as a prophecy. Even so, the cut wood cast into the water gives readers an image that helps them understand what the cross accomplishes. In the story, a man of God takes a stick he has cut and casts it into the place of loss. The iron ax head that had sunk beyond the borrower's reach rises, and he receives back the tool he had borrowed and lost. The gospel speaks of a far greater rescue through the wood of the cross. Peter writes that Christ "Himself bore our sins in His own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24). Paul says that God has made us alive, forgiven all our trespasses, and wiped out "the handwriting of requirements that was against us," "having nailed it to the cross" (Colossians 2:13–14). The stick at the Jordan is an image of that rescue, not an explicit prophecy of it, and the comparison is a fitting one. Humanity had sunk under a weight it could not lift, and God brought the wood of the cross into that very place to raise us up.

Song of Solomon 4:2 also fits into the Bible's larger story, by way of comparison. In the Song, the washed and shorn flock pictures the bride's white, even smile, and the bridegroom's words show how much he delights in her. Scripture repeatedly calls God's people His bride, and the New Testament states plainly what Christ has done for His bride: He loved the church, gave Himself for her, and cleanses her "with the washing of water by the word," so that He might present her to Himself "not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing" (Ephesians 5:25–27). The Song makes no statement about the church, but its picture of a bridegroom delighting in a washed and spotless bride helps readers feel the warmth of what Ephesians teaches. Christ does not merely tolerate those He has made clean. He presents them to Himself as "a glorious church" (Ephesians 5:27).

7. Spirit & Life

Elisha's question, "Where did it fall?", is worth taking personally. In this story God's help is not vague. Elisha goes to the exact place where the iron sank, and believers may bring to God the exact place where something slipped away: a lost trust, a responsibility that collapsed, a debt that cannot be paid. The man showed Elisha the spot. Bringing a loss to God often means naming honestly where it happened. Then, once the iron rose, Elisha told him to pick it up himself, and he reached out and took it. Grace does the impossible part, and it also invites us to take hold of what God has restored.

The bridegroom's praise speaks to believers who see mainly their own stains. In the Song he looks at his bride and says, "there is no spot in you" (4:7). The Song is describing a bride, not passing a verdict on the church, but Ephesians 5 tells believers what Christ Himself has done: He gave Himself for His people to cleanse them and present them to Himself holy and without blemish. For those who belong to Christ, that is His actual purpose and work, grounded in the cleansing He has provided. It is not flattery or wishful thinking.

The two uses of the word suggest a further reflection. Elisha cut off a stick and threw it into the water, and that cut wood became the means of rescue. In the Song, the shorn flock is one detail in a picture of beauty. This is an analogy, and the word itself does not teach it. The truth that God sometimes cuts back for the sake of growth comes from Jesus Himself: "every branch that bears fruit He prunes, that it may bear more fruit" (John 15:2). Sometimes the Father cuts away what we have relied on or what has grown heavy on us, and Jesus says His purpose in that pruning is more fruit, not loss.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Qâtsab appears only in 2 Kings 6:6 and Song of Solomon 4:2, and both passages are discussed above. Song of Solomon 6:6 is a useful companion verse. It repeats the comparison of the bride's teeth to a flock "which have come up from the washing," with every one bearing twins, but it leaves out the word "shorn." The bridegroom's repetition of his praise suggests that his delight in her is lasting.
