PURPLE - (ʼargâmân, Strong's H0713)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼargâmân means "purple." It names the costly reddish-purple dye of the ancient world and the wool or thread colored with it. The dye came from sea snails and was gathered in tiny amounts, so purple cloth was rare and expensive. It became the recognized color of kings, high officials, and great wealth.

Most of the Bible's uses of this word appear in Exodus, where God commands purple thread to be woven into the curtains, entry screens, and veil of the tabernacle and into the high priest's garments. A nation of former brick-makers in Egypt carried at the center of their camp a royal house fit for the Great King, with a priest dressed to stand in His presence. Purple also covered the altar of sacrifice when Israel traveled. Outside the sanctuary, purple dresses kings, honors Mordecai, clothes the wise woman of Proverbs 31, adorns idols, and fills Tyre's proud markets. When Roman soldiers threw a purple robe on Jesus to mock Him, they dressed the true King and Priest in the color Scripture had long associated with majesty.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Purple dye was taken from the glands of Murex sea snails along the Mediterranean coast. Thousands of snails yielded only a small amount of dye. The famous "Tyrian purple" carries the name of the Phoenician city most associated with the trade. Few people could afford garments of this color, so purple worked as a visible sign of rank in the way a crown or a throne does.

The word ʼargâmân occurs 38 times in 38 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and the NKJV translates every one of them "purple." Twenty-six are in Exodus, in the instructions for building the tabernacle and making the priests' clothing and in the account of that work being done. The remaining uses fall in royal courts, wisdom poetry, love poetry, and prophetic rebukes. The New Testament uses Greek words for the purple placed on Jesus: porphyra in Mark 15:17 and the related adjective porphyrous in John 19:2.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one stable meaning, the royal purple color. Context shows whether the writer means the color itself or the material colored with it:

1. Purple dye or the color itself, as in Song of Solomon 7:5, where the beloved's hair "is like purple."

2. Thread or fabric dyed purple, as in the offerings Israel brought for the tabernacle: "blue, purple, and scarlet thread, fine linen" (Exodus 25:4).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the sanctuary passages, purple almost always appears in a set group: blue, purple, and scarlet thread with fine linen. In these woven fabrics the colors appear together. That repeated grouping becomes the signature of holy things. When Solomon builds the temple, he receives from Tyre a craftsman skilled in purple (2 Chronicles 2:14) and repeats the pattern in the veil (2 Chronicles 3:14).

In royal settings, purple marks power and honor. The kings of Midian wore purple garments (Judges 8:26). The Persian palace hung with purple cords (Esther 1:6). Mordecai left the king's presence dressed in royal blue and white with a purple linen robe (Esther 8:15), which tells the reader that his rise was complete. The same color can also be placed on things that do not deserve it, such as idols and an arrogant city. Purple announces honor wherever it appears. The context shows whether that honor is true, bestowed, or counterfeit.

4. Theological Significance

The tabernacle used one of the most prestigious colors of the ancient world, and that choice says something about God. The LORD did not live in a plain shelter. He told His people to build His dwelling with the materials of royalty, and He described Himself through a visual language the nations around Israel understood. The God of Israel is King, and coming near Him means approaching a throne room.

Purple also shows that this King shares His honor. The priest wears it, the altar is covered with it, and even a household woman of wisdom is clothed in it. The color moves outward from God's presence to those who serve Him and walk in His ways.

The misuse of purple shows the other side. People seem to sense that deity deserves royal splendor, and they put purple on gods they have made and on empires they have built. Scripture treats that borrowed majesty as empty, and the prophets announce judgment on it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The curtains and veil (Exodus 26:1, 31; 2 Chronicles 3:14). God commanded that the tabernacle be made "with ten curtains of fine woven linen and blue, purple, and scarlet thread; with artistic designs of cherubim" (Exodus 26:1). The same materials formed the veil that guarded the Most Holy Place and the screens at the entrances (Exodus 26:31, 36; 27:16). The point becomes clearer when we remember who built these curtains. Only months earlier, Israel had been a nation of slaves making bricks for Pharaoh. Now one of the most valuable dyes of the ancient world hung at the center of their camp. God had brought a poor people out of bondage and set the palace of the King in their midst. The veil itself, the barrier to His presence, was woven in royal color with cherubim, as if guards stood at the door of a throne room.

The high priest's garments (Exodus 28:5–8, 15, 33). The priest's ephod was made "of gold, blue, purple, and scarlet thread, and fine woven linen, artistically worked" (Exodus 28:6), and the same work appeared in its woven band and in the breastplate of judgment, while purple also adorned the pomegranates along the robe's hem. The priest was dressed in the same fabric as the sanctuary. God said the garments were for glory and for beauty (Exodus 28:2). The man who carried Israel's names into God's presence was clothed to match the King's house, a royal priest serving in a royal dwelling.

The purple cloth on the altar (Numbers 4:13). When the camp moved, each holy object received its own covering. The ark's outer covering was blue (Numbers 4:6). The bronze altar of burnt offering received something different: "they shall take away the ashes from the altar, and spread a purple cloth over it" (Numbers 4:13). Its utensils were laid on the purple, and badger skins went over everything (Numbers 4:14). Purple was given to this altar alone in that chapter, and it was the place of fire and blood, where animals died for the sins of the people. God honored the place of sacrifice with the color of kings. The royal cloth lay hidden under the skins, unseen by the crowds, yet the altar always traveled dressed in majesty. Sacrifice and kingship were joined in Israel's daily movements long before the cross revealed how fully they belong together.

Borrowed glory (Jeremiah 10:9; Ezekiel 27:7, 16). Jeremiah mocks idols covered with imported silver and gold: "Blue and purple are their clothing; They are all the work of skillful men" (Jeremiah 10:9). The idol wears the sanctuary's colors, yet everything about it, including its dignity, was made by human hands. Ezekiel describes proud Tyre as a splendid ship: "Blue and purple from the coasts of Elishah was what covered you" (Ezekiel 27:7), with purple also among its trade goods (27:16). The color God consecrated to His own house became the canopy of a city's self-glory, and Ezekiel's lament ends with that ship sinking.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament's use of purple gives the scene at Jesus' trial its full weight. "And they clothed Him with purple; and they twisted a crown of thorns, put it on His head" (Mark 15:17). John records that "they put on Him a purple robe" (John 19:2). The soldiers meant the robe as a joke about a powerless man who claimed to be king. Without knowing it, they were telling the truth.

The Gospels present the robe as mockery of His kingship, but the canonical connections reach further. The purple that dressed Israel's high priest now rests on the Priest who would enter God's presence with His own blood (Hebrews 9:11–12). The purple that covered the altar of sacrifice now covers the One who is Himself the offering. When they had finished, the soldiers took the purple off Him and led Him out to be crucified (Mark 15:20). King, Priest, and Sacrifice met in one Person on the way to Golgotha.

Scripture's story of purple ends with one more contrast. Babylon the harlot "was arrayed in purple and scarlet, and adorned with gold and precious stones" (Revelation 17:4), the final picture of worldly wealth and power gone corrupt. Like Tyre, she falls. The purple placed on Jesus in scorn led to a cross and then to a throne. The purple Babylon wears in pride leads to her ruin.

7. Spirit & Life

The tabernacle's purple shows the kind of God we serve. He did not wait for Israel to become impressive before giving them royal things. He took slaves and set His royal house among them, and He gave them a priest dressed in glory. Peter tells believers in Christ that they too are a royal priesthood (1 Peter 2:9). The honor comes from the King who dwells among us, and we never earn it.

Numbers 4:13 speaks to anyone whose faithfulness goes unseen. The altar's purple traveled under plain skins, hidden from everyone but God. The cross looked like shame to those who passed by, yet heaven saw a King offering Himself. Sacrifice made for God is never dishonored in His eyes, even when no one else sees its dignity.

Proverbs 31:22 brings purple into an ordinary home: "She makes tapestry for herself; Her clothing is fine linen and purple." This woman is a wise wife who fears the LORD, works with her hands, and cares for her household and the poor. Her purple marks her as honored and prosperous. Readers who know Exodus will hear an echo of the sanctuary's fine linen and purple, though Proverbs does not draw that link directly. Either way, Scripture dresses a faithful life lived in the fear of God in the colors of royalty.

Jeremiah and Ezekiel give the warning. The human heart still dresses its idols in purple, giving honor and devotion to careers, possessions, or self-image. That borrowed purple always fades. The glory worth having is the glory God gives, and it was worn first by Jesus under mocking hands.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 8:26 — After Gideon's victory, the spoil included the purple garments worn by Midian's kings, a sign of how fully their royal power had been broken.

  • Esther 8:15 — Mordecai goes out from the king wearing a purple linen garment. A man once marked for death now wears the clothing of high honor.

  • Song of Solomon 3:10 — The king's carriage has "pillars of silver, Its support of gold, Its seat of purple," a picture of royal splendor at a wedding procession.

  • Song of Solomon 7:5 — "The hair of your head is like purple; A king is held captive by your tresses." Here purple describes beauty rich enough to capture a king.

  • 2 Chronicles 2:14 — Huram's skill in purple prepared him to help build Solomon's temple, carrying the tabernacle's pattern into the permanent house of God.

10. Other Relevant Insights

In the sanctuary lists, purple usually stands between blue and scarlet. Some teachers see in this a picture of heaven (blue, the sky) and earth (scarlet, often linked with blood and humanity) meeting in one royal color. Scripture does not explicitly explain the colors this way, so this is a devotional illustration. As an illustration, it points to Christ, in whom God and humanity meet.
