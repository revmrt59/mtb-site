KORAH - (Qôrach, Strong's H7141)

Summary

Korah is the name of several men in the Old Testament. Edom's family records mention one or two Korahs (the references may concern the same chief or clan), and one appears in the genealogy of Judah. The Korah who matters most is the Levite, son of Izhar and grandson of Kohath, who led a rebellion against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness (Numbers 16). The name is traditionally understood to mean "baldness," and some connect it with a related word for "ice" or "frost." That derivation is a probable explanation rather than a certainty, and Scripture never builds a lesson on it. Korah already belonged to the clan that carried the holiest furnishings of the tabernacle. He wanted the priesthood itself, and he dressed that ambition in the language of equality: "all the congregation is holy, every one of them" (Numbers 16:3). God answered by opening the earth beneath the rebels' tents and sending fire on the two hundred fifty men who presumed to offer incense. Korah became Israel's lasting example of rebellion against the LORD, and Jude still uses him that way in the New Testament (Jude 11).

The story does not end in the pit. Numbers adds a quiet sentence that changes everything that follows: "Nevertheless the children of Korah did not die" (Numbers 26:11). Their descendants became gatekeepers of God's house and singers in David's worship. Eleven psalms carry the heading "of the sons of Korah," and they are some of the most God-hungry songs in Scripture. Their father demanded a place near God that was not his. His descendants sang, "I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness" (Psalm 84:10). One of their songs, Psalm 45, is applied in Hebrews 1:8 to the Son of God. The family name that stood for rebellion came to stand for longing, worship, and the reign of Christ, which is a clear picture of how God's mercy can overrule a family's history.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Strong's entry H7141 covers several separate people who share one name. They must not be blended together.

Korah the son of Esau. Esau's wife Aholibamah bore him Jeush, Jaalam, and Korah (Genesis 36:5, 14). This Korah became one of the chiefs of Edom (Genesis 36:18), and he is listed again among Esau's sons in 1 Chronicles 1:35.

"Chief Korah" among the sons of Eliphaz. Genesis 36:16 names a Chief Korah among the descendants of Eliphaz, Esau's firstborn. However, Korah is missing from the earlier list of Eliphaz's sons (Genesis 36:11–12) and from the parallel list in 1 Chronicles 1:36. This may be a second Edomite Korah, or it may be the same clan chief counted in another grouping. The text does not settle the question.

Korah the son of Hebron. He appears in the family of Caleb within the tribe of Judah (1 Chronicles 2:43). Nothing more is said about him.

Korah the Levite. He was the son of Izhar, the son of Kohath, the son of Levi (Exodus 6:21; Numbers 16:1). That made him a first cousin of Moses and Aaron, whose father Amram was Izhar's brother (Exodus 6:18–20). His sons were Assir, Elkanah, and Abiasaph (Exodus 6:24). He lived during the wilderness years after the exodus. Most of the occurrences of the name, 31 of the 37, belong to him and to the family that carried his name: "the sons of Korah." A related term, "Korahite" (H7145), is indexed separately and names the same family in passages such as Numbers 26:58 and 1 Chronicles 9:19 and 26:1. The New Testament mentions him once, in Jude 11, using the Greek form of the name (G2879).

The name's meaning. Korah (Qôrach) is traditionally connected with a Hebrew root meaning "to be bald," so it is often rendered "baldness." Some scholars link it with a related noun meaning "ice" or "frost." Both proposals are reasonable, but neither is certain. The Bible makes no wordplay on the name and draws no lesson from it. Korah's significance comes from what he did and from what God did with his family afterward, not from what his name may have meant.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

The Edomite and Judahite Korahs. The Edomite references, which may concern one or two figures, and the Judahite Korah appear only in genealogies. Genesis 36 records how Esau's line grew into clans with recognized chiefs in Seir, which fulfilled God's word that Esau, too, would become a nation (Genesis 25:23). The Korah of 1 Chronicles 2:43 belongs to the Chronicler's careful tracing of Judah's families. These names show that God kept track of these households, but Scripture tells us nothing more about these men.

Privilege and resentment (Numbers 16:1–3). The Levite Korah's rebellion took place in the wilderness after Israel refused to enter Canaan (Numbers 14) and was sentenced to forty years of wandering. Leadership was under strain. Korah did not act alone. Numbers 16:1–2 says that he, "with Dathan and Abiram the sons of Eliab, and On the son of Peleth, sons of Reuben, took men; and they rose up before Moses with some of the children of Israel, two hundred and fifty leaders of the congregation, representatives of the congregation, men of renown."

Their accusation sounds almost godly: "You take too much upon yourselves, for all the congregation is holy, every one of them, and the LORD is among them. Why then do you exalt yourselves above the assembly of the LORD?" (Numbers 16:3). Much of that is true. God had called Israel a holy nation (Exodus 19:6), and He did dwell among them. But Korah used those truths to tear down what God Himself had established, which was the priesthood of Aaron.

Moses exposed the real issue. God had already set Korah's Levite family apart to serve at the tabernacle and stand before the congregation. Was that a small thing to them? Now they were seeking the priesthood as well (Numbers 16:8–10, paraphrased). The Kohathites carried the ark, the table, the lampstand, and the altars (Numbers 4:1–15). Korah stood closer to holy things than almost anyone in Israel, and it was not enough. Psalm 106:16 names the root sin plainly: they envied Moses and Aaron.

The Reubenites had grievances of their own. Dathan and Abiram refused even to come when Moses called them, and they accused him of making himself a prince over them (Numbers 16:12–14). Reuben was Jacob's firstborn, but he lost his preeminence (Genesis 49:3–4; 1 Chronicles 5:1). Scripture does not say that this loss fueled their resentment, though some readers have suggested it. Numbers does tell us something practical: Reuben camped on the south side of the tabernacle (Numbers 2:10), and so did the Kohathite clans (Numbers 3:29). It is a reasonable inference that men who lived as neighbors had many opportunities to share their complaints until those complaints became a conspiracy.

The test and the judgment (Numbers 16:4–35). Moses placed the matter before the LORD. Korah and his company were to bring censers with fire and incense, and God would show whom He had chosen (Numbers 16:5–7, 16–18). Offering incense was a priestly act, so the test went straight to the heart of the claim. When the glory of the LORD appeared to the whole congregation (Numbers 16:19), God told Moses and Aaron to separate themselves so that He could consume the people. The two men fell on their faces and pleaded that the whole nation not perish for one man's sin (Numbers 16:20–22).

The people were then warned to move away from the tents of these wicked men and touch nothing of theirs (Numbers 16:26, paraphrased). Moses declared that if the LORD did something new and the ground opened and they went down alive into the pit, all would know these men had despised the LORD (Numbers 16:28–30). The ground split. It swallowed Dathan, Abiram, their households, and the men and goods belonging to Korah (Numbers 16:31–33). Fire came out from the LORD and consumed the two hundred fifty men offering incense (Numbers 16:35).

Scripture's accounts of Korah's own death do not give every detail. Numbers 16 does not say exactly where he stood when judgment fell. Numbers 26:10 says the earth "swallowed them up together with Korah when that company died, when the fire devoured two hundred and fifty men; and they became a sign." Psalm 106:17 mentions only Dathan and Abiram being swallowed. The point every account agrees on is clear: Korah and his company died under God's judgment.

The aftermath (Numbers 16:36–17:13). The bronze censers were hammered into a covering for the altar. They served as a permanent reminder that no one outside Aaron's line should come near to offer incense, or he would become like Korah and his company (Numbers 16:38–40). The very next day, the whole congregation grumbled that Moses and Aaron had killed the LORD's people (Numbers 16:41). A plague broke out. At Moses' word, Aaron took a censer, ran into the midst of the assembly, and "stood between the dead and the living," and the plague stopped. That was after 14,700 had died, besides those who died in the matter of Korah (Numbers 16:46–49). God then confirmed Aaron's calling by making his rod bud, blossom, and bear almonds (Numbers 17).

A name that became a byword (Numbers 26–27). In the later wilderness census, the record remembered Dathan and Abiram as those who "contended against Moses and Aaron in the company of Korah, when they contended against the LORD" (Numbers 26:9). When the daughters of Zelophehad asked for their father's inheritance, they were careful to say that he had died in his own sin and had not been part of Korah's company that gathered against the LORD (Numbers 27:3, paraphrased). To be linked with Korah had become a mark of disgrace.

"Nevertheless." In the middle of that census record stands a single sentence: "Nevertheless the children of Korah did not die" (Numbers 26:11). The households of Dathan and Abiram went down into the earth (Numbers 16:27–32), but Korah's sons survived. Scripture does not explain why. It simply records the mercy, and that mercy reaches across the rest of the Old Testament.

Gatekeepers and singers (1 Chronicles). The Chronicler traces the line from Korah to the age of David. Heman, one of the three chief singers David placed "over the service of song in the house of the LORD," was a Kohathite. His genealogy runs back through Korah, the son of Izhar (1 Chronicles 6:31–33, 37–38). The same list includes Elkanah and Samuel (1 Chronicles 6:33–34). In the Chronicler's record, Samuel the prophet, who anointed Israel's first kings, came from the rebel's line. Other Korahites were "gatekeepers of the tabernacle," and their fathers had been keepers of the entrance to the camp of the LORD (1 Chronicles 9:19). Later lists again place Korahites among the gatekeepers of God's house (1 Chronicles 26:1, 19). In Jehoshaphat's day, Levites from the Kohathites and Korahites stood to praise the LORD with loud voices before a battle God won without Israel lifting a sword (2 Chronicles 20:19).

The psalms of the sons of Korah. Eleven psalms carry the heading "of the sons of Korah": Psalms 42, 44–49, 84, 85, 87, and 88. Psalm 43 is often read as the closing part of Psalm 42. The Hebrew heading could mean these songs were written by, for, or preserved by this family of singers. Either way, the songs are tied to their name. Psalm 42 opens with a soul panting for God like a deer for water and remembers leading the festive procession to God's house. Psalm 44 is a lament from a faithful people suffering defeat. Psalm 45 celebrates a royal wedding and addresses the King as God. Psalms 46–48 celebrate God as refuge, King, and the defender of Zion. Psalm 49 warns that wealth cannot ransom a life but declares that God will redeem the soul from the power of the grave (49:15). Psalm 84 longs for God's courts. Psalm 85 prays for revival and pictures mercy and truth meeting together. Psalm 87 sees nations once hostile to Israel enrolled as those born in Zion. Psalm 88, linked also with Heman, is the darkest prayer in the Psalter. It is spoken by a man who feels counted among those who go down to the pit, and yet he keeps crying out, "O LORD, God of my salvation" (Psalm 88:1).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Korah's rebellion was more than a quarrel over leadership. Scripture calls it contending "against the LORD" (Numbers 26:9). God had appointed Aaron's family to approach Him with incense and sacrifice on Israel's behalf. To seize that office was to say that God's chosen way of drawing near did not matter. The bronze plates on the altar, the plague stopped by Aaron's censer, and the budding rod all make the same point: God decides how sinful people may come into His presence. His holiness is real, and approaching Him carelessly is deadly.

That is why Korah's words are so instructive. He was right that the LORD was among His people. He was wrong to conclude that God's presence erased God's order. When the holiness of the whole people is used to reject the specific way God has appointed to draw near, a true doctrine has been turned into a weapon. Scripture sets Korah alongside others who grasped at holy things on their own terms: Nadab and Abihu with their unauthorized fire (Leviticus 10:1–2), Saul offering the sacrifice he was told to wait for (1 Samuel 13:8–14), and Uzziah, who entered the temple to burn incense and was struck with leprosy (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). The Uzziah story is especially close. Like Korah, he tried to burn incense, and like Korah, he was resisted by priests who said the act belonged only to Aaron's sons.

Equally important is what Scripture states about Korah's sons: they did not die (Numbers 26:11). God's judgment did not have the final word over this family. Elsewhere Scripture insists that each person bears his own sin (Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20), and the Korahite story shows that principle at work long before Ezekiel. The sons were not condemned to repeat their father's life. A carefully drawn implication follows from the Chronicler's record: the family most associated with rebellion at the sanctuary was later entrusted with guarding its doors and leading its songs. The text does not tell us whether the sons opposed their father. It shows us what God made of them.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jude's explicit use of Korah. Jude warns the church about ungodly men who have crept in, men who reject authority and speak evil of what they do not understand. He says they have gone in the way of Cain, run greedily in the error of Balaam, and perished in the rebellion of Korah (Jude 8–11, paraphrased). Jude speaks of their perishing as though it has already happened. Their end is as certain as Korah's. For Jude, Korah is the example of those who reject the authority God has established and push forward on their own terms. That warning still applies wherever people treat Christ's lordship and the apostolic gospel as optional.

The rightful High Priest. The New Testament does not name Korah when it discusses priesthood, but Hebrews takes up exactly the issue his rebellion raised. No one takes the honor of high priest for himself; he must be called by God, as Aaron was. Even Christ did not glorify Himself but was appointed by the Father (Hebrews 5:4–5, paraphrased). Korah grabbed at a priesthood that was never given to him. The Son, who had every right to glory, waited for the Father's appointment. This is strongly supported canonical development, not a type explicitly named by the New Testament. The scene of Aaron standing "between the dead and the living" with incense (Numbers 16:48) also points forward to the one Mediator between God and humanity (1 Timothy 2:5). The New Testament does not directly draw this connection either, but it fits the Bible's steady teaching that sinners are spared through a mediator God provides.

A Korahite song for the Son. The most remarkable connection is explicit. Psalm 45, a song of the sons of Korah, addresses the King: "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever." Hebrews quotes this verse and applies it directly to Jesus: "But to the Son He says: 'Your throne, O God, is forever and ever; A scepter of righteousness is the scepter of Your kingdom'" (Hebrews 1:8). The family whose ancestor tried to seize what was not his supplied Israel with a song that honors the true and rightful King.

Other Korahite psalms in the New Testament. Paul quotes Psalm 44:22 in Romans 8:36 to describe believers who suffer for Christ's sake, and then declares that nothing can separate them from God's love. Psalm 87's vision of foreign nations counted as Zion's own children resonates with the New Testament's teaching that believers belong to the heavenly Jerusalem (Galatians 4:26; Hebrews 12:22). That second link is a thematic resonance rather than a direct quotation.

From the pit to redemption. One further pattern deserves notice. Korah's company went down alive into the pit as the earth "opened its mouth" (Numbers 16:30–33; 26:10). Generations later, his descendants sang that God would redeem their souls from the power of the grave (Psalm 49:15). In Psalm 88 they prayed from the edge of the pit and still addressed Him as "O LORD, God of my salvation" (Psalm 88:1). No New Testament writer connects these passages to Numbers 16, but the contrast is real. It anticipates the hope fulfilled in Christ, who was raised from the dead and holds the keys of death and Hades (Revelation 1:18).

5. Spirit & Life

Korah's sin did not begin with a lack of privilege. It began with ingratitude for privilege. His clan carried the holy furnishings once the priests had covered them (Numbers 4:15), and he stood near God's dwelling, and envy made it all seem small (Psalm 106:16). That warning reaches anyone who serves in God's house. The danger is not only in being far from God. It is also in growing used to being near Him, until a calling becomes a ladder and other people's callings become threats. Moses' question still searches the heart: is it a small thing that God has brought you near to serve Him?

Korah also shows how truth can be bent into a tool of rebellion. "All the congregation is holy" sounds like faith. In Korah's mouth, it was a way to cast off God's appointed order. Scripture's answer is not to deny God's presence among His people but to receive it on His terms. For Christians, those terms are settled in Christ, the High Priest God appointed, through whom we now come boldly to the throne of grace (Hebrews 4:14–16).

Then comes the word "nevertheless." God did not erase the record of Korah's sin. The censers stayed on the altar as a warning. But He did not make Korah's children prisoners of it either. The sons of Korah guarded the doors their father had defied and sang where their father had rebelled. Many believers come from families marked by unbelief, pride, or open rebellion against God. This story does not tell them to pretend the past never happened. It tells them that God can take a name that once meant disgrace and fill it with worship.

Psalm 84:10 gathers all of this into one line. When Moses warned Israel to leave the tents of these wicked men (Numbers 16:26), the people had to choose where they would stand. The sons of Korah later sang, "I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness." The shared language of "tents" and "wickedness" is very likely an echo of that day, though the psalm does not say so directly. Whether or not the psalmist intended the echo, the contrast is plain. The father despised his assigned place near God and wanted the highest one. The sons counted the lowest post at God's threshold better than anything the world could offer, and the doorkeeper's post was their actual assignment (1 Chronicles 9:19; 26:1). They had learned that the gift is not the position but God Himself: "a day in Your courts is better than a thousand." When we stop reaching for status in God's house and start longing for God, we have learned what the sons of Korah learned.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:5, 14, 16, 18; 1 Chronicles 1:35 — The Edomite Korah(s), sons or chiefs in Esau's line.

  • 1 Chronicles 2:43 — Korah, son of Hebron, in Caleb's family within Judah.

  • Exodus 6:18–24 — The Levite Korah's lineage, his kinship with Moses and Aaron, and his sons.

  • Numbers 4:1–15 — The Kohathites' sacred duties, which show how privileged Korah already was.

  • Numbers 16:1–35 — The rebellion, Korah's half-true appeal to the holiness of the whole congregation, Moses' exposure of his desire for the priesthood, the warning to leave the rebels' tents, and God's judgment.

  • Numbers 16:36–17:13 — The censers as a memorial, Aaron standing between the dead and the living, and the budding rod.

  • Numbers 26:9–11 — The rebellion remembered as contending against the LORD, and the decisive word: "Nevertheless the children of Korah did not die."

  • Numbers 27:3 — Zelophehad's daughters distance their father from Korah's company.

  • Psalm 106:16–18 — Envy named as the root, with the judgment recalled.

  • 1 Chronicles 6:31–38; 9:19; 26:1 — Korah's descendants as singers (Heman, and in this record, Samuel's line) and as gatekeepers of God's house.

  • 2 Chronicles 20:19 — Korahites praising the LORD in Jehoshaphat's day.

  • Psalms 42, 44–49, 84, 85, 87, 88 — The songs of the sons of Korah, filled with longing for God's presence, confidence in Zion's God, and hope beyond the grave.

  • Psalm 84:10 — The doorkeeper's choice, which reverses the father's ambition.

  • Psalm 45:6 with Hebrews 1:8 — A Korahite song applied to the eternal throne of the Son.

  • Psalm 44:22 with Romans 8:36 — A Korahite lament taken up by Paul for suffering believers.

  • Hebrews 5:4–5 — No one takes the priestly honor to himself; Christ was appointed by the Father.

  • Jude 11 — Korah's rebellion as the New Testament's warning example for those who reject God's authority.
