PLUCK, GATHER - (ʼârâh, Strong's H0717)

Summary

The Hebrew verb ʼârâh means to pluck or gather produce from a plant, such as picking fruit from a vine or gathering fragrant myrrh in a garden. The word occurs 2 times in 2 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and the two uses could hardly be further apart. In Psalm 80:12 strangers strip the fruit from Israel's vine after God has broken down its hedges. In Song of Solomon 5:1 the bridegroom enters his own garden and gathers his myrrh with his spice, in answer to his bride's invitation to come.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

The word itself is a plain farming word. Its weight comes from who does the gathering and whether the plant is protected. When the hedge is gone, fruit meant for the owner falls to anyone passing by. When the garden is enclosed and kept for its rightful owner, he comes in and gathers what has grown there with joy. Read together, the two verses raise a question every believer should ask: who is harvesting the fruit of my life? They also point to the God who both guards His people and delights in what He has grown in them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼârâh is a Hebrew verb from the world of vineyards, orchards, and gardens. It describes the ordinary harvest work of pulling ripe produce from the plant that bore it. Both occurrences are in Hebrew poetry. One is a lament psalm attributed to Asaph, and the other is a love song. The word never appears in narrative, law, or prophecy, so everything Scripture teaches through it comes through poetic images. In ancient Israel a vineyard or garden was usually surrounded by a hedge or wall to keep out thieves and animals. That everyday detail explains a great deal about both passages.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʼârâh means to pluck, pick, or gather the produce of a plant. The NKJV renders it "pluck" in Psalm 80:12 and "gathered" in Song of Solomon 5:1. The act is the same in both places: produce is taken from where it grew. The verb carries no built-in sense of either violence or delight. Plundering or rejoicing comes from the setting, the person doing the gathering, and that person's right to the harvest. One definition covers both uses.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The two uses differ in three ways.

The one gathering. In Psalm 80:12 the subject is "all who pass by the way," meaning nameless travelers with no claim on the vine. In Song of Solomon 5:1 the subject is the owner himself, who says "my garden," "my myrrh," and "my spice."

The condition of the boundary. The psalm's vine has had its hedges broken down. The Song's garden has just been called "a garden enclosed" (Song of Solomon 4:12).

The result. The psalm's plucking is part of a wider devastation. In the next verse the wild boar uproots the vine and the beast of the field devours it (Psalm 80:13). The Song's gathering is part of a feast, as the bridegroom eats and drinks, and the friends are invited to share it.

4. Theological Significance

Both passages assume that the plant belongs to someone who cultivated it. In Psalm 80 God brought the vine out of Egypt, cleared the land, and planted it. In the Song the garden is the bridegroom's own; the bride calls it "his garden" (Song of Solomon 4:16). The fruit of God's people is meant for Him.

The passages also show that God's protection is real and that it can be withdrawn. The psalmist does not blame chance or enemies for the broken hedges: "Why have You broken down her hedges?" He speaks to the God who planted the vine as the One who removed its hedge. Isaiah's vineyard song, in its own setting, gives a reason. When the vineyard bore wild grapes, the Lord said, "I will take away its hedge, and it shall be burned" (Isaiah 5:5). When God lifts His protection in judgment, what He grew becomes plunder for others.

Read as a picture of the Lord and His people (see Section 6), the Song shows the other side of God's heart. He does more than guard fruit or demand it. He comes to gather it and rejoices over it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 80:12, the vine left open. Asaph tells Israel's history as the story of a vine: "You have brought a vine out of Egypt; You have cast out the nations, and planted it" (Psalm 80:8). God "prepared room for it, and caused it to take deep root, and it filled the land" (Psalm 80:9). Its shade covered the hills, and its branches reached from the Sea to the River (Psalm 80:10–11). All this care makes verse 12 painful: "Why have You broken down her hedges, So that all who pass by the way pluck her fruit?" A vine that God had planted, defended, and enlarged now stands open to anyone on the road. The plucking is careless and indiscriminate, because the passersby have no stake in the vine's future. They take what they want and walk on. The psalm does not end in despair. It turns to prayer: "Return, we beseech You, O God of hosts; Look down from heaven and see, And visit this vine" (Psalm 80:14). The vine's hope lies with the God who planted it and made its branch strong for Himself (Psalm 80:15).

Song of Solomon 4:12–5:1, the garden kept and given. The bridegroom describes his bride: "A garden enclosed Is my sister, my spouse, A spring shut up, A fountain sealed" (Song of Solomon 4:12). Inside are pomegranates, henna, spikenard, saffron, cinnamon, frankincense, myrrh, and aloes (Song of Solomon 4:13–14). The garden is lush, and it is closed to everyone but him. Then the winds are called to blow on the garden so that its spices may flow out, and the bride invites him in: "Let my beloved come to his garden And eat its pleasant fruits" (Song of Solomon 4:16). Her words are humble. She calls it his garden. He answers at once: "I have come to my garden, my sister, my spouse; I have gathered my myrrh with my spice." This gathering fulfills her invitation, and it ends in a feast that spills over to others: "Eat, O friends! Drink, yes, drink deeply, O beloved ones!"

The two together: hedge and enclosure. These are the only two times Scripture uses this verb, and the contrast between them turns on a boundary. In Psalm 80 the hedge is broken, and strangers do the plucking. In the Song the garden is enclosed, and the rightful owner does the gathering. In both cases fruit is taken, because fruit exists to be harvested. The boundary decides who gets it. A vine without its hedge feeds every passerby. A garden kept sealed for its lord yields its myrrh and spice to him, and he receives it with delight. The setting also matters. In the psalm the hedge is broken by God's own hand in judgment. In the Song the enclosure is part of who the bride is, and she freely opens the garden to her beloved. Both kinds of boundary come from Him. Losing His protection is grief, and living within it is joy.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Israel's vine imagery reaches its goal in Jesus. Psalm 80 prays for "the branch that You made strong for Yourself" (Psalm 80:15). Jesus later calls Himself the true vine and His Father the vinedresser, and He says the Father is glorified when His disciples bear much fruit (John 15:1–8). In His parable of the wicked vinedressers, a landowner plants a vineyard, sets a hedge around it, and sends for its fruit (Matthew 21:33–41). The parable echoes Isaiah 5 and shows that God still expects a harvest from what He planted. In Christ the vine is restored, and the fruit that strangers once carried off is finally borne for its Owner.

The Song of Solomon is first of all a song of human love. Christians have long heard in it an echo of the larger story of Christ and His bride. That reading is a canonical resonance, not a stated New Testament fulfillment, but Scripture invites it. Paul describes Christ loving the church, giving Himself for her, and preparing her as His own (Ephesians 5:25–27). The bride who is "a garden enclosed," set apart for one beloved, fits the church that belongs wholly to her Lord. The bridegroom who comes to gather and feast looks ahead to the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:7–9).

7. Spirit & Life

Every believer's life will produce something: time, energy, affection, attention, and the fruit of the Spirit's work. That fruit will be gathered by someone. The question these two verses press is who will gather it. A Christian whose protective boundaries have collapsed through neglected prayer, tolerated sin, or a heart open to every passing influence finds that what God grew is being taken by things that care nothing for the vine. Distraction, bitterness, and the world's demands do not tend the vine. They only pluck it.

Psalm 80 also teaches what to do when the hedges are down. Asaph does not try to rebuild the wall himself. He cries out to God to return, look, and visit the vine. Restoration begins with prayer to the One who planted us.

The Song shows the other half. The bride's garden is enclosed, yet it is not closed to her beloved. She calls the garden his and asks him to come and eat. A guarded life is meant to be a life offered to Him, not a sealed fortress. The bridegroom's response is immediate: "I have come to my garden." Christ does not receive the fruit of His people grudgingly. He gathers it as His own and rejoices in it, and from His joy the feast overflows to others.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

ʼârâh occurs only in Psalm 80:12 and Song of Solomon 5:1, and both are developed in Section 5. Two related verses help explain these uses, though they do not contain the word. In Isaiah 5:5 the Lord says, "I will take away its hedge," which sheds light on the broken hedge in Psalm 80. In Song of Solomon 4:16 the bride invites her beloved to "come to his garden And eat its pleasant fruits," and his gathering in 5:1 answers that invitation.
