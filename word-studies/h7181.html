LISTEN, HEED, ATTEND - (qâshab, Strong's H7181)

Summary

The Hebrew verb qâshab means to listen closely, to pay attention, to give heed. It describes a person deliberately turning ear and mind toward someone who is speaking. The NKJV usually renders it "heed," "listen," "attend," or "give attention." Scripture uses the word in two directions. People are called to listen to God, to His wisdom, and to His prophets. God is asked, and sometimes said, to listen to His people.

Those two directions carry the word's spiritual weight. Samuel told Saul that "to heed" is better "than the fat of rams" (1 Samuel 15:22), which means God wants an attentive heart more than impressive religion. Proverbs makes careful listening the doorway to wisdom. The prophets, especially Jeremiah and Zechariah, show what happens when people refuse to listen. The psalmists ask God to "give heed to the voice of my cry" (Psalm 5:2). In Malachi 3:16 the Lord listens to the quiet conversation of people who fear Him. The God who asks to be heard is a God who listens.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Qâshab is a Hebrew verb found mostly in poetry and prophecy. It appears in the Psalms, Proverbs, Isaiah, and Jeremiah, with scattered uses in narrative books, Job, Daniel, and the Minor Prophets. It often stands beside other verbs for hearing in the parallel lines of Hebrew poetry. Psalm 17:1 is a good example: "Hear a just cause, O LORD, Attend to my cry; Give ear to my prayer." Stacking these verbs is a way of pressing hard for real attention.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To listen attentively, to pay close attention. The word describes deliberately directing one's attention toward a voice. It goes beyond sound reaching the ear. A father says this to his son in Proverbs 4:1, and a psalmist says it to God in Psalm 61:1.

2. To heed, to take to heart and respond. In some contexts, listening includes acting on what is heard. That is why 1 Samuel 15:22 places "to heed" beside "to obey."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning changes with who is listening and to whom.

God's people calling on God to listen. In the Psalms the word is almost always a plea: "Attend to my cry" (Psalm 17:1; compare 55:2; 61:1; 86:6; 142:6). The psalmist asks God to turn His full attention to one person's particular trouble.

God calling people to listen. Fathers in Proverbs, prophets in Isaiah and Micah, and the Servant in Isaiah 49 all use the word as a command: give attention, take heed.

People refusing to listen. The word frequently appears in a negative statement. Jeremiah's hearers told the watchmen, "We will not listen" (Jeremiah 6:17), and Manasseh's people "would not listen" (2 Chronicles 33:10; compare Proverbs 1:24; Jeremiah 6:19; Zechariah 1:4; Nehemiah 9:34).

Attention given to the wrong voice. Proverbs 17:4 and 29:12 use the same verb for a wicked person who listens to false lips and a ruler who pays attention to lies. So qâshab does not always describe a virtue. Everyone pays attention to something, and the question is whose voice receives it.

The command forms ("Attend," "Listen") matter because they appear on both sides of the relationship. The same urgent word that God speaks to His people, His people may speak back to Him.

4. Theological Significance

These passages present God as a speaking God who expects to be heard and a listening God who can be approached. Neither half works without the other. Israel's prophets did not describe a deity who simply received rituals. They described a Lord who spoke words that could be heard, understood, and obeyed, and who considered refusal to listen as rebellion.

The indictment passages make this plain. Jeremiah says his hearers' ear is "uncircumcised" and that the word of the Lord has become a reproach to them, so that they cannot give heed (Jeremiah 6:10). God set watchmen over them, but they said they would not listen (6:17). Zechariah 7:11 describes the people refusing to heed, turning a stubborn shoulder, and stopping their ears. In these passages, failing to listen is an act of will. A shoulder pulled away and ears deliberately stopped picture people resisting God's voice, not people who missed it. Proverbs 1:24 uses the same verb for Wisdom's call: she stretched out her hand, and no one regarded it.

Proverbs also shows the positive side. Wisdom is never presented as something absorbed by accident. A father tells his son to incline his ear to wisdom (Proverbs 2:2), to give attention to know understanding (4:1), and to give attention to his words (4:20; 5:1; 7:24). The wise life begins with ears that have been deliberately turned toward instruction.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Samuel 15:22 — heeding over sacrifice. God had told Saul to destroy Amalek completely. Saul spared the best livestock and then explained that it was meant for sacrifice. Samuel's answer is the clearest statement of this word's importance: "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, As in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, And to heed than the fat of rams." Saul tried to cover disobedience with worship, offering God something God had not asked for in place of what He had asked. The fat of rams was the choicest portion of an offering, and yet attentive listening outranks it. The prophets say the same thing in their own words. In Isaiah 1, God is weary of "the fat of fed cattle" and calls on the people to "Cease to do evil, Learn to do good" (Isaiah 1:11, 16–17). In Psalm 50, God has no need of bulls because "the cattle on a thousand hills" already belong to Him (Psalm 50:10). Hosea 6:6 gives His desire as "mercy and not sacrifice." Worship that will not listen to God's voice is not worship He accepts.

Malachi 3:16 — the Lord who listens in. In Malachi's day, cynical voices claimed that serving God was useless and that the proud were the ones who prospered (Malachi 3:13–15). Then Malachi turns to a different group. Those who feared the Lord spoke with one another, and the Lord listened and heard them, and a book of remembrance was written before Him for those who fear Him and meditate on His name (3:16, paraphrased). The scene is a conversation among believers, not a formal prayer in the temple. God pays attention to it all the same and has it recorded. In a discouraging time, the honest talk of faithful people about God was not lost. He was listening, and He remembered.

Daniel 9:19 — the listening that leads to action. Daniel read in Jeremiah that the seventy years of exile were nearly over. He did not take that as a reason to wait passively. He fasted, confessed Israel's sin, and prayed (Daniel 9:2–18). His prayer ends in a rush of short cries: Lord, hear; Lord, forgive; Lord, listen and act, and do not delay, for Your city and people bear Your name. Here qâshab is the link between God receiving the prayer and God moving in history. Daniel bases his request on God's name and mercy, not his own righteousness (9:18), and he expects that when God truly attends, He will also act.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

In Isaiah 49:1 the Servant of the Lord speaks to the far edges of the world, telling the coastlands to listen to Him and the distant peoples to take heed. The command to listen now reaches past Israel to the nations. Isaiah 51:4 makes a similar call, with the Lord's law and justice going out as a light to the peoples. The New Testament applies this Servant's mission to Jesus and His gospel. Simeon calls the infant Christ "a light to bring revelation to the Gentiles" (Luke 2:32), and Paul and Barnabas apply Isaiah 49:6 to their preaching to the nations (Acts 13:47).

The Servant who commands attention is also a listener. In the next song, Isaiah 50:4–5, the Lord awakens His ear morning by morning, and He is not rebellious. This passage uses different Hebrew words, but it fits the pattern. Saul would not heed, and Israel stopped its ears, but the Servant listens perfectly. At the Transfiguration the Father points to His Son and says, "Hear Him" (Mark 9:7). Hebrews repeats the old warning not to harden hearts when God's voice is heard (Hebrews 3:7–8). The long story of refused listening in the prophets finds its answer in a Son who heard the Father fully and who now calls the whole world to hear Him.

7. Spirit & Life

The Psalms give believers both permission and words to ask God for His full attention. "Give heed to the voice of my cry, My King and my God" (Psalm 5:2) is a prayer from someone who expects to be heard. In Psalm 55 the trouble is betrayal by a friend. In Psalm 142 the psalmist is brought very low and hunted by enemies. Psalm 66:19 then records the answer: God has attended to the voice of prayer. Crying out to God is not speaking into empty air.

Malachi 3:16 widens this comfort. God listens to more than our crises. When believers talk with one another about Him, especially when the culture says faith is pointless, He pays attention and remembers. Ordinary conversations between Christians matter more than they appear to.

These passages also warn about attention. We all heed something. Proverbs 17:4 shows that a heart can give itself to false lips. Saul shows that busy religious activity can hide an ear that has stopped listening. Jeremiah shows that refusing God's word again and again leaves an ear unable to receive it. The way of wisdom is to turn our attention back toward God's voice every day, in Scripture and in prayer, the way the Servant's ear was opened morning by morning.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 33:31 — Elihu urges Job to give ear and listen before he speaks. Careful listening is a form of humility in a painful conversation.

  • Isaiah 21:7 — Told to declare what he sees (21:6), the watchman "listened earnestly with great care." Attentiveness is the watchman's whole task.

  • Isaiah 32:3 — In the coming righteous kingdom, the ears of those who hear will listen. Restored attention is part of God's promised renewal.

  • Jeremiah 8:6 — God listened and heard, but His people did not speak what was right, and no one repented. Here God's listening exposes the people.

  • Jeremiah 23:18 — God asks which of the false prophets has truly marked and heard His word. True prophecy begins with listening to the Lord.

  • Micah 1:2 — All peoples and the whole earth are told to listen, because the Lord comes as a witness against them.
