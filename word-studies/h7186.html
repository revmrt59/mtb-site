HARD, STIFF, SEVERE - (qâsheh, Strong's H7186)

Summary

The Hebrew word qâsheh describes something hard: hard to bear, hard to move, or hard to soften. English translations render it many ways, including "hard," "rough," "cruel," "harsh," "stiff," "stubborn," "burdensome," and even "sorrowful." The variety reflects how widely the word is used. It can describe the crushing labor Pharaoh forced on Israel, the rough words of a foolish king, the stiff neck of a people who refuse God's leading, and the heavy grief of a woman praying in tears.

The word shows that hardness is not always sin. Hannah's "sorrowful spirit" is a life pressed down by pain, which is very different from Israel's "stiff neck," a will set against God. The word also describes the LORD's "severe sword," with which He will finally punish Leviathan, the twisted serpent. Across Scripture, qâsheh traces a story of tyrants who rule with hardness, a people whose hearts grow hard toward God, and a God who rescues the oppressed, bears with the stubborn, and sets His unbending justice against evil. That story moves toward Jesus, who meets hard hearts with grief and offers the weary a yoke that is easy.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Qâsheh is a Hebrew adjective. It appears from Genesis through Ezekiel, with a strong concentration in Exodus and Deuteronomy, where Israel is repeatedly called "stiff-necked." The word attaches itself to many kinds of things: labor, speech, rulers, battles, news, visions, wind, grief, jealousy, and the human neck, face, and heart. Because the word fits so many settings, its English translation changes often. Readers of the NKJV will meet the same Hebrew word behind "roughly" in Genesis 42:7, "hard" in Exodus 1:14, and "stiff" in Exodus 32:9.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, qâsheh means hard, in the sense of severe, difficult, or unyielding. Context decides which kind of hardness is meant:

1. Heavy and oppressive. Something is hard to bear, such as forced labor or a burdensome rule. Example: "hard bondage" (Exodus 1:14) and "burdensome service" (1 Kings 12:4).

2. Harsh in manner. A person or speech is rough, cruel, or severe toward others. Example: Joseph "spoke roughly" to his brothers (Genesis 42:7); Nabal was "harsh" (1 Samuel 25:3).

3. Unbending in will. Someone refuses to yield, which appears as "stiff-necked," "obstinate," or "hard-hearted." Example: Exodus 32:9; Ezekiel 3:7.

4. Weighed down in spirit. Distress presses hard on the soul. Example: Hannah's "sorrowful spirit" (1 Samuel 1:15).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The moral weight of qâsheh depends on what is hard. When it describes the things people suffer, such as hard bondage, a hard day (Job 30:25), or hard news (1 Kings 14:6), it names a burden. When it describes how people treat others, as with Rehoboam's rough answer or the sons of Zeruiah who were "too harsh" for David (2 Samuel 3:39), it exposes a fault. When it describes a person's stance toward God, it names rebellion.

Much of the word's force comes from the body parts it joins. With the neck, it pictures an ox stiffening against the yoke. With the face (Ezekiel 2:4, "impudent"), it describes people who no longer feel shame and can look straight at a prophet without flinching. With the heart (Ezekiel 3:7, "hard-hearted"), it describes a refusal located at the center of a person, where conviction should take hold. Read together, these images describe the same resistance from three sides: a neck that refuses to be led, a face that refuses to blush, and a heart that refuses to hear. Ezekiel 3:7 states it plainly: Israel "will not listen to you, because they will not listen to Me."

4. Theological Significance

Qâsheh exposes two kinds of hardness in a fallen world. One is pressed onto people from outside: the hard labor of Egypt, the heavy yoke of Solomon's later years, and the hard bondage of Babylon. The other grows from inside: the stiff neck and hard heart that resist God. Scripture shows God dealing with both. He rescues His people from the first, and He confronts, bears with, and pardons the second.

The word shows that earthly power often relies on hardness. Pharaoh, Rehoboam, and Babylon all rule by severity. God's way is different. He hears the cry of those under hard bondage (Deuteronomy 26:6) and promises them rest (Isaiah 14:3). Isaiah 19:4 even shows a reversal: Egypt, which once imposed hard bondage, is handed over to "a cruel master."

When the word is used of the LORD's actions, it describes firmness rather than cruelty. In Isaiah 27:1 the adjective describes His sword, not God Himself, but the picture shows His unyielding justice: the sword is severe because evil will not yield, and His justice will not bend. Even His discipline is measured. Isaiah 27:8 says He contended with His people "in measure," removing them "by His rough wind," and there the same word describes the wind. The purpose was to purge idolatry (27:9).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 32–34: the stiff-necked people and the presence of God. After the golden calf, the LORD says, "I have seen this people, and indeed it is a stiff-necked people!" (Exodus 32:9). The picture comes from the farm. A stubborn animal locks its neck so the yoke cannot be placed and the driver cannot turn it. Israel's problem was not weakness. They refused to be led. Because of this, God tells Moses He will not go up in their midst, lest He consume them on the way, "for you are a stiff-necked people" (Exodus 33:3, 5). Moses then makes his plea: "O Lord, let my Lord, I pray, go among us, even though we are a stiff-necked people; and pardon our iniquity and our sin, and take us as Your inheritance" (Exodus 34:9). Moses does not deny Israel's stubbornness. He brings it to God as the very reason the people need His presence and His pardon. Some readers note that the Hebrew connecting word can also be heard as "for," which makes Moses' plea bolder still. The thing that made God's nearness dangerous becomes the ground for asking Him to stay. Moses asks for this after God has proclaimed Himself "merciful and gracious" (34:6), and he is asking God to act on that name. Deuteronomy 9:4–6 uses the same description when Moses explains why Israel would receive the land. Israel's stiff neck rules out the people's own righteousness as the reason: the LORD was "not giving you this good land to possess because of your righteousness, for you are a stiff-necked people" (9:6). The reasons Moses does give are the wickedness of the nations being driven out and the LORD's oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (9:4–5).

1 Kings 12: hard rule divides a kingdom. The people tell Rehoboam, "lighten the burdensome service of your father, and his heavy yoke which he put on us, and we will serve you" (1 Kings 12:4). They offer loyalty in exchange for relief. The elders advise kindness, but "the king answered the people roughly" (12:13) and promised a heavier yoke. Ten tribes left, and the kingdom never reunited. Rehoboam assumed that hardness would secure his authority, and it cost him most of his kingdom.

1 Samuel 1:15: a hard spirit that is not a hard heart. Eli sees Hannah's lips moving without sound and accuses her of drunkenness. She answers, "I am a woman of sorrowful spirit… but have poured out my soul before the LORD." Her "sorrowful spirit" is literally a "hard" spirit, pressed down by barrenness and Peninnah's provocation. Nothing in her is set against God. She brings her heaviness to Him. Eli misread her, and believers can make the same mistake, treating someone crushed by sorrow as though that person were resisting God.

Isaiah 27:1: the severe sword. "In that day the LORD with His severe sword, great and strong, Will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan that twisted serpent." Leviathan is the twisting, slippery enemy, a picture of evil that writhes and escapes. Against it, the LORD's sword is qâsheh. The sword is what Isaiah calls hard: it does not bend, glance off, or lose its edge. Human hardness resists God, but the hardness of His sword pictures His fixed resolve against evil. For people who have suffered under cruelty, this is good news. Evil is persistent, but God's justice is firmer.

Ezekiel 2:4 and 3:7: a hard face and a hard heart. Ezekiel is sent to people who are "impudent and stubborn children" and "impudent and hard-hearted." God does not promise that they will listen. He promises to make Ezekiel's face and forehead strong against theirs (Ezekiel 3:8–9). God's answer to their hardness is to strengthen His messenger so that he can stand firm without being broken.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of qâsheh runs from Egypt's hard bondage, to Rehoboam's heavy yoke, to Babylon's hard service. Each time, God's purpose is rest (Isaiah 14:3). Jesus speaks into that history when He invites the weary to take His yoke, calling Himself gentle and lowly and His yoke easy (Matthew 11:29–30). Unlike Pharaoh and Rehoboam, He offers a yoke that does not crush the people who carry it.

Jesus also faced the inward hardness the prophets described. In Mark 3:5 He looked at His opponents "with anger, being grieved by the hardness of their hearts," and then healed a withered hand. Stephen used Moses' language against the council: "You stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears! You always resist the Holy Spirit" (Acts 7:51). These New Testament passages use Greek words, so they are canonical echoes, not occurrences of qâsheh. Even so, they show that the stiff neck of Sinai was still present when the Son of God stood among His people.

The severe sword of Isaiah 27:1 also points forward. Revelation pictures Christ with "a sharp sword" from His mouth (Revelation 19:15), and Scripture's final scenes show the ancient serpent defeated. There is also a fitting resonance with Zechariah 13:7, which Jesus applied to Himself (Matthew 26:31): the sword awakens against God's Shepherd. Christ received judgment in order to free people from it. In His work, Moses' prayer in Exodus 34:9 is answered fully. God has come to live among a stiff-necked people, and He pardons them.

7. Spirit & Life

Hannah and the wilderness generation show two very different kinds of hardness. Israel's hard neck led to judgment and death in the wilderness. Hannah's hard spirit, poured out before the LORD, led to the birth of Samuel, the prophet who would anoint David. God does not despise the heaviness His people carry. Psalm 62:8 speaks to people in Hannah's condition: "Pour out your heart before Him; God is a refuge for us." The urgent question is not whether life feels hard but whether we bring that hardness to God or harden ourselves against Him.

The stiff neck, the hard face, and the hard heart together are a warning. Each pictures resistance to God from a different side: refusing His direction, losing the ability to blush, and no longer hearing Him. Hebrews 3 urges believers not to harden their hearts while it is still "Today." Hannah and Moses show the difference between being weighed down and refusing God's leading. Hannah's heaviness was not resistance, and she poured it out before the LORD. Israel's stiff neck was resistance, and Moses did not excuse it. He named it and asked the Lord to go among them anyway and pardon them (Exodus 34:9).

People in leadership should learn from Rehoboam. Fathers, pastors, and employers are tempted to think a firmer hand will produce loyalty. Scripture shows rough words producing division. Yet firmness has its proper place. Ezekiel needed a face hard enough to withstand a hard people, and the Song of Songs pairs love "as strong as death" with "Jealousy as cruel as the grave" (Song of Songs 8:6). In that verse the word means that love will not let go of the one it loves, as the grave does not release the dead. God is gentle toward the weary and immovable in His love for His own.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 42:7 – Joseph speaks "roughly" to his brothers. His harshness hides his love and tests their hearts, and it ends in tears and reconciliation.

  • Exodus 6:9 – Israel would not heed Moses "because of anguish of spirit and cruel bondage." Oppression can make people too crushed to hope.

  • Exodus 18:26 – The "hard" cases were brought to Moses. Here the word simply means difficult.

  • Deuteronomy 26:6 – At the offering of firstfruits, Israel confesses that Egypt "laid hard bondage on us." Remembering past hardness becomes part of thanksgiving.

  • Job 30:25 – Job wept for the one "in trouble," literally the one whose day was hard. Compassion enters into another person's hardness.

  • Psalm 60:3 – God showed His people "hard things." Even severe providence comes from His hand.
