SEE, APPEAR, PROVIDE - (râʼâh, Strong's H7200)

Summary

Râʼâh is the ordinary Hebrew verb "to see," and it fills the Old Testament with eyes that look, notice, examine, and understand. Yet Scripture uses this plain word for some of its most important truths. In Genesis 1, God sees what He has made and judges it good. In the wilderness, Hagar names Him the God who sees her. On Mount Moriah, Abraham says that God will "see to" the lamb, and the NKJV rightly translates it "provide." When the same verb describes God being seen, English translates it "appeared." God shows Himself to Abraham, to Moses, and to Solomon on His own initiative.

The word also exposes the human eye. Eve saw the fruit, Achan saw the garment, and David saw Bathsheba, and in each case desire followed the eye into sin. The prophets describe a people who have eyes but do not see. Against that darkness, Scripture holds out a different kind of looking: the dying Israelite who looked at the bronze serpent and lived, and the believer who looks to Jesus. The hope that runs through the whole Bible is that God's people will one day see Him face to face.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Râʼâh is one of the most frequent verbs in the Hebrew Bible. It appears in every major section of the Old Testament: the creation and patriarch stories, the laws of Leviticus, the histories, the Psalms and wisdom books, and the visions of the prophets. Most of its uses describe ordinary eyesight, such as a man looking up and seeing travelers or a watchman spotting a runner. The theological weight of the word comes from particular clusters of passages, and those clusters should not be read into every occurrence.

One early use of the word became a title. A man who received insight from God was called a "seer," a form of this same verb that means one who sees. The narrator of 1 Samuel explains the older term: "for he who is now called a prophet was formerly called a seer" (1 Samuel 9:9). The title presents the prophet as someone whose eyes God had opened to realities that other people could not see.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning is to see with the eyes. From that core the word reaches naturally into several related senses:

1. To see, look at, or observe. This is the ordinary sense, as when Abraham lifted his eyes and saw the place far off (Genesis 22:4).

2. To perceive, consider, or examine. Seeing can mean grasping or inspecting something carefully. In Leviticus 13 the priest "examines" a skin disease, and in Psalm 8:3 David "considers" the heavens.

3. To appear or be seen. In this use the person is seen by others rather than doing the seeing, and it is the regular way of saying that the LORD "appeared" to someone (Genesis 12:7; 1 Kings 3:5).

4. To show, or cause someone to see. God "showed" Moses the pattern of the tabernacle (Exodus 25:9) and showed Amos his visions (Amos 7:1).

In a few places, seeing a need means attending to it, and the NKJV translates the verb "provide" (Genesis 22:8, 14; 1 Samuel 16:1). Context also produces renderings such as "select," "enjoy," and "regard."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes everything. When God is the one who sees, the word describes His judgment, His compassion, or His intention to act. When people are the ones seeing, the word can describe desire, discovery, worship, or failure to understand.

The difference between the forms that mean be seen and the forms that mean cause to see matters. When God "appears," He is the one who lets Himself be seen. When He "shows" a prophet something, the prophet receives sight he could not produce himself. In these passages people do not discover God by sharper observation. The vision comes from God's side.

One concentrated use deserves notice. Leviticus 13–14 uses the verb again and again for the priest's examination of skin diseases and contaminated houses. The diseased person could not declare himself clean. The priest had to look, and the priest's verdict decided whether that person lived inside or outside the camp.

4. Theological Significance

God's sight defines what is real. Six times in Genesis 1 God sees what He has made and finds it good (Genesis 1:4, 10, 12, 18, 21, 25). The seventh time, at the end of the sixth day, He sees everything He has made and calls it very good (Genesis 1:31). The same eyes later see that human wickedness has become great and that every intent of man's heart is only evil continually (Genesis 6:5). God does not take His measure of the world from appearances. When Samuel was impressed by Eliab's height, the LORD told him that people look at the outward appearance while the LORD looks at the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). Isaiah carries that contrast to the Messiah, who will judge differently from us: "He shall not judge by the sight of His eyes" (Isaiah 11:3). The writer of Hebrews states the same truth plainly: "all things are naked and open to the eyes of Him to whom we must give account" (Hebrews 4:13).

God's seeing moves Him to act. Hagar was Sarai's Egyptian maidservant. She had conceived by Abram, and when Sarai dealt harshly with her, she fled (Genesis 16:1–6). The Angel of the LORD found her by a spring of water in the wilderness, and she named Him You-Are-the-God-Who-Sees (Genesis 16:13). Centuries later God told Moses that He had surely seen the oppression of His people in Egypt (Exodus 3:7), and in the next verse He announces that He has come down to deliver them (3:8). When the elders heard that the LORD had looked on their affliction, they bowed their heads and worshiped (Exodus 4:31). The idols are different, because "Eyes they have, but they do not see" (Psalm 115:5). The living God sees and comes to help.

God makes Himself seen. The repeated phrase "the LORD appeared" (Genesis 12:7; 17:1; 1 Kings 9:2) shows that revelation is a gift. At the burning bush, Scripture says that "the Angel of the LORD appeared to him," and Moses "looked" (Exodus 3:2). Then, when the LORD saw that Moses had turned aside to look, God called to him from the bush (3:4). God appears to give a promise, issue a call, or speak to a king, as when "the LORD appeared to Solomon in a dream by night" (1 Kings 3:5). No one in these stories summons Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 22: The LORD will see to it. Isaac carried the wood up the mountain and asked his father where the lamb was. Abraham answered that God would provide the lamb for Himself (Genesis 22:8). The verb he used is râʼâh. When the ram appeared in the thicket, Abraham named the place The-LORD-Will-Provide (22:14). Abraham's answer placed the need for a lamb in God's hands: it was God's to see to. Here the Hebrew verb for seeing carries the sense of seeing to something, attending to it, and the ram shows how God saw to the offering in this account. Abraham "lifted his eyes and looked," found the ram caught by its horns, and "offered it up for a burnt offering instead of his son" (22:13). On Moriah, God's seeing and God's providing came together. The passage does not turn that into a promise about every need; it shows what God did on this mountain and why Abraham could trust Him with the offering. The saying that grew from this event looked forward: on the mountain of the LORD, it would be provided. That forward look leaves room for a greater provision still to come.

Exodus 33, Isaiah 6, and Job 42: Seeing God. Moses asked to see God's glory, and God told him that no one can see His face and live (Exodus 33:20). Isaiah's experience confirms how dangerous such sight is. When he saw the Lord high and lifted up, he cried out that he was undone, because his eyes had seen the King, the LORD of hosts (Isaiah 6:1, 5). Sinful people cannot stand before holiness unprotected. Yet Scripture also holds out the hope of seeing God as a gift of His grace. Through long exchanges with his friends, Job had protested his innocence and appealed for God to answer him: "Oh, that the Almighty would answer me" (Job 31:35). When the LORD finally answered him out of the whirlwind (Job 38:1), Job did not receive an explanation. He received God Himself: "I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear, But now my eye sees You" (Job 42:5). Knowing about God and meeting Him are different things, and for Job it was the meeting that brought him to repentance (Job 42:6).

Isaiah 6:9–10 and the prophets: Eyes that do not see. Immediately after his own vision, Isaiah was sent to a people who would keep on seeing without perceiving (Isaiah 6:9–10). Jeremiah called Judah a foolish people with eyes who do not see (Jeremiah 5:21), and Ezekiel lived among a rebellious house with eyes to see who did not see (Ezekiel 12:2). These people could see perfectly well with their physical eyes, but their hearts refused God. Isaiah also shows the other side of this blindness. The idol-maker cannot see what he is doing because God has shut his eyes (Isaiah 44:18). Persistent refusal to see can become God's judgment of no longer being able to see. Moses had already told Israel that the LORD had not yet given them eyes to see (Deuteronomy 29:4), which means that true sight is something God must give.

Numbers 21:8–9: Look and live. When venomous serpents struck the camp, the LORD told Moses, "Make a fiery serpent, and set it on a pole; and it shall be that everyone who is bitten, when he looks at it, shall live" (Numbers 21:8). The word "looks" in that promise is râʼâh. Moses made a bronze serpent and put it on a pole, and whenever someone who had been bitten "looked at the bronze serpent, he lived" (21:9). Verse 9 uses a different Hebrew verb for looking, but it records God's promise in verse 8 coming true. The cure required no ritual and no effort beyond looking at the remedy God had provided, and looking meant acting on what God had said.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The threads of this word come together in Jesus. On Moriah, Abraham told Isaac that God would provide the lamb (Genesis 22:8), yet the animal he offered in Isaac's place was a ram (22:13). Read alongside John's Gospel, Abraham's words gain a fuller meaning, for John the Baptist saw Jesus coming and said, "Behold! The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Moriah gave a picture, and Paul's words about the cross echo it: "He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all, how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things?" (Romans 8:32). The God who saw to the offering on Moriah gave His own Son as His greatest provision.

Jesus Himself drew the line from Numbers 21: "as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have eternal life" (John 3:14–15). In Jesus' comparison, the dying Israelite's look corresponds to the believer's faith.

The God who once "appeared" briefly at an altar or in a dream has now been seen in a human life: "we beheld His glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth" (John 1:14). Jesus could say, "He who has seen Me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). John adds that the glory Isaiah saw in the temple was Christ's glory (John 12:41). Jesus' healing of blind eyes matched Isaiah's promise that the eyes of the blind would see (Isaiah 29:18) and pictured the deeper healing He came to bring.

The story ends with sight on two scales. Isaiah promised that the glory of the LORD would be revealed and all flesh would see it together (Isaiah 40:5), and that all the ends of the earth would see God's salvation (Isaiah 52:10). Revelation answers, "every eye will see Him" (Revelation 1:7). The promise is also personal. Job longed to see his Redeemer: "Whom I shall see for myself, And my eyes shall behold, and not another" (Job 19:27). Isaiah promised that the righteous would see the King in His beauty (Isaiah 33:17). What Exodus 33 said no one could do and live becomes the promise of the redeemed: "They shall see His face" (Revelation 22:4), "for we shall see Him as He is" (1 John 3:2).

7. Spirit & Life

Believers who feel overlooked can take Hagar's name for God as their own. She was a foreign maidservant in someone else's household, given to Abram by her mistress and then treated harshly by that same mistress (Genesis 16:3, 6). She was alone in the wilderness when the Angel of the LORD found her (16:7). The same God who looks at the heart also sees the tears, and "The eyes of the LORD are on the righteous, And His ears are open to their cry" (Psalm 34:15). Being seen by Him means being both fully known and fully cared for.

That same gaze also searches us. The diseased Israelite in Leviticus 13 could not pronounce himself clean. He had to show himself to the priest. In a similar way, believers now bring themselves to a greater High Priest, and David's prayer gives the right words: search me, and see if there is any wicked way in me (Psalm 139:23–24). Asking God to look closely at us is the path to cleansing.

Our own eyes need redeeming too. Eve saw that the tree was good for food (Genesis 3:6). Achan confessed, I saw, I coveted, I took (Joshua 7:21). David saw a woman bathing from his rooftop (2 Samuel 11:2). In each story, sin followed a similar order: the eye saw, desire grew, and the hand reached out. The answer is to give the eyes something better to look at, "looking unto Jesus, the author and finisher of our faith" (Hebrews 12:2). Paul describes what follows: "beholding as in a mirror the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from glory to glory" (2 Corinthians 3:18). Paul's words suggest that we become like what we keep looking at.

Job's confession shows the goal of all of this. Many believers have heard about God for years, and the invitation of Psalm 34:8 still stands: "Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good." The psalm invites God's people to know Him for themselves, not only secondhand.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 12:13 — On Passover night God says that when He sees the blood, He will pass over. Protection depended on what God saw on the doorposts.

  • Deuteronomy 29:4 — Israel had witnessed great signs and still lacked "eyes to see." Sight that understands is God's gift.

  • Psalm 115:5 — "Eyes they have, but they do not see." Idols with blind eyes stand in contrast to the living God who sees.

  • Ecclesiastes 1:14 — The Preacher has "seen all the works that are done under the sun." His repeated "I saw" frames honest observation of a fallen world.

  • Isaiah 53:11 — The Servant "shall see the labor of His soul, and be satisfied," which means He will see the fruit of His suffering.

  • Malachi 3:18 — God promises that His people will again "discern" between the righteous and the wicked, a sense of seeing that means moral perception.
