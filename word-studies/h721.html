ARVADITE - (ʼArvâdîy, Strong's H0721)

Summary

"Arvadite" is a Hebrew name for a people: the Arvadites, a Canaanite clan who lived in Arvad, an island city off the northern coast of Phoenicia (modern Arwad, Syria). The word appears only twice in the Bible, in Genesis 10:18 and 1 Chronicles 1:16. Both verses list the Arvadites among the descendants of Canaan, the son of Ham, in the "Table of Nations," the record of how the families of the earth spread out after the flood.

A name in a genealogy may look like it has little to teach, but its placement says a good deal. The Arvadites lived at the far northern edge of the Canaanite world, well beyond the lands Israel would later enter, and Scripture still names them. The God who called Abraham also kept account of a distant island clan that Israel would hardly ever meet. Later, through the place name Arvad, the prophet Ezekiel shows these people rowing and guarding for proud Tyre and sharing in the fall God announced against that city. A single family name in Genesis becomes a quiet witness that no nation lives outside God's knowledge, His government, or His purposes of mercy.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼArvâdîy is a gentilic noun, a word that names a people by their place or ancestor, the way "Texan" names someone from Texas. It is formed from the place name Arvad (H0719), the island city where this clan settled. The Arvadite word stays inside genealogies. The place name appears later in prophecy, in Ezekiel's lament over Tyre.

Arvad was a small, rocky island just off the Phoenician coast, north of Sidon and Tyre. Its people were seafarers. In Genesis 10 they belong to Canaan's line, listed with clans whose names became very familiar to Israel, such as the Jebusite and the Amorite, and with others, like the Arvadite, who lived at the margins of Israel's story.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one meaning: an Arvadite, a member of the Canaanite people descended from Canaan and identified with the city of Arvad. It carries no figurative or spiritual sense of its own. Whatever it teaches comes from where Scripture places the name and from the later history connected to Arvad.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Both occurrences sit inside the same list. Genesis 10:15–18 names Canaan's descendants: "Canaan begot Sidon his firstborn, and Heth; the Jebusite, the Amorite, and the Girgashite; the Hivite, the Arkite, and the Sinite; the Arvadite, the Zemarite, and the Hamathite" (Genesis 10:15–18). First Chronicles 1:13–16 repeats the list almost word for word.

The grammar of the list helps the reader. Sidon and Heth are named as sons, but from "the Jebusite" onward the list shifts to singular gentilic names, "the Arvadite" among them. Each singular stands for a whole people. Genesis is tracing nations as well as individuals, and "the Arvadite" names a community that grew from Canaan's family and took root on a particular island.

The setting differs in the two books. Genesis places the name at the moment of dispersion, just before the line "Afterward the families of the Canaanites were dispersed" (Genesis 10:18). Chronicles places it in a survey running from Adam to Israel. There the nations are named first, and only then does the record narrow to Abraham, David, and the people of God.

4. Theological Significance

The listing of the Arvadites shows that God's attention reaches beyond the borders of His chosen people. Genesis 10 follows Canaan's descendants to the edges of the known world, and the Arvadites stand near the farthest northern point. Israel would fight Jebusites and Amorites. It had little reason to think about an island clan up the coast past Sidon. God's record still includes them. Scripture's account of history covers the whole human family, including peoples who never become important to Israel's story.

The list also tells us something about how nations come to exist. Genesis 10 presents the spread of peoples as the unfolding of families under God's oversight, and not as random drift. Chronicles reinforces this by beginning Israel's history with all the nations. Before the Chronicler tells of the temple and the throne of David, he shows that Israel belongs to a larger humanity, all of it known to God.

There is a sober side as well. Canaan's line stands under Noah's word, "Cursed be Canaan; A servant of servants He shall be to his brethren" (Genesis 9:25). The Arvadites are part of that line. Ezekiel's portrait of Arvad, developed below, shows that distance from Israel's battles did not place them beyond the reach of God's judgment on human pride.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 10:18–19 — the edge of the Canaanite world. The Arvadite is named in the last group of Canaan's sons, beside the Zemarite and the Hamathite, who are also northern peoples. Then the text turns: "Afterward the families of the Canaanites were dispersed. And the border of the Canaanites was from Sidon as you go toward Gerar, as far as Gaza; then as you go toward Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim, as far as Lasha" (Genesis 10:18–19). The border described runs from Sidon southward. It traces the Canaanite heartland, not a complete map of the land promised to Abraham. Joshua 13:4–6 still counts "all Lebanon," the land "as far as the entrance to Hamath," and "all the Sidonians" among the territory yet to be possessed. The Arvadites lived north of Sidon, beyond the border Genesis describes and far from the battles Joshua records. Genesis therefore distinguishes the Canaanite heartland that Israel would contend for from Canaanite families who scattered beyond it. Moses could have recorded only the peoples Israel would face. Instead he names the far-off clans too. Much later Paul would declare to the Athenians that God "has determined their preappointed times and the boundaries of their dwellings" (Acts 17:26), and Genesis 10 is a list of exactly such boundaries.

Ezekiel 27:8, 11 — Arvad in the service of Tyre. This passage uses the place name Arvad (H0719), not the Arvadite word. It still shows what became of this Canaanite family. Ezekiel's lament pictures Tyre as a magnificent merchant ship: "Inhabitants of Sidon and Arvad were your oarsmen; Your wise men, O Tyre, were in you; They became your pilots" (Ezekiel 27:8). A few verses later, the men of Arvad stand with Tyre's army on her walls (Ezekiel 27:11). The seafaring clan of Genesis 10 now supplies muscle at the oars and soldiers for a city's defenses, putting its skill to work for the wealthiest trading power on the coast. Ezekiel then describes that great ship broken by the east wind in the heart of the seas, with its crew and fighting men going down with it (Ezekiel 27:26–27). Arvad's people escaped the battles of Joshua's day by living far from them. They did not escape the judgment God pronounced on the proud commercial empire they served.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Genesis 10 comes right before God calls Abraham and promises that in him all the families of the earth will be blessed (Genesis 12:3). The Table of Nations, Arvadites included, is the list of families that promise has in view. Chronicles makes the same movement, from all nations to Israel, because Israel's story was always meant to serve the world's story.

When Jesus came, He went into "the region of Tyre and Sidon," the very coastline Ezekiel described. There a woman Matthew calls "a woman of Canaan" begged Him for mercy for her daughter (Matthew 15:21–28). Scripture does not say she was an Arvadite, but she came from the same Canaanite family and the same northern coast. Canaan's line stood under a curse, and Tyre stood under prophetic judgment, yet a Canaanite mother left Jesus' presence with her daughter healed and her faith commended. Paul's sermon in Acts 17 carries this forward: the God who fixed the boundaries of the nations did it "so that they should seek the Lord" (Acts 17:27, paraphrased). The scattering in Genesis 10 looks ahead to the gathering of a people from every nation through Christ.

7. Spirit & Life

A name most readers pass over shows how closely God sees. The Arvadites made no covenant with Israel, fought no famous battle, and produced no prophet. Their name is still written in two books of Scripture. If God keeps account of a small island people at the edge of the map, then no person is too remote or too unremarkable for His notice.

The story of Arvad also warns anyone who feels safely distant from God's dealings. Arvad sat on its island far from the conquest and lived for centuries by trade and hired service. Ezekiel shows where that ended: rowing a proud city's ship until it went under. Skill, location, and powerful alliances can postpone a reckoning, but they cannot cancel it. Being on the edge of the story is no protection. The safe place is with the God who rules the whole map.

Then there is the Canaanite woman. The same coast that Ezekiel saw sinking under judgment is where Jesus answered a desperate mother's faith. Mercy reached the far edge of the line of Canaan, and it can reach anyone today who comes to Christ.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word occurs only in Genesis 10:18 and 1 Chronicles 1:16, and both have been discussed above. 1 Chronicles 1:16 — "the Arvadite, the Zemarite, and the Hamathite" — is worth reading in its setting. The Chronicler preserves the Genesis list unchanged and leaves this distant clan in Israel's own story of its beginnings.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H0719, ʼArvad (Arvad) — lexical relative. This is the place name from which the gentilic is formed. It names the island city whose inhabitants served Tyre as oarsmen and soldiers (Ezekiel 27:8, 11).
