HEAD, TOP, CHIEF, BEGINNING - (rôʼsh, Strong's H7218)

Summary

The Hebrew word rôʼsh means "head." Most often it is the physical head of a person or animal, but Hebrew speakers naturally extended the word to whatever stands first or highest. It can be the top of a mountain or tower, the beginning of a year or a river, the chief of a family or army, the finest of the spices, or the full sum of a count. Wherever it appears, it marks the place of first rank.

Scripture builds some of its weightiest truths around the head. God's first promise after the fall is that the woman's Seed will bruise the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15). In Israel's worship, the offerer laid his hand on the head of the sacrifice, and the high priest confessed the nation's sins over the head of the scapegoat. Kings and priests were anointed on the head. God met Moses on the top of Sinai. David, driven from his throne, confessed the Lord as "the One who lifts up my head." The rejected stone became the "chief" stone of the building. These threads lead to Jesus Christ, who crushes the enemy's head, bears His people's sin, and is now "the head of the body, the church" (Colossians 1:18).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rôʼsh is a common Hebrew noun found in nearly every part of the Old Testament. It appears throughout the narratives and in sacrificial law, which describes the head of the offering and the anointed head of the priest. It is especially frequent in the genealogies and tribal records of Numbers and 1 Chronicles, where it names the "heads" of the fathers' houses. Poetry and prophecy use it for judgment, honor, mourning, and hope.

The NKJV translates it with an unusually wide range of words: head, top, chief, leader, captain, company, beginning, first, census, sum, choicest, and even "riverheads" (Genesis 2:10). That range comes from the ordinary way Hebrew stretched one familiar word to cover many situations. It does not mean the word carries hidden layers of meaning.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is the head, and from it comes the sense of what is first, highest, or foremost. The principal senses are:

1. The physical head of a person or animal, as when David cuts off Goliath's head (1 Samuel 17:51) or Aaron lays his hands on the goat's head (Leviticus 16:21).

2. The top or highest point of a mountain, tower, pillar, or ladder, such as the top of Mount Sinai (Exodus 19:20).

3. A leader or chief, the one at the head of a family, tribe, army unit, or nation (Numbers 1:16; Judges 11:11).

4. The beginning, the first, the best, or the total: the beginning of months (Exodus 12:2), the finest spices (Exodus 30:23), the sum of God's thoughts (Psalm 139:17).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting decides which sense applies. In Leviticus the head belongs to an animal on the altar. In Chronicles it belongs to the men who lead clans. In Isaiah and Micah it belongs to the mountain of the Lord's house.

One idiom deserves attention. To "lift the head" of someone could mean restoring him to honor, as Pharaoh restored the cupbearer (Genesis 40:13). It could mean executing him, as Pharaoh "lifted" the baker's head off his body (Genesis 40:19). It could also mean taking a census, literally lifting the head of the people (Exodus 30:12; Numbers 1:2). The phrase stays the same, and the surrounding words tell the reader whether a man is being honored, counted, or killed.

Moses also sets rôʼsh against "tail." Israel would be "the head and not the tail" if it obeyed (Deuteronomy 28:13), and the foreigner would become the head if it rebelled (28:44). In this pairing, "head" means leading and "tail" means being led.

4. Theological Significance

In Scripture, the head stands for the whole person, so what happens to the head describes what happens to the person. That is why blessing and consecration land there. Jacob deliberately crossed his arms to set his right hand on the head of Ephraim, the younger son, knowingly giving Ephraim the greater blessing despite Joseph's protest (Genesis 48:14, 17–19). Moses poured the anointing oil on Aaron's head when he set him apart as priest (Leviticus 8:12). Psalm 133:2 pictures that oil as "upon the head, running down on the beard, the beard of Aaron, running down on the edge of his garments." What is poured on the head does not stay there. It runs down over the rest.

The same logic applies to leadership. The heads of Israel carried the people with them into faithfulness or into ruin. God promised that obedience would make Israel the head among the nations (Deuteronomy 28:13). Isaiah later announced that the Lord would cut off "head and tail" from Israel and named the head as the elder and the honorable man (Isaiah 9:14–15). When Israel's leaders were corrupt, the whole nation suffered with them.

Finally, the "top" of the mountain is where God chooses to meet people. The Lord came down on the top of Sinai and called Moses up to Him (Exodus 19:20; 34:2). The prophets carried that picture forward. In the latter days the mountain of the Lord's house will be established on the top of the mountains, and the nations will stream to it (Isaiah 2:2; Micah 4:1). Ezekiel adds that the whole area on top of that mountain will be most holy (Ezekiel 43:12). The highest place belongs to the Lord, and from there He draws the world to Himself.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 3:15 and the crushed head. God's sentence on the serpent contains the first promise of the gospel: "He shall bruise your head, And you shall bruise His heel." The contrast matters. A wound to the heel hurts, while a blow to the head is fatal. That first promise establishes a pattern that runs through Israel's history. Jael drives the tent peg through Sisera's head (Judges 5:26). Dagon falls before the ark, and his head lies broken on the threshold (1 Samuel 5:4). David takes Goliath's own sword and cuts off his head (1 Samuel 17:51). The psalmists and prophets announce the same outcome for every enemy of God: "God will wound the head of His enemies" (Psalm 68:21), and Habakkuk sees the Lord striking the head from the house of the wicked (Habakkuk 3:13). In Scripture, the war against evil ends with its head crushed.

Leviticus 1:4 and 16:21 and the head of the substitute. The worshiper bringing a burnt offering "shall put his hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it will be accepted on his behalf to make atonement for him" (Leviticus 1:4). The same gesture appears with peace offerings and sin offerings (3:2; 4:4, 29). By pressing his hand on the animal's head, the worshiper identified himself with the animal that would die in his place. On the Day of Atonement the act reached its fullest form. Aaron was to "lay both his hands on the head of the live goat, confess over it all the iniquities of the children of Israel... putting them on the head of the goat" (16:21). The head became the place where the people's sin was transferred to the substitute that carried it away.

Genesis 40 and Psalm 3:3 and the God who lifts the head. In Joseph's prison, the same idiom meant restoration for one man and death for another (Genesis 40:13, 19). David took that ordinary court expression and made it a confession of faith. Fleeing Jerusalem from his son Absalom, he climbed the Mount of Olives weeping with his head covered (2 Samuel 15:30). Psalm 3 comes from that flight, and in it David says, "But You, O LORD, are a shield for me, My glory and the One who lifts up my head" (Psalm 3:3). He had lost his throne and his city, and his own son wanted him dead. No king or court could restore him, so he looked to the Lord to raise his head again. Later he could sing, "And now my head shall be lifted up above my enemies all around me" (Psalm 27:6).

Psalm 118:22 and the chief cornerstone. "The stone which the builders rejected Has become the chief cornerstone." Here rôʼsh is translated "chief." The stone the builders threw aside as useless becomes the most important stone in the whole structure. The builders' verdict was reversed, and the reversal was the Lord's doing (118:23).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament places Jesus at the center of each of these lines. The heel-wound and head-blow of Genesis 3:15 find their meaning at the cross, where the Seed of the woman suffered and the serpent's power was broken. Psalm 110, a royal psalm that the New Testament repeatedly applies to Christ, describes the Priest-King who "shall execute the heads of many countries" (110:6). The pattern of the crushed head ends with the Messiah's victory.

The hands on the head of the sacrifice point to the One on whom the Lord laid the iniquity of us all (Isaiah 53:6). The scapegoat bore Israel's sins into an uninhabited land (Leviticus 16:21–22). In a distinct part of the same ritual, the bodies of the sin offerings were burned outside the camp (16:27). Hebrews connects this with Jesus suffering outside the gate to sanctify His people (Hebrews 13:11–12). He also bore the shame of His own head being treated with contempt, when soldiers crowned Him with thorns and struck Him on the head (Matthew 27:29–30).

The cornerstone is an explicit fulfillment. Jesus applied Psalm 118:22 to Himself (Matthew 21:42), and Peter told the council that rejected Him, "This is the 'stone which was rejected by you builders, which has become the chief cornerstone'" (Acts 4:11). The crucifixion did not overturn God's building plan. God carried it out through the cross.

The prophets also looked for one Head over a reunited people. After centuries of division between Judah and Israel, Hosea promised that the two would be gathered together and "appoint for themselves one head" (Hosea 1:11). Micah saw the Lord breaking open the way for His captive people, with their King passing before them and "the LORD at their head" (Micah 2:13). Israel had known hundreds of heads of houses, tribes, and companies, and many of them failed. The hope was for a Head who would not fail. The New Testament names Him: God "gave Him to be head over all things to the church" (Ephesians 1:22), and "He is the head of the body, the church, who is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead" (Colossians 1:18). In Christ, the senses of rôʼsh come together: He is the chief, the beginning, and the Head of His people.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 133 gives a picture worth keeping. The oil poured on Aaron's head ran down his beard to the edge of his garments. The psalm is about brothers living together in unity, and the image shows where that unity comes from. It flows down from the anointed head. Believers do not produce their life together by effort. We share in what has been poured out on Christ, our Head. The same Shepherd who anointed David's head with oil (Psalm 23:5) still pours His favor on His people.

The word also speaks to shame. When Tamar was violated, she put ashes on her head and laid her hand on it (2 Samuel 13:19). Jeremiah warned Judah that it would come away from its false alliances with its hands on its head (Jeremiah 2:37). Hands on the head meant that honor was gone. David knew that condition when he climbed the Mount of Olives with his head covered, and he also knew what God would do about it. The Lord lifts the heads of those who cannot lift their own. A believer bowed down by sin, loss, or another person's cruelty does not have to manufacture dignity. God restores it.

This word also gives a believer confidence in battle. Our Head has already suffered the bruised heel and dealt the fatal blow. Temptation and accusation are real, but they come from a defeated enemy.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 12:2 — The Passover month is made the beginning of months for Israel. Redemption reset the calendar, and Israel's year began with God's deliverance.

  • Proverbs 8:23 — Wisdom speaks of being set up from everlasting, from the beginning, before the earth existed.

  • Isaiah 41:4 — The Lord calls the generations from the beginning and identifies Himself as the First and the Last. Rôʼsh here ties the beginning of history to God Himself.

  • Psalm 119:160 — The entirety of God's word is truth. "Head" here means the whole sum, so God's word is true taken all together as well as in each of its parts.

  • Psalm 139:17 — David marvels at how great the sum of God's thoughts toward him is.

  • Isaiah 35:10 — The ransomed return to Zion with everlasting joy on their heads, a crown in place of ashes.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Two passages call for care. In Genesis 47:31, Jacob bows at the head of his bed, while Hebrews 11:21 says he worshiped leaning on the top of his staff. The Hebrew consonants can be read as either "bed" or "staff." Rôʼsh means "head" or "top" either way; only the object changes. In Ezekiel 38:3 and 39:1, the NKJV renders the word as a proper name, "Rosh," though the same words can also be read as "chief prince." Readers should not build prophetic systems on a single disputed rendering.

Scripture itself identifies two heads as signs. The Nazirite let no razor touch his head because "his separation to God is on his head" (Numbers 6:5, 7). If he was accidentally defiled, he shaved his head and began again (6:9). When the vow ended, he shaved his head at the tabernacle door and burned the hair in the fire under the peace offering (6:18). His head displayed his vow to everyone who saw him. At the opposite end of the law stood the leper, whose head was to be left uncovered and his clothes torn as he cried "Unclean!" (Leviticus 13:45). The anointed priest and the leper represent the two extremes of Israel's worship: the head marked as holy and the head marked as defiled.

Genesis sets two "tops" side by side. At Babel, people proposed a tower "whose top is in the heavens" (Genesis 11:4), trying to reach God's realm by their own building. At Bethel, God showed Jacob a ladder whose top reached heaven, with angels ascending and descending (28:12). What human pride tried to build, God gave as a gift. Jesus drew on Jacob's vision when He told Nathanael that he would see angels ascending and descending on the Son of Man (John 1:51). Christ is the true connection between heaven and earth. Genesis 2:10 also uses the word for the "riverheads" of the river flowing out of Eden, which ties the source of life-giving water to the place of God's presence. Ezekiel 47 and Revelation 22 later picture a river flowing from God's dwelling and throne.

Because a census was literally a "lifting of the head," and Exodus 30:12 required each man counted to give a ransom so that no plague would come on the people, some see in it a picture of God numbering those He has ransomed. The idiom and the ransom are both in the text, but the spiritual connection is an analogy rather than a stated teaching.
