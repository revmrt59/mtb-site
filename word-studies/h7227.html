MANY, GREAT, ABUNDANT - (rav, Strong's H7227)

Summary

The Hebrew word rav measures how much of something there is. It describes the many people, the great size, the long time, the large amount, and in some passages the older brother or the chief officer. The word itself is morally neutral. It can describe a crowd of enemies, a flood of water, a heap of treasure, or a pile of sin. Scripture's most important uses come when this same measuring word is turned toward God. In Genesis 6:5 the LORD saw that "the wickedness of man was great," using this word. At Sinai, after the golden calf, the LORD proclaimed Himself "abounding in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6), using the same word again. Human evil is great, and God's mercy is greater still. Moses, Nehemiah, David, Joel and Jonah all return to that confession.

The word also traces the scope of God's saving purpose. Isaiah's suffering Servant justifies "many" and bears "the sin of many." Daniel sees "many" rising from the dust, and the wise turning "many" to righteousness. The prophets see "many" peoples streaming up to the mountain of the LORD. Along the way, rav exposes what people trust: many waters, many soldiers, abundant treasure. God subdues those things, judges them, or deliberately reduces them so that His own power is seen.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rav is one of the most common descriptive words in the Hebrew Old Testament. It appears throughout the Law, the histories, the Psalms, wisdom literature and the prophets. It is especially frequent in the Psalms and in Jeremiah and Ezekiel. Most of the time it works as an adjective, telling us that something is many or great. Sometimes it stands on its own as a noun: the many, the great one, or the chief.

In the later historical books, rav becomes part of Babylonian official titles. Nebuzaradan is "the captain of the guard" (2 Kings 25:8), and Ashpenaz is the "master" of Nebuchadnezzar's eunuchs (Daniel 1:3). In these titles, rav means chief or head over. The related Jewish title Rabbi means my master.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rav means abundant or large in measure. The English translation depends on what is being measured:

1. Many or numerous (number). Many people, many days, or many nations. "Many are the afflictions of the righteous" (Psalm 34:19).

2. Great, much or abundant (size, amount or intensity). Great wickedness, a great deep, abundant mercy. "Abundant in mercy to all those who call upon You" (Psalm 86:5).

3. Older or chief (seniority or rank). The firstborn in "the older shall serve the younger" (Genesis 25:23), or the head officer in a title such as "captain of the guard."

4. Enough (an amount that has reached its limit). The word means that is sufficient or stop, as in "Enough, O princes of Israel!" (Ezekiel 45:9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's meaning stays steady, but the tone changes with what is being measured. When rav describes enemies, it expresses danger: "Many are they who rise up against me" (Psalm 3:1). When it describes God's mercy, it brings comfort. When it describes wealth, it often carries a warning.

Two fixed phrases deserve attention. "Many waters" is a recurring poetic image for overwhelming forces that only God can master (Psalm 18:16; 93:4). It also describes the noise of invading nations (Isaiah 17:12–13). "The many" can become more than an indefinite crowd. In Isaiah 53 and Daniel 11–12, it names the large company of people affected by God's saving work.

A third pattern appears with "too many" and "enough." In Judges 7:2 the LORD tells Gideon that his army is "too many" for Him to give them victory. In 1 Chronicles 21:15 He tells the destroying angel that it is "enough." In these passages the word marks a limit that God Himself sets.

4. Theological Significance

Rav gives Scripture a way to weigh things against one another. Genesis 6:5 uses it for human evil at its worst: "the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intent of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually." The flood answered that greatness. But when Israel sinned at Sinai, God did not describe Himself by a smaller measure. He described Himself by a larger one: "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). The same word sets God's mercy on the scale opposite human sin, and God's mercy outweighs it. Paul states the same truth with Greek words: "where sin abounded, grace abounded much more" (Romans 5:20).

The word also shows how God treats human greatness. In Genesis 25:23 God tells Rebekah that "the older shall serve the younger." The older son had the greater claim by birth, and God set that claim aside. Paul explains that God spoke before the children were born, "that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11). Gideon's story follows the same pattern. "The people who are with you are too many for Me to give the Midianites into their hands, lest Israel claim glory for itself against Me" (Judges 7:2). God cut thirty-two thousand men down to three hundred. Jonathan knew this about God when he said the LORD can save by many or by few (1 Samuel 14:6). God "has chosen the weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty" (1 Corinthians 1:27).

The prophets turn this truth into judgment. Babylon is addressed as the city that dwells "by many waters, Abundant in treasures," and the next words are "Your end has come" (Jeremiah 51:13). Tyre’s trade satisfied many peoples and enriched the kings of the earth (Ezekiel 27:33). Assyria grew tall like a cedar fed by abundant waters (Ezekiel 31:5–7). Then each one fell. Great wealth persuaded these nations that they did not need God, and their greatness did not save them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 34:6 and its echoes. God revealed His character to Moses right after the golden calf, when Israel had just broken faith with Him. Israel's later prayers kept returning to the phrase "abounding in mercy." Moses appealed to it when he interceded after the spies' report (Numbers 14:18). The Levites confessed it after the exile (Nehemiah 9:17). David prayed it: "Longsuffering and abundant in mercy and truth" (Psalm 86:15). Joel made it the basis for repentance: "Return to the LORD your God, For He is gracious and merciful, Slow to anger, and of great kindness" (Joel 2:13).

Jonah used the same words as a complaint. He fled to Tarshish because "I know that You are a gracious and merciful God, slow to anger and abundant in lovingkindness" (Jonah 4:2). Jonah understood exactly what God is like, and he resented that God's mercy reached as far as Nineveh. The book ends with God using rav once more. He asks whether He should not pity the great city with its "much livestock" (Jonah 4:11). God's mercy is large enough to reach the people His prophet wanted judged.

Isaiah 52:13–53:12. The fourth Servant Song begins and ends with "many." Many were astonished at the Servant's disfigured appearance, and He will sprinkle many nations (52:14–15). By His knowledge the righteous Servant will justify many, because He bears their iniquities (53:11). God will give Him a portion with the great, because He poured out His soul to death and bore the sin of many (53:12). One Servant suffers alone, and the effect of His suffering reaches a multitude. The repetition of "many" at the beginning and end of the song shows how far His work extends.

Daniel 12:2–3. Daniel's last vision was given to a people facing intense persecution, in which even those who understood God's ways would "fall by sword and flame" (11:33). The promise to them is framed with the same word: "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, Some to everlasting life, Some to shame and everlasting contempt" (12:2). The same awakening leads to two very different ends. One group rises to life that never ends; the other rises to lasting disgrace. Then Daniel turns to the faithful: "Those who are wise shall shine Like the brightness of the firmament, And those who turn many to righteousness Like the stars forever and ever" (12:3). The wise had instructed "many" while they suffered (11:33). The resurrection shows that their faithfulness was not wasted. The God who saw them teach under the threat of the sword will make them shine forever.

Many waters. David remembers that God "drew me out of many waters" (Psalm 18:16). Psalm 29:3 declares, "The LORD is over many waters." Psalm 93:4 adds, "The LORD on high is mightier Than the noise of many waters." Revelation then explains the image directly. The harlot "sits on many waters" (Revelation 17:1), and the angel says, "The waters which you saw, where the harlot sits, are peoples, multitudes, nations, and tongues" (17:15). Babylon's river setting in Jeremiah 51:13 becomes a picture of the restless nations that support the world's proud system. The image does not always carry this symbolic meaning; in Song of Solomon 8:7, many waters cannot quench love. Ezekiel heard the voice of God's glory like the sound of many waters (Ezekiel 43:2). The One who rules the roaring waters is louder than they are.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus took up the language of Isaiah 53 when He said He came to give His life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45) and when He called the cup His blood of the new covenant, shed for many (Matthew 26:28). Hebrews says plainly that "Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many" (Hebrews 9:28). Paul builds his argument in Romans 5 on the same contrast between one person and the many: "by one Man's obedience many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19). In the same passage he speaks of "those who receive abundance of grace" (5:17). The abundant mercy announced at Sinai is given in full through the one obedient Man, Jesus Christ.

The prophets also foresaw how far the gospel would spread. Many people will say, "Come, and let us go up to the mountain of the LORD" (Isaiah 2:3; Micah 4:2). Many nations will be joined to the LORD (Zechariah 2:11), and many peoples and strong nations will seek Him in Jerusalem (Zechariah 8:22). The "many waters" of rebellious nations in Revelation 17 stand in contrast to the many nations who come willingly to worship.

The title Rabbi is related to this Hebrew word. Jesus addressed the title directly. He told His disciples not to seek that title, because One is their Teacher, the Christ, and they are all brothers. He added that the greatest among them must be their servant (Matthew 23:8, 11). Babylon's chief officers held power by ruling over others. The true Master of His people chose the place of the Servant who bears the sin of many.

7. Spirit & Life

"Many are the afflictions of the righteous, But the LORD delivers him out of them all" (Psalm 34:19). This verse does not make trouble seem small. It admits that trouble can be many. The promise is measured against that number: the LORD delivers out of all of them. When the soldiers did not break Jesus' legs, John wrote that this happened so that the Scripture would be fulfilled: "Not one of His bones shall be broken" (John 19:36). John does not name the passage he has in mind. The next line of this psalm, "He guards all his bones; Not one of them is broken" (Psalm 34:20), may be echoed there. So may the Passover command that not one bone of the lamb be broken (Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12). Either way, the Righteous One went through the greatest affliction, and God brought Him through it.

The word "enough" can describe both human exhaustion and God's limits. Under the broom tree, Elijah said "It is enough" and asked to die (1 Kings 19:4). The angel answered with the same word: the journey was too great for him, so he must eat (19:7). God did not scold Elijah for his weakness. He agreed that the road was too long and fed him for it. God also says "enough" to stop His own judgment (1 Chronicles 21:15), and to end Moses' pleading over the Promised Land (Deuteronomy 3:26). The God whose mercy is abundant also decides where trials end and where requests must rest.

When believers feel outnumbered or overwhelmed, they can remember Gideon's three hundred and the LORD who is mightier than many waters. Being few or weak does not mean God has failed. Often it is the setting in which God shows that the victory belongs to Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Lamentations 3:23 — In the middle of Jerusalem's ruin, the poet confesses that God's faithfulness is great, and he uses this word to do it.

  • Psalm 25:11 — David asks God to pardon his iniquity for His name's sake, because the iniquity is great. He makes the size of his sin the reason to seek mercy, not a reason to hide.

  • Psalm 119:165 — Those who love God's law have great peace, and nothing makes them stumble.

  • 2 Kings 6:16 — Elisha tells his frightened servant that those with them are more than those with the enemy, and then God opens the servant's eyes to see the horses and chariots of fire.

  • Proverbs 19:21 — A person's heart holds many plans, but the LORD's counsel is what stands.

  • Ezekiel 45:9 — "Enough, O princes of Israel!" God sets a limit on leaders who use violence and plunder His people.
