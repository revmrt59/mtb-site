TEN THOUSAND, MYRIAD - (rᵉbâbâh, Strong's H7233)

Summary

The Hebrew word rᵉbâbâh means "ten thousand" or "a myriad," the largest round number in ordinary Hebrew speech. It is often used to describe a crowd too large to count. The NKJV usually translates it as "ten thousand" or "ten thousands." Scripture often places it right after "thousand," so the number jumps upward line by line. That jump is the point. Five of Israel chase a hundred, but a hundred chase ten thousand. Saul has his thousands, but David has his ten thousands. A thousand may fall at the believer's side, and ten thousand at the right hand, yet harm does not come near.

Through this word Scripture shows a God who works on a scale that overturns human arithmetic. The myriad describes the holy host that comes with the Lord at Sinai, the countless descendants God promised His people, and the enemies who cannot overcome those He shelters. It also rings through the song that praised David, the king God had chosen. In the Song of Solomon the word takes a different turn: the beloved is "chief among ten thousand," and so the word for vast numbers becomes a way to praise one person who has no equal. Micah adds a warning. God cannot be won over by ten thousand rivers of oil. His power is beyond counting, but what He asks of His people is personal: justice, mercy, and a humble walk with Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rᵉbâbâh belongs to a family of Hebrew words for being many or great. It is a noun, the term for ten thousand. It appears in blessings (Genesis 24:60; Deuteronomy 33), in battle accounts and war songs (Leviticus 26; 1 Samuel 18), in psalms of trust (Psalms 3 and 91), in love poetry (Song of Solomon 5:10), and in prophetic rebuke (Micah 6:7).

Hebrew poetry often says something once and then says it again more strongly. Number pairs suit this style, and "thousand … ten thousand" is the most common pair used with this word. When a reader sees the two numbers together, the second line is meant to go beyond the first.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Ten thousand as an actual figure in counting. Judges 20:10 uses it this way when Israel chose men to fetch provisions for the army: "a thousand out of every ten thousand." What is being counted is people, one man in every ten sent to gather food.

2. A myriad, meaning a multitude too large to count. Psalm 3:6 uses this sense: "ten thousands of people." Genesis 24:60 also uses it when Rebekah is blessed to become the mother of a vast number of descendants.

In Ezekiel 16:7 the noun is applied to a person. The Hebrew says, in effect, I made you a myriad. The NKJV translates the idea as "I made you thrive." God caused Israel to increase like plants in a field.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's main pattern is escalation. When "ten thousand" follows "thousand," the second number goes beyond the first and makes the contrast sharper. Sometimes the contrast compares two people, as with Saul and David. Sometimes it compares danger with safety, as in Psalm 91:7. Sometimes it shows how out of proportion a victory or a defeat really is (Leviticus 26:8; Deuteronomy 32:30).

What the word describes depends on what is being counted. It can count the holy ones who come with God (Deuteronomy 33:2) or the people among whom He dwells (Numbers 10:36). It can count enemies who surround a believer (Psalm 3:6) or descendants promised to a family (Deuteronomy 33:17). It can even count offerings that impress no one in heaven (Micah 6:7). Only once does the word serve to praise excellence instead of size, in Song of Solomon 5:10.

4. Theological Significance

The passages that use this word show that outcomes in Scripture do not depend on headcount. In Leviticus 26:8 the promise for obedience reads, "Five of you shall chase a hundred, and a hundred of you shall put ten thousand to flight." In the first group the odds are twenty to one; in the larger group they rise to a hundred to one. Adding more of God's people does not just add more strength. It multiplies the result far beyond the numbers involved, because the victory is part of the blessing the Lord Himself promises in that chapter.

Deuteronomy 32:30 shows the same reasoning from the other side. In Moses' song the question is how one enemy could chase a thousand Israelites, and two could put ten thousand to flight: "Unless their Rock had sold them, And the LORD had surrendered them?" This time Israel is the side that runs. An absurd defeat can be explained the same way as an absurd victory: God decides the outcome. Joshua later gives the positive form of the same truth: one man of Israel would chase a thousand because the Lord fought for them (Joshua 23:10). In these passages the size of the armies did not settle the outcome. What settled it was whether God was with His people.

The myriad also shows God's glory surrounded by multitudes. At Sinai the Lord came with ten thousands of holy ones, and a fiery law came from His right hand (Deuteronomy 33:2). In the wilderness, whenever the ark set out, Moses called on the Lord to rise up and scatter His enemies (Numbers 10:35). When the ark came to rest, he prayed, "Return, O LORD, To the many thousands of Israel" (Numbers 10:36). Heaven surrounds God with a countless host, and on earth He chooses to dwell among a countless people. Taken together, these passages point to God's purpose to gather a great multitude around Himself.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Samuel 18:7–8 and its echoes. After David killed Goliath, the women of Israel sang, "Saul has slain his thousands, And David his ten thousands" (18:7). The song celebrated David's victories. Saul heard something else in it: a threat to his throne. "They have ascribed to David ten thousands," he complained, "and to me they have ascribed only thousands. Now what more can he have but the kingdom?" (18:8). From that day he kept a jealous eye on David. The song traveled further than Saul could have wanted. In Gath the servants of Achish repeated it and called David "the king of the land" (1 Samuel 21:11). Later the Philistine commanders repeated it again to refuse David a place in their army (1 Samuel 29:5). The narrator does not say that the Philistines saw God's hand in David's rise. They knew the song and feared the warrior it praised. The reader knows more. The LORD had already anointed David (1 Samuel 16:13), and Saul feared David "because the LORD was with him" (18:12). Read in that light, Saul's fight against the song became a fight against God's choice, and it showed how far he had wandered from God.

Psalm 3:6 and Psalm 91:7. The heading of Psalm 3 places David in flight from his own son Absalom. Yet David writes, "I will not be afraid of ten thousands of people Who have set themselves against me all around." The verses before this explain his confidence: he lay down, slept, and woke because the Lord sustained him. Psalm 91:7 widens the promise to anyone who dwells in the shelter of the Most High: "A thousand may fall at your side, And ten thousand at your right hand; But it shall not come near you." In both psalms the danger is counted honestly. The believer is not asked to pretend the enemies are few. The psalmist sets their great number against a God whose protection is greater still.

Song of Solomon 5:10. The bride is asked what makes her beloved better than any other. She answers, "My beloved is white and ruddy, Chief among ten thousand." Here the word for a crowd too large to count becomes the backdrop for one man who stands out from all of them. Gather ten thousand, and he is still the one she would pick out of the crowd. Christians have long heard Christ in this love song, and the line fits what the New Testament says plainly about Him. He is the one "that in all things He may have the preeminence" (Colossians 1:18). He became "so much better than the angels" (Hebrews 1:4), and God has given Him "the name which is above every name" (Philippians 2:9).

Micah 6:6–8. Israel's question grows until it becomes ridiculous: "Will the LORD be pleased with thousands of rams, Ten thousand rivers of oil? Shall I give my firstborn for my transgression?" The myriad here stands for religious effort pushed to its limit, with offerings so large that nobody could actually bring them. God's answer does not involve any number at all: "to do justly, To love mercy, And to walk humbly with your God" (6:8). Psalm 50 helps explain why. The cattle on a thousand hills already belong to Him (Psalm 50:10). A God who owns everything cannot be bought with anything.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The myriad follows God's purposes across the whole Bible. God promised a vast family. Rebekah was blessed to become the mother of thousands of ten thousands (Genesis 24:60), and Moses spoke of the ten thousands of Ephraim (Deuteronomy 33:17). Ezekiel looks back on Israel's growth as pure grace. God made the abandoned child a myriad, yet she was still "naked and bare" until He covered her (Ezekiel 16:7–8). Her growth was His gift long before she deserved anything.

The New Testament continues these threads using the Greek word for "myriad." Jude, quoting Enoch, speaks of the Lord coming with ten thousands of His saints (Jude 14), which recalls the holy ones at Sinai. In Revelation the angels around the throne number "ten thousand times ten thousand," and they cry, "Worthy is the Lamb who was slain" (Revelation 5:11–12). John then sees a great multitude from every nation that no one could number (Revelation 7:9). The countless host of heaven and the countless family God promised meet in one great act of worship, and the Lamb is at the center.

David's victories also point beyond David. The women's song praised the young warrior God had already anointed as king while Saul still sat on the throne. Jesus, the Son of David, won a victory over sin and death that no battlefield count could measure, and Revelation shows the heavenly myriads gathered around Him as the Lamb who was slain (Revelation 5:11–12). Revelation does not present those myriads as the fulfillment of the women's song. The link is a comparison drawn through David's kingship: the God who gave David his victories has given the Son of David the greater victory, and heaven's countless host now praises Him for it.

7. Spirit & Life

Believers are tempted to judge their situation by counting. We count the problems lined up against us, the resources we lack, and the people who oppose us. Psalm 3 does not ask us to stop counting the danger. It asks us to count God as well. David could sleep in the middle of a rebellion because the Lord who sustained him was not one more fighter on the scale. Leviticus 26:8 and Deuteronomy 32:30 make the same point from both directions. When God is with His people, their small numbers are no barrier. When He withdraws, their large numbers are no help.

The Song of Solomon gives the believer a different place to look. The psalms count myriads of enemies, while the Song looks at one person who outshines a myriad. Fear grows by multiplying, as one threat becomes ten and ten become ten thousand. But a single sight of Christ, chief among ten thousand, outweighs the whole list. This is an analogy drawn by placing the two passages side by side, and it is a sound one. The believer surrounded by many threats keeps watching the One who surpasses any crowd.

Micah protects this confidence from turning into a bargain. Because God works on such a vast scale, we may think we must answer Him with a vast amount of religious effort. Micah 6:7 takes that idea to its limit and shows that it fails. The God of the myriads asks for a humble walk with Him, not ten thousand rivers of oil. His power is beyond counting, and His welcome rests on grace. He has already made His people a myriad, and He invites each one of them to walk humbly with Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 24:60. Rebekah's family sends her away with a blessing for countless descendants. The blessing reaches toward God's promise of offspring like the stars, first given to Abraham.

  • Deuteronomy 33:17. Moses describes Joseph's tribes as a powerful ox pushing peoples with its horns, the ten thousands of Ephraim and the thousands of Manasseh. Their great numbers were part of their blessing.

  • Numbers 10:36. When the ark came to rest, Moses prayed for the Lord to return to the many thousands of Israel. God's presence among His people was the center of their life on the journey.

  • Judges 20:10. Israel chooses "a thousand out of every ten thousand" men to fetch provisions for the army. Here the word is a plain number counting the people assigned to that task.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G3461 (myrias), Greek. This is a distinct Greek word, not a Hebrew relative, though it carries the same idea of "myriad." It appears in Jude 14 for the saints who come with the Lord and in Revelation 5:11 for the angels around the throne. It continues the Hebrew picture of God's countless holy host into the New Testament.
