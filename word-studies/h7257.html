LIE DOWN, CROUCH, REST - (râbats, Strong's H7257)

Summary

The Hebrew verb râbats describes an animal folding its legs beneath it and settling to the ground. Sheep lie down in a pasture, a donkey sinks under a heavy load, a mother bird sits on her eggs, and a lion stretches out in its den. The posture is the same each time. Context decides whether it means safety, exhaustion, protection, menace, or royal ease. When the verb takes a causative form, it means "to make lie down," as a shepherd settles his flock.

Scripture builds large truths from this ordinary posture. Its first use pictures sin crouching at Cain's door like a beast waiting to spring. Its most loved use is David's confession that the Lord "makes me to lie down in green pastures" (Psalm 23:2). In Isaiah's vision of the Messiah's reign, the leopard lies down beside the young goat. Between these points the word traces the story of Scripture. Sin crouches, judgment settles on the proud, and the Shepherd-King brings His people to a rest that nothing can disturb.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Râbats belongs to the world of herders and the open country. Most of its uses describe four-legged animals, and that sets it apart from shâkab (H7901), the common verb for people lying down to sleep. The word fits naturally in the blessings of Genesis and Deuteronomy, in shepherd poetry such as Psalm 23 and the Song of Solomon, and in the prophets. Isaiah, Ezekiel, Jeremiah, and Zephaniah use it to picture both ruined cities and restored flocks.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to lie down or stretch out by bending the limbs underneath. The word describes the posture and nothing more. It does not carry "peace" or "danger" within itself, so whether the posture signals rest or threat depends on who is lying down and where.

1. To lie down or rest, used of animals and occasionally of people: flocks by a well (Genesis 29:2), the needy who lie down in safety (Isaiah 14:30).

2. To crouch or lie in wait, used of a predator or of something pictured as one: sin crouching at the door (Genesis 4:7), Pharaoh as a great monster lying in his rivers (Ezekiel 29:3).

3. To sit over and cover, used of a mother bird on her young or eggs (Deuteronomy 22:6).

4. In the causative form, to make lie down, used of a shepherd settling a flock (Psalm 23:2), and once by extension of setting stones in place (Isaiah 54:11).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes everything. A sheep lying down shows that it feels secure. Balaam's donkey "lay down under Balaam" (Numbers 22:27) because it refused to go on in the presence of the Angel of the LORD. In Exodus 23:5 an enemy's donkey lies helpless under its burden, and Israel is commanded to help it. Lions lie down in their dens when the sun rises (Psalm 104:22). The posture is the same, and the meaning differs with each animal.

One grammatical pattern matters. In the causative form, someone else brings about the lying down. Shepherds make their flocks lie down (Jeremiah 33:12). Babylon will be so desolate that no shepherd will make his flock lie down there (Isaiah 13:20). Most importantly, the Lord Himself makes His people lie down (Psalm 23:2; Ezekiel 34:15). That form moves the focus from the sheep's condition to the Shepherd's work.

4. Theological Significance

The uses of râbats show two kinds of crouching weight on the side of rebellion. Sin crouches at the door of the heart (Genesis 4:7). In Deuteronomy 29:20, every curse written in the book of the covenant will settle on the man who hears God's warnings yet blesses himself in his heart and assumes he will have peace. That curse settles on him as a beast settles over its prey. God's judgments are personal and they hold fast to the one who presumes on His mercy.

The prophets apply the same verb to proud empires. Babylon (Isaiah 13:20–21), the cities of Aroer (Isaiah 17:2), the fortified city of Isaiah 27:10, and Nineveh (Zephaniah 2:14) all end as places where flocks and wild animals lie down. Walls built to last forever become pasture and lairs. God rules the map of the earth, and human self-exaltation ends with sheep grazing in its throne rooms.

Against this the Lord reveals Himself as the One who gives rest. Sheep are prey animals, and lying down in good pasture pictures the rest and security the Shepherd provides. When Scripture says God makes His people lie down, it is saying that He has dealt with what threatened them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 4:7 — sin crouching at the door. The first use of the word is a warning to Cain. God has not accepted his offering, and Cain is angry. God asks whether he would not be accepted if he did well. Then He warns that if Cain does not do well, sin lies at the door, its desire is for him, and he must rule over it. The verb is an animal word, so sin appears as a living thing crouched at the threshold, patient and hungry. It waits at the entrance, the place where a decision turns into action, and it takes its position when worship has gone wrong and a heart resents God's correction. Cain does not rule over it, and in the next verse it springs. He kills his brother in the field. James 1:14–15 traces the same movement from desire to sin to death, and Peter warns that the devil prowls like a roaring lion (1 Peter 5:8).

Psalm 23:2 and Ezekiel 34:14–15 — the Shepherd who makes His flock lie down. David's words, "He makes me to lie down in green pastures," describe the result of the Shepherd's care. Ezekiel 34 shows what that care costs. Israel's shepherds fed themselves, ruled harshly, and let the sheep scatter and become food for wild beasts. The Lord answers by taking the work on Himself. He will feed His flock in good pasture and make them lie down. Rest for God's people does not come from failed human leaders or from the sheep's own effort. The Shepherd removes the threats and provides the pasture. Zephaniah 3:13 gives the same promise to the humble remnant, who will feed and lie down with no one to make them afraid.

Isaiah 11:6–7 — predator and prey at rest together. Isaiah pictures the reign of the Branch from Jesse's stem. The leopard lies down with the young goat, and the young of the cow and the bear lie down together. Elsewhere this verb describes a predator crouching to kill or a prey animal resting where no predator can reach it. Here both postures meet in the same place, and neither animal is a threat. The Messiah's reign goes further than restraining a violent creation. It heals the hostility itself.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jacob's blessing of Judah pictures a lion that bows down and lies down, and asks who would dare rouse him (Genesis 49:9). A resting lion is not vulnerable. It rests because nothing threatens it. Balaam later uses the same image for all Israel, with another Hebrew verb for lying down: "He bows down, he lies down as a lion; And as a lion, who shall rouse him?" (Numbers 24:9). The picture reaches its fulfillment in Revelation 5:5: "Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has prevailed." The Lion who rests in unchallenged authority is also the Shepherd who makes His flock lie down. Jesus calls Himself the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11). Ezekiel's promise that the Lord Himself would shepherd His flock comes true in a Shepherd who is both God and Man.

Isaiah 11 then opens onto the whole creation. Paul writes that creation "was subjected to futility" and "groans and labors with birth pangs together until now," waiting to be "delivered from the bondage of corruption" (Romans 8:20–22). Through Christ, God will "reconcile all things to Himself... having made peace through the blood of His cross" (Colossians 1:20). Isaiah's leopard and goat lying side by side show what that reconciliation will look like when it reaches the animal world.

The story ends with a building. To Jerusalem, "afflicted one, Tossed with tempest, and not comforted," God says, "I will lay your stones with colorful gems, And lay your foundations with sapphires" (Isaiah 54:11). The same God who allowed the city to be ruined now sets its stones down with His own hand. The word that began with sin crouching at a door ends with God laying a foundation that will not be moved.

7. Spirit & Life

Genesis 4:7 teaches that temptation is rarely far off. It waits at the threshold, the doorway where a resentful thought becomes a word or a deed. God did not tell Cain that sin was harmless or that it would leave. He told him to rule over it. This is mercy, a warning given before the door opens. A believer who feels anger rising after God's correction can recognize what is crouching at the door and turn to God before it springs.

Scripture also distinguishes between kinds of rest. Issachar is a strong donkey lying down between two burdens (Genesis 49:14). He saw that rest was good, bowed his shoulder, and became a forced laborer (49:15). That rest was bought by accepting a yoke to avoid a fight. The rest of Psalm 23 comes from the Shepherd who has already fought for His sheep. Many Christians settle for Issachar's bargain and give up freedom for comfort. The Lord offers something better, the rest of sheep whose Shepherd is awake.

Song of Solomon 1:7 adds one more detail. The bride asks where her beloved makes his flock "rest at noon," the hottest hour of the day. She wants to find him when the sun is fiercest instead of wandering among other flocks. The best rest is found in the Shepherd's presence while the heat is still on.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 23:5 — An enemy's donkey lying under its burden must be helped. Compassion reaches even an enemy's animal.

  • Deuteronomy 33:13 — Joseph's land is blessed with "the deep lying beneath," water stored underground for his fruitfulness.

  • Job 11:19 — Zophar promises the repentant man that he will lie down with no one to make him afraid. The phrase becomes a standard picture of security.

  • Jeremiah 33:12 — The desolate land will again have shepherds causing their flocks to lie down. Restoration is measured in quiet pastures.

  • Ezekiel 29:3 — Pharaoh is a great monster lying in the midst of his rivers. He claims the Nile as his own creation, and God judges that claim.

  • Zephaniah 2:7 — The remnant of Judah will lie down in the abandoned houses of Ashkelon.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • (a) Contrasting near-synonym: shâkab (H7901), "to lie down," used mainly of people resting or sleeping. Râbats is the animal posture (compare Genesis 49:9 with Numbers 24:9).

  • (b) Canonical pairing: tehôm (H8415), "the deep," appears with râbats in the blessing of "the deep that lies beneath" (Genesis 49:25; Deuteronomy 33:13).

10. Other Relevant Insights

Some scholars link Genesis 4:7 to an Akkadian word for a crouching demon at doorways. The connection is debated, and the verse's meaning does not depend on it. Not every use carries symbolic weight, either. The flocks lying by the well in Genesis 29:2 are simply sheep waiting for water.

Genesis 4:7 deliberately pictures sin as a crouching creature at the threshold of human action.

Lying down in safe pasture is Scripture's standing image for security under God's care (Psalm 23:2; Ezekiel 34:15; Zephaniah 3:13). Wild beasts lying down in ruined cities is a standard image of judgment (Isaiah 13:21; Zephaniah 2:14). The mother bird sitting over her young (Deuteronomy 22:6) belongs with the wider biblical picture of protective covering, such as God bearing Israel on eagles' wings and Jesus longing to gather Jerusalem as a hen gathers her chicks. That verse is a practical law about birds' nests, and it should not be read as an allegory.

Israel's neighbors often portrayed the deep as a chaotic monster. In Genesis 49:25 the deep "lies beneath" like an animal at rest, and it supplies water for Joseph's blessing. Scripture does not say this in so many words, but the picture fits its consistent witness that God rules the waters others feared, so that even the deep serves the fruitfulness of His people.
