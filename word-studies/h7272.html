FOOT, FEET - (regel, Strong's H7272)

Summary

The Hebrew word regel is the ordinary Old Testament word for "foot" or "leg." It names the feet that travelers washed, the feet that stood on holy ground, and the legs of furniture in the tabernacle. Because feet carry a person wherever they go, Scripture also uses the word for a person's walk and direction, for following someone, and even for counting "times." Feet run to evil or keep to God's path, slip in danger or stand on a rock. To fall at someone's feet is to plead or submit. To place an enemy under one's feet is to conquer him completely.

The word also leads to some of Scripture's largest pictures of God. The Almighty, whose throne is heaven, chose a place on earth as His "footstool," where His people could worship at His feet. He promised His anointed King that every enemy would be put beneath His feet. That promise reaches its goal in Jesus Christ, who washed His disciples' dusty feet and will reign until death itself lies under His feet. Along the way, the believer's walk is consecrated by sacrifice, kept from slipping by God, and sent out with good news.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Regel is one of the basic body words of Hebrew. It appears from Genesis to Malachi, in stories, laws, psalms, proverbs, and prophetic visions. In the ancient world people walked nearly everywhere on dusty roads in sandals. That made the feet a part of the body that was constantly dirty and tired, and it made foot-washing a basic courtesy a host offered a guest (Genesis 18:4; 24:32).

The feet were also where a person touched the ground. That simple fact explains much of the word's biblical range. Feet claim land, stand on holy ground, press down on a defeated enemy, and show which way a life is actually going.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The physical foot or leg of a person or animal, including the legs of objects such as the table of showbread (Exodus 25:26). This is by far the most common sense.

2. A step, walk, or pace, and by extension following someone. Jacob moves "at a pace" his children can endure (Genesis 33:14), and Abigail's maids "attended" her, literally going at her feet (1 Samuel 25:42).

3. "Times," meaning occurrences: "Three times you shall keep a feast to Me in the year" (Exodus 23:14). The same idiom counts the three times Balaam struck his donkey (Numbers 22:28).

4. A polite way of referring to bodily functions. Covering the feet means relieving oneself (Judges 3:24; 1 Samuel 24:3). This sense explains certain stories but carries no theological weight.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning shifts with the setting. In Proverbs and the Prophets, feet stand for conduct: "For their feet run to evil" (Proverbs 1:16; compare Isaiah 59:7). The wise are told to ponder the path of their feet and turn their foot away from evil (Proverbs 4:26–27). In these passages the feet carry out what the heart already wants. A person's real loves become visible in where they go.

Three common phrases change the sense:

  • "At the feet" expresses pleading or submission. Esther falls at the king's feet (Esther 8:3), and the Shunammite woman grabs Elisha's feet in her grief (2 Kings 4:27).

  • "Under the feet" expresses conquest (Psalm 47:3).

  • "Sole of the foot" often describes stepping onto land and taking possession of it (Deuteronomy 11:24).

The "times" sense in Exodus 23:14 deserves a note. That verse sits among commands for Israel's men to appear before the LORD at three yearly feasts (Exodus 23:17). Later Jewish usage named these the three pilgrimage feasts after this word. The idiom itself simply means "times," but in Exodus 23 those times were journeys made on foot to meet God.

4. Theological Significance

The feet passages reveal a God who is utterly above His creation, yet chooses to draw near to it. When He appears, Scripture often points to His feet. Beneath them the elders of Israel saw something like a sapphire pavement (Exodus 24:10), and "the clouds are the dust of His feet" (Nahum 1:3). By contrast, the psalmist says of the idols of the nations, "Feet they have, but they do not walk" (Psalm 115:7). A god who cannot move cannot come to rescue anyone. The living God comes, and He comes in judgment and in salvation.

These passages also show that God claims the whole of human life, including where we walk. He gives the land: "Every place that the sole of your foot will tread upon I have given you" (Joshua 1:3). Yet Israel had to step onto it. Caleb received the land "where your foot has trodden" because he had "wholly followed the LORD my God" (Joshua 14:9). God later warned that Israel's foot would stay in the land "only if they are careful to do all that I have commanded them" (2 Chronicles 33:8). The gift is God's, and faithful walking is how His people live in it. Even the land's water supply taught this. Moses contrasted Egypt, where Israel had sown seed and "watered it by foot, as a vegetable garden" (Deuteronomy 11:10), with the promised land, which "drinks water from the rain of heaven" and on which the LORD's eyes rest from the beginning of the year to its end (Deuteronomy 11:11–12). In Egypt, water reached the fields through human effort. In the promised land, it came as God sent the rain. God's gift meant depending on Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

God's footstool (1 Chronicles 28:2; Psalms 99:5; 132:7; Isaiah 66:1; Ezekiel 43:7). David wanted to build "a house of rest for the ark of the covenant of the LORD, and for the footstool of our God" (1 Chronicles 28:2). The ark and God's footstool stand together in David's description of the planned house. The verse does not spell out whether the footstool is the ark itself or the sanctuary that would hold it, but the picture is clear: the King enthroned in heaven rests His feet among His people in Jerusalem. Isaiah 66:1 widens the picture: heaven is God's throne and the whole earth is His footstool, so no human building can hold Him. Even so, God told Ezekiel that the temple was the place of the soles of His feet, where He would dwell among Israel forever (Ezekiel 43:7). The God whom no building can contain still chooses to come near His people. That is why the psalmist can say, "Exalt the LORD our God, And worship at His footstool— He is holy" (Psalm 99:5). Worship at God's footstool is both close and lowly. Being near God is not the same as being safe in sin, though. Lamentations 2:1 grieves that in His anger the Lord did not remember His footstool.

Feet on the necks of kings (Joshua 10:24; Psalms 8:6; 110:1). After the victory at Gibeon, Joshua had his captains put their feet on the necks of five captured kings. He then explained the act: this is what the LORD will do to all your enemies (Joshua 10:25). One scene from a battlefield in Canaan became a promise. Psalm 8:6 speaks of humanity's God-given rule: "You have put all things under his feet." Psalm 110:1 lifts the promise to David's Lord: "Sit at My right hand, Till I make Your enemies Your footstool." The image remains the same, but the scale grows from five kings to every enemy of God's King.

Blood on the right foot (Leviticus 8:23–24; 14:14–17). Two different ceremonies share the same three marks. At Aaron's ordination as priest, Moses put the sacrifice's blood on the tip of Aaron's right ear, on the thumb of his right hand, and on "the big toe of his right foot" (Leviticus 8:23), and then did the same for Aaron's sons (Leviticus 8:24). Leviticus 14 describes a different occasion: the cleansing of a leper whose disease had been healed and who was being restored to the community after living as an outcast. The priest put the blood of the trespass offering on the same three places, and then put oil on them, "on the blood of the trespass offering" (Leviticus 14:14, 17). One rite set a man apart for priestly service. The other brought an outcast back in. Leviticus does not explain the three marks, but an illuminating reading sees the whole person in them: the ear that hears, the hand that acts, and the foot that walks. Read that way, the new priest and the restored outcast both had their hearing, their doing, and their walk claimed by a sacrifice before they came into God's presence. Later Scripture connects anointing oil with the Spirit, and that connection fits the order in Leviticus 14: first the blood, then the oil.

Holy ground (Exodus 3:5; Joshua 5:15; Ecclesiastes 5:1). At the burning bush, and again before the Commander of the LORD's army, God's servants were told to remove the sandals from their feet. The ground was holy because God was present there. Taking off sandals likely meant setting aside ordinary protection and the dust of common roads. Solomon gives every worshiper a related warning. "Walk prudently when you go to the house of God" literally means guard your feet (Ecclesiastes 5:1). The way a person approaches God matters.

Abigail at David's feet (1 Samuel 25:24, 41). Abigail fell at David's feet to plead for her household. When David asked her to become his wife, she answered that she would be a servant to wash the feet of his servants (1 Samuel 25:41). Washing a guest's feet was already lowly work. Abigail offered to wash the feet of the lowest men in David's household. Her words show how low a person could go in service, and they offer a striking comparison with what the Son of David would later do.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament uses a different Greek word for "foot," but it takes up these Hebrew pictures directly. Psalm 110:1 and Psalm 8:6 become central texts for Christ's reign: "For He must reign till He has put all enemies under His feet. The last enemy that will be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:25–26). Hebrews 2:8 admits honestly, "But now we do not yet see all things put under him." It then points to Jesus, who is already crowned. Paul even includes the church in the victory: "the God of peace will crush Satan under your feet shortly" (Romans 16:20). Paul's wording echoes the promise first given in Eden, that the serpent's head would be crushed (Genesis 3:15).

Isaiah's herald fits the same story. "How beautiful upon the mountains Are the feet of him who brings good news... Who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'" (Isaiah 52:7; compare Nahum 1:15). Feet were dusty and lowly, yet these feet are called beautiful because of the message they bring. Paul applies the verse to everyone sent to preach the gospel (Romans 10:15).

The deepest surprise is that the Lord whose footstool is the earth took on human feet. Psalm 22:16 speaks of pierced hands and feet, and the risen Jesus showed His disciples His hands and feet (Luke 24:39–40). A woman washed His feet with her tears (Luke 7:38). Then He knelt and washed His disciples' feet (John 13:4–14). Abigail offered to wash the feet of servants; the King Himself did the washing. Zechariah 14:4 foresees the LORD's feet standing on the Mount of Olives. Jesus ascended from that same mountain, with the promise that He would return in the same way (Acts 1:11–12). Acts does not quote Zechariah, but the setting points readers in that direction.

7. Spirit & Life

The believer's walk has three sides, and these passages hold them together. We worship at His feet, in humility and nearness. We guard our feet, because where we go shows what we love. And we depend on Him to keep our feet, because we cannot keep them ourselves.

That third truth carries great comfort. Asaph confessed, "my feet had almost stumbled; My steps had nearly slipped" (Psalm 73:2). He had looked at the prosperity of the wicked until he nearly lost his footing. The psalms never pretend the path is safe. Yet God "set my feet upon a rock, And established my steps" (Psalm 40:2). He "shall pluck my feet out of the net" (Psalm 25:15). He "makes my feet like the feet of deer, And sets me on my high places" (Psalm 18:33). God does not only lift His people out of danger and set them down. He steadies them so they can keep climbing on steep and rocky ground. "He will not allow your foot to be moved; He who keeps you will not slumber" (Psalm 121:3). His word is a lamp to our feet (Psalm 119:105), and when Ezekiel lay face down, the Spirit entered him and set him on his feet (Ezekiel 2:2).

That steadied walk is meant to carry something to others. Feet consecrated by blood and kept by grace are the feet God sends out with good news.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joshua 3:15 — The Jordan stopped flowing when the priests' feet dipped into the edge of the water (Joshua 3:15–16). God's provision met His people as they stepped forward in obedience.

  • Deuteronomy 8:4 — During forty years in the wilderness, Israel's feet did not swell. God cared even for the physical toll of the journey.

  • Judges 1:6–7 — Adoni-Bezek lost his thumbs and big toes, the same punishment he had inflicted on seventy kings. He admitted that God had repaid him for what he had done.

  • Song of Solomon 5:3 — "I have washed my feet; how can I defile them?" Washed feet belonged to someone who was home for the night. The bride's reluctance to get up is the hesitation that delays love.

  • 2 Samuel 9:13 — David sent for Mephibosheth, showed him kindness for Jonathan's sake, and gave him a place at his table (2 Samuel 9:5–7). The chapter ends by noting that he ate continually at the king's table and "was lame in both his feet."
