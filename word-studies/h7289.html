VEIL, ROBES - (râdîyd, Strong's H7289)

Summary

The Hebrew word râdîyd names a woman's wide outer wrap: a large veil, shawl, or light cloak that covered her head and shoulders. It appears only twice in the Old Testament. The NKJV translates it "veil" in Song of Solomon 5:7 and "robes" in Isaiah 3:23. A garment like this gave the woman who wore it covering and protection in public.

Both times the word appears, the garment is being taken away, but the two scenes are opposites. In the Song, the city watchmen beat a woman searching for the one she loves and tear the veil from her. That is an injustice against someone faithful. In Isaiah, the Lord Himself strips the proud daughters of Zion of their luxurious robes, and that is righteous judgment on arrogance. Together the two passages raise a searching question: which covering are we holding on to? God removes the finery of pride. A person who follows Him with a whole heart may also lose the world's approval along the way, and Christ bore that same reproach before us.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Râdîyd is a rare Hebrew noun. It appears once in love poetry (Song of Solomon) and once in prophetic judgment (Isaiah). Strong's connects it to the verb râdad, which means to spread out or beat out. That link fits a garment wide enough to drape over the head and shoulders, though the word's meaning comes from how it is used, not from its root. Because it is so rare, its exact cut and style are uncertain. What is clear is that it was a woman's outer covering, and having it torn away in public would have been humiliating.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Râdîyd has one meaning: a woman's large outer wrap, such as a veil, shawl, or mantle. The NKJV's two renderings, "veil" and "robes," describe the same kind of garment from different angles. It was a covering that could be worn over the head and could also be part of fine dress. The word itself carries no symbolic meaning. Its significance comes from what happens to it in the two passages.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What changes between the two uses is who removes the garment and why. In Song of Solomon 5:7, the woman's veil is taken by "the keepers of the walls," men whose job was to guard the city. In Isaiah 3:23, the robes are taken by the Lord as the last item in a long list of luxury goods (3:18–23).

Isaiah 3:19 also mentions "veils," but that is a different Hebrew word. The robes of 3:23 belong to a broader category of costly outer garments, listed alongside "the mirrors," "the fine linen," and "the turbans." The two settings also differ in tone. One garment is the ordinary covering of a vulnerable woman. The other is part of a wardrobe of display.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, the two passages show that covering and exposure belong to God's moral world. Some coverings are rightly honored and some are rightly removed. When the veil is torn from an innocent seeker, Scripture calls it wounding: "They struck me, they wounded me" (Song 5:7). When proud robes are torn from the arrogant, God calls it justice.

Isaiah's scene fits a larger prophetic pattern. The prophets repeatedly picture the judgment of a proud city or nation as a woman being stripped. Babylon is told to "Remove your veil, take off the skirt" (Isaiah 47:2). Unfaithful Jerusalem is left "naked and bare" (Ezekiel 16:39). The Lord says to Nineveh, "I will show the nations your nakedness, and the kingdoms your shame" (Nahum 3:5). Each of these passages uses different Hebrew words. Still, they share the meaning of Isaiah 3: to be stripped is to be shown powerless before a greater Power. The proud cannot keep their coverings when God decides to expose them.

This pattern goes back to Eden. After their sin, Adam and Eve "sewed fig leaves together and made themselves coverings" (Genesis 3:7). God then clothed them Himself with garments of skin (Genesis 3:21). From the beginning, people have tried to cover their own shame, and from the beginning, God has provided the covering they could not make.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:6–8 — the veil torn from a faithful seeker. The scene begins with a failure. The bride was slow to open when her beloved knocked (5:2–5). When she finally opens, "my beloved had turned away and was gone... I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer" (5:6). She goes out into the city at night to look for him. The watchmen who should protect her instead strike her, wound her, and take her veil away. Earlier in the Song she met the watchmen and passed by unharmed (3:3). This second meeting is darker.

The Song's own details make the humiliation plain. The watchmen strike her and wound her, and then the keepers of the walls forcibly take her outer covering away from her in the city at night (5:7). Genesis offers a related scene. When Rebekah learned that the man walking toward her was Isaac, she "took a veil and covered herself" (Genesis 24:65). There a woman covers herself as she meets the man she will marry; here a woman seeking her beloved has her covering torn from her. The poem reports what the watchmen did without telling us why they did it. Whatever their reason, the guardians of the city beat and expose a woman whose heart belongs to her beloved.

Her response is the striking part. She does not go home to recover her dignity. She turns to the daughters of Jerusalem and says, "If you find my beloved, that you tell him I am lovesick!" (5:8). Losing her veil does not end her search. It only makes plain how much she wants him.

Isaiah 3:16–24 — the robes stripped from proud women. The Lord names the sin first: "the daughters of Zion are haughty, and walk with outstretched necks and wanton eyes" (3:16). Then He lists their wardrobe item by item, from anklets and scarves to perfume boxes and nose jewels, and ends with "the fine linen, the turbans, and the robes" (3:23). None of these things is evil in itself. In Isaiah's charge they had become tools of haughty self-display. The setting makes this worse: just before this passage, the Lord charges Jerusalem's elders and princes with crushing His people and grinding the faces of the poor (3:14–15), and the daughters of Zion parade their finery in that same city. God's response reverses everything they relied on: "Instead of a sweet smell there will be a stench... Instead of a rich robe, a girding of sackcloth; and branding instead of beauty" (3:24). Sackcloth was the clothing of mourning and repentance. Stripped of their robes, the women are left to face what their finery had covered.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of coverings reaches its center in Jesus. He is the faithful One rejected by those who should have recognized Him. The chief priests, the guardians of Israel's religious life, handed Him over to Pilate (John 18:35). Pilate had Him scourged, the soldiers struck Him (John 19:1–3), and they stripped Him (Matthew 27:28). Isaiah had spoken of the Servant who said, "I gave My back to those who struck Me," and "I did not hide My face from shame and spitting" (Isaiah 50:6). At the cross the soldiers divided His garments among themselves (John 19:23–24), and John says this fulfilled the Scripture of Psalm 22:18: "They divide My garments among them, And for My clothing they cast lots." The Song does not prophesy the cross. Even so, the picture of a devoted one wounded and uncovered by the keepers of the walls finds a striking parallel in Christ, who "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Hebrews 12:2).

Because Christ was uncovered, sinners can be clothed. God's provision of skins in Eden points toward the robe of righteousness He gives (Isaiah 61:10) and the white garments Christ offers those who know they are naked (Revelation 3:17–18). Isaiah's stripping of proud robes was never the end of God's purpose for Zion. The next chapter, which follows the stripping of the daughters of Zion, promises a cleansed people sheltered by the LORD's cloud by day and fire by night: "For over all the glory there will be a covering" (Isaiah 4:4–6).

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 30:1, from a different setting, sheds light on the heart behind the robes of Isaiah 3. The Lord pronounces woe on those "who devise plans, but not of My Spirit." The Hebrew phrase behind "devise plans" can also be read as weaving or casting a covering, which is how older translations render it. Read that way, the verse describes God's people making a covering that does not come from His Spirit. The daughters of Zion wore one woven of luxury and status. We are tempted to weave ours from competence, reputation, or religious respectability. When the Lord removes such coverings, it can be an act of mercy, because a person who still trusts in fig leaves will not ask for the garment God provides. When He strips away something we leaned on, the right response is the sackcloth of Isaiah 3:24 rather than a scramble for new finery.

The Song shows the other kind of stripping. Someone who has been emptied of pride and seeks the Lord with her whole heart may find that the "watchmen" do not understand her. Respectable people may treat her as foolish or extreme. Hebrews names this calling directly: "Therefore let us go forth to Him, outside the camp, bearing His reproach" (Hebrews 13:13). The Shulamite's torn veil is a picture of what that can cost. Her lovesick words show what keeps a seeker going. She wanted her beloved more than her reputation.

Sooner or later, a believer is likely to lose one covering or the other. We can hold on to proud finery until God takes it away, or we can let Him remove it and then follow Christ willingly, even when the world's respect is the cost. Either way, the covering that finally matters is the one He gives.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Râdîyd occurs only in Song of Solomon 5:7 and Isaiah 3:23, and both are discussed above. Other passages cited in this study, including Genesis 24:65, Isaiah 47:2, Ezekiel 16:39, and Nahum 3:5, use different Hebrew words for veils and garments. They provide related imagery but are not occurrences of this word.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7286 (râdad) — Lexical relative. Strong's identifies this verb, meaning to spread out, beat out, or subdue, as the source of râdîyd. It is used of gold beaten over carved doors (1 Kings 6:32).
