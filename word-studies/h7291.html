PURSUE, CHASE, PERSECUTE, FOLLOW - (râdaph, Strong's H7291)

Summary

Râdaph is the Hebrew verb for running after something in order to catch it. It describes armies chasing a beaten enemy, a hunter tracking game, a hostile man hounding the innocent, and a person who gives himself wholeheartedly to a goal. Most often the word sounds dangerous. Pharaoh's chariots pursue Israel to the sea, Saul hunts David through the wilderness, and the psalmists cry out against those who persecute them.

That background gives Psalm 23:6 its force. David, who knew what it was to be hunted, uses this same verb for God: "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me / All the days of my life." Scripture also turns the word toward the believer's own devotion, calling God's people to pursue justice, peace, righteousness, and the knowledge of the LORD. It warns that those who chase sin and idols end up pursued themselves. Every life is running after something, and râdaph shows that what a person chases has a great deal to do with what comes after them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Râdaph is a common Old Testament verb found from Genesis to Nahum. It is most at home in the battle narratives of Joshua, Judges, and Samuel, where one side breaks and the other runs it down. It is also prominent in the Psalms and Lamentations, where it becomes the language of persecution. Proverbs and the Prophets use it for moral and spiritual pursuit. The NKJV renders it with a range of English words, including "pursue," "chase," "follow," "persecute," "hunt," and, in one unusual verse, "past."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, râdaph means to go after someone or something with the aim of overtaking it. It implies sustained, purposeful effort. Context determines whether the pursuit is hostile, loyal, or moral.

1. To chase or pursue physically, especially a fleeing enemy or fugitive. The Egyptians "pursued them" and "overtook them camping by the sea" (Exodus 14:9).

2. To persecute, hounding a person with hostile intent. David prays, "Save me from all those who persecute me" (Psalm 7:1).

3. To strive after or eagerly follow a goal or way of life. "You shall follow what is altogether just" (Deuteronomy 16:20); "Seek peace and pursue it" (Psalm 34:14).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject and object decide the tone. When armies are the subject, the word means military pursuit. Joshua's feigned retreat at Ai works because the city's men are "called together to pursue them" and are "drawn away from the city" (Joshua 8:16). When the object is a righteous person, the NKJV usually shifts to "persecute," a cluster especially dense in Psalm 119. When the object is righteousness, peace, or the knowledge of God, the verb describes devotion. When the subject is something other than a person, such as disease, curses, evil, or goodness, these realities are pictured as pursuers in their own right.

One striking use is Ecclesiastes 3:15: "God requires an account of what is past." The phrase translated "what is past" uses râdaph and refers to something pursued or driven away. The Preacher pictures time as constantly slipping out of human reach, with days chased off and gone beyond recovery. What vanishes from us does not vanish from God. He seeks out and calls to account what we can no longer get back.

4. Theological Significance

Râdaph shows that God is sovereign over every pursuit. At the Red Sea, God Himself says, "I will harden Pharaoh's heart, so that he will pursue them; and I will gain honor over Pharaoh" (Exodus 14:4). Even the enemy's chase serves God's purpose to make Himself known.

The word also reveals a moral order. God's promises in Leviticus 26 picture obedient Israel as the pursuer: "Five of you shall chase a hundred" (26:8). For the disobedient the picture reverses. Enemies pursue them, and diseases and the sword "shall pursue you until you perish" (Deuteronomy 28:22). Proverbs condenses this into a principle: "Evil pursues sinners" (Proverbs 13:21). Sin is not a dead end that a person simply walks away from. It turns around and follows.

Finally, the word shows that God wants His people to be wholehearted. Justice, peace, and righteousness do not drift into a life by accident. Deuteronomy 16:20 attaches life and inheritance to following "what is altogether just," and Proverbs 15:9 says the LORD "loves him who follows righteousness."

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Samuel 26 and Psalm 23:6: the hunted man and the pursuing mercy. In 1 Samuel 26, David calls out to Saul across the valley: "Why does my lord thus pursue his servant?" (26:18). He describes himself as a flea and Saul as one who "hunts a partridge in the mountains" (26:20). Both verbs are râdaph. David knew from years of experience what this word felt like: hiding in caves, sleeping lightly, and never knowing how close the king's men were.

The same man writes that "goodness and mercy shall follow me / All the days of my life" (Psalm 23:6). The verb is again râdaph. The word behind "mercy" is chesed, God's loyal, faithful love. David takes the vocabulary of his worst years and turns it around. Something is still on his trail every day, but now it is God's goodness and steadfast love. The psalm has already set a table "in the presence of my enemies" (23:5). Enemies are real, but they are not the only pursuers, and they are not the ones who will catch him. God's mercy will overtake him and bring him home to "dwell in the house of the LORD / Forever."

Leviticus 26:36–37: the terror of an empty chase. Among the warnings for persistent rebellion, God says He will send faintness into the hearts of those scattered among their enemies. They will run at "the sound of a shaken leaf," and "they shall fall when no one pursues" (26:36). Verse 37 repeats the image: they stumble over one another as though fleeing the sword, though no one is pursuing. The judgment is not only outward defeat. Guilt itself becomes the pursuer. Proverbs 28:1 makes the same observation in general form: the wicked flee when no one pursues, but the righteous are bold as a lion.

Hosea 2:7; 8:3; 12:1: chasing the wind. Hosea pictures Israel as an unfaithful wife who will chase her lovers but not overtake them. Only then will she think of returning to her first husband (2:7). Ephraim feeds on wind and pursues the east wind (12:1), chasing alliances and idols that give nothing back. The result is announced in 8:3: Israel has rejected the good, and the enemy will pursue him. The movement is clear. The people who ran after emptiness become the ones being run down. The good Israel rejected in Hosea 8:3 is the same goodness that pursues David in Psalm 23:6.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The psalms of persecution form a recognizable pattern: the righteous person is hunted without cause and entrusts himself to God. Psalm 69 is one of these, and it lays its charge against those who "persecute" the one God has struck (69:26). The New Testament reads this psalm in light of Jesus and His betrayal (John 15:25; Acts 1:20). Jesus is the righteous sufferer the pattern anticipated. He was hunted from His infancy in Bethlehem to the cross. In John 15:20 He told His disciples that the world which persecuted Him would persecute them, and in Matthew 5:10–12 He pronounced the persecuted blessed. Those who suffer for Him are not alone in it. They stand where He has already stood.

The call to pursue also carries into the New Testament. Peter quotes Psalm 34 directly, telling believers to seek peace and pursue it (1 Peter 3:11). Paul, who once pursued and persecuted the church, later describes his Christian life as pressing on toward the prize of God's upward call in Christ (Philippians 3:6, 12–14). The man who hunted believers became a man consumed with chasing Christ. Behind it all stands the Good Shepherd, who "gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). He fulfills what Psalm 23 promised: a goodness that does not wait at a distance but comes after His sheep.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 23:6 is written for believers who feel hunted, whether by illness, by people who wish them harm, by the memory of failure, or by fear. David did not deny that he had pursuers. He simply knew that God's goodness and mercy were also on his trail and would not lose it. A believer can look back over the worst stretch of life and see that God's steadfast love never fell behind.

Proverbs 21:21 adds a quiet symmetry: "He who follows righteousness and mercy / Finds life, righteousness, and honor." The one who pursues chesed in Proverbs is the one whom chesed pursues in Psalm 23. Our pursuit of God is a response to a God who first came after us. Hosea 6:3 joins the two: "Let us pursue the knowledge of the LORD," followed immediately by the promise that "He will come to us like the rain." Knowing God takes the effort of a chase, but the One being sought is already on His way.

That leaves an honest question for self-examination: what am I running after? A life spent chasing the east wind ends in fear of rustling leaves. A life spent chasing peace, righteousness, and the knowledge of the LORD is followed home by goodness and mercy.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 14:14–15 — Abram arms his trained servants and goes in pursuit of Lot's captors as far as Dan. This is the first pursuit in Scripture, and it is a rescue.

  • Joshua 20:5 — The avenger of blood pursues the manslayer, but the city of refuge must not hand him over. God provided shelter from a lawful but deadly pursuit.

  • 1 Samuel 30:8 — David asks the LORD whether he should pursue the raiders of Ziklag and is told to pursue, for he will surely recover everything. Here the pursuit is undertaken at God's word.

  • Psalm 119:161 — The psalmist says princes persecute him without a cause, yet his heart stands in awe of God's word. Persecution drives him deeper into Scripture.

  • Isaiah 51:1 — God addresses those who follow after righteousness and seek the LORD, and calls them to look to Abraham, the rock from which they were hewn.

  • Lamentations 1:3 — Judah's persecutors overtake her in her distress. The warnings of Leviticus and Deuteronomy have come true.
