BOLD, PLEAD, OVERCOME, INSOLENT - (râhab, Strong's H7292)

Summary

The Hebrew verb râhab describes pressing forward with strong, surging force. It means acting boldly, pressing hard, overwhelming someone, or pushing past boundaries that should be honored. This word appears only four times in the Old Testament. In each place the NKJV chooses a different English word: "bold," "Plead," "overcome," and "insolent." All four share the same energy of force pressing against resistance. Context decides whether that force is good or destructive.

Those four verses form a small but searching portrait of human intensity. In Isaiah 3:5 the force turns ugly: "The child will be insolent toward the elder" as a society under judgment comes apart. In Proverbs 6:3 the same force becomes urgent pleading that rescues a person from a foolish pledge. In Song of Solomon 6:5 it describes the overwhelming effect of a bride's eyes on the one who loves her. In Psalm 138:3 God Himself supplies it: "You answered me, and made me bold with strength in my soul." God does not ask His people to become passive. He takes the force of the human heart away from pride and turns it toward humility, love, and courage.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Râhab belongs to Hebrew poetry and wisdom writing. It occurs once each in Psalms, Proverbs, Song of Solomon, and Isaiah, and nowhere in the Old Testament's narrative books.

The word belongs to a family that includes the noun Rahab. In poetry, that noun names a proud, monstrous power of the sea, and it also serves as a name for Egypt. Isaiah 51:9 asks the LORD's arm, "Are You not the arm that cut Rahab apart, And wounded the serpent?" Psalm 87:4 uses Rahab as a name for Egypt among the nations. This Rahab is unrelated to Rahab of Jericho, whose name is spelled differently in Hebrew. The noun's associations with bluster and arrogance help explain the verb's sense of swelling, pressing force. Even so, each of the verb's four uses still has to be read in its own context.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, râhab means to act with storming, assertive force: to press, to overpower, or to push against something. Three related senses appear:

1. To press hard on someone with an urgent request, to plead without letting up. Proverbs 6:3 tells a man trapped by a rash pledge, "Plead with your friend."

2. To overwhelm or to make someone bold. In this sense, one person causes a forceful effect in another. In Psalm 138:3 God "made me bold." In Song of Solomon 6:5 the bride's eyes "have overcome me."

3. To act insolently, pushing aggressively against someone owed respect. Isaiah 3:5 describes the child who is "insolent toward the elder."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The direction of the force decides the moral meaning. In Isaiah, the force is aimed against people who deserve honor. In Proverbs, it is aimed at a friend, as someone who has pledged himself for another person appeals urgently to be set free. In the Psalm and the Song, the force comes from someone else and works on the speaker.

One grammatical pattern matters. In Psalm 138:3 and Song of Solomon 6:5, the speaker receives the force instead of producing it. The NKJV shows this clearly with "made me bold" and "have overcome me." The psalmist does not gather up his own courage. God gives it. The bridegroom does not decide to be moved. Her gaze moves him. In Proverbs 6:3 the word is a command, which makes the urgency a matter of instruction: do this now.

4. Theological Significance

These four verses show that God cares about the direction of human intensity, and that He judges its misuse.

Scripture never treats forcefulness as evil in itself. The same word can describe rebellion, wise urgency, love's power, and God-given courage. The difference lies in what the force serves. When it serves pride, it tramples the honor God built into families and communities. When it serves wisdom, it humbles itself to get free. When it comes from God, it strengthens a person to stand and praise.

The family connection to the noun Rahab sharpens this picture. Psalm 89:10 says to God, "You have broken Rahab in pieces, as one who is slain." The arrogant, swelling power that Scripture pictures as a sea monster is something God conquers. He opposes swaggering pride, yet He is also the One who makes a weak person bold. God crushes the arrogance and gives the boldness.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 3:5 — insolence as a sign of judgment. Isaiah 3 opens with the LORD taking away from Jerusalem "the whole supply of bread and the whole supply of water." He also removes "the mighty man and the man of war, The judge and the prophet... the elder" (vv. 1–2). In their place, "I will give children to be their princes" (v. 4). The result follows in verse 5: people oppress one another, "The child will be insolent toward the elder, And the base toward the honorable." Insolence here is a symptom of judgment as well as a sin. When God withdraws the people who held a society together, raw human force rushes into the gap and pushes over every boundary of honor.

Proverbs 6:3 — humble yourself and press hard. The father speaks to a son who has become "surety for your friend" and has "shaken hands in pledge for a stranger." He tells him plainly, "You are snared by the words of your mouth" (vv. 1–2). The son has put himself "into the hand of your friend," so the father's counsel sounds like a paradox: "Go and humble yourself; Plead with your friend" (v. 3). The son must swallow his pride and press his appeal with relentless urgency at the same time. The passage does not explain exactly how the release will come, but it leaves no doubt about the urgency: "Give no sleep to your eyes" (v. 4), and "Deliver yourself like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter" (v. 5). Pride would rather stay trapped than ask. Wisdom bows low and keeps pleading until it is free.

Song of Solomon 6:5 — overwhelmed by love. "Turn your eyes away from me, For they have overcome me." The man who speaks is not weak. He is undone by the gaze of the woman he loves. In Scripture, love at its fullest is not mild. It has force enough to overpower the one who receives it. Many readers in the church have heard in this Song an echo of the Lord's delight in His people. That reading is a canonical resonance rather than a stated fulfillment, but it fits a Lord who rejoices over His people.

Psalm 138:3 — boldness given in answer to prayer. David begins, "Before the gods I will sing praises to You" (v. 1). He praises God in the presence of rival powers, whether those are false gods or earthly rulers who claim authority. Then he gives the reason for his confidence: "In the day when I cried out, You answered me, And made me bold with strength in my soul." God's answer does not say the threat vanished. What changed was the man inside. God put strength into his soul, and that strength made him bold enough to praise openly where others might have kept quiet.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Acts 4 records a prayer and an answer that closely resemble Psalm 138:3. Threatened by the authorities, the believers prayed together, quoting Psalm 2 as they did, and asked, "Now, Lord, look on their threats, and grant to Your servants that with all boldness they may speak Your word" (v. 29). God answered: "they were all filled with the Holy Spirit, and they spoke the word of God with boldness" (v. 31). Luke writes in Greek and uses a different word, so Acts 4 is neither an occurrence of râhab nor a quotation of Psalm 138. The pattern is the same, though. God's people cry out under threat, God answers, and He fills them with boldness to speak before hostile powers. In Christ, the gift David received becomes the shared inheritance of the Spirit-filled church.

The larger story also moves from Rahab defeated to the arm of the LORD revealed. Isaiah 51:9 recalls the arm that "cut Rahab apart." Two chapters later, Isaiah asks to whom that arm has been revealed (53:1), and he answers with the suffering Servant. God's final victory over swelling pride comes through a Servant who humbled Himself.

Proverbs 6 points in the same direction by contrast. A foolish man must humble himself and plead urgently to get free of a pledge he never should have made. Christ willingly became "surety of a better covenant" (Hebrews 7:22). He took on the debt that others could not pay, and He did not ask to be released from it.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers assume that holiness means having little intensity. These four verses suggest something else. God's concern is the direction of our force, so He does not simply flatten it. The same energy that makes a child insolent toward an elder can, when brought low before God, become the pleading of Proverbs 6, the devotion of the Song, and the courage of Psalm 138.

Proverbs 6 says something pointed to anyone caught in a foolish commitment, a sinful habit, or a mess of their own making. Getting free usually takes two things pride hates: humbling yourself and asking hard, more than once. Sleeplessness, in that passage, is wisdom.

Psalm 138 speaks to the fearful. Boldness is not a personality type reserved for strong people. It is something God gives "in the day when I cried out." The order matters. David cried out first, and the strength came afterward. Believers who feel timid before hostile voices can ask for what David received and what the church in Acts received.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Râhab occurs only four times, and all four are developed in Section 5: Psalm 138:3, Proverbs 6:3, Song of Solomon 6:5, and Isaiah 3:5. No further occurrences remain to list.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7293 / H7294 (rahab, Rahab): Lexical relatives. These nouns carry the sense of pride and bluster and serve as a poetic name for a proud sea power and for Egypt (Psalm 87:4; Psalm 89:10; Isaiah 51:9).

  • Canonical parallel (Greek, different word): Acts 4:29–31 presents boldness given by God in answer to prayer, which closely echoes Psalm 138:3. This is a thematic connection, not a lexical one.
