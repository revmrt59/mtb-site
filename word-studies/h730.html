CEDAR - (ʼerez, Strong's H0730)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼerez means "cedar." In Scripture it almost always refers to the great cedars of Lebanon, which grew tall, rooted deep, stayed green through the year, and produced fragrant wood that resisted rot. Kings prized cedar as one of the finest timbers. David lived in "a house of cedar," Solomon lined the Temple with it until "there was no stone to be seen," and the priests of Israel used a piece of cedar wood when they cleansed a healed leper.

Because the cedar stood so tall above the trees around it, the prophets and poets used it to picture greatness. Sometimes that greatness is proud and doomed. God's voice splinters the cedars, and the day of the LORD falls on every cedar "high and lifted up." Sometimes it is a gift. The righteous grow like a cedar in Lebanon. In Ezekiel 17, God Himself takes a tender twig from the top of the cedar and plants it until it becomes a majestic tree where birds of every kind find shelter. That promise points to Jesus Christ, the King from David's line whom God planted and whose kingdom gives the nations a home.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The cedar of Lebanon grew on the high mountains north of Israel. Israel had nothing like it, so cedar had to be cut by skilled Phoenician woodsmen, floated down the coast in rafts, and hauled inland (1 Kings 5:6–10). Moving timber that way cost a great deal of money and labor, and that cost helps explain why cedar became a sign of royal wealth. Almost every one of the word's appearances falls into one of four settings: building palaces and the Temple, priestly cleansing rites, prophetic pictures of rulers and nations, and poetry about beauty and flourishing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʼerez names one kind of tree and the wood it produces.

1. The living cedar tree, usually the cedar of Lebanon: "The cedars of Lebanon which He planted" (Psalm 104:16).

2. Cedar timber or wood used for beams, boards, pillars, or ritual: "he paneled the temple with beams and boards of cedar" (1 Kings 6:9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The largest group of occurrences describes construction. Cedar went into David's palace (2 Samuel 5:11), Solomon's Temple and royal buildings (1 Kings 6–7), and later the rebuilt Temple (Ezra 3:7). In the Law, a small piece of "cedar wood" appears beside scarlet and hyssop in rites of cleansing (Leviticus 14:4; Numbers 19:6).

In parables and prophecy, the cedar stands for the noble and the powerful. In Jotham's parable the bramble threatens to "devour the cedars of Lebanon" (Judges 9:15). King Jehoash casts himself as a cedar and his rival as a mere thistle (2 Kings 14:9). Ezekiel spells the image out: "Indeed Assyria was a cedar in Lebanon" (Ezekiel 31:3). The same picture can also describe health and blessing. Balaam sees Israel's camp spread out "Like cedars beside the waters" (Numbers 24:6). That picture is striking because the cedar is a mountain tree. Balaam sees it well watered, and the image shows a people richly supplied and flourishing.

4. Theological Significance

The cedar first belongs to God. Psalm 104:16 calls the cedars "The trees of the LORD," trees "which He planted" and keep full of sap without any human gardener. The tree that towered over Israel's world was God's own planting.

For that reason, Scripture treats a cedar that boasts as a cedar marked for judgment. Psalm 29:5 says that the voice of the LORD breaks the cedars and splinters the cedars of Lebanon. Isaiah announces that the day of the LORD comes "Upon all the cedars of Lebanon that are high and lifted up" (Isaiah 2:13). God reminds Israel that He destroyed the Amorite, "Whose height was like the height of the cedars," pulling up both "his fruit above And his roots beneath" (Amos 2:9). Strength that seemed rooted for centuries could not stand when God spoke.

God does not oppose height itself. He opposes height that forgets where it came from. Ezekiel 17:24 states the principle: the LORD brings down the high tree and lifts up the low one. The same God who splinters proud cedars also plants His own.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The cedar house of God (2 Samuel 7; 1 Kings 6). David pointed out a contrast to Nathan the prophet: "See now, I dwell in a house of cedar, but the ark of God dwells inside tent curtains" (2 Samuel 7:2). God answered that He had never asked anyone to build Him a house of cedar (7:7). Still, He let David's son build one. Solomon lined the inner walls with cedar boards from floor to ceiling, carved the wood with "ornamental buds and open flowers," and finished the work so completely that "All was cedar; there was no stone to be seen" (1 Kings 6:15, 18). The inner sanctuary, where the ark would rest, was cedar overlaid with gold (1 Kings 6:19–20). Inside, the Temple looked like a carved garden of fragrant, lasting wood. Israel gave God the finest material it could obtain. Yet in the middle of the building account, the word of the LORD interrupts: if Solomon walks in God's statutes, "I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will not forsake My people Israel" (1 Kings 6:12–13). God's presence would depend on His people obeying Him, not on how good the timber was.

Cedar in the cleansing rites (Leviticus 14; Numbers 19). For a healed leper, the priest took "two living and clean birds, cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop" (Leviticus 14:4). The same three items were thrown into the fire that burned the red heifer, whose ashes cleansed people from contact with the dead (Numbers 19:6). Both rites dealt with decay: leprosy eating at the body, and death itself. Solomon's wisdom spanned plants "from the cedar tree of Lebanon even to the hyssop that springs out of the wall" (1 Kings 4:33). That verse suggests that pairing cedar with hyssop brings together the greatest and the least of growing things. The Law does not explain why cedar was chosen. Still, it is fitting that a wood known for resisting decay was placed beside the remedy for corruption and death.

"Shall you reign because you enclose yourself in cedar?" (Jeremiah 22). King Jehoiakim built himself a large palace, paneled it with cedar, and refused to pay his workers (Jeremiah 22:13–14). God asked him, in effect, whether wrapping himself in cedar made him a king. Then God pointed to his father Josiah, who did justice, defended the poor, and so truly knew the LORD (22:15–16). The palace had become so closely tied to its wood that God could address Jerusalem's royal house as "O inhabitant of Lebanon, Making your nest in the cedars," warning that pain would come on it like labor pains (22:23). Solomon had built "the House of the Forest of Lebanon," with its four rows of cedar pillars (1 Kings 7:2). By Jehoiakim's day, that imported forest had become a hiding place from God.

Two cedars in Ezekiel (Ezekiel 17; 31). In Ezekiel 17, a great eagle snaps off the top of a cedar. That picture stands for Babylon carrying the king of Judah into exile (17:3, 12). Then the LORD God announces that He Himself will take a tender twig from the topmost shoots of the high cedar and plant it on a high mountain of Israel. There the twig will grow into a majestic cedar, and birds of every sort will nest in the shade of its branches (Ezekiel 17:22–23). Ezekiel 31 shows a different cedar: Assyria, tall and shading the forest, with birds nesting in its boughs, until pride brought it crashing down. Both trees offer shelter, and both owe their greatness to God. Of Assyria's cedar He says, "I made it beautiful with a multitude of branches" (Ezekiel 31:9). But Assyria's "heart was lifted up in its height" (Ezekiel 31:10), and God brought down the proud tree whose greatness He Himself had given. The twig God plants from David's royal line grows into the kingdom He has promised, and that tree lasts.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The tender twig of Ezekiel 17 comes from the royal cedar, the line of David, when that line had been cut down. Isaiah uses a similar image of a Branch from the stump of Jesse (Isaiah 11:1). Jesus fulfills the picture. He came small and vulnerable, from a family with no throne. Yet God raised Him to the highest place, and His kingdom now shelters people from every nation. Jesus' parable of the mustard seed, which grows until the birds nest in its branches (Matthew 13:31–32), echoes Ezekiel's language. The nations that once sought safety under the proud cedars of empire now find a home under the King God planted.

The cedar Temple also points forward. Solomon's house of carved wood was burned, and the cedar of Ezra 3:7 rebuilt it in humbler form. Jesus spoke of His own body as the temple (John 2:21). God's dwelling with His people now rests on the risen Son, not on imported timber.

The cleansing rites offer a further connection, though Scripture does not state it directly. Cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop were used to cleanse decay and death. At the cross, the One who "hangs on a tree" became a curse for us (Galatians 3:13). Just before He died, a sponge of sour wine was put on hyssop and lifted to His mouth (John 19:29–30). Wood, hyssop, and blood met at the place where God dealt with the corruption of sin once and for all.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 92:12–13 says that the righteous will grow like a cedar in Lebanon, planted in the house of the LORD and flourishing in the courts of God. This is the same tree that Isaiah 2 marked for judgment. The difference is not height but where the tree is planted. God has no wish to keep His people small. He wants them rooted in His presence, green in old age (Psalm 92:14), and strong because of where they are planted rather than what they have built. Jehoiakim tried to surround himself with cedar. God offers to make His people into cedars.

That contrast exposes a common temptation. Every generation has its own paneling, whether reputation, savings, a fine house, or a ministry name. These things are not evil, any more than Solomon's cedar was evil. They become dangerous when we nest in them and begin to think they make us secure. God's question to Jehoiakim still searches the heart: are you trusting your cedar, or are you doing justice and knowing the LORD?

The Song of Solomon adds a warmer note. The lovers rejoice that the beams of their house are cedar (Song of Solomon 1:17). The beloved is "Excellent as the cedars" (5:15). If the little sister proves to be a door, she will be enclosed with boards of cedar to guard her (8:9). The picture suggests that love as God designs it is meant to be like cedar: fragrant, lasting, and guarded against decay. A Christian home and a Christian friendship can be built that way.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 9:15 — The bramble threatens to "devour the cedars of Lebanon," a warning that worthless leaders can destroy noble ones.

  • 2 Kings 14:9 — Jehoash compares himself to a cedar and his rival to a thistle. His proud riddle shows how easily kings claimed the cedar's dignity for themselves.

  • Psalm 148:9 — Fruitful trees and all cedars are called to praise the LORD, so even the mightiest tree takes its place in worship.

  • Isaiah 41:19 — God promises to plant the cedar in the wilderness, a picture of His power to bring life where nothing grew.

  • Zechariah 11:1–2 — "Open your doors, O Lebanon, That fire may devour your cedars." The fallen cedar sets the whole forest wailing as judgment falls on the land's proud leaders.
