WIND, BREATH, SPIRIT - (rûwach, Strong's H7307)

Summary

Rûwach is the Hebrew word for moving air. Depending on the setting, the Old Testament uses it for the wind that sweeps across land and sea, the breath that keeps a creature alive, the inner spirit of a person, and the Spirit of God Himself. English needs several words to cover what Hebrew holds in one. Each use describes something unseen that is known by what it does: the sea parts, a body lives, a heart turns proud or humble, a prophet speaks.

The word carries some of the largest themes in Scripture. God's rûwach moves over the waters at creation, gives every creature its breath, and takes that breath back at death. The same word describes the broken spirit God receives from a repentant sinner, the Spirit who equipped craftsmen, elders, judges, and kings, and the Spirit promised to rest on the coming Messiah. Through Ezekiel and Joel, God promised to put His Spirit within His people and pour Him out on all flesh. Jesus fulfilled these promises when He was anointed with the Spirit and later sent the Spirit at Pentecost. To study rûwach is to learn that life, courage, holiness, and fruitful service all come from God's own breath.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rûwach is a common Hebrew noun found across the Old Testament. It is concentrated in Ezekiel, Isaiah, the Psalms, Job, and Ecclesiastes. Ancient Israel did not divide wind, breath, and spirit into separate categories the way English does. A storm off the desert, the air in a person's lungs, a person's mood or resolve, and the presence of God could all be called rûwach. The NKJV translates it as "wind," "breath," "spirit," and "Spirit," and occasionally as "mind," "courage," "anger," or "feelings." In Ezekiel's temple measurements and in 1 Chronicles 9:24, it names a "side" or "direction," following the common idea of the four winds as the four points of the compass.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core idea is air in motion. It cannot be seen, but its effects are real. Context determines which of these related senses is meant:

1. Wind, a moving force in nature, as when the LORD drove back the Red Sea with a strong east wind (Exod 14:21). By extension, the word also names a direction or side (Ezek 42:16–20).

2. Breath, the life-breath of living creatures, as with "all flesh in which is the breath of life" destroyed by the flood (Gen 6:17).

3. The human spirit, meaning a person's inner life: resolve, mood, attitude, and character. When Jacob heard that Joseph was alive, "the spirit of Jacob their father revived" (Gen 45:27). When the Canaanites heard what God had done, no courage remained in them (Josh 2:11).

4. Spirit as a being: above all the Spirit of God (Gen 1:2), and also created spirits who stand before the LORD (1 Kgs 22:21).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject and the setting determine the sense. When God sends the rûwach across sea or desert, it is a wind under His command, such as the wind that brought quail to Israel (Num 11:31). When the word appears with "flesh" or "dust," it is breath. When it describes a person's condition, it covers the whole range of human inner life: Hannah was "a woman of sorrowful spirit" (1 Sam 1:15), and a haughty spirit comes before a fall (Prov 16:18).

One pattern is worth noticing. The phrase "the Spirit of the LORD" names God's own Spirit. A construction such as "the spirit of jealousy" (Num 5:14) or "the spirit of harlotry" (Hos 4:12) describes a controlling disposition that drives a person. The same grammar can therefore point to the Holy Spirit or to an inner force that ruins a life, and the reader must look at what the passage says the spirit produces.

The verbs also matter. The Spirit "came upon" the judges for particular acts of deliverance (Judg 3:10; 14:6). In the prophets, the Spirit is said to "rest upon" someone, or God promises to put the Spirit "within" His people. That difference in wording sets up the larger story traced below.

4. Theological Significance

Wind shows what God is like. Amos calls Him the One "who forms mountains, And creates the wind" (Amos 4:13). The psalmist pictures Him as One "Who walks on the wings of the wind" (Ps 104:3). God sent wind to split the sea and to feed His people, and in judgment His breath withers the grass (Isa 40:7). No one sees God's rûwach or controls it. People see only what it does. Jesus drew on this understanding when He told Nicodemus, "The wind blows where it wishes... So is everyone who is born of the Spirit" (John 3:8).

God also gives His Spirit to equip those He calls. He filled Bezalel with His Spirit for skilled work on the tabernacle (Exod 31:3), so even craftsmanship for worship was the Spirit's gift. He took of the Spirit on Moses and put it on seventy elders to share the burden of leading the people (Num 11:17, 25). Moses responded by wishing that all the LORD's people were prophets with the Spirit upon them (Num 11:29). The Spirit came upon David from the day he was anointed, and on that same occasion the Spirit departed from Saul (1 Sam 16:13–14). When Zerubbabel faced a ruined temple and few resources, God told him the work would be finished neither by might nor by power, but by His Spirit (Zech 4:6). God's people have always done God's work in God's strength.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 1:1–3: the Spirit over the waters. Rûwach first appears in the Bible's second verse. "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Gen 1:1), but the earth is not yet a place where anything can live: "The earth was without form, and void; and darkness was on the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters" (Gen 1:2). Genesis names what is missing. The earth has no shape, nothing fills it, and darkness lies over the deep waters. Over that dark and empty scene the text sets the Spirit of God, present and near. Then God speaks: "Let there be light"; and there was light (Gen 1:3). Before anything is formed or filled, God's Spirit is already there, and God's word brings the first light. Genesis does not explain how the Spirit acted, and the single word "hovering" should not be stretched into a theory of how creation works. What the passage plainly shows is that the life and order of the world began with God's presence and God's spoken word. Psalm 33:6 joins word and breath in the same way: the heavens were made "by the word of the LORD" and their host "by the breath of His mouth." Later Scripture builds on this beginning. Psalm 104 and Job 33, discussed next, speak of God's Spirit making each living creature. When the slain in Ezekiel's valley receive breath and God explains, "I will put My Spirit in you, and you shall live" (Ezek 37:14), and when the risen Jesus breathes on His disciples and says, "Receive the Holy Spirit" (John 20:22), God is doing for His people what He did at the beginning: bringing life where there was none. Those links come from reading the whole Bible together. Genesis 1 itself describes the first creation, and its picture of the Spirit over the waters does not mean that every wind or breath in Scripture is the Holy Spirit.

Psalm 104:29–30: borrowed breath. The psalm describes every creature waiting on God. "You take away their breath, they die and return to their dust. You send forth Your Spirit, they are created; And You renew the face of the earth." The same Hebrew word stands in both lines, with two different meanings. In verse 29 rûwach is the creatures' own breath, which God can take away. In verse 30 it is God's Spirit, whom He sends to create and renew. A creature's breath is not the Holy Spirit, yet the psalmist deliberately ties the two together: the breath in every living thing depends on the Spirit who gives life. Elihu says the same about himself: "The Spirit of God has made me" (Job 33:4). Ecclesiastes ends with the spirit returning to God who gave it (Eccl 12:7). No creature owns its life. Every breath is on loan from God.

Psalm 51: the spirit God renews. After his sin with Bathsheba, David asked for more than a cleared record. He prayed, "Create in me a clean heart, O God, And renew a steadfast spirit within me" (Ps 51:10). He then begged God not to take His Holy Spirit from him (51:11), a prayer made sharper by what David had seen happen to Saul. He also understood that the sacrifice God accepts is a broken spirit (51:17). Elsewhere the psalms say the LORD "saves such as have a contrite spirit" (Ps 34:18). David wanted pardon, and he also wanted to be made different inside.

Ezekiel 36–37: a new spirit and the Spirit within. Through Ezekiel, God answered the problem David felt. He promised to give His people a new heart and a new spirit, to remove the heart of stone, and to put His own Spirit within them so that they would walk in His statutes (Ezek 36:26–27). The next chapter shows this promise in a vision. Bones fill a valley, and Ezekiel's preaching brings them together and covers them with flesh, yet "there was no breath in them" (37:8). Ezekiel is then told to prophesy to the breath, calling it from the four winds to breathe on the slain, and they stand up alive. In this one chapter rûwach means breath, wind, and Spirit, and God explains the vision Himself: He will put His Spirit in His people, and they will live (37:14). Obedience will come from the Spirit God puts within His people, because they cannot produce it themselves.

1 Kings 22 and 1 Samuel 16: spirits under God's rule. The word can also describe spirit beings other than God's Spirit. A distressing spirit troubled Saul after the LORD's Spirit left him (1 Sam 16:14), and God sent a spirit of ill will between Abimelech and the men of Shechem (Judg 9:23). In Micaiah's vision, a spirit stood before the LORD and offered to be a lying spirit in the mouths of Ahab's prophets (1 Kgs 22:21–23). Micaiah then told Ahab exactly what had happened. The king heard the truth plainly, rejected it, and died at Ramoth Gilead. These passages show that no spirit acts outside God's authority. They also show that God's judgment on hardened pride often means giving people over to the lies they prefer.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The judges received the Spirit for a battle or a task, and Saul lost the Spirit altogether. Isaiah foresaw something different for the Branch from Jesse: "The Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon Him, The Spirit of wisdom and understanding, The Spirit of counsel and might, The Spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the LORD" (Isa 11:2). The Spirit would remain on Him, and His gifts would be complete. God says of His Servant, "I have put My Spirit upon Him" (Isa 42:1). This anointed One would preach good news to the poor and proclaim liberty (Isa 61:1).

The New Testament states the fulfillment directly. In Nazareth, Jesus read, "The Spirit of the LORD is upon Me, Because He has anointed Me To preach the gospel to the poor" (Luke 4:18), and declared that the Scripture was fulfilled that day. John the Baptist bore witness: "I saw the Spirit descending from heaven like a dove, and He remained upon Him" (John 1:32). The word "remained" matches Isaiah's word "rest." Isaiah adds that this King would strike the wicked "with the breath of His lips" (Isa 11:4). Paul draws on the same picture when he describes the Lord's return (2 Thess 2:8).

The Spirit who rested on Christ is then given to His people. Joel promised, "I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh," including "My menservants and... My maidservants" (Joel 2:28–29). Moses' wish in Numbers 11 becomes God's promise. Zechariah foresaw a Spirit of grace and supplication poured out so that people would look on the One they pierced (Zech 12:10). After His resurrection, Jesus breathed on His disciples and said, "Receive the Holy Spirit" (John 20:22). At Pentecost, the sound of a rushing mighty wind accompanied the Spirit's coming, and Peter explained the event by quoting Joel. Ezekiel's valley of bones had begun to come to life.

7. Spirit & Life

Every breath you take belongs to God. Psalm 104 removes any idea that we live on our own power. This truth humbles us, and it also brings comfort, because the God who gives our breath has also promised to give His Spirit.

Ecclesiastes repeatedly calls human striving under the sun a chasing after wind. The Teacher's choice of word is telling. The rûwach he cannot catch with wealth, pleasure, or achievement is the same word for the spirit God placed in him. The book does not draw this connection as a teaching, but the reader can hear it: a person can spend a life grasping at wind while the spirit within remains empty. What that spirit needs is God's own Spirit, freely given.

God does not wait for us to fix our own spirit. David's prayer for a steadfast spirit is answered by Ezekiel's promise of a new spirit and by God's Spirit placed within us. The broken spirit God welcomes is the place where His renewing work begins. The equipping Spirit is also given for ordinary faithfulness. Bezalel worked with his hands, the elders carried the burdens of the people, and Zerubbabel rebuilt with few resources. When the work is larger than our strength, Zechariah 4:6 still applies.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 41:38 — Pharaoh, a pagan king, recognized that Joseph was a man in whom was the Spirit of God. Others could see the Spirit's wisdom at work in him.

  • Exodus 35:21 — Everyone whose spirit was willing brought offerings for the tabernacle. Here the human rûwach is the place where generous worship begins.

  • Numbers 27:18 — God chose Joshua as Moses' successor, describing him as a man in whom is the Spirit.

  • Nehemiah 9:20 — The Levites remembered that God gave His good Spirit to instruct Israel in the wilderness.

  • Psalm 31:5 — David committed his spirit into God's hand. Jesus spoke these words from the cross (Luke 23:46).

  • Psalm 139:7 — David asked where he could go from God's Spirit. No place exists beyond God's presence.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Ezekiel's breath and the four winds. In Ezekiel 37, God Himself identifies the breath that revives the bodies as His Spirit (37:14). In this vision, wind and breath stand openly for the Spirit who gives new life. That does not make every wind in Scripture the Spirit, but it lays the groundwork for Jesus' comparison in John 3:8. The "four winds" stand for God's rule over every direction of the earth. Zechariah's angel explains the four chariots as "four spirits of heaven" going out from the Lord of all the earth (Zech 6:5), and in Daniel a fallen kingdom is divided toward the four winds (Dan 8:8; 11:4). These winds represent God's agents and His providence over the nations. They do not refer to the Holy Spirit Himself.

The east wind and the breath of God's mouth. The east wind came off the desert, and Scripture often uses it for testing and judgment. It brought the locusts (Exod 10:13), dried up Ephraim's spring (Hos 13:15), and beat on Jonah's head as he sulked (Jonah 4:8). It once split the sea for Israel's rescue (Exod 14:21), so not every east wind brings judgment. The "breath of His mouth" pictures God acting directly and without effort: the heavens were made by it (Ps 33:6), and the Messiah judges by it (Isa 11:4).

The breeze in the garden: a meditation. In Genesis 3:8, God walked in the garden in "the cool of the day," which in Hebrew is the rûwach of the day. The NKJV rightly understands this as the evening breeze. Still, a reader may notice that the same word later describes God's Spirit and God's presence. The hour that had once been ordinary fellowship with God became an hour of hiding once sin entered. This connection is a reflection on the text, not a correction of the translation.
