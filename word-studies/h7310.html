ABUNDANCE, RUNS OVER, RICH FULFILLMENT - (rᵉvâyâh, Strong's H7310)

Summary

The Hebrew noun rᵉvâyâh describes abundance as a state of being filled to the point of overflow. It belongs to a word family that speaks of drinking deeply and having one's thirst fully quenched. The word appears only twice in the Bible, both times in the Psalms, and both times God is the One who brings it about. In Psalm 23:5 David says, "My cup runs over," while the Lord spreads a table for him in front of his enemies. In Psalm 66:12 Israel looks back on fire, water, and affliction and confesses, "But You brought us out to rich fulfillment."

The two verses together show what God's abundance looks like. It does not wait until every enemy is gone, and it does not skip the fire. It comes in the presence of hostility and on the far side of testing, and it is always God's gift. The overflowing cup also takes on fuller meaning in the light of another cup in Scripture, the cup of judgment that Jesus accepted in Gethsemane. Because He drank that cup, His people can lift a cup that runs over.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rᵉvâyâh is a Hebrew noun related to the verb ravah (H7301), which means to drink one's fill, to be watered, or to be saturated. The family resemblance helps explain the picture. This is the abundance of a thirsty field after soaking rain, or of a cup filled past its brim.

Both uses occur in worship poetry. One is personal: David speaks of "my cup." The other is shared by the community: "You brought us out." Older English versions rendered Psalm 66:12 as a "wealthy place." Modern readers can easily hear that as financial prosperity. The word points to fullness and satisfaction given by God, and money is not its subject.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rᵉvâyâh has one core meaning: saturation, overflowing abundance, or fullness beyond need. The NKJV translates it according to context. In Psalm 23:5 a cup "runs over," and in Psalm 66:12 God brings His people out "to rich fulfillment." Both renderings express the same sense. One describes a vessel that cannot hold everything poured into it. The other describes a condition of life after deprivation has ended.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting changes the nuance more than the meaning. In Psalm 23 the abundance is tied to a meal: the table, the anointing oil, and the cup. In Psalm 66 it is a destination. The people are brought out of something and into something.

Psalm 23:5 has a simple construction that is worth noticing. The line describes the cup with the noun itself, roughly "my cup is abundance." David does not say how much he received. He names what the cup has become.

In both verses God acts and the worshiper receives: "You prepare," "You anoint," "You brought us out." Scripture never presents this abundance as something a person produces for himself.

4. Theological Significance

These two verses show something about the character of God's giving. In Psalm 23 the Lord is first the Shepherd who makes sure David will "not want" (v. 1). By verse 5 David speaks of far more than having no lack. God's care moves beyond keeping His people alive to filling them past what they can contain.

The verses also show that God's abundance is not the absence of trouble. In Psalm 23 it arrives with enemies looking on, and in Psalm 66 it comes after the net, the burden, and the fire. Both psalms also locate the hard places inside God's purposes. In Psalm 66 the same "You" who tested Israel is the "You" who brought them out. God remains in control from the beginning of the trial to its end, and the end He intends is fullness.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 23:5: the Host who fills the cup. Psalm 23 moves from pastures and still waters (v. 2) through "the valley of the shadow of death" (v. 4) to a banquet hall: "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies; You anoint my head with oil; My cup runs over." The Shepherd has become a Host. In the ancient world, a host who set a table for a guest took responsibility for that guest's welfare. David's enemies are still present. They have not been removed or silenced, and yet they are made to watch while the Lord serves His guest generously. The overflowing cup shows publicly that the person belongs to God and is under His protection. The threat around the table cannot reduce what is poured inside it.

Psalm 66:10–12: from the net to rich fulfillment. This psalm does not soften what Israel went through. "For You, O God, have tested us; You have refined us as silver is refined. You brought us into the net; You laid affliction on our backs. You have caused men to ride over our heads; We went through fire and through water." The psalmist does not blame chance or merely human enemies. God tested His people, God brought them into the net, and God laid the weight on their backs. Then comes the turn: "But You brought us out to rich fulfillment." The trial confined them in a net, under a load, and beneath men riding over their heads. The word that ends the line describes open, satisfied fullness. Refining silver aims at pure metal, and God's refining of His people aims at rᵉvâyâh.

The verse also contains a contrast in its imagery. The "water" Israel passed through is the water of danger, the kind that drowns, as in the Flood or the Red Sea. The destination named by rᵉvâyâh comes from a word family about drinking deeply. God brought His people through water that threatened to drown them and into a fullness like a thirst finally quenched.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The cup in Psalm 23:5 belongs to a larger set of cup imagery in Scripture, where a cup can stand for the portion God assigns to a person. For the wicked that portion is judgment: "Fire and brimstone and a burning wind Shall be the portion of their cup" (Ps 11:6). In Psalm 75:8 the LORD holds a cup of red wine, "fully mixed," whose dregs "all the wicked of the earth" must "drain and drink down." Isaiah 51:17 calls it the cup of God's fury.

Against that background, Jesus prayed in Gethsemane that if it were possible, the cup might pass from Him, yet He yielded to the Father's will (Matt 26:39). The Gospels do not label His cup with the words of Psalm 75. Still, the canonical pattern is clear enough to recognize: the Good Shepherd took the cup of judgment so that the sheep could receive the cup that runs over. The overflow is then more than a pleasant picture of plenty. It is the inheritance of people whose judgment has already been borne. The believer's answer is the one found in Psalm 116:13: "I will take up the cup of salvation, And call upon the name of the LORD."

Jesus also described His purpose as a Shepherd in similar terms: "I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly" (John 10:10). That verse is Greek and does not contain rᵉvâyâh, but it fulfills the same hope in Christ. The Shepherd of Psalm 23 gives life in overflowing measure.

7. Spirit & Life

These two verses correct a shallow idea of blessing. If abundance meant life without enemies or fire, then neither David nor Israel would qualify. The overflowing cup was poured while enemies watched, and rich fulfillment came only after the furnace. Believers who are walking through a valley or carrying affliction on their backs have not been excluded from God's abundance. Psalm 66 shows that the hardship can be the road God uses to bring them there. Scripture does not promise constant material excess. Paul learned to be content both when he was full and when he was hungry (Phil 4:12). Overflow describes what God supplies and where He is leading His people, not a guarantee about the size of a bank account.

Scripture also shows a movement in how God gives. In the wilderness the manna was measured, an omer for each person, so that the one who gathered much had nothing left over and the one who gathered little had no lack (Exod 16:16–18). That measured provision taught Israel daily dependence. Jesus later said that God does not give the Spirit by measure (John 3:34), and He promised that rivers of living water would flow from the heart of the one who believes (John 7:38). He also spoke of a good measure that is pressed down, shaken together, and running over (Luke 6:38). The overflowing cup of Psalm 23 is not a technical prophecy of these sayings, but it fits the direction they describe. Life in the Spirit is meant to spill past the edges of our own needs and refresh others.

This abundance always comes from God. Both psalms speak to God in the second person: You prepare, You brought us out. No one fills his own cup. The right response is to come to the table and receive what the Host pours.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Rᵉvâyâh occurs only in Psalm 23:5 and Psalm 66:12, and both verses are developed above. The surrounding verses Psalm 23:1–4 and Psalm 66:10–11 do not contain the word, but they provide the context that gives it its force.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H7301 (ravah), to drink one's fill or be saturated. It is the verb behind the noun and supplies its picture of quenched thirst.

  • Canonical pairing (thematic): the cup of judgment (Ps 11:6; 75:8) and the cup of salvation (Ps 116:13), which set the overflowing cup of Psalm 23:5 within Scripture's wider cup imagery.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Scripture does not present rᵉvâyâh as a standalone symbol. It describes the cup, and the cup is the symbol. Two established images do gather around it.

The first is the contrast between two kinds of water in Psalm 66:12, discussed above. The water that drowns gives way to the fullness of a thirst satisfied.

The second is the pairing of the anointing oil and the cup in Psalm 23:5. Elsewhere anointing oil runs down, as on Aaron's beard and onto the edge of his garments (Ps 133:2). In Psalm 23:5 the oil on David's head and the cup that runs over both picture blessing that keeps flowing past its container. God's favor is shown as something in motion.

The movement from measured manna to the unmeasured Spirit (Section 7) is an illustration drawn from Scripture's larger story rather than a meaning stated in these two psalms.
