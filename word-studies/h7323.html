RUN, RAN, RUNNERS, GUARDS, COURIERS - (rûwts, Strong's H7323)

Summary

The Hebrew verb rûwts means to run. Scripture uses it for ordinary haste, like a servant hurrying to a well or a messenger racing from a battlefield. It also uses it as a title for the "runners" who guarded Israel's kings and the "couriers" who carried a king's decrees. Running in the Old Testament is rarely casual. People run when something matters: Abraham runs to greet his guests, Aaron runs into a plague with atoning incense, and David runs toward Goliath.

The word also says a great deal about the heart. The wicked "run to evil," while the psalmist prays, "I will run the course of Your commandments, For You shall enlarge my heart" (Psalm 119:32). The righteous run to the name of the LORD as a strong tower. False prophets run without being sent. Those who wait on the LORD "shall run and not be weary" (Isaiah 40:31). Even God's word "runs very swiftly" (Psalm 147:15). Taken together, these passages show that where a person runs reveals what that person loves. They also show that the only running that lasts is running God has authorized and God Himself empowers.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rûwts is a common Hebrew verb, found from Genesis through Zechariah. It appears in family scenes, battles, palace intrigue, wisdom sayings, and prophetic visions. In the historical books it often describes a royal role. Kings kept men who "ran" before their chariots and guarded the palace doors. Persian kings sent "couriers" across the empire with their edicts.

Even when the Esther couriers rode horses (Esther 8:10), they were still called "runners." The word described their office, a messenger sent at full speed, as much as the way they traveled.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To run or hurry physically, often with urgency, eagerness, or alarm. Abraham "ran from the tent door to meet them" (Genesis 18:2).

2. To run figuratively, describing the eager pursuit of a path, whether good or evil, or the swift movement of something personified. The psalmist runs "the course of Your commandments" (Psalm 119:32), and God's word "runs very swiftly" (Psalm 147:15).

3. As a noun, runner: a royal guard, escort, or courier. Examples include the "guard" at Rehoboam's palace door (1 Kings 14:27), the "escorts" who protected young Joash (2 Kings 11:4), and the "couriers" of Esther (Esther 3:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of running depends on who runs, and toward what. In Genesis, running expresses eager welcome. Abraham runs to his visitors and then to the herd (Genesis 18:2, 7). Rebekah runs to water a stranger's camels and then home with the news (Genesis 24:20, 28). Esau runs to embrace the brother he once vowed to kill (Genesis 33:4).

On the battlefield, running shows courage or collapse. David runs toward Goliath (1 Samuel 17:48), while Midian's army runs in panic (Judges 7:21).

In Wisdom literature and the prophets, running becomes moral. The feet of sinners run to evil and hurry to shed blood (Proverbs 1:16; 6:18; Isaiah 59:7). The prophets also use the word for judgment. Joel's invading army runs "like mighty men" and runs "to and fro in the city" (Joel 2:7, 9). Nahum's chariots "run like lightning" through Nineveh's streets (Nahum 2:4). In these visions, running conveys a force no wall can stop.

One translation note helps here. In 2 Kings 23:12, the NKJV says Josiah "pulverized" the idolatrous altars. That rendering likely reflects a reading from a similar Hebrew verb meaning to crush. It does not show that rûwts itself means crush.

4. Theological Significance

The passages using this word show that human beings are always running toward something. The question is never whether we will run but where. Proverbs pictures feet trained in eagerness for evil. Psalm 119:32 pictures the same eagerness redirected toward God's commands. The change in direction comes from God: "For You shall enlarge my heart." Obedience in this verse is not a reluctant trudge. A heart God has freed and widened finds it natural to run.

The Song of Solomon adds the relational side: "Draw me away! We will run after you" (Song of Songs 1:4). The running follows the drawing. Love from the king produces the pursuit.

God's own word moves with the same speed. "He sends out His command to the earth; His word runs very swiftly" (Psalm 147:15). The surrounding verses describe snow, frost, and melting ice obeying His voice. The picture is of a God whose spoken will is never stalled in transit. What He says is already on its way to being done.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 18:10 — Running into God's name. "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run to it and are safe." In Scripture, God's name is His revealed character: who He has shown Himself to be. A tower was the place people fled to when an enemy appeared. The proverb does not tell the righteous to stand firm in their own strength. It tells them to run. Trust here is an action, a reflex toward God taken in a moment of danger. The word "safe" carries the idea of being set high, out of reach. Safety depends on where one runs, and the righteous run to God Himself.

Jeremiah 23:21 and 2 Samuel 18 — Unsent runners and an incomplete message. God's charge against false prophets is sharp: "I have not sent these prophets, yet they ran." Their energy proved nothing, because God had not commissioned them. Second Samuel 18 shows a related but different problem. After Absalom's death, Ahimaaz asks to carry the news to David. Joab first refuses him and sends a Cushite instead. When Ahimaaz presses again, Joab asks, "Why will you run, my son, since you have no news ready?" but then tells him, "Run" (18:22–23). Ahimaaz outruns the Cushite and arrives first. What he reports is true as far as it goes: "Blessed be the LORD your God, who has delivered up the men who raised their hand against my lord the king!" (18:28). But when David asks the question closest to his heart, "Is the young man Absalom safe?", Ahimaaz can only say that he saw a great tumult and did not know what it was about (18:29). Unlike Jeremiah's prophets, he was allowed to go. His failure was that he had speed without a complete message: he announced the victory but could not give the king clear news about his son.

Habakkuk 2:2 supplies the contrast. God tells the prophet to "Write the vision And make it plain on tablets, That he may run who reads it." Interpreters differ on whether the runner is a herald carrying the message or a reader who can take it in at a glance. Either way, the running depends on a clear word that God has given.

Isaiah 40:31 and 1 Kings 18:46 — Strength exchanged. Isaiah first admits the limit of human strength: "Even the youths shall faint and be weary" (Isaiah 40:30). Then he makes a promise: "those who wait on the LORD Shall renew their strength… They shall run and not be weary." The promise does not describe better training. It describes an exchange, in which people who wait on the everlasting God, who "neither faints nor is weary" (Isaiah 40:28), receive strength that is not their own.

Elijah shows what this looks like. After the contest at Carmel, "the hand of the LORD came upon Elijah; and he girded up his loins and ran ahead of Ahab to the entrance of Jezreel." Ahab rode in his chariot, while Elijah ran on foot ahead of him all the way to Jezreel. The writer does not describe a race or compare their speed. He credits Elijah's running to the hand of the LORD. The scene pictures God's power, given for God's purpose, carrying a man on foot ahead of a king's chariot.

Esther 3 and 8 — Two sets of couriers. Persia's royal couriers carried the king's sealed letters across a vast empire. In Esther 3:13, they carry Haman's decree "to destroy, to kill, and to annihilate all the Jews." Persian law could not revoke that decree. The king's answer was a second decree, sent by couriers "hastened and pressed on by the king's command" (Esther 8:14), giving the Jews the right to defend their lives. The decree of life had to reach the provinces before the day of death arrived. The scene offers a vivid illustration of gospel urgency, though Esther does not itself teach this. People already live under a sentence, and the King's message of rescue must reach them in time.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Royal runners belong to the story of kingship. Samuel warned that a king would take Israel's sons to "run before his chariots" (1 Samuel 8:11). Later, when Absalom and Adonijah tried to seize David's throne, each one provided "fifty men to run before him" (2 Samuel 15:1; 1 Kings 1:5). Adonijah's version states the motive outright: he "exalted himself, saying, 'I will be king.'" Jesus, the true Son of David, entered Jerusalem with no chariot and no runners, riding a donkey as Zechariah 9:9 foretold. The contrast exposes the difference between grasping a crown and receiving one from the Father.

Aaron's running points in the same direction. When a plague broke out against the rebels, Aaron "ran into the midst of the assembly; and already the plague had begun among the people. So he put in the incense and made atonement for the people" (Numbers 16:47). The high priest hurried into a place where death was already at work and "stood between the dead and the living" (16:48). The passage is not labeled as a prophecy, but it closely resembles the work of Christ. He did not hold back from our ruin. He came into it as Priest and Sacrifice and stood between us and death.

The New Testament takes up the language of running with a different Greek word: "Run in such a way that you may obtain it" (1 Corinthians 9:24), and "let us run with endurance the race that is set before us" (Hebrews 12:1). The Old Testament has already prepared the runner. That runner waits on the LORD for strength, runs to His name for safety, and runs only where He has sent.

7. Spirit & Life

Every believer can ask a searching question: where do my feet hurry? Proverbs shows that the same eagerness can go either way, and that zeal alone proves nothing. The answer is not to become slow. It is to pray Psalm 119:32, asking God to enlarge the heart until His commands feel like open ground rather than a fence.

Ahimaaz warns against running ahead of a complete message. What he said was true, but it was not all the king needed to hear. Activity can move faster than understanding. Before running, the servant of God needs a word from God, received plainly and carried faithfully and fully.

When weariness comes, Isaiah 40 does not tell us to try harder. It tells us to wait on the LORD. The strength for the task He assigns comes from Him, sometimes in startling measure, as Elijah discovered on the road to Jezreel. And when fear comes, the first move of faith is to run to His name.

The couriers of Esther give that faith a direction. People around us live under a sentence they cannot cancel. The King has issued a better decree, and those who carry it should do so with the urgency of couriers pressed on by the king's command.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 3:5 — The boy Samuel runs to Eli when he hears his name called, eager to answer before he even knows the voice is God's.

  • 1 Samuel 17:48 — David runs toward the Philistine army to meet Goliath, faith moving toward the danger.

  • 1 Kings 19:20 — Elisha leaves his oxen and runs after Elijah, answering God's call without delay.

  • 2 Chronicles 30:6 — Hezekiah's runners carry the call, "Children of Israel, return to the LORD God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel," inviting a scattered nation back to Passover.

  • Jeremiah 12:5 — God asks Jeremiah how he will contend with horses if running with footmen has wearied him, preparing the prophet for harder days.

  • Isaiah 55:5 — Nations who did not know Israel will run to her because of the LORD her God.
