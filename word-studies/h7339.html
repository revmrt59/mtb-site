SQUARE, STREET - (rᵉchôb, Strong's H7339)

Summary

The Hebrew word rᵉchôb names the broad open place of an ancient town: the plaza or public square, usually near the city gate, where people met, traded, judged cases, mourned, and celebrated. English Bibles render it "square," "open square," or "street." Its meaning comes from a word for being wide. It is the roomy, shared center of town, as opposed to a narrow lane between houses.

Because the square was where a city's common life could be seen, Scripture often uses it to show the real condition of a people. Sodom and Gibeah reveal their wickedness by how they treat strangers in the square. Wisdom calls aloud there, and Ezra reads God's Law there to the gathered exiles. In judgment the squares fill with fainting children and wailing. In restoration God promises that old men and women will again sit in Jerusalem's streets while boys and girls play around them. The Bible's story of the square ends in the New Jerusalem, whose street is pure gold and holds the tree of life. When God redeems a people, He does not stop at their private hearts. He also brings His peace to their shared life.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rᵉchôb belongs to the Hebrew word family for breadth and spaciousness, and its plain sense fits that family: a wide place. Walled towns had little open ground, so the plaza by the gate was valuable. There, Job took his seat among the elders (Job 29:7). There, Hezekiah gathered his military captains (2 Chronicles 32:6), and there the crowds assembled in Nehemiah's Jerusalem (Nehemiah 8:1).

The word appears in narrative, law, wisdom, poetry, and prophecy, from Genesis to Zechariah. Hebrew has another common word for outside or streets, and the two often appear side by side. In Jeremiah 5:1, the NKJV's "streets of Jerusalem" translates that other word, while "open places" translates rᵉchôb. The pairing shows the difference: rᵉchôb points to the public squares rather than every passage between buildings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rᵉchôb means a broad open area in a town or city. It has one basic sense with two shades:

1. A public square or plaza, often beside a gate, used for gathering, waiting, or assembly. In Judges 19:15 the Levite "sat down in the open square of the city," waiting for someone to offer him lodging.

2. More generally, the open streets or public ways of a city. In Lamentations 2:11 children faint "in the streets of the city," and Nahum 2:4 describes chariots racing through the city's "roads."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The square served ordinary civic purposes. It was where leaders addressed people and where reform could begin. Hezekiah gathered the priests and Levites in "the East Square" before cleansing the temple (2 Chronicles 29:4). The square was also where honor and shame were displayed. Mordecai was paraded on the king's horse through the city square (Esther 6:9, 11). The Philistines hung the bodies of Saul and Jonathan in the street of Beth Shan, where everyone would see them (2 Samuel 21:12).

The law used the square's public character as well. If an Israelite city turned to other gods, "you shall gather all its plunder into the middle of the street, and completely burn with fire the city and all its plunder, for the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 13:16). The city's idolatry had been public, so its judgment would be public too.

Often the word stands for the people who filled the squares. When Isaiah says "truth is fallen in the street" (Isaiah 59:14), he is describing a society's shared life, not pavement.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats the square as a window into a city's soul, and God looks through it. In Jeremiah 5:1 He says: "Run to and fro through the streets of Jerusalem... And seek in her open places If you can find a man, If there is anyone who executes judgment, Who seeks the truth, And I will pardon her." God measures Jerusalem by what can be found in its squares. Psalm 55:11 laments a city where "Oppression and deceit do not depart from its streets." Isaiah 59:14 pictures truth as a person collapsed in the plaza while equity "cannot enter." Righteousness that never reaches the public square has not reached the city.

The square also exposes how bold sin has become. Ezekiel charges Jerusalem with building "a high place for yourself in every street" (Ezekiel 16:24; compare 16:31). Idolatry practiced in secret is still sin, but shrines built in the open streets show a people who have stopped being ashamed.

The same openness that makes sin visible also makes room for God's voice. "Wisdom calls aloud outside; She raises her voice in the open squares" (Proverbs 1:20). God does not keep His wisdom inside sacred buildings for a select few. He sends it into the busiest place in town, where the scorners are.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Sodom and Gibeah: two squares, one sickness (Genesis 19:2; Judges 19:15–20). When the two angels reached Sodom, Lot urged them into his house. They answered, "No, but we will spend the night in the open square." Lot "insisted strongly" (19:3). The text does not give his reason, but that night the men of the city surrounded his house. Judges 19 retells the scene almost detail for detail. A Levite travels toward "the house of the LORD" and stops at Gibeah, a town of Israel's own tribe of Benjamin. He sits "in the open square of the city, for no one would take them into his house." No household of Gibeah's own Benjamite people offers him lodging. The welcome finally comes from an old man coming in from the field, who was from the hill country of Ephraim and only staying in Gibeah (19:16). He takes the Levite in and warns him not to spend the night in the square (19:20). The mob that follows matches Sodom's. The parallels are deliberate, and they leave the reader with an awful conclusion: an Israelite town had come to act like Sodom. The test in both cities was simple. What happens to a stranger who has nowhere to go but the public square?

The Word read in the square (Nehemiah 8:1–16; Ezra 10:9). After the exile, "all the people gathered together as one man in the open square that was in front of the Water Gate" and asked Ezra to bring the Book of the Law (Nehemiah 8:1). He read from morning until midday while men, women, and all who could understand listened. The people wept, were told to rejoice, and then built booths in the squares by the Water Gate and the Gate of Ephraim (8:16). Some years earlier, Ezra 10:9 had shown the men of Judah sitting "in the open square of the house of God, trembling" in the rain over their sin. The size of the crowds required an open space, but the setting still carries meaning. Before the exile, Ezekiel had condemned Jerusalem for building a high place "in every street" (Ezekiel 16:24). Scripture does not say that the square before the Water Gate was one of those places, but after the exile the city's open squares were put to a very different use: repentance, the hearing of Scripture, and joy. The wisdom that Proverbs pictured calling in the squares was now literally read aloud there.

From Lamentations to Zechariah: the square undone and restored (Lamentations 2:11–12; 4:18; Zechariah 8:3–5). Lamentations describes Babylon's siege from the plaza. The poet's eyes fail with tears "Because the children and the infants Faint in the streets of the city" (2:11). Little ones collapse there like the wounded and die in their mothers' arms (2:12). Later, the enemy "tracked our steps So that we could not walk in our streets" (4:18). Jeremiah had warned that death would come in and cut off "the young men— no longer on the streets!" (Jeremiah 9:21), and Amos foresaw "wailing in all streets" (Amos 5:16). Zechariah answers that grief directly. God says, "I will return to Zion, And dwell in the midst of Jerusalem" (8:3), and then He describes what His presence looks like: "Old men and old women shall again sit In the streets of Jerusalem, Each one with his staff in his hand Because of great age" (8:4). He adds that the city's streets will be full of boys and girls playing (8:5). The two ends of life return to the very place where children had fainted. In God's picture of peace, the weakest people in the city sit and play safely in the open.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Daniel 9:25 speaks of Jerusalem being restored and built, with the promise that "The street shall be built again, and the wall, Even in troublesome times." The same verse sets this rebuilding within a reckoning of time that leads to "Messiah the Prince." Interpreters read the details of that timetable in different ways, and many Christians see it pointing to Jesus. For this study the main point is plainer: God promised that Jerusalem's square would be rebuilt even in troubled times, and that restored city becomes part of the setting into which His Anointed would come.

In the Gospels, Jesus brings the street into the kingdom's invitation. In His parable of the great supper, the invited guests refuse to come. The master then says, "Go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in here the poor and the maimed and the lame and the blind" (Luke 14:21). Luke uses the Greek word for a broad street, the New Testament counterpart to rᵉchôb. In Israel's history the square was often where the stranger went unsheltered and the starving collapsed. In Jesus' parable it becomes the place where the master's servants find guests for his table. Jesus did what Gibeah refused to do.

The story closes in Revelation. The New Jerusalem's "street of the city was pure gold, like transparent glass" (Revelation 21:21), and "In the middle of its street... was the tree of life," whose leaves "were for the healing of the nations" (22:2). Revelation does not cite Zechariah 8 at this point, but the movement is clear. The public center of human life, so often stained by cruelty, idolatry, and grief, becomes the place where God's life grows and the nations are healed.

7. Spirit & Life

The square asks how a person's faith appears in shared life. Jeremiah 5:1 shows God searching the open places for one person who does justice and seeks truth. Job's honor in the square came from rescuing the poor and the fatherless (Job 29:7, 12). God still looks at how His people treat strangers, the poor, and the defenseless in the places where everyone else can see.

The Sodom and Gibeah accounts also warn God's own people. Gibeah was not a pagan city. Its people were Israelites, yet no household among them opened its door to a traveling Levite, and the welcome he finally received came from a newcomer from Ephraim. A church or a household can slip in the same way, until the person sitting alone in the square is never noticed.

The square also holds great hope. Wisdom still calls in the busy places, and God's Word still gathers broken people into one assembly, as it did at the Water Gate, where weeping gave way to the joy of the LORD. Zechariah's elderly and children show what God means by peace. His presence makes life safe for the most vulnerable, and believers live toward that city now by making room for the weak in the places they share.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Esther 4:6 – Hathach meets Mordecai in the city square before the king's gate. Mordecai mourns publicly, and from there the plan to save the Jews begins.

  • Proverbs 7:12 – The seductress lurks in the square, a reminder that folly also calls in public places, competing with Wisdom.

  • Proverbs 22:13; 26:13 – The lazy man excuses himself because "there is a lion" in the streets. He would rather stay home than face the public world where work happens.

  • Song of Solomon 3:2 – The bride searches the streets and squares for the one she loves, a picture of earnest longing that will not stay indoors.

  • Psalm 144:14 – The psalmist prays for a land at peace, with no outcry in its streets.

  • Jeremiah 50:30 – Babylon's young men fall in her streets. The judgment Babylon once brought on Jerusalem's squares comes back on her own.
