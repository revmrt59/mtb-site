REHOBOTH - (Rᵉchôbôwth, Strong's H7344)

Summary

Rehoboth is the name of three different places in Genesis and 1 Chronicles. The name means "broad places," "open spaces," or "streets," and comes from a Hebrew root meaning to be wide or roomy. The first Rehoboth is Rehoboth Ir, a city in Assyria listed among the early building projects connected with Nimrod (Genesis 10:11). The second is a well in the southern wilderness that Isaac named after two bitter disputes with the herdsmen of Gerar (Genesis 26:22). The third is "Rehoboth-by-the-River," the hometown of Saul, an early king of Edom who should not be confused with Israel's King Saul (Genesis 36:37; 1 Chronicles 1:48).

The well carries most of the spiritual weight. Isaac's servants dug a well and the locals claimed it, so he named it Esek, "contention." They dug a second, and the same thing happened, so he named it Sitnah, "hostility." Instead of fighting, Isaac moved on and dug a third, and this time no one quarreled. He called it Rehoboth and explained why: "For now the LORD has made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land." The name is Isaac's testimony that the open space came from God and that God's promises to Abraham's family were still moving forward. The Psalms later take up the same picture when they describe God bringing His people out of distress and setting them "in a broad place" (Psalm 118:5). That picture finds its fullest answer in Christ, who did not grasp for His rights, and who promised that the meek will inherit the earth.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The name. Rehoboth (Hebrew רְחֹבוֹת) is a feminine plural noun. Its root, rachab, means "to be wide, broad, or large." The singular form refers to a wide, open area, such as a town square or street, so the plural means "broad places," "open spaces," or "streets." The meaning is well established. What it signifies depends on which Rehoboth is in view.

At Isaac's well, Scripture itself explains the name with deliberate wordplay. Isaac calls the well Rehoboth "because he said, 'For now the LORD has made room for us'" (Genesis 26:22). The verb translated "made room" comes from the same root as the name, so the Hebrew sounds roughly like "Broad-Places, because the LORD has made it broad for us." The name and the reason given for it belong together.

The name meaning belongs to the places, not to any person's character. Saul of Rehoboth, for example, is identified by where he came from. Nothing in the text suggests that his life was "spacious."

Three distinct places. The single Strong's entry covers three separate locations. They should not be merged.

1. Rehoboth Ir (Genesis 10:11) was a city in Assyria named alongside Nineveh, Calah, and Resen. Ir means "city," so the full name is usually understood as "city of broad streets." Its location is unknown. Some interpreters read the phrase as a description of part of Nineveh, something like "the open places of the city," rather than as a separate town. The Hebrew of the verse is also ambiguous about who did the building. The NKJV reads it as Nimrod going out "to Assyria," while other translations read "Asshur went out." Either way, the city belongs to the early Mesopotamian kingdom building described in Genesis 10.

2. Isaac's Rehoboth (Genesis 26:22) was a well Isaac dug after leaving the valley of Gerar, in the dry southern country of Canaan near the Philistine region. The text says only that he "moved from there." A site southwest of Beersheba called er-Ruheibeh is traditionally proposed because it appears to preserve the name, but the identification is not certain.

3. Rehoboth-by-the-River (Genesis 36:37; 1 Chronicles 1:48) was the hometown of Saul, the sixth king in Edom's list of early rulers. In Hebrew narrative, "the River" usually means the Euphrates, which would make Saul a man from far to the northeast. Other interpreters think a local Edomite stream is meant. The question remains open.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Isaac's Well (Genesis 26)

The most important Rehoboth comes in the middle of a hard chapter in Isaac's life. A famine drove him to Gerar, and there the LORD told him not to go down to Egypt. God renewed the promises He had sworn to Abraham: land, descendants, and blessing for all nations (Genesis 26:1–5). Isaac stayed. His record in Gerar was not spotless, since he lied about Rebekah just as his father had done (26:7). Yet God blessed him anyway. He sowed and reaped a hundredfold, and his flocks and household grew until the Philistines envied him (26:12–14). They filled in the wells Abraham had dug, and King Abimelech finally told Isaac to leave because he had grown too powerful (26:15–16).

Isaac moved into the valley of Gerar and reopened his father's wells, calling them by Abraham's names (26:17–18). Then the contest over new water began. "Isaac's servants dug in the valley, and found a well of running water there. But the herdsmen of Gerar quarreled with Isaac's herdsmen, saying, 'The water is ours.' So he called the name of the well Esek, because they quarreled with him" (26:19–20). Running water in that dry country was worth contending for, and Gerar's herdsmen contended for it. "Then they dug another well, and they quarreled over that one also. So he called its name Sitnah" (26:21). Sitnah means hostility or opposition. It comes from the same root as the Hebrew word satan, "adversary," so the second dispute seems to have been more bitter than the first.

Isaac did not answer with force. He gave up both wells and moved again. "And he moved from there and dug another well, and they did not quarrel over it. So he called its name Rehoboth, because he said, 'For now the LORD has made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land'" (26:22). The word "now" carries the relief of a man who has finally stopped being pushed around. Isaac gives the credit to the LORD. He does not claim he outlasted his rivals or found a lucky spot. God made the room.

The story does not end at the well. Isaac went up to Beersheba, and that same night the LORD appeared to him, told him not to fear, and promised again to be with him and bless him for Abraham's sake (26:23–24). Isaac built an altar there, called on the name of the LORD, pitched his tent, and his servants dug yet another well (26:25). Soon Abimelech himself came with his officials to seek a peace treaty, because they could see that the LORD was with Isaac (26:26–31). The same rulers who had driven Isaac away now asked for his friendship. Rehoboth stands at the turning point of the chapter. Before it, Isaac meets one quarrel after another. After it come worship, a fresh word from God, and peace with his enemies.

Rehoboth Ir (Genesis 10:11)

The first Rehoboth appears in the Table of Nations, in the passage about Nimrod, "a mighty hunter before the LORD," whose kingdom began in Babel and the land of Shinar (Genesis 10:8–10). "From that land he went to Assyria and built Nineveh, Rehoboth Ir, Calah" (10:11). The verse names it and moves on. Its setting still matters, though. Genesis places it among the first great cities of the world after the flood, and the literary setting links it with Shinar, where Genesis 11 tells of people saying, "Let us build ourselves a city" (11:4). The Table of Nations and the Babel account are not arranged as a simple dated sequence, so this is a literary connection rather than a chronology. Here "broad places" are something human power builds.

Rehoboth-by-the-River (Genesis 36:37; 1 Chronicles 1:48)

The third Rehoboth appears in the list of kings who reigned in Edom "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31). The list is repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:43–50. In both places the record is the same: "And when Samlah died, Saul of Rehoboth-by-the-River reigned in his place." The list does not describe father-to-son succession. It gives city notices for some rulers and places of origin for others; Samlah is "of Masrekah" and Saul "of Rehoboth-by-the-River," so Saul came from a different place than his predecessor. The list does not establish every transfer of capital or a complete political system. He shares a Hebrew name with Israel's first king but is a different man in a different nation and era.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

What Scripture states plainly is that Isaac received Rehoboth as God's doing and tied it to the future: "we shall be fruitful in the land" (Genesis 26:22). That second phrase matters. God had just promised Isaac the land and many descendants (26:3–4). Isaac had inherited his father's estate (25:5), including the field and cave of Machpelah that Abraham had bought (23:17–20), but he did not possess the promised land as a nation; he lived as a sojourner who had to dig for water and could be sent away. Yet he read one undisputed well as evidence that God's promise was alive and working. The word "fruitful" also recalls God's first blessing on humanity, "Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28), which God later repeats to the patriarchs (35:11). The room at Rehoboth was small, but Isaac treated it as a first installment on something much larger.

The sequence of Esek, Sitnah, and Rehoboth also shows how the promise moved forward. Isaac did not win his inheritance by contest. God opened a place where no one fought him. The text does not praise Isaac's restraint in so many words. Still, the order of events speaks clearly: he yielded twice, God gave him room, and his former opponents eventually sought peace with him (26:28–31).

The other two Rehoboths fit into God's purposes in quieter ways. Rehoboth Ir belongs to Genesis 10's account of how the nations spread across the earth, including the Assyrian power that would later trouble Israel. Rehoboth-by-the-River belongs to the record of Edom, Esau's descendants, whom God allowed to establish kings and a settled kingdom long before Israel had one. Scripture's careful record of Edom's kings shows that God's choice of Jacob's line did not make Him careless about Esau's.

The contrast between the first two Rehoboths is an observation drawn from Genesis itself, not a point the text spells out. In Genesis 10, "broad places" are built by a mighty founder of cities in the land that produced Babel. In Genesis 26, a sojourner who has just given up two wells receives "broad places" as a gift. Genesis never sets the two side by side, and Genesis 10 does not condemn the building of cities as such. Still, its larger story favors the second path. It moves away from Babel's self-made name in chapter 11 and toward Abraham, whose name God promises to make great (12:2).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The proper noun Rehoboth does not appear again after Chronicles, but the idea behind it runs through the rest of Scripture. In Hebrew, distress is often pictured as narrowness, being hemmed in or squeezed by enemies. Salvation is pictured as space. "I called on the LORD in distress; the LORD answered me and set me in a broad place" (Psalm 118:5). "And have not shut me up into the hand of the enemy; You have set my feet in a wide place" (Psalm 31:8). David says something similar in Psalm 18:19, where God brings him out into a broad place because He delights in him. In Psalm 4:1, "You have relieved me in my distress," the Hebrew verb behind "relieved" comes from the same root as Rehoboth. Older translations such as the KJV render it "thou hast enlarged me." These psalms use related words, not the place name itself. They show that what Isaac said at his well, that the LORD makes room for His people, became part of Israel's prayer language.

Psalm 118 deserves special attention, because the New Testament repeatedly applies it to Jesus (Matthew 21:42; Acts 4:11). The one who cries from distress and is brought into a broad place is also the rejected stone that becomes the chief cornerstone. Christ entered the deepest distress anyone has known and was brought out of it in resurrection, and those who belong to Him share that deliverance. This is a strongly supported canonical connection, not a claim that the well of Rehoboth was a designed picture of Christ.

The link between Isaac's yielding and Jesus' words is thematic, but it is a real one. Jesus said, "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matthew 5:5), echoing Psalm 37:11. That psalm urges God's people not to fret over evildoers but to rest in the LORD and wait for Him. Isaac's story at the wells is a living picture of that kind of trust. Jesus Himself, "when He was reviled, did not revile in return" but committed Himself to God (1 Peter 2:23). Hebrews also reminds us that Isaac lived in the land of promise as a foreigner and, with Abraham and Jacob, looked for a city whose builder and maker is God (Hebrews 11:9–10). Read that way, Rehoboth was a small, real foretaste of a larger inheritance that the patriarchs did not yet hold.

Some readers also connect Isaac's well with the "living water" Jesus offers in John 4. That is a fitting resonance, since Jesus spoke those words beside a patriarch's well, but the connection is devotional rather than textual.

5. Spirit & Life

Isaac's three wells describe something many believers live through. You dig, and someone claims what you dug. You try again, and the opposition grows sharper. Isaac named his disappointments honestly: Esek, Sitnah. He did not pretend the quarrels were pleasant. But he also refused to let them become his permanent address. He kept moving and kept digging, and he left the outcome with God.

When the room finally came, Isaac knew where it came from. "The LORD has made room for us." That sentence is worth learning to say. The space God gives seldom comes because we finally out-argued or outlasted everyone. It comes because God goes ahead of His people and opens what they could not force open. Isaac also immediately looked forward: "we shall be fruitful." He did not treat the well as a resting point for his own comfort but as evidence that God's purposes for his family were still on track.

Notice too what followed. God met Isaac at Beersheba with "Do not fear, for I am with you," and Isaac responded with an altar and prayer (Genesis 26:24–25). The room God made led to worship, not only to relief. In time even Abimelech could see that the LORD was with him. People who stop fighting for every inch and trust God to make room often find that God makes peace with their enemies as well (compare Proverbs 16:7).

The Psalms invite every believer who feels hemmed in to pray the way David did: to call on the LORD from the narrow place and expect Him to answer by setting our feet in a wide place. In Christ, that answer is sure. Whatever confines us now, the God who raised Jesus has already secured an inheritance no one can quarrel over.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:8–11 – Rehoboth Ir among the Assyrian cities connected with Nimrod's kingdom.

  • Genesis 26:1–5 – God renews His promises of land and descendants to Isaac in Gerar.

  • Genesis 26:12–18 – Isaac's prosperity, the Philistines' envy, the stopped wells, and Isaac's departure.

  • Genesis 26:19–22 – Esek, Sitnah, and Rehoboth. Isaac's explanation of the name is the heart of this study.

  • Genesis 26:23–31 – The LORD appears at Beersheba, Isaac builds an altar, and Abimelech seeks peace.

  • Genesis 36:31, 37; 1 Chronicles 1:48 – Saul of Rehoboth-by-the-River among Edom's early kings.

  • Psalm 4:1; 18:19; 31:8; 118:5 – God brings the distressed into a "broad place," using related vocabulary.

  • Psalm 37:11; Matthew 5:5 – The meek shall inherit the earth.

  • Hebrews 11:9–10 – Isaac as a sojourner in the land of promise, looking for God's city.

  • 1 Peter 2:23 – Christ did not retaliate but entrusted Himself to God.
