RAFTER - (rᵉchîyṭ, Strong's H7351)

Summary

The Hebrew word rᵉchîyṭ means rafter, one of the crossing timbers that hold up a roof or form a paneled ceiling. It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 1:17. There the bride says, "The beams of our houses are cedar, And our rafters of fir." Just before, she has said, "Also our bed is green" (Song of Solomon 1:16). Many readers take this to mean that the lovers are resting outdoors among living trees, so that in her eyes the branches overhead have become the ceiling of a royal house. The poem does not settle the point, but that reading fits both the green bed and the building language.

The woods she names give the verse its depth. Solomon lined the walls of the temple where God chose to dwell with cedar and covered its floor with cypress (1 Kings 6:15). When the bride names cedar and fir for the beams and rafters above her, her words echo the woods of that sanctuary. The Song does not draw the comparison itself, but the resonance sets the place of love between the king and his bride beside the language of God's house. This single rafter points toward a truth Scripture develops from Eden to the New Jerusalem: God's deepest purpose is to dwell with His people. In Christ, that dwelling becomes living, growing, and open to those He loves.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rᵉchîyṭ is a Hebrew noun. Because it occurs only in Song of Solomon 1:17, its meaning is worked out from that verse. The verse is a two-line poem in which the second line echoes the first. "Beams" of cedar in the first line stand beside "rafters" of fir in the second, so the word clearly names part of a roof or ceiling structure. Some translators prefer paneling or galleries, but all of these options describe the overhead framework of a building.

The Hebrew manuscripts preserve a small spelling difference in this word. The consonants written in the text and the reading noted in the margin differ by one letter. The difference does not change the general meaning, which remains a roof timber or ceiling panel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rᵉchîyṭ means a rafter: one of the timbers laid across the main beams of a building to carry its roof or form its ceiling. In Song of Solomon 1:17 the word may well be used figuratively, with the "rafters" being tree branches arching over the lovers, though the poem does not say so outright. The meaning of the word itself stays simple and architectural.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's one use depends on its pairing with "beams" in the line before it. The bride does not say her surroundings are like a house. She speaks as though the house already stands around her: "the beams of our houses... our rafters." She also uses the plural "houses," which may reflect the grandeur of what she sees. If the lovers are outdoors, the effect is to turn a grassy resting place into a palace, and to do so through the eyes of love.

The possessive "our" matters as much as the timber. The house belongs to both of them. Nothing in the verse suggests a guest admitted to someone else's hall. The bride speaks as one who belongs under that roof.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture often uses buildings to speak about God's presence. The tabernacle and temple were built so the Lord could dwell among His people, and the materials were chosen with care. Solomon's builders brought cedar and cypress down from Lebanon (1 Kings 5:8–10) for the temple; these were prized building woods. Cedar is fragrant and resists decay, and both trees keep their green through every season.

Scripture uses these trees as pictures of lasting life. "The righteous... shall grow like a cedar in Lebanon" (Psalm 92:12). In Hosea 14:8, the Lord speaks of Himself this way: "I am like a green cypress tree; Your fruit is found in Me." The God who calls Ephraim back from idols describes Himself as an evergreen, a living shelter that does not fail with the seasons. When the bride declares that the rafters above her are fir, her words picture a covering that is lasting, fragrant, and secure. Her language agrees with the way the rest of Scripture speaks of what God provides for those who belong to Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:16–17 and 1 Kings 6:15 — a living sanctuary. First Kings 6:15 describes the temple's interior: "he built the inside walls of the temple with cedar boards; from the floor of the temple to the ceiling he paneled the inside with wood; and he covered the floor of the temple with planks of cypress." Second Chronicles 3:5 adds that the larger room was "paneled with cypress which he overlaid with fine gold, and he carved palm trees and chainwork on it." Inside, God's house was cedar, cypress and gold carved with trees.

Then comes a poem associated with Solomon himself, the king who built that temple. In it, a bride speaks of a green bed and calls the cedar and fir above her beams and rafters. The NKJV calls the Song's tree "fir" and the temple's tree "cypress," but the two Hebrew words are spelled almost alike, and both are generally understood to name the cypress. The match is not exact: at the temple, cypress covered the floor (1 Kings 6:15) and paneled the larger room (2 Chronicles 3:5), not the rafters. The poem is a love song and should be read first as one. Still, a Solomonic setting, a royal lover, and the two great woods of the temple naturally bring the sanctuary to mind as a resonance.

The direction of the comparison is what makes it striking. The temple was a forest cut down, planed into boards, and carved with palm trees. If the bride is resting outdoors, the trees above her are still alive, and she needs no carved image of a forest. Under the old order, the priests ministered in the Holy Place, and only the high priest entered the innermost room, once a year (Hebrews 9:6–7). Under these green rafters, the bride rests at ease beside the king and calls the house "ours." In the poem, love makes her resting place something like a sanctuary.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Bible's story of God's dwelling begins under living trees. "The LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden" (Genesis 2:8) and made "every tree grow that is pleasant to the sight" (2:9). Humanity's first meeting place with God had no walls, only a canopy of His own planting. Sin closed that garden. Afterward, God's presence was housed in structures made by human hands, first a tent and then a temple of cut cedar and cypress.

The Song's green bed and the trees the bride names recall that first garden, where love and fellowship were sheltered by trees rather than boards. Scripture does not expressly name the Song as a picture of Eden. Still, the resemblance fits the direction of the whole biblical story. That story ends in a city with a garden at its heart. On either side of the river grows "the tree of life" (Revelation 22:2), and a voice announces, "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3).

Between the garden lost and the garden restored stands Jesus Christ. In Him, God's house becomes something that grows. Believers are "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ Himself being the chief cornerstone, in whom the whole building, being fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord" (Ephesians 2:20–21). Peter calls them "living stones... being built up a spiritual house" (1 Peter 2:5). The temple of dead wood gives way to a temple that is alive. The restricted entry of the old sanctuary gives way to "boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus" (Hebrews 10:19). The bride's delight beneath her rafters of fir offers a glimpse of what Christ secures: His people at home with Him, under a roof that is His own life.

7. Spirit & Life

Look at what makes the bride's roof a palace. She has no new timber, only the presence of the one she loves. "Behold, you are handsome, my beloved!" comes before "our rafters of fir." The same is true for the believer: what matters is Christ's presence, not the room. Paul tells believers that they "are being built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:22). The dwelling Paul describes is the people themselves, and they carry that reality wherever they gather. When believers meet in a hospital room, at a kitchen table, or on a bench under a tree, God is present among them there, because they are His house. Communion with God was never meant to be confined to a building, however fine its paneling.

A rafter also has a plain purpose: it holds up the roof that keeps out sun and storm. Isaiah promises that over Zion "over all the glory there will be a covering," and "a tabernacle for shade in the daytime from the heat, for a place of refuge, and for a shelter from storm and rain" (Isaiah 4:5–6). The Lord who called Himself "a green cypress tree" (Hosea 14:8) is the One who provides that covering. Whether the bride rests under the trees or under a real roof, she speaks as one who feels no exposure. She looks up and sees rafters. Believers live in the open, among real heat and real storms, but they live under the covering of Christ's love. That covering is evergreen and fragrant, and the seasons do not wear it away. Those who know His love can lie down in peace and call that shelter "ours."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Rᵉchîyṭ occurs only in Song of Solomon 1:17, which is discussed above. Its meaning depends on the verse just before it. In Song of Solomon 1:16, "our bed is green" suggests to many readers that the "rafters" are living branches overhead rather than the timbers of an actual building, though the poem leaves the setting open.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6982 (qôrâh), beam: a distinct word paired with rᵉchîyṭ in the same poetic verse ("The beams of our houses are cedar," Song of Solomon 1:17). The two words describe the roof structure together, with the main beams supporting the rafters. The pairing occurs in the verse itself, though the two words do not share a root.
