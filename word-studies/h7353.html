EWE, SHEEP - (râchêl, Strong's H7353)

Summary

The Hebrew word râchêl means ewe, a grown female sheep. It is a specific word. Hebrew uses other words for a sheep or goat in general or for a ram, but râchêl names the mother of the flock, the animal whose wool, milk, and lambs kept a shepherd's household alive. Scripture uses it only four times. In Genesis the ewes are Jacob's working wealth, the breeding stock he guarded through twenty hard years. The Song of Solomon turns a flock of freshly washed ewes, every one bearing twins, into a picture of clean, flawless beauty.

The word's most important use comes in Isaiah 53:7, where the suffering Servant is silent "as a sheep before its shearers." The animal that depended on a shepherd's costly care becomes a picture of the Servant, whom the New Testament reveals as Jesus, the Good Shepherd Himself, who went to His suffering without opening His mouth. The Gospels show Jesus fulfilling this before the priests and Pilate. The quiet ewe under the shears gives us one of Scripture's clearest views of how Christ went to the cross: willingly, without retaliation, entrusting Himself to God.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

In the pastoral world of Israel's patriarchs, a man's flock was his bank account, and the ewes were its principal. Rams were few. A healthy herd was built on many females who lambed year after year. Jacob's gift to Esau in Genesis 32:14 shows the ratio: "two hundred ewes and twenty rams."

The word also appears to be the source of a famous name. Rachel, Jacob's beloved wife, bears a name (Strong's H7354) spelled the same way in Hebrew, and it is generally understood to mean ewe. This shepherd's daughter, met at a well where flocks were watered (Genesis 29), carried the name of the animal her family lived by.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Râchêl has one sense: a ewe, an adult female sheep. The NKJV translates it "ewes" in Genesis and "sheep" in Song of Solomon 6:6 and Isaiah 53:7. Its range never stretches beyond the animal itself. What changes from passage to passage is which quality of the ewe the writer has in mind: her value, her fruitfulness, or her quiet vulnerability under the shepherd's hands.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The four occurrences fall into three settings. In Genesis 31:38 and 32:14 the ewes are literal livestock, a measure of wealth and of the care that produced it. In Song of Solomon 6:6 they become a poetic image of whiteness and health. In Isaiah 53:7 the ewe becomes a figure for the Servant's silence.

Isaiah's line depends on a parallel: "He was led as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before its shearers is silent." "Lamb" translates a general word for a flock animal (seh). "Sheep" translates râchêl. The two lines describe two different experiences, being killed and being shorn. Both animals go quietly.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these passages show God's people as a flock that depends on a shepherd. Ewes cannot guard themselves. In Genesis 31 Laban's ewes did not miscarry under Jacob's care, and Jacob himself absorbed the losses from wild beasts and thieves. Their safety came through someone else's watchfulness, and Jacob openly credits God with his prosperity (Genesis 31:9, 42).

Isaiah 53 reverses the picture. Earlier in the same chapter Isaiah has said that "all we like sheep have gone astray" (Isaiah 53:6). Then the Servant who bears their iniquity takes the place of the sheep. Here God does more than guard His flock from a distance. In the person of His Servant, He enters the flock's helplessness and receives the harm meant for them. The holiness that judges sin and the mercy that carries it meet in one silent figure.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 31:38–40: the ewes and the shepherd who paid for them. Jacob has fled Laban, and Laban has chased him down. Jacob answers with twenty years of grievance: "your ewes and your female goats have not miscarried their young, and I have not eaten the rams of your flock." Then he tells what that cost him. "That which was torn by beasts I did not bring to you; I bore the loss of it." He describes his days and nights: "In the day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night, and my sleep departed from my eyes." Jacob sets two things side by side: the flock did not lose its young, and he himself made good every animal torn by beasts or stolen (31:39). Both belong to the same faithful service, and Jacob credits God, who "has seen my affliction and the labor of my hands" (31:42).

Song of Solomon 6:6: a flock without a flaw. The bridegroom praises his beloved: "Your teeth are like a flock of sheep which have come up from the washing; every one bears twins, and none is barren among them." The image works on two levels. The ewes are freshly washed, so they are white and clean. Every one is also paired and whole, and "none is barren among them." The literal blessing of Genesis, ewes that do not miscarry, becomes poetry for complete, unblemished beauty in the eyes of one who loves. Nothing is missing, and nothing is spoiled.

Isaiah 53:7: silent before the shearers. "He was oppressed and He was afflicted, yet He opened not His mouth." Isaiah says it twice, at the beginning and end of the verse, so the silence frames everything between. People who are wronged usually defend themselves, accuse their accusers, and argue their case. The Servant does none of this. Isaiah's own comparison is simple: "as a sheep before its shearers is silent, So He opened not His mouth." The likeness lies in the silence. As the ewe makes no sound before those who shear her, the Servant makes no protest before those who wrong Him.

Isaiah's distinction between slaughter and shearing also deserves attention. Shearing strips away what covers and warms the animal. As an inference rather than an explicit statement of the text, the ewe's line suggests the humiliation that came before Christ's death: soldiers "stripped Him and put a scarlet robe on Him" (Matthew 27:28), and at the cross they divided His garments (John 19:23). He was stripped before He was slain.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Gospels show Isaiah 53:7 fulfilled in detail. "While He was being accused by the chief priests and elders, He answered nothing" (Matthew 27:12). When Pilate pressed Him, "He answered him not one word, so that the governor marveled greatly" (Matthew 27:14). In Acts 8:32–35, Philip begins with this very verse when he tells the Ethiopian official about Jesus. The New Testament explicitly reads Isaiah's silent sheep as Christ.

Peter explains what the silence meant: "when He was reviled, did not revile in return; when He suffered, He did not threaten, but committed Himself to Him who judges righteously" (1 Peter 2:23). Jesus was not silent because He was guilty or powerless. He was silent because He had given Himself into His Father's hands. Paul describes the same willing descent: "He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross" (Philippians 2:8).

Here the two halves of this word's story come together. Jacob's ewes were kept safe by a shepherd who bore losses for them. Jesus says, "The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11), and He promises that no one will snatch His sheep from His hand (John 10:28–29). Jacob's sacrifice offers a faint human outline of Christ's. Jacob bore losses and complained of them, while Christ bore sin and kept silent. The Shepherd who guards the flock is also the One who goes silently, like a ewe before her shearers, to His suffering. His people are made clean because He "Himself bore our sins in His own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24). The washed flock of the Song makes a fitting picture of that cleansed people, though the Song itself praises a bride's beauty, not the cross.

7. Spirit & Life

Christ's silence before His accusers is meant to be followed. Peter wrote 1 Peter 2:23 to servants who were suffering unjustly, and he said Christ left "us an example" (1 Peter 2:21). Christ's silence was not weakness. It was trust. Jesus could keep quiet because He had committed His case to the One who judges righteously. A believer who is misrepresented, mistreated, or stripped of reputation does not have to win every argument or settle every score. That believer can do what Jesus did and hand the matter to God.

The ewe passages also speak to what fruitfulness costs. The washed flock in which "none is barren among them" is lovely, and Genesis 31 shows such a flock thriving under a shepherd who bore heat, frost, sleeplessness and loss. The church's cleansing and fruit rest on a far greater cost: Christ's own sacrifice for our sins. A believer who sees this will not take spiritual growth for granted or treat it as self-made. It is the fruit of the Good Shepherd who "gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 32:14: "two hundred ewes and twenty rams." Jacob, afraid of meeting Esau, sends ahead a large gift drawn from the very ewes he had built up in Laban's service. The flock that cost him so much becomes his offering of peace to the brother he had wronged.

The other three occurrences (Genesis 31:38; Song of Solomon 6:6; Isaiah 53:7) are discussed above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7354, Rachel: The proper name spelled the same way as râchêl and generally understood to mean ewe (Genesis 29:6).

  • Canonical pairing: H7716 (seh), translated lamb in Isaiah 53:7 – The general Hebrew word for a flock animal stands beside râchêl in the same verse. The lamb goes to slaughter and the ewe goes to the shearers.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Because Rachel's name is generally understood to mean ewe, her story adds a suggestive echo, though it rests on the name rather than on this word itself. A ewe's value lay in her offspring. Rachel, whose name pictured a mother ewe, died giving birth to Benjamin "on the way to Ephrath (that is, Bethlehem)" (Genesis 35:19). Jeremiah later hears "Rachel weeping for her children, refusing to be comforted for her children, because they are no more" (Jeremiah 31:15). Matthew applies these words to the children killed in Bethlehem after Jesus' birth (Matthew 2:18). The mother ewe mourns her lost lambs near the place where the Lamb of God was born, the One who would go silently to the shearers and the slaughter so that God's scattered flock might be brought home.
