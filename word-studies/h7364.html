WASH, BATHE - (râchats, Strong's H7364)

Summary

The Hebrew verb râchats means to wash or bathe. It is used for water applied to a person's body, such as feet, hands, face or the whole body, and for the parts of an animal prepared for sacrifice. It first appears in Abraham's tent, where weary travelers were welcomed with water for their feet. It then becomes one of the working words of the tabernacle. Priests washed before they served, and unclean Israelites bathed so they could return to the worshiping community. Washing in Scripture is about who may come near and live in the presence of a holy God.

The prophets and poets carry the word further. Job admits that even snow water cannot make him clean before God. Isaiah hears God command Judah to wash themselves, and then hears Him promise that He will wash away Zion's filth. Ezekiel pictures the Lord rescuing an abandoned newborn whom no one had washed, raising her to womanhood, and then, after He has bound Himself to her in covenant, washing off her blood Himself. These passages lead to the upper room, where Jesus takes a basin and towel and tells Peter, "If I do not wash you, you have no part with Me" (John 13:8).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

In a land of dusty roads and open sandals, washing was a daily necessity. The first act of welcome for a guest was water for the feet (Genesis 18:4; 24:32; 43:24). Washing also marked the end of one condition and the start of another. People washed after mourning, before an important meeting, and before worship.

Israel's law took this ordinary act and set it inside the life of the tabernacle. That is why more than half of the word's uses fall in Exodus, Leviticus and Numbers. English Bibles use "wash" for several different Hebrew words. Râchats usually describes washing a person or a body. Leviticus normally uses a different verb, kâbas, for washing clothes. Many purity laws use both verbs in one sentence: a person washes his clothes and bathes in water (Leviticus 15:5).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Râchats means to wash with water, either part of the body or the whole of it. Its principal senses are these:

1. To wash part of the body, especially feet, hands or face. Joseph washed his face so he could hide his tears before his brothers (Genesis 43:31).

2. To bathe the whole body. Pharaoh's daughter went down to bathe at the river (Exodus 2:5), and the unclean bathed in water (Leviticus 15).

3. To wash the parts of a sacrifice before they were burned on the altar (Leviticus 1:9).

4. In a figurative sense, to cleanse a person morally or to declare oneself innocent. In this sense God may be the one who washes (Isaiah 4:4; Ezekiel 16:9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of each use depends on who is washing and why. In the hospitality scenes, the washing is a gift to a guest. Abraham offers water to his visitors (Genesis 18:4). Lot does the same (Genesis 19:2), and so does the old man of Gibeah (Judges 19:21). Abigail goes lowest of all. She offers to be a servant who washes the feet of David's servants (1 Samuel 25:41).

In the purity laws, a person usually washes himself. Leviticus 15 uses the word again and again for people defiled by bodily discharges. The point of these laws was not hygiene. Uncleanness kept an Israelite away from the sanctuary and from the shared life of the camp. Bathing, often together with washing clothes and waiting until evening, was the way God provided for such a person to come back (Leviticus 15:13; Numbers 19:19).

The grammar of the word that matters most is the shift between commands and promises. "Wash yourself" puts responsibility on people: on Naaman (2 Kings 5:10), on Ruth (Ruth 3:3) and on Judah (Isaiah 1:16). When God becomes the subject, so that He says "I washed you" (Ezekiel 16:9), washing changes from a duty into a gift.

The word also carries a legal symbol. When a murder was unsolved, the elders of the nearest town were to "wash their hands over the heifer" (Deuteronomy 21:6). Then they declared that their hands had not shed this blood (21:7). The psalmist draws on this practice: "I will wash my hands in innocence; So I will go about Your altar, O LORD" (Psalm 26:6). Asaph uses the same phrase when he wonders bitterly whether his integrity was worth anything (Psalm 73:13). Washed hands declared that a person was innocent. They could not create innocence where there was none.

4. Theological Significance

Washing in Scripture rests on a simple fact: God is holy, and nothing unclean may come casually into His presence. The laver stood between the altar and the tent of meeting (Exodus 30:18). No priest could step past it unwashed. God treated the approach to Himself as serious enough to cost a life. Yet His purpose was to bring people near. Every bathing law in Leviticus describes a way back into the community and its worship, not only a way out.

Scripture is equally clear that human washing reaches only so far. Job says, "If I wash myself with snow water, And cleanse my hands with soap, Yet You will plunge me into the pit" (Job 9:30–31). Proverbs describes a generation "pure in its own eyes, Yet is not washed from its filthiness" (Proverbs 30:12). People can deceive themselves into thinking they are clean while they remain filthy. Pilate later washed his hands and declared himself innocent of Jesus' blood (Matthew 27:24). Matthew uses a different, Greek word, but the scene shows how hollow the old gesture becomes when the heart is guilty. The deepest need, then, is for God Himself to wash.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The laver and the two washings (Leviticus 8:6; Exodus 30:18–21; John 13:10). At the priests' ordination, Moses brought Aaron and his sons and washed them with water (Leviticus 8:6). This was a single washing at the beginning of their service, done to them. After that, every time they entered the tent or approached the altar, they washed their hands and feet at the bronze laver, so that they would not die (Exodus 30:20–21; 40:31–32). One washing set them apart for God. The other washing was repeated as they served.

Jesus' words to Peter follow a similar pattern, and the priestly washings make a helpful comparison, though John does not draw the link himself. When Peter asks to be washed head to toe, Jesus answers: "He who is bathed needs only to wash his feet, but is completely clean" (John 13:10). John writes in Greek, and Jesus uses two different words, one for a full bath and one for washing part of the body. A disciple who has been made clean by Christ does not need to be cleansed from the beginning again. He does need his feet washed, because the road gets them dirty.

Commanded, then promised (Isaiah 1:16; 4:4). Isaiah opens with God's call to rebellious Judah to wash themselves, make themselves clean, and put away their evil deeds (Isaiah 1:16). The following chapters show how little that call produced. Then, in chapter 4, the same verb returns with God as the one acting: "When the Lord has washed away the filth of the daughters of Zion, and purged the blood of Jerusalem from her midst, by the spirit of judgment and by the spirit of burning" (Isaiah 4:4). The washing God demanded, He Himself will accomplish. It will be fiery and searching rather than gentle. Many readers see God's own Spirit at work in "the spirit of judgment," and the passage clearly makes the cleansing His doing.

The abandoned infant (Ezekiel 16:4, 9). Ezekiel describes Jerusalem's beginnings as a newborn thrown into an open field. No one cut her cord, and no one washed her in water (16:4). The Lord passes by while she lies struggling in her blood and says to her, "Live!" (16:6). Under His care she grows into a young woman (16:7). When He passes by again, He spreads His wing over her and enters into a covenant with her, and she becomes His (16:8). Only then does He say, "Then I washed you in water; yes, I thoroughly washed off your blood, and I anointed you with oil" (16:9). The washing she never received at birth is given to the grown woman the Lord has made His own, and He does it Himself. She does nothing to earn any of it. God does not wait for her to become presentable before He comes near. He gives her life, binds Himself to her, and then makes her clean.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word's story moves toward a basin in the upper room. In Genesis, a host offered water and a servant did the washing. Abigail offered to wash the feet of servants. In John 13, the Lord of the house is the one who stoops. Jesus "rose from supper and laid aside His garments, took a towel and girded Himself" (13:4). He did this knowing "that the Father had given all things into His hands" (13:3). Simon the Pharisee did not even provide water for His feet (Luke 7:44). Now Jesus serves with His own hands.

His words to Peter, "If I do not wash you, you have no part with Me" (John 13:8), carry the message of Ezekiel 16 and Isaiah 4. Fellowship with God depends on being washed by Him. Hebrews then gathers the priestly language and applies it to every believer: "let us draw near... having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water" (Hebrews 10:22). What the laver taught a few priests now describes the whole people of God.

The burnt offering may also point to Christ. Before it was burned as "a sweet aroma to the LORD," its entrails and legs were washed (Leviticus 1:9). The text does not explain the detail. Still, a sacrifice made clean inside and out before it was wholly given to God fits well with the One who offered Himself without spot.

7. Spirit & Life

The laver and the basin bring real freedom. Jesus' words "He who is bathed needs only to wash his feet, but is completely clean" (John 13:10) assure a disciple that the One who washed him has truly made him clean. In the upper room, Jesus Himself puts the weight on having a part with Him (13:8) and on serving one another (13:14). He does not explain the foot washing as a picture of confession. Still, the priestly pattern of one washing at ordination and repeated washing for service is a helpful comparison, and John's first letter supplies the promise for the daily dirt of the road: "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9). A believer who has failed in the course of a day has not lost his place at Christ's table. He needs to confess honestly and receive that cleansing afresh, which is not the same as doubting his salvation. The priest who skipped the basin was in danger, and so is the Christian who stops coming to be cleansed.

Naaman shows the humility this requires. He was ordered to wash seven times in the Jordan. He was furious and asked whether the rivers of Damascus were not better (2 Kings 5:12). The Jordan's water had no power in itself. Naaman was healed when he stopped arguing with God's word and went down into the river. Pride still wants a cleansing that suits our sense of dignity.

Finally, Jesus turns the gift into a calling: "you also ought to wash one another's feet" (John 13:14). Those He has washed become people who stoop to serve others.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ruth 3:3 – "Therefore wash yourself and anoint yourself." At Naomi's direction, Ruth washes and prepares herself to approach Boaz at the threshing floor, where she will ask him to take her under his wing as her close relative (Ruth 3:9).

  • 2 Samuel 12:20 – "So David arose from the ground, washed and anointed himself... and he went into the house of the LORD and worshiped." David washes as he ends his mourning and accepts God's decision.

  • 2 Samuel 11:2 – Bathsheba's bathing becomes the occasion of David's sin. The same ordinary act can be innocent or become an occasion for temptation.

  • Song of Solomon 5:3 – "I have washed my feet; How can I defile them?" The bride's comfort delays her answer to her beloved's knock. Washing serves as an excuse not to rise, the reverse of Genesis 18.

  • Ezekiel 23:40 – Jerusalem washes herself for foreign lovers. Preparation that once belonged to love is spent on unfaithfulness.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3526 (kâbas), wash (clothes) – This is a distinct Hebrew verb, not a relative of râchats. The purity laws pair the two often, as in "wash his clothes and bathe in water" (Leviticus 15:5). David uses kâbas in his prayer, "Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity" (Psalm 51:2), where he pictures sin as a deep stain that must be scrubbed out.
