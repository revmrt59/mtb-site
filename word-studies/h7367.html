WASHING - (rachtsâh, Strong's H7367)

Summary

The Hebrew word rachtsâh means washing. In the Bible it describes one familiar country scene: a flock of sheep climbing out of the water after being washed, their wool clean and white. The word appears only twice, and both times it comes from the bridegroom in the Song of Solomon as he praises his bride: "Your teeth are like a flock of shorn sheep Which have come up from the washing" (Song of Solomon 4:2). In Song of Solomon 6:6 he repeats the same washing comparison, this time without the word "shorn." The picture suggests a smile that is bright, even and complete, with no tooth missing and every tooth matched.

The image means more because of where it stands. In chapter 4 the groom sums up his praise with "there is no spot in you" (Song 4:7). Then, after the bride hesitates to open the door and loses him for a time (Song 5:2–8), he praises her again in chapter 6 with almost exactly the same words. Other passages of Scripture use related pictures of a washed, spotless and fruitful people. God promises to cleanse His people and multiply them like a flock (Ezekiel 36). Christ washes His church so that He can present her "without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rachtsâh is a Hebrew noun related to rachats (H7364), the ordinary verb for washing. That verb is used mainly for washing or bathing people or parts of the body and includes the ceremonial washings of the Law. Washing clothes is usually expressed by a different verb, kâbaç (H3526). The noun is much narrower. It never appears in the Law, the histories or the Prophets. Its only two uses come in the Song of Solomon, inside poems where the groom describes his bride's beauty one feature at a time.

The setting is pastoral. Israel was a land of shepherds, and the people who first heard these verses would have known the sight well: a crowded line of sheep coming up out of a stream or pool after washing, dripping and bright. Song 4:2 joins the washing to shearing ("shorn sheep"), which shows that clean, freshly cut wool is the picture in view.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rachtsâh means a washing, either the act of washing sheep or the place where it happens. In both verses "the washing" is the water the flock "come up from." The word has a single meaning. It does not mean ritual purification or forgiveness. Its spiritual depth comes from how the Song uses the image and how the rest of Scripture develops pictures of washing, flocks and white wool.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word always appears inside a simile, a comparison that uses "like." The bride's teeth are "like a flock." Three details fill out the comparison. The first is whiteness: the sheep are newly washed. The second is symmetry: "every one of which bears twins," which probably means each tooth is matched by its partner. The third is completeness: "none is barren among them," so no gap breaks the smile.

The verb "come up" matters to the picture. It shows the flock in motion, rising out of the water at the moment they are cleanest. Song 6:6 drops the word "shorn" and changes "every one of which bears twins" to "every one bears twins." Otherwise the two verses match.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats the body and its wholeness as good gifts. The groom's delight in a whole, healthy smile is worth celebrating in its own right. God made bodies, and the Song does not apologize for enjoying them.

The image also belongs to a larger pattern in Scripture: washing produces whiteness, and whiteness stands for cleanness before God. The Lord says through Isaiah, "Though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; though they are red like crimson, they shall be as wool" (Isaiah 1:18). Washed wool is exactly what the flock in the Song carries up out of the water. Isaiah takes that ordinary sight and makes it a picture of complete forgiveness. Red stains become white wool, and only God can make that change.

Scripture also joins cleanness to fruitfulness. The washed flock is clean and also bears twins, with none barren. Elsewhere, too, God joins cleansing His people with making them fruitful (Ezekiel 36).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 4:2 and 4:7. The first use comes in the groom's long praise of his bride. He moves from her eyes to her hair to her teeth, then gathers everything into one statement: "You are all fair, my love, and there is no spot in you" (Song 4:7). The washed flock belongs to that verdict. Bright, matched and complete teeth are one part of a bride in whom he finds nothing to correct. In its first sense the poem celebrates the love between a husband and wife. It also gives the Bible an early and vivid picture of a bride declared spotless.

Song of Solomon 6:6 after 5:2–8. The repetition of the image is the most striking feature of these two verses. Between chapter 4 and chapter 6, the relationship goes through real trouble. The groom knocks at night, calling her "my perfect one" (5:2). She delays and gives her reasons: "I have taken off my robe… I have washed my feet; how can I defile them?" (5:3). By the time she opens the door, "my beloved had turned away and was gone" (5:6). She searches the city, the watchmen strike her, and she ends up "lovesick" (5:8).

When the groom speaks to her again in chapter 6, he does not scold her or lower his opinion of her. He repeats the same praise: a flock come up from the washing, every one bearing twins, none barren (6:6). His words after her failure match his words before it. The Song does not call this doctrine, but it shows a love that holds its view of the beloved steady through her lapse.

Ezekiel 36:25, 37–38. This passage does not use rachtsâh, but it joins the same two ideas: cleansing and the flock. God promises, "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean; I will cleanse you from all your filthiness and from all your idols" (Ezekiel 36:25). A few verses later He promises to increase His people like a flock, as full as the flocks that crowded Jerusalem at the feasts (Ezekiel 36:37–38). The order matters. God washes first and then multiplies. The flock in the Song can serve as a concrete picture of that promise: sheep coming up clean, fruitful and complete because Someone has brought them through the water.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Bible's bride story reaches its fulfillment in Christ. Paul writes that Christ gave Himself for the church "that He might sanctify and cleanse her with the washing of water by the word, that He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing, but that she should be holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:26–27). Paul does not quote the Song, but both passages picture a bride without blemish: the groom praises his bride, "there is no spot in you" (Song of Solomon 4:7), and Christ cleanses His church so that she will have no spot and be without blemish. Paul himself links marriage to Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:32), which gives readers solid ground for comparing the groom's delight in his bride with Christ's delight in the church He has washed.

The story ends at a wedding. "The marriage of the Lamb has come," and the bride is "arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright" (Revelation 19:7–8). Jesus calls Himself "the good shepherd" (John 10:11), and He is also the Bridegroom of Ephesians. He cleanses His people, makes them fruitful and brings them to Himself.

Jesus also shows that being washed is something He does for us and in us. When Peter refused to let Him wash his feet, Jesus said, "If I do not wash you, you have no part with Me," and then added, "He who is bathed needs only to wash his feet, but is completely clean" (John 13:8, 10). Christ's washing makes a person clean. The passage sets no schedule of cleansing, but elsewhere the New Testament teaches that a believer who sins is restored through confession (1 John 1:9) without undoing that first washing.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride in Song 5 refused her beloved partly because of her own washing: "I have washed my feet; how can I defile them?" Her stated concern for staying clean kept her from opening the door. When the groom praises her again, though, the cleanness he names is the flock come up from the washing, and that cleanness is his view of her, not her own protective effort. Believers sometimes hold Christ at a distance for similar reasons, guarding their comfort or their sense of being in order instead of answering His knock. The remedy is to open the door. In the Song, the groom called his bride "my perfect one" while she was still slow to answer (Song of Solomon 5:2). Christ's love for those He has washed is no less patient.

The repetition between chapters 4 and 6 speaks plainly to anyone who has failed Christ after once delighting in Him. The groom's praise in chapter 6 is not a reduced version of what he said before. The New Testament gives believers solid ground for expecting that steadiness from Christ. John writes, "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9). A lapse calls for confession and cleansing, not for a new foundation. The believer comes back to be forgiven and restored to fellowship, not to earn acceptance a second time.

The washed flock is also a fruitful flock. Christ cleanses His people for life and fruitfulness, the way God in Ezekiel washes first and then multiplies. Cleanness in Scripture does not mean sitting idle in a protected, spotless state. It is the condition in which God's people bear fruit.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Rachtsâh appears only in Song of Solomon 4:2 and 6:6, and both verses are developed in Section 5 above. No other occurrences remain to list.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7364 (rachats), wash. This is the lexical relative of rachtsâh: the common Hebrew verb for washing or bathing people or parts of the body, including ritual washing, from the same word family. In Song of Solomon 5:3, "I have washed my feet" translates this verb, close to the occurrences of rachtsâh.

  • Thematic connections. Isaiah 1:18 (wool made white), Ezekiel 36:25 (clean water) and Ephesians 5:26 (the washing of water by the word) share the image of washing, but they use other words.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Both verses picture the flock "coming up" from the water. Scripture often joins rising from water with new life, and Paul speaks of believers buried with Christ through baptism so that "we also should walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4). The Song does not make this connection itself. Still, the picture of a clean flock rising out of the washing and going on to bear twins fits well with what baptism signifies: going down into the water, coming up clean, and walking out into a fruitful new life.
