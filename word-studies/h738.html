LION - (ʼărîy, Strong's H0738)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼărîy is the Old Testament's ordinary word for the lion, one of the strongest and most feared animals ancient Israel knew. Scripture uses it for real lions that raided flocks and killed travelers. It also uses it for the lions carved into Solomon's temple furnishings and set beside his throne. Most often, the lion serves as a picture. Judah is "a lion's whelp." The wicked lie in wait like lions. The Lord Himself roars like a lion, both when He warns His people of judgment and when He calls His scattered children home.

The lion has two faces in the Bible. It pictures God's appointed King, and it pictures the enemy who devours. David and Samson each face a lion before they deliver Israel. The sufferer of Psalm 22 cries out to be saved from the lion's mouth. The prophets look ahead to a day when "the lion shall eat straw like the ox." In Revelation, the elder announces "the Lion of the tribe of Judah," and John turns to see a Lamb who has been slain. Through this word, believers learn which Lion to fear. They are to fear the Lord, whose roar brings His people home. They are to stand firm against the devil, who only prowls and roars like a lion.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Lions lived in the thickets of the Jordan valley and the wild country around Israel. Shepherds and villagers knew them well. Hebrew has several words for lions at different stages of life. ʼĂrîy, which also appears in the closely related spelling ʼaryēh, is the general word for the full-grown animal. It appears throughout the Old Testament: in Jacob's blessing, in the stories of Samson and David, in the Psalms and Proverbs, and especially in the prophets. Neighboring kingdoms placed lions at palace gates and beside royal seats as symbols of a king's power. Israel used the same image but tied the lion's strength to the God who made it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means "lion." Its uses fall into three related kinds:

1. A real, living lion, as when "a young lion came roaring against" Samson in the vineyards of Timnah (Judges 14:5).

2. A carved or crafted lion figure, such as the lions on the panels of the temple carts (1 Kings 7:29) and the twelve lions on the steps of Solomon's throne (1 Kings 10:20).

3. A lion as a picture of a person, nation, or power, usually in a comparison such as "like a lion." Wicked men, a destroying army, and the Lord Himself are all described this way.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the lion is a real animal, it is almost always a threat to the helpless. In those stories the lion is the predator and a lamb, a traveler, or a flock is its prey. When the lion becomes a picture, the details that matter are its roar, its sudden spring from hiding, and the way it tears its prey with no one to rescue the victim. David prays, "Lest they tear me like a lion, Rending me in pieces, while there is none to deliver" (Psalm 7:2). Proverbs applies the same image to government: "Like a roaring lion and a charging bear Is a wicked ruler over poor people" (Proverbs 28:15).

Who or what the lion stands for decides the meaning of the picture. When the lion is Judah or Israel, it stands for strength and the right to rule (Numbers 23:24; 24:9). When the lion is an enemy, it stands for cruelty. When the lion is the Lord, it stands for a voice and an action no one can resist. The carved lions in Solomon's temple and palace carry the image of royal majesty into Israel's center of worship and government. Of the throne, Scripture says, "nothing like this had been made for any other kingdom" (1 Kings 10:20).

4. Theological Significance

The Bible never lets the lion's strength stand on its own. The animal that seems most self-sufficient is fed by God: "The young lions roar after their prey, And seek their food from God" (Psalm 104:21). God asks Job, "Can you hunt the prey for the lion, Or satisfy the appetite of the young lions" (Job 38:39). These verses use other Hebrew words for lions, but they belong to the same family of images. One of the fiercest creatures Israel knew depends on its Creator for every meal.

God also commands lions. When foreign settlers in Samaria "did not fear the LORD," Scripture says plainly, "therefore the LORD sent lions among them" (2 Kings 17:25). Even the report sent to the king of Assyria recognized what was happening: the God of the land "has sent lions among them" (2 Kings 17:26).

Above the earthly lions stands the heavenly one. In Ezekiel's vision of God's glory, each living creature had "the face of a lion on the right side" (Ezekiel 1:10; compare 10:14). John later sees a creature "like a lion" before the throne (Revelation 4:7). The majesty people admire and fear in the lion is a small reflection of the strength that serves around the throne of God. This helps explain why the lion can stand for God's King and also for God's enemies. Strength belongs to the Lord, and people can either receive it in righteousness or seize it for cruelty.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 49:9 and Ezekiel 19 — the royal lion and its corruption. Jacob blesses his fourth son: "Judah is a lion's whelp; From the prey, my son, you have gone up. He bows down, he lies down as a lion; And as a lion, who shall rouse him?" The picture is of a lion that has already won. It returns from the hunt and lies down at rest, and no one dares disturb it. The next verse promises that the scepter will not depart from Judah, so the lion becomes the emblem of the royal line God would raise from that tribe.

Centuries later, Ezekiel takes up the same image as a funeral song. The mother of Israel's princes is a lioness who raised cubs among lions, but each young lion "learned to catch prey, And he devoured men" (Ezekiel 19:3, 6). The first cub was "trapped in their pit, And they brought him with chains to the land of Egypt" (19:4). This fits Jehoahaz, whom Pharaoh carried off to Egypt. A second cub ends up caged and taken to Babylon. Judah's kings were meant to be lions at rest, guarding their people. They became lions that preyed on their people instead, and they were hunted down like common beasts. The blessing never gave Judah permission to oppress. By the time of the exile, the lion of Judah was still waiting for a King who would rule righteously.

Amos 3 and Hosea 11 — the Lord who roars. Amos builds his message with a series of questions about cause and effect: "Will a lion roar in the forest, when he has no prey?" (Amos 3:4). "Will a bird fall into a snare on the earth, where there is no trap for it?" (3:5). Each question has an obvious answer. He ends with this point: the lion has roared, so who will not be afraid? The Lord God has spoken, so who can keep from prophesying (3:8)? A roar means something real is coming. God's warning is not empty noise, and the prophet cannot keep silent once he has heard it. Jeremiah hears the same roar in the Babylonian army: "The lion has come up from his thicket, And the destroyer of nations is on his way" (Jeremiah 4:7).

Hosea 11 uses the same image for a different purpose. That chapter is full of the Lord's tenderness toward Israel, the child He taught to walk. Then comes the promise: "They shall walk after the LORD. He will roar like a lion. When He roars, Then His sons shall come trembling from the west" (Hosea 11:10). Here the roar gathers instead of scattering. The children tremble, but they come home. The same voice that warns rebels of judgment is the voice that calls exiles back to their Father.

1 Kings 13:24–28 — the lion that did not eat. A man of God delivered the Lord's word at Bethel, then disobeyed the Lord's direct command not to eat there. On his way home, "a lion met him on the road and killed him." What the lion did next is the point of the story. The old prophet found "the donkey and the lion standing by the corpse. The lion had not eaten the corpse nor torn the donkey" (13:28). A hungry lion would have done both. This lion killed and then stood by the body, which made its deed a sign to everyone who passed (13:25). The old prophet understood: "the LORD has delivered him to the lion... according to the word of the LORD" (13:26). God's judgment did exactly what His word said and nothing more.

Psalm 22 — the sufferer among lions. Psalm 22 begins with the cry Jesus spoke from the cross: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" The psalm then describes enemies circling like beasts, with mouths open like a roaring lion (22:13), and ends its plea with a cry to be saved from the lion's mouth (22:21). Verse 16 in the NKJV reads, "They pierced My hands and My feet." This line involves a real difference among ancient copies. The standard Hebrew text reads, literally, "like a lion my hands and my feet." The ancient Greek translation and one of the Psalms scrolls found near the Dead Sea point to a verb such as "they dug" or "they pierced." Either reading leaves the sufferer surrounded by savage attackers who maul his hands and feet. The Gospels show this psalm being fulfilled at Calvary, where soldiers even divided His garments as verse 18 describes (John 19:24). There the Messiah stood in the jaws of every devouring power on behalf of His people.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The lion who failed in Ezekiel 19 is answered in Revelation 5. John weeps because no one is worthy to open God's scroll, and an elder says, "Do not weep. Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has prevailed" (Revelation 5:5). This title comes straight from Genesis 49:9. When John turns to look, he sees a Lamb standing as though it had been slain (5:6). Jesus is the Lion of Judah, the One with the full right to rule and whom no one can rouse or overthrow. Yet He won His victory by being slain. The Lion did not stop being strong. He showed that God's strength conquers through obedience, sacrifice, and resurrection.

The deliverers of Israel point toward Him. The Spirit of the Lord rushed upon Samson, and he tore the roaring lion apart (Judges 14:6). David told Saul that when "a lion or a bear came and took a lamb out of the flock" (1 Samuel 17:34), he went after it and rescued the lamb. David also gave the Lord the credit for delivering him from the lion's paw (1 Samuel 17:37). Benaiah "killed a lion in the midst of a pit on a snowy day" (2 Samuel 23:20). Samson and David each faced a lion before they served God's people more widely, and Benaiah's lion is counted among the deeds that marked him as one of David's mighty men. David's story fits Jesus' words most closely. The hired hand runs away, but "The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). David risked his life for one lamb. Christ gave His life for the flock.

The story ends with the lion changed. "The lion shall eat straw like the ox" (Isaiah 11:7), and Isaiah 65:25 repeats the promise for the new heavens and new earth. In that day God will do more than shield His people from lions. He will remove the violence from creation itself, so that the apex predator becomes a grazer. Christ's reign reaches that far.

7. Spirit & Life

Peter tells believers, "your adversary the devil walks about like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour" (1 Peter 5:8). His words echo the Psalms' picture of the wicked as lions. The command that opens the verse shows his aim: "Be sober, be vigilant." The call is to watchfulness rather than panic, because the devouring enemy is real and too strong for anyone to face alone. The Psalms have already taught God's people where to run: to the Lord, who delivers when "there is none to deliver."

Notice also what the devil's roar is: an imitation. Satan prowls like a lion. The true Lion of Judah has already prevailed. Believers do not need to treat the adversary's noise as if it were the final word.

Proverbs adds a sharp warning about imaginary lions: "The lazy man says, 'There is a lion outside! I shall be slain in the streets!'" (Proverbs 22:13; compare Proverbs 26:13). The excuse is ridiculous: he exaggerates danger to excuse doing nothing. That is the point of the proverb. A reluctant heart will invent dangers to justify staying in bed. Many believers have avoided a hard conversation, an act of obedience, or a step of service by describing lions that were never there. The Lord's roar calls His people to come; the sluggard's lion gives him a reason to stay where he is.

Samson's riddle offers one more picture. When he returned to the lion's carcass, he found bees and honey inside it. Out of the eater came something to eat, and out of the strong came something sweet (Judges 14:8, 14). The Bible does not interpret the riddle as a symbol, but it fits the gospel well. When the Anointed One conquers the devourer, what once threatened death becomes food for God's people. Trials met in Christ's strength often leave behind something sweet.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 24:9 — Balaam, hired to curse Israel, instead describes the nation lying down like a lion that no one dares to rouse. He uses the same picture Jacob used for Judah.

  • Deuteronomy 33:22 — Moses calls Dan "a lion's whelp." The lion image of strength belonged to more than one tribe.

  • 2 Samuel 1:23 — David laments that Saul and Jonathan were swifter than eagles and stronger than lions. Even the strongest fall in death.

  • Ecclesiastes 9:4 — The Preacher observes that a living dog is better than a dead lion. Life and hope matter more than past glory.

  • Isaiah 31:4 — The Lord comes down to fight for Mount Zion like a lion growling over its prey, unafraid of the shepherds shouting against it. Here His fierceness protects His people.

  • Lamentations 3:10 — The grieving poet feels that God has been to him like a lion in hiding. Scripture lets suffering believers speak that honestly before they reach "His compassions fail not."
