POMEGRANATE - (rimmôwn, Strong's H7416)

Summary

The Hebrew word rimmôwn names the pomegranate, a red, crowned fruit packed with juicy seeds, and the small tree that bears it. Israel knew it as everyday food from the land God promised them. Scripture places it in a surprising range of settings. Pomegranates are carried back by the spies as proof that the land was good. They are woven onto the hem of the high priest's robe and cast in bronze by the hundreds on the tops of the temple pillars. The prophets use the pomegranate tree to measure blessing and judgment, and the Song of Solomon uses the fruit to describe a bride's beauty and the sweetness of married love.

Taken together, these uses tie the pomegranate to the good, abundant life God gives His people. It belongs to the harvest of the land, it is reproduced as an ornament in the places of worship, it withers in the prophets' judgments and it is promised again when God restores His people. Scripture does not explain the ornaments, but placing the fruit of the land on the priest's robe and at the entrance of God's house sets His gifts right beside His presence. For Christians, these pictures point to Jesus Christ, whom the New Testament calls our High Priest and the true temple. In Him the life these pomegranates pictured is given to His people.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

The pomegranate grew well in Israel's hill country. Its leathery rind holds hundreds of seeds, each wrapped in deep red, sweet-tart juice, and the top of the fruit forms a small crown. People ate it fresh and pressed it for juice. The Bible's uses fall into a few clear settings: the farming life of the Promised Land, the furnishings of the tabernacle and temple, the warnings and promises of the prophets, and the love poetry of the Song of Solomon. One occurrence simply marks a place. Saul sat "under a pomegranate tree which is in Migron" (1 Samuel 14:2).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rimmôwn is the ordinary Hebrew name for the pomegranate. The context shows which of three related uses is meant:

1. The fruit itself, as food and as part of the land's harvest (Numbers 13:23; Deuteronomy 8:8).

2. The tree that bears the fruit, as in Saul's pomegranate tree (1 Samuel 14:2) and Joel's list of withered trees (Joel 1:12).

3. A crafted ornament shaped like the fruit. These ornaments were woven in colored yarn on the priest's robe (Exodus 28:33) or cast in bronze for the temple pillars (1 Kings 7:20).

The word itself means pomegranate. The richer meanings come from where Scripture places the fruit.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting changes what the pomegranate communicates. In the Torah it is evidence: the land God promised really does produce good things. In Exodus and Kings it becomes art. Craftsmen reproduce the fruit in yarn and bronze at the edges of holy places, so it is something to be seen rather than eaten. In Joel and Haggai the living tree becomes a measure of how things stand between God and His people. In the Song of Solomon the fruit's color and juice become the language of love.

The pomegranate often appears alongside the vine, the fig tree and the olive tree (Deuteronomy 8:8; Haggai 2:19). Together these plants stand for the whole goodness of the land.

4. Theological Significance

The pomegranate shows that God's gifts of life and His presence belong together. The same fruit that marked the Promised Land as good was placed at the entrance of God's house and on the robe of the man who served there. The juxtaposition invites readers to see the harvest and the sanctuary together. The God who filled the land with fruit was the God who lived among His people.

Many interpreters read the temple's carved palms, flowers and fruit as echoes of Eden, the first place where God and humanity lived together. Scripture never explains the ornaments directly, but the pattern is hard to miss. Wherever God makes a dwelling with His people, Scripture surrounds it with fruit.

The pomegranate also shows that God's blessing follows His people's relationship with Him. In Joel's judgment the trees wither (Joel 1:12). When the people return to building His house, He promises fruit again (Haggai 2:19). His gifts are never separated from Himself.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The priest's hem (Exodus 28:33–35). God commanded pomegranates "of blue, purple, and scarlet, all around its hem, and bells of gold between them all around" (Exodus 28:33), "a golden bell and a pomegranate, a golden bell and a pomegranate" (Exodus 28:34). The next verse explains why the bells mattered: their sound was to be heard when Aaron went in and out of the holy place, so that he would not die. Exodus explains the bells but not the pomegranates, yet the two are set side by side on one garment. The bells point to the seriousness of approaching a holy God. Beside them hangs a fruit of the Promised Land, so the man who represented Israel before God carried images of the land's fruitfulness to the very edge of that holiness, into the place where sinful people could not otherwise survive. God's holiness was never meant to end in death. He provided a way for His people to come near and live.

The crowned pillars (1 Kings 7:18–20, 42). Solomon set two great bronze pillars, named Jachin and Boaz, at the porch of the temple. Their capitals, the decorated tops of the pillars, were ringed with "two rows of pomegranates above the network" (1 Kings 7:18), "two hundred such pomegranates in rows on each of the capitals all around" (1 Kings 7:20). That made "four hundred pomegranates for the two networks" (1 Kings 7:42). The pomegranates were placed at the highest point of the pillars that marked the way into God's house. Jachin is usually understood to mean He establishes, and Boaz in Him is strength. Scripture does not explain the fruit on the capitals, but the pairing is meaningful: pillars whose names speak of God's strength were crowned with the fruit of His good land. The same bronze fruit appears again in the record of Jerusalem's fall, when the Babylonians broke the pillars apart and carried the bronze away (2 Kings 25:17; Jeremiah 52:22–23). The fruit that greeted worshipers became plunder once the people abandoned the God who lived there.

Fruit from the land and grumbling in the wilderness (Numbers 13:23; 20:5; Deuteronomy 8:8). The spies returned from the Valley of Eshcol with grapes carried on a pole between two men, and "some of the pomegranates and figs" (Numbers 13:23). The people held real proof of God's promise in their hands, yet they refused to enter. Years later, still in the wilderness because of that unbelief, they complained that Moses had brought them to a place with no grain, figs, vines or pomegranates (Numbers 20:5). They contended with Moses over the very fruit their unbelief had kept from them (Numbers 20:3–5). Later, on the plains of Moab, Moses described the land God was still giving: "a land of wheat and barley, of vines and fig trees and pomegranates" (Deuteronomy 8:8). The pomegranate was evidence that God keeps His word and gives generously, even to people who have doubted Him.

The withered tree and the promised blessing (Joel 1:12; Haggai 2:19). In Joel's day locusts and drought stripped the land: "The pomegranate tree, the palm tree also, and the apple tree—all the trees of the field are withered; surely joy has withered away from the sons of men." Joel ties the dead orchard to the loss of joy among the people. Haggai speaks to the people after they returned to rebuilding God's house, and his words reverse the scene. God dates His promise from the day the temple's foundation was laid (Haggai 2:18), then says: "As yet the vine, the fig tree, the pomegranate, and the olive tree have not yielded fruit. But from this day I will bless you" (2:19). God gave His word while the branches were still bare. The people had to trust the promise before they saw any fruit.

The bride's beauty and sweetness (Song of Solomon 4:3; 6:7; 8:2). The groom says, "Your temples behind your veil are like a piece of pomegranate" (4:3; repeated in 6:7). A cut pomegranate shows glowing red seeds that were hidden inside the rind. The comparison pictures the bride's beauty as vivid and alive, yet veiled and reserved for her husband. Later the bride offers "spiced wine, of the juice of my pomegranate" (8:2). Her love is pictured as something sweet and joyful that she gives freely. The Song celebrates married love as one of God's good gifts, and the same Scripture uses the image of a bride elsewhere for God's love for His people.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pomegranate meets us at two places of approach to God: the high priest's robe and the temple's pillars. The New Testament reveals Jesus as both. He is "a great High Priest who has passed through the heavens" (Hebrews 4:14), and when He spoke of the temple, "He was speaking of the temple of His body" (John 2:21). The pomegranates on Aaron's hem and on Solomon's capitals pictured life at the doorway of God's presence. Jesus does more than wear or display such pictures. He says, "I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through Me" (John 14:6).

The life Israel glimpsed in Eshcol's fruit, saw plundered when Babylon broke the bronze pillars, and heard promised again over Haggai's bare orchard finds its fullest expression in Christ. Scripture's story moves from the garden of Eden toward the tree of life that heals the nations in Revelation 22.

7. Spirit & Life

Haggai's promise was given to the returned exiles at a particular moment, and it does not guarantee good harvests to every believer. It does show how God works with His people. He said "from this day I will bless you" before a single pomegranate had ripened. Faith rests on His word before any visible results appear. The people who set God's house first heard His promise first and saw the fruit later.

The bells and pomegranates on the priest's robe have long been read as a picture of the believer's life, since Christians are now "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9). The ringing bell suggests a clear confession of the truth. The silent fruit suggests a life marked by "love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control" (Galatians 5:22–23). This is an analogy, not the meaning of the passage, but it makes a sound point. A testimony with no fruit rings hollow, and fruit without a clear confession of the truth leaves people without the message that explains it.

Joel's withered orchard offers a sober warning as well. Joel describes a judgment on the whole land, and he ties the withered trees to joy that "has withered away from the sons of men" (Joel 1:12) before calling the people to "return to the LORD your God" (Joel 2:13). Not every loss of joy signals spiritual failure; many faithful people in Scripture grieved. Joel's warning is that a shared dryness among God's people can have its root in turning from Him, and the remedy he names is to return.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 39:24–26 records that the craftsmen made the pomegranates and bells exactly as God commanded. Their careful obedience followed the pattern God had given.

  • 1 Samuel 14:2 places Saul "under a pomegranate tree" at Migron. Here the tree simply marks the place where Saul waited.

  • 2 Chronicles 3:16; 4:13 repeat the account of the temple pomegranates, confirming the four hundred on the pillar networks.

  • Song of Solomon 4:13 describes the bride as "an orchard of pomegranates with pleasant fruits," a garden of delight.

  • Song of Solomon 6:11; 7:12 watch for the pomegranates to bloom. Their blossoming signals spring and the readiness of love: "There I will give you my love."
