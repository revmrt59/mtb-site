DROPS, BITS - (râçîyç, Strong's H7447)

Summary

The Hebrew spelling râçîyç stands for two different words that Strong's lists under one number, H7447. Each appears in just one verse, and the two scenes could hardly feel more different. In Song of Solomon 5:2 the first describes the "drops of the night" on the hair of a lover who stands at his beloved's door, knocking and calling. In Amos 6:11 the second describes the "bits" a great house is broken into when the LORD commands judgment on a proud and self-satisfied nation.

The two words are distinct, and their shared spelling proves nothing about either verse. Read side by side, however, the two passages raise a common theme. In both, the problem is ease. The bride in the Song is too comfortable to get up and open the door. The leaders in Amos are "at ease in Zion," lounging on ivory beds while the nation falls apart. The Song's lover is a human bridegroom, but his knock offers a picture, by analogy, of Christ, who says, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock" (Revelation 3:20). In the same message Christ tells a complacent church, "Be zealous and repent" (Revelation 3:19), and Amos shows that God will not leave entrenched pride standing forever. Read alongside Revelation 3, these passages call God's people to wake up and answer Christ's knock.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Râçîyç is the spelling of two Hebrew nouns that Strong's groups under one number, each found in only one verse: one in a love poem, the other in a prophetic oracle of judgment. Most standard Hebrew lexicons list two separate entries, one meaning drop (of dew) and one meaning fragment or bit (of a ruined building). The older Strong's gloss, dripping to pieces, tries to hold both meanings under one idea, but the better evidence points to two words with the same form. Readers should not build a word picture that blends tenderness and destruction into one Hebrew term. Any connection between the passages comes from their themes, not their spelling.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Drop, droplet of moisture. Small drops of night dew gathered on hair, as in "my locks with the drops of the night" (Song of Solomon 5:2).

2. Fragment, small broken piece. The rubble of a shattered structure, as in "He will break the great house into bits" (Amos 6:11).

Both words describe something small. In the Song, the small thing is part of a lover's appeal at the door. In Amos, it is what remains of a home once considered secure.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Song, the drops are part of a lover's appeal. He gives them as his reason for asking the bride to open: "For my head is covered with dew." The wet hair shows he has been out in the night; the poem does not say how long.

In Amos, the word sits in a pair of parallel lines: "He will break the great house into bits, and the little house into pieces." The parallel lines show that the judgment reaches both mansion and cottage. The setting is a decree. It begins "the LORD gives a command," so the ruin is presented as the LORD's purposeful act. It is not described as an accident of war or weather.

4. Theological Significance

Set side by side, Amos and the Song (read by analogy as a picture of divine love) show a range in how God deals with His people that comes from one consistent character. The God who loves seeks fellowship, and He is willing to wait for it. The God who is holy cannot be mocked forever by people who use His blessings to insulate themselves from Him and from the suffering of others.

Amos makes God's attitude toward pride unmistakable: "I abhor the pride of Jacob, and hate his palaces" (Amos 6:8). The LORD says this under oath, swearing "by Himself." The houses that will lie in bits belong to the proud people whose palaces He hates, monuments to their self-trust. His judgment is also impartial. The "great house" and the "little house" fall together, because no level of wealth or status puts anyone beyond His reach.

The Song is about human love, but its knocking lover pictures the other side of the same God. Christ uses the same image when He says, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock" (Revelation 3:20). By that analogy, love that knocks, calls by tender names and asks rather than forces illustrates a Lord who desires a willing response.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:2–3 — the lover in the dew. The bride is half-asleep: "I sleep, but my heart is awake." She hears his voice and his knock. He pleads with four names of affection: "my sister, my love, my dove, my perfect one." Then he explains why she should hurry. His head is soaked, "my locks with the drops of the night." He has not broken in, and he has not left. He stands outside and asks.

Her answer is painfully ordinary: "I have taken off my robe; how can I put it on again? I have washed my feet; how can I defile them?" (5:3). Nothing she says is wicked. She is simply settled in for the night, and the inconvenience of getting up outweighs the voice at the door. The verses that follow show the cost. When she finally rises and opens, he has gone, and she has to go out into the city searching for him. Comfort did not end the love, but it interrupted the fellowship. The scene shows that the moment to open is when he knocks.

Amos 6:1–11 — the houses broken into bits. Amos addresses people who are "at ease in Zion" and who "trust in Mount Samaria" (6:1). He describes their comfort in vivid detail, speaking to those "who lie on beds of ivory, stretch out on your couches," who eat the best lambs and calves, "who sing idly to the sound of stringed instruments," and "who drink wine from bowls, and anoint yourselves with the best ointments" (6:4–6). The final line of the indictment carries the charge: they "are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph" (6:6). Their nation is sick and breaking apart, and they do not grieve.

They also "put far off the day of doom" (6:3) and assume the reckoning is a long way off. God's answer is that it is coming now. "Those who recline at banquets shall be removed" (6:7), and the homes that held their couches will be broken into bits. Their comfort is the very thing the judgment targets. They have used it to feel secure apart from God and indifferent toward their suffering brothers.

The two passages side by side. The bride's ease is a sleepy hesitation, and the leaders' ease in Amos has hardened into arrogance and indifference to their neighbors' affliction. The two differ in seriousness, and the outcomes differ accordingly. The bride loses a night of fellowship and must seek her beloved. Israel loses its houses and goes into captivity. Taken together, the two passages illustrate one danger. When comfort becomes more important than responding to God, the heart grows slow. The bride shows a passing slowness; the leaders in Amos show settled complacency.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jewish and Christian readers have long heard in the Song a picture of the Lord's love for His people. The New Testament does not cite Song of Solomon 5:2 directly. The image of the knocking lover returns, however, in Jesus' words to the church at Laodicea: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20).

The Laodicea passage echoes both of these passages, though it quotes neither. That church sounds like the people of Amos 6. They say, "I am rich, have become wealthy, and have need of nothing," without knowing they are "wretched, miserable, poor, blind, and naked" (Revelation 3:17). Christ warns that their lukewarmness will bring His rejection: "I will vomit you out of My mouth" (3:16). Yet in the same message He stands at their door like the lover in the dew, knocking and offering a meal. The One who judges complacency also patiently seeks the complacent.

Jesus teaches the same readiness in Luke 12:36. His servants are to be like men waiting for their master "when he will return from the wedding, that when he comes and knocks they may open to him immediately." The bride in the Song delayed; Luke's servants are told to open immediately. The whole biblical story moves toward the Bridegroom's return. The question for His people is whether He will find them awake and ready to answer His knock.

7. Spirit & Life

The Song's lover is a human bridegroom, but by analogy his knock pictures how Christ deals with drowsy believers. In Revelation 3 He does not answer a lukewarm church with an immediate crushing blow. He rebukes because He loves, calls them to "be zealous and repent," and stands at the door and knocks (Revelation 3:19–20). That patience is real, and it should move us. It is not permission to stay in bed. The bride's excuses sound reasonable: she is already undressed and her feet are washed. Most of our delays sound just as harmless. In the Song a closed door on a quiet night cost the bride her beloved's nearness and a hard search through the city. By analogy, delay can cost a believer the sense of Christ's nearness, and recovering it may mean seeking Him with effort we could have spared ourselves.

Amos shows where unchecked ease leads. He does not say how Israel's leaders reached this point, only where they stand: at ease, trusting in Samaria, and no longer grieved over "the affliction of Joseph." One test of whether comfort has become dangerous is whether we can still feel the pain of God's people and still hear His voice when it is inconvenient. A believer who has stopped grieving over what grieves God should take Amos seriously. Both passages point to Christ's answer: repent and open the door (Revelation 3:19–20).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The two words occur only in Song of Solomon 5:2 ("drops") and Amos 6:11 ("bits"), both discussed above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

Possible lexical roots. The word meaning drop is commonly linked to a verb meaning to moisten, listed as H7450 (Ezekiel 46:14). The word meaning fragment is linked to a separate verb meaning to crush or to break in pieces, listed as H7533. These derivations are probable, not certain, and support reading the uses as two words.

Thematic pairing. Song of Solomon 5:2 and Revelation 3:20 share the image of the knocking lover; Amos 6:1–7 and Revelation 3:15–17 share the theme of comfortable self-sufficiency under rebuke. These are connections of image and theme, not lexical relatives.
