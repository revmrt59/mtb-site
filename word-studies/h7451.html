EVIL, BAD, CALAMITY - (raʻ, Strong's H7451)

Summary

The Hebrew word raʻ is the Old Testament's ordinary word for what is bad. It is the opposite of "good," and it covers a wide range. It can describe moral wickedness: the evil a person plans, speaks, or does against God and neighbor. It can also describe what is harmful or painful without any sin implied, such as sickly cattle, a heavy task, a sad face, a dangerous animal, or a national disaster. English translations reflect this range with words like evil, wickedness, harm, trouble, adversity, calamity, and disaster. Context decides which kind of "bad" is meant.

That range matters most when Scripture says God brings raʻ. When the Lord declares in Isaiah 45:7 that He creates "calamity," He is claiming authority over disaster, and He is not confessing to sin. The word follows the whole story of Scripture. It begins at the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and runs through God's diagnosis of the human heart before and after the flood, the prophets' warnings, and the promise that God will turn from sending disaster when people turn from their evil. It also appears in the psalmist's confidence: "I will fear no evil; for You are with me."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Raʻ is a common Hebrew word that works as an adjective ("bad," "evil") and as a noun ("evil," "harm," "disaster"). Its natural partner is "good," and the two often stand side by side. Examples include the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, Moses setting life and good, death and evil before Israel (Deuteronomy 30:15), and Isaiah's woe on those who call evil good and good evil (Isaiah 5:20).

The word appears in nearly every part of the Old Testament: law, history, psalms, wisdom, and prophecy. Jeremiah uses it far more than any other book. This fits his message, which deals with Judah's wickedness and the disaster coming because of it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, raʻ means bad, harmful, or displeasing, whatever falls short of what is good. Its main senses are:

1. Morally evil or wicked. This sense describes thoughts, deeds, and people opposed to God's will. Jeremiah calls Jerusalem to wash her heart from wickedness (Jeremiah 4:14).

2. Harmful, painful, or calamitous. This sense describes trouble, adversity, or disaster that falls on people, often as judgment. Jeremiah warns of disaster coming from the north (Jeremiah 4:6).

3. Bad in quality or condition. This sense describes things that are poor, ugly, sickly, or unpleasant with no moral meaning at all. Examples are Pharaoh's thin, ugly cows (Genesis 41:3), the "burdensome" task of searching out life under the sun (Ecclesiastes 1:13), and Nehemiah's sad face before the king (Nehemiah 2:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the historical books, raʻ is part of a repeated verdict. Again and again, Israel or its king did what was evil in the sight of the Lord (Judges 2:11; 1 Kings 11:6; 2 Kings 21:2). The phrase locates the standard of evil in God's own judgment. Popular opinion, political success, and the king's preference do not set it.

In the prophets, the word often appears twice in one sentence with two different senses. It names the people's wickedness and the disaster God sends in response. In wisdom literature, it marks the path the wise person refuses to walk (Proverbs 4:14, 27). In the Psalms, it names both the schemes of the wicked and the dangers from which the faithful cry for rescue.

When Saul is troubled by a "distressing spirit" (1 Samuel 16:14), the word describes the torment Saul experienced. It does not describe a sin he committed. Readers who assume raʻ always means "sinful" will misread many passages.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture is clear that God is never morally evil. The psalmist confesses that God takes no pleasure in wickedness and that evil cannot dwell with Him (Psalm 5:4). Habakkuk says God's eyes are too pure to look on evil with approval (Habakkuk 1:13). This must stay in view whenever the word is used of God's actions.

Because God is holy and chose to live among His people, moral evil could not be left alone in Israel. Deuteronomy repeats one command across very different cases: put away the evil from among you. The command applies to the false prophet who leads people after other gods (Deuteronomy 13:5), the idolater (17:7), the one who defies the priest and judge (17:12), the false witness (19:19), the persistently rebellious son (21:21), sexual immorality (22:21–22), and the kidnapper (24:7). Each case was handled through witnesses and judges. The refrain shows that evil does not stay private. It spreads through a community, and a people who bear God's name must deal with it. God's holiness is a reason for His people to be clean, and it is also the reason His presence among them is possible.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 2:9, 17; 3:5, 22). The word's first appearances are tied to a tree and a command. God set humanity in a garden full of good and reserved one thing to Himself: the right to say what is good and what is evil. The serpent's offer went beyond tasting something forbidden. He promised that the man and woman would be like God, knowing good and evil (Genesis 3:5). They would decide for themselves. When they reached for that fruit, they chose to judge good and evil on their own terms, and the death God warned of followed (2:17). Much of the later evil in Scripture grows from this root. People who decide for themselves what is good soon call evil good.

The heart before and after the flood (Genesis 6:5; 8:21). Before the flood, the Lord saw that every intention of the human heart was nothing but evil, all the time. After the flood, with Noah's family safe and a fresh start begun, God gave nearly the same diagnosis: the inclination of the human heart is evil from youth. The flood removed a generation, but the heart that survived in the ark was the same kind of heart. In Genesis 8 that sobering fact leads to mercy. God promises never again to strike every living thing as He had done. God's patience is extended to people whose hearts still need changing. The problem in Genesis 8 waits for the answer Ezekiel later announces: a new heart and a new spirit, after which God's people will remember their evil ways and loathe them (Ezekiel 36:26, 31).

God over calamity (Isaiah 45:7; Amos 3:6). In Isaiah 45 the Lord speaks to Cyrus, a pagan king raised up for Israel's sake, and makes a sweeping claim: "I form the light and create darkness, I make peace and create calamity; I, the LORD, do all these things." The surrounding nations spread the world between rival powers, with gods of blessing on one side and forces of ruin on the other. Isaiah allows no such division. There is no second power running the dark half of the world. Amos makes the same claim through a question from city life: "If there is calamity in a city, will not the LORD have done it?" (Amos 3:6). In Amos the calamity is judgment on Israel's sin, so the disaster carries a message. Here the word clearly means disaster. It is not wickedness, and the Holy One is not doing wrong. Job speaks from the same conviction in his grief: "Shall we indeed accept good from God, and shall we not accept adversity?" (Job 2:10). Lamentations 3:38 says the same from the ruins of Jerusalem: both woe and good come from the mouth of the Most High.

The two evils of Jeremiah 18 and Jonah 3. At the potter's house, God explains how His warnings work: "if that nation against whom I have spoken turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster that I thought to bring upon it" (Jeremiah 18:8). The same Hebrew word appears twice in that sentence. It names the nation's evil and the disaster God intends. Jonah shows the principle carried out in a pagan city: "God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God relented from the disaster that He had said He would bring upon them, and He did not do it" (Jonah 3:10). God's warnings of judgment are an open door. The disaster He threatens is meant to turn people away from the deeper ruin of their sin. Jonah resented this, because he knew God's character. He knew the Lord is gracious and slow to anger and relents from doing harm (Jonah 4:2).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of raʻ begins with humanity grabbing the knowledge of good and evil. Isaiah 7:15–16, which Matthew connects to the birth of Jesus (Matthew 1:23), describes the child Immanuel as one who will know to refuse the evil and choose the good. Adam reached for that knowledge apart from God. Here is a Son who knows good and evil and chooses rightly. Isaiah does not state that contrast, but the resonance is real. Christ is the one human whose heart was not evil from youth.

Joseph's words to his brothers give the clearest picture of how God deals with human evil: "But as for you, you meant evil against me; but God meant it for good, in order to bring it about as it is this day, to save many people alive" (Genesis 50:20). The pattern is unmistakable. At the cross, the pattern reaches its height. Lawless hands put Jesus to death, and by God's determined purpose His death became the salvation of many (Acts 2:23). The worst moral evil ever committed did not escape God's rule. It became the means by which the evil of the human heart is forgiven and the promised new heart is given.

7. Spirit & Life

Proverbs gives a practical answer to the evil heart: the fear of the Lord. To fear Him is to hate evil (Proverbs 8:13), and by the fear of the Lord a person turns away from evil (Proverbs 16:6). Willpower aimed at sin seldom holds. A heart that has come to stand in awe of God begins to find evil distasteful because it opposes the One it loves. Proverbs 3:7 sets this against self-made wisdom: do not be wise in your own eyes, fear the Lord, and depart from evil. That is Eden reversed. The fear of the Lord gives God back His right to define good and evil.

For the evil that comes as trouble, Psalm 23 is the believer's confession: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; for You are with me." David is walking through that valley, not around it. His courage rests on who walks beside him, and the danger is still there. Psalm 121:7 adds the promise behind that courage: "The LORD shall preserve you from all evil; He shall preserve your soul." The believer may still face hardship. The promise is that no evil can take a soul out of the Lord's keeping. Because Isaiah 45 and Amos 3 rule out any rival power, the believer's dark valley is never ruled by some other god. The One who allows the valley is the Shepherd who walks through it with him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 1:1. Job is described as a man who feared God and turned away from evil. The pairing anticipates Proverbs and sets up the test of his faith.

  • Psalm 34:14. The psalmist urges the reader to turn from evil and do good, and to seek peace and pursue it. Turning from evil is paired with an active pursuit.

  • Isaiah 5:20. Isaiah pronounces woe on those who call evil good and good evil. Moral confusion is treated as a judgment-worthy sin, and it continues what began in Eden.

  • Jeremiah 24:2–3. Jeremiah sees a basket of very bad figs, too bad to eat. The word describes spoiled fruit, which then becomes a picture of a spoiled people.

  • Jeremiah 29:11. God tells the exiles His thoughts toward them are of peace and not of evil, meant to give them a future and a hope. The same God who sent disaster in judgment intends good beyond it.

  • Zephaniah 3:15. With judgment removed and the Lord as King in her midst, Zion will no longer see disaster. This points ahead to a day when God's presence ends the fear of calamity.
