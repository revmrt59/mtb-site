NEIGHBOR, FRIEND, COMPANION - (rêaʻ, Strong's H7453)

Summary

The Hebrew word rêaʻ names the person beside you. That may be the neighbor across the field boundary, the companion in the camp, the trusted friend whose counsel you seek, or even a lover. In its plainest use it simply means another. The Hebrew phrase a man to his rêaʻ is the ordinary way of saying one to another. The setting decides how close the relationship is, but the word always places one person in relation to someone else.

This everyday word carries much of the Old Testament's teaching about life with others. The Ten Commandments protect a neighbor's reputation, marriage and property. Leviticus 19:18 commands, "you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD," and Jesus later placed this command beside love for God. Job and Proverbs test what real friendship is. The prophets often measure Israel's faithfulness by how neighbors treat one another.

The word also reaches toward God. In Exodus 33:11 the LORD speaks with Moses "as a man speaks to his friend." The verse compares the way God spoke with Moses to the open way friends talk. The word that governs how we treat the people next to us also describes that nearness. The New Testament carries the theme further when Jesus calls His disciples friends and makes known to them what He heard from the Father (John 15:15).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rêaʻ is a common Hebrew noun found from Genesis to Malachi. It appears most often in the laws of Exodus and Deuteronomy, in Proverbs, and in Jeremiah. The NKJV usually translates it "neighbor," but also "friend," "companion," "another," and occasionally "lover" or "husband."

In Israel's world, a neighbor was a familiar person you dealt with every day. He farmed the next plot, borrowed your ox, held your silver for safekeeping and stood with you at the city gate, where disputes were settled. The laws of Exodus 22:7–14 assume exactly this kind of shared life. That is why so many laws about the rêaʻ deal with practical matters such as boundary stones, borrowed animals and grain in a neighbor's field (Deuteronomy 19:14; 23:25).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, rêaʻ means a fellow person standing in some relationship to oneself. Four senses stand out:

1. Another person; each other. The word can be almost colorless, as when the builders at Babel "said to one another" (Genesis 11:3), or when the sailors spoke to one another and cast lots (Jonah 1:7).

2. Neighbor or fellow member of the community. This is the person whose life, goods and good name the Law protects, as in "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16).

3. Friend or companion. The word describes a close and chosen relationship, such as Hushai, "the king's companion" (1 Chronicles 27:33), the friends of Job and the friend of Proverbs.

4. Lover or marriage partner. The bride in the Song of Solomon says of her beloved that he is her friend (Song of Solomon 5:16). The prophets use the word for Israel's "lovers," the rivals that drew her loyalty away from the LORD (Jeremiah 3:1), and for the husband she betrayed (Jeremiah 3:20).

Because the meaning is so flexible, closeness must come from the passage. In one battle scene the word refers to an "opponent" (2 Samuel 2:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Law, rêaʻ marks the person whose rights Israel must protect. In Proverbs it names both the neighbor to be treated fairly and the friend whose loyalty is tested. In the Prophets it becomes a way of measuring a whole society's health.

One grammatical pattern matters. The phrase each man to his rêaʻ expresses mutual action: people doing something to each other. In passages of divine judgment, that phrase turns violent. When the LORD threw the Midianite camp into panic, He "set every man's sword against his companion" (Judges 7:22). The same thing happened to the Philistines in 1 Samuel 14:20 and to the armies that came against Jehoshaphat (2 Chronicles 20:23), and Zechariah 14:13 foresees it among the nations. The phrase that describes ordinary human connection becomes, under judgment, a picture of that connection collapsing.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture ties a person's treatment of the neighbor directly to his relationship with God. The last commandments of the Decalogue protect the rêaʻ: Israel must not bear false witness against a neighbor or covet a neighbor's house, wife, servants or ox (Exodus 20:16–17; Deuteronomy 5:20–21). The command against coveting reaches behind every outward act to the heart's desires. The commandment calls for inward contentment with what God has given. Harm to a neighbor often begins when a person starts wanting what belongs to the man next door, and it can grow into theft, false witness or adultery.

Proverbs explains why betraying a neighbor is so serious: "Do not devise evil against your neighbor, For he dwells by you for safety's sake" (Proverbs 3:29). Living beside someone is an act of trust. To plot against that person is to turn the safety of home into a trap. For this reason, "He who despises his neighbor sins" (Proverbs 14:21).

The prophets link this kind of betrayal with unfaithfulness to God. Israel had "played the harlot with many lovers" (Jeremiah 3:1). The word normally used for a friend here names the rivals who received the love that belonged to the LORD. In Jeremiah these are above all false gods (Jeremiah 3:9), but the prophet also rebukes Judah for running to Egypt and Assyria for help (Jeremiah 2:18, 36). In Jeremiah the two failures go together: the people who deceive their neighbors are the same people who "do not know Me" (Jeremiah 9:3). Jeremiah's message suggests that when people give their deepest loyalty to substitutes for God, faithfulness among neighbors is soon in danger.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 19:13–18. The famous command to love one's neighbor stands inside a chapter about holiness. The verses around it are concrete:

  • Do not keep a hired worker's wages overnight until morning (Leviticus 19:13). Deuteronomy 24:14–15 adds a rule for the hired servant who is poor and needy: he must be paid each day, before the sun goes down.

  • Do not spread slander (Leviticus 19:16).

  • Do not take vengeance or nurse a grudge (Leviticus 19:18).

Again and again the chapter ends a command with "I am the LORD." Loving a neighbor is an expression of reverence for the God who stands behind each command. The chapter leaves no room to claim reverence for Him while cheating the worker or slandering the man next door. Jesus named this command second only to loving God with all one's heart, and He said the whole Law and the Prophets hang on these two (Matthew 22:37–40). Paul adds that every commandment about the neighbor is "summed up in this saying, namely, 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself'" (Romans 13:9).

Exodus 33:11. After Israel's sin with the golden calf, Moses met God at the tent outside the camp, and "the LORD spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend." Hebrew uses rêaʻ overwhelmingly for relationships between people. Here the verse uses that word in a comparison: the LORD spoke with Moses in the open, direct way a man speaks with his friend.

The same chapter guards against misunderstanding. When Moses asks to see God's glory, he is told that no one can see God's face and live (Exodus 33:20). "Face to face" therefore describes direct, personal speech rather than a full sight of God's glory. Even so, the holy God chose to speak with Moses the way friends speak.

Job 2:11; 6:14; 42:7–10. Job's three companions are called his rêaʻ. At first they acted like true friends. They "made an appointment together to come and mourn with him, and to comfort him" (Job 2:11). Their comfort then turned into accusation, and Job protested, "To him who is afflicted, kindness should be shown by his friend" (Job 6:14) and "My friends scorn me; My eyes pour out tears to God" (Job 16:20).

The ending of the book is striking. God's wrath was against the friends, "for you have not spoken of Me what is right" (Job 42:7). Yet God sent them to Job so that Job would pray for them. Then comes the turning point of the whole story: "And the LORD restored Job's losses when he prayed for his friends" (Job 42:10). The man they had wounded became the one who prayed for them.

Jeremiah 9 and Zechariah 8. Jeremiah describes a nation so corrupt that everyone must beware of his neighbor and trust no brother. People deceive their neighbors, train their tongues to lie, and speak peace with their mouths while setting an ambush in their hearts (Jeremiah 9:4–8).

Zechariah looks back on a similar time, when God "set all men, everyone, against his neighbor" in judgment (Zechariah 8:10). He then records what God promises. The LORD will return to Zion and dwell in the midst of Jerusalem (Zechariah 8:3), and He is determined to do good to Jerusalem and the house of Judah (Zechariah 8:14–15). Then the LORD tells the restored community what they must do: speak the truth each man to his neighbor, give judgment in their gates for truth, justice and peace, and let none think evil in his heart against his neighbor or love a false oath, for He hates these things (Zechariah 8:16–17). In Jeremiah, broken trust between neighbors is the sign of a people far from God. In Zechariah, the promise that God will return and dwell among His people comes with His command that they deal truthfully with their neighbors, so that renewed trust is part of the life He calls them to.

Proverbs 17:17 and 27. Proverbs says that a friend loves at all times and that a brother is born for adversity (Proverbs 17:17). Chapter 27 gathers its counsel about friendship:

  • A friend's heartfelt counsel is as sweet as perfume (Proverbs 27:9).

  • Do not forsake your own friend or your father's friend (Proverbs 27:10).

  • A loud blessing shouted early in the morning can feel like a curse (Proverbs 27:14).

  • As iron sharpens iron, a man sharpens his friend's countenance (Proverbs 27:17).

Real friendship in Proverbs is active. It stays loyal, speaks sincerely and accepts the friction that comes when one life sharpens another.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of the rêaʻ begins with a broken bond. At Babel, people spoke "to one another" as they set out to build a city and tower and "make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:3–4). God answered by confusing their language so that they could not understand one another's speech (11:7). Their project ended with God's judgment separating them from each other. At Pentecost the Spirit enabled people from many nations to hear God's works in their own languages (Acts 2:6–11). Acts does not call this a reversal of Babel, but many readers see a meaningful parallel between the two scenes.

The prophets present Israel's unfaithfulness as a broken love story. In Hosea 3:1 God tells Hosea to "love a woman who is loved by a lover and is committing adultery, just like the love of the LORD for the children of Israel, who look to other gods." The verse itself makes the comparison: Hosea's love for this woman is to be like the LORD's love for Israel. Chapter 3 does not name her. Many read her as Gomer, Hosea's wife from chapter 1, and interpreters also differ on whether the "lover" is Hosea or another man. However those questions are answered, the LORD makes Hosea's love for an unfaithful woman a living picture of His own love for Israel. Through Jeremiah, He still calls, "Yet return to Me" (Jeremiah 3:1).

The psalms of the righteous sufferer show a deeper loneliness: "My loved ones and my friends stand aloof from my plague" (Psalm 38:11), and "Loved one and friend You have put far from me" (Psalm 88:18). The Gospels do not quote these psalms at the cross, but the cross offers a close parallel. At His arrest the disciples "all forsook Him and fled" (Mark 14:50). At the cross His acquaintances and the women from Galilee watched from a distance (Luke 23:49), while John places His mother, several other women and the disciple whom He loved near the cross (John 19:25–27). The One called "a friend of tax collectors and sinners" (Matthew 11:19) was deserted by His disciples at His arrest, and through His death God reconciled those who had been His enemies (Romans 5:10). He told His disciples that no one has greater love than one who lays down his life for his friends (John 15:13). He also said, "No longer do I call you servants… but I have called you friends, for all things that I heard from My Father I have made known to you" (John 15:15). Moses' face-to-face meetings with God were unique to him (Numbers 12:8; Deuteronomy 34:10). Yet the friendship and openness that Exodus 33:11 pictures, Jesus extends to all who follow Him: He calls them friends and tells them what the Father has said.

Jesus also refused to let anyone use "neighbor" as a way to limit love. The lawyer asked, "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29). Jesus answered with the story of a Samaritan who stopped for a wounded stranger. Then He turned the question around: "So which of these three do you think was neighbor to him who fell among the thieves?" (Luke 10:36). When the lawyer answered, "He who showed mercy on him," Jesus said, "Go and do likewise" (Luke 10:37). The parable's point is that command to show mercy. By analogy, many readers have also seen Christ Himself in the rescuer who binds wounds and pays the cost, though the parable does not say so. Jeremiah looked ahead to a new covenant in which no one would need to teach his neighbor, "Know the LORD," because all would know Him (Jeremiah 31:34).

7. Spirit & Life

Exodus 33:11 shows that God is not content to rule from a distance. Scripture uses a word for human friendship to describe how He spoke with Moses. Believers are not promised Moses' unique face-to-face meetings, but in Christ they are called friends and told what Jesus heard from the Father (John 15:15). Prayer is speech with Someone who has chosen to make His purposes known.

That nearness to God changes how we treat the people beside us. Leviticus 19 does not let love remain a feeling. Love pays wages promptly, refuses gossip and lets go of grudges, all under the words "I am the LORD." The neighbor down the street or across the office is the place where reverence for God becomes visible.

Job's story shows how far this goes. The book records no apology from his friends. The restoration came when Job prayed for the men who had misrepresented him and God. In a similar way, Jesus prayed for those who crucified Him (Luke 23:34). A believer who has been wounded by friends can become their intercessor rather than their accuser.

Proverbs adds that true friends sharpen one another (Proverbs 27:17). Their honest counsel may cause friction, and sometimes it hurts. That kind of friendship is part of how God makes His people wise.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 19:4–5 — A man who kills his neighbor by accident, such as when an axe head flies off, may flee to a city of refuge. God's law distinguished between tragedy and murder.

  • Proverbs 3:28 — Do not tell your neighbor to come back tomorrow when you can help him today. Love for a neighbor includes prompt help.

  • 2 Samuel 16:17 — Absalom challenges Hushai's loyalty to "your friend" David. In fact Hushai had returned at David's request to defeat Ahithophel's counsel (2 Samuel 15:34).

  • Esther 9:19, 22 — After their deliverance, the Jews celebrated by sending presents to one another. Rescue overflowed into generosity toward neighbors.

  • Zechariah 3:10 — In the day of God's cleansing, each person will invite his neighbor to sit under his vine and fig tree. Peace with God brings neighbors together in safety and rest.

  • Malachi 3:16 — Those who feared the LORD spoke to one another, the LORD listened, and a book of remembrance was written before Him. God notices faithful conversation among His people.
