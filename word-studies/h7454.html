THOUGHT, THOUGHTS - (rêaʻ, Strong's H7454)

Summary

The Hebrew word rêaʻ means a thought: what a person has in mind, including intentions and purposes. It is one of the rarest words in the Old Testament. It appears only twice, and both times in Psalm 139. In verse 2 David uses it for his own mind: "You understand my thought afar off." Fifteen verses later he uses it for God's mind: "How precious also are Your thoughts to me, O God!"

The pairing is what makes this word worth studying. The same uncommon word describes the thinking of a man and the thinking of God, and the psalm sets the two side by side. God reads David's thought easily and from a distance. When David tries to take in God's thoughts, he cannot even count them. The human mind is fully open to God, while God's mind is far beyond human grasp. Yet those vast thoughts are turned with care toward the very person they surpass. Because of this, David's dread of being exposed becomes a prayer: "Search me, O God, and know my heart" (Ps. 139:23).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rêaʻ belongs entirely to Psalm 139, David's meditation on the God who knows him, surrounds him, and formed him in the womb. The usual Old Testament words for "thought" are far more common. That makes David's choice of this rare term stand out, especially since he uses it at two important turns in the same poem.

Lexicons give rêaʻ the sense of thought, purpose, or aim, so the word seems to carry a sense of intention and not only passing ideas. Its meaning has to be read from Psalm 139 itself, since there are no other occurrences to compare.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rêaʻ means a thought, meaning the content of the mind, including what a person intends or purposes. With only two occurrences, both in one context, a single definition is enough. In verse 2 it refers to David's inner life before any word reaches his tongue. In verse 17 it refers to God's purposes and considerations. The word itself simply means "thought." What makes God's thoughts precious comes from the surrounding psalm, not from the dictionary meaning.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The two uses form a deliberate pair. The first describes human thought under God's gaze (v. 2). It comes right after the outward details of daily life: "You know my sitting down and my rising up." The psalmist moves from what anyone could observe to what no one could, which is what is going on in his mind.

The second describes divine thought under human wonder (v. 17). Here the direction reverses. David stops describing how God looks at him and starts trying to look into God.

The NKJV's renderings follow a small but telling difference. David speaks of "my thought," singular, and of God's "thoughts," plural, which are so many that David marvels at "the sum of them." One human mind stands before a God whose thoughts cannot be numbered.

4. Theological Significance

Psalm 139 teaches that God's knowledge reaches all the way into the human person. He knows what we do and also what we are thinking before we say it. No part of the mind is closed off to Him.

The psalm also refuses to present this knowledge as cold surveillance. The God who knows David's thought is the God who formed him in the womb and saw his days before they unfolded (vv. 13–16). Knowledge and care belong together in Him. He knows us as a Maker knows His work and as a Father knows His child.

The pairing of rêaʻ also reveals God's transcendence without distance. His thoughts are higher than ours, as the LORD says in Isaiah: "My thoughts are not your thoughts" and "as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are My ways higher than your ways, and My thoughts than your thoughts" (Isa. 55:8–9). Isaiah's statement uses the more common Hebrew vocabulary, but it states the same truth Psalm 139 shows through its two uses of this rare word. God's thoughts are infinitely greater, and they are directed toward His people with purpose.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 139:2 — "You understand my thought afar off." The phrase "afar off" can be read in two complementary ways. In terms of space, the God of heaven understands a man on earth without needing to come near to inspect him. In terms of time, God grasps a thought at its very beginning, before it is fully formed, let alone spoken. The next verses support the second reading: before a word is on David's tongue, the LORD knows it completely (v. 4). David is not saying that God eventually works out what he meant. God understands his thought before David himself has finished thinking it. There is no private room in the soul where a person can withdraw from God.

Psalm 139:17 — "How precious also are Your thoughts to me, O God!" "Precious" describes what is weighty, rare, and highly valued. In verse 17, God's thoughts are precious "to me," which means David treasures them. The context shows why. The verses just before describe God forming David's inward parts, seeing his unformed substance, and writing his days in His book (vv. 13–16). God's thoughts are the purposes He has been carrying out in David's life since before his birth. They concern this one man, and that is why they are dear to him.

The contrast between the two verses. The deepest insight comes from reading these verses together. In verse 2 God takes in the whole of David's thought, and He does it effortlessly and from afar. In verse 17 David attempts the reverse and is overwhelmed: "How great is the sum of them!" He goes on to say that if he tried to count them they would outnumber the sand (v. 18). The same word is used in both directions, but the result is very different. God contains the human mind completely, and the human mind cannot begin to contain God's. This is why the psalm moves into worship. David is surrounded by a mind he can never measure, and that mind is thinking about him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Both truths in these two verses come to their fullest expression in Jesus Christ. The knowledge David ascribes to the LORD appears in the incarnate Son: Jesus "knew all men, and had no need that anyone should testify of man, for He knew what was in man" (John 2:24–25). The One who understands our thought afar off walked among people and read their hearts. Hebrews applies the same searching knowledge to God's word, which is "a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart," and declares that "all things are naked and open to the eyes of Him to whom we must give account" (Heb. 4:12–13).

The precious, countless thoughts of Psalm 139:17 also find their goal in Christ. Paul says God has "made known to us the mystery of His will," His purpose that "He might gather together in one all things in Christ," and that in Him "we have obtained an inheritance, being predestined according to the purpose of Him who works all things according to the counsel of His will" (Eph. 1:9–11). David marveled at purposes he could not count. In Christ, God has revealed what those purposes aim at. The New Testament does not cite Psalm 139 in these passages, but the connection is real. The God who searches the heart and the God whose thoughts toward His people are vast meet in one Person.

7. Spirit & Life

Being known completely can feel like a threat. Most of us manage what others see and keep our real thoughts out of view. David's opening lines carry some of that unease, since he cannot escape a God who understands his thought before he speaks it.

The psalm does not leave him there. The same word that described David's exposed mind is used for God's precious thoughts, and that changes how the exposure feels. The One who sees everything in us is also thinking toward us with care and purpose too vast to count. That is why the psalm can end with an invitation: "Search me, O God, and know my heart; try me, and know my anxieties" (Ps. 139:23). David asks for the very scrutiny he could not escape, because he now trusts the mind behind it.

This is what a believer can live in. You do not need to hide your anxious, tangled, or sinful thoughts from God, because He already understands them from afar. Honest prayer is not telling God something new. It is agreeing to be known by Someone whose thoughts toward you are good. The fear of being seen gives way when you learn who is looking.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Rêaʻ occurs only in Psalm 139:2 and 139:17, and both uses are developed above. Nearby verses such as Psalm 139:23 ("Search me, O God, and know my heart") belong to the same meditation and are cited for context. They use different Hebrew words and are not further occurrences of rêaʻ.
