SHEPHERD - (raah, Strong's H7462)

Summary

The Hebrew verb raah means to pasture a flock: to lead animals out to graze and look after them. When animals are the subject, it means to graze or feed. Used as a noun, it names the person who tends the flock, the shepherd or herdsman. The NKJV often translates the word as "feed," so a reader may not notice that "feed" and "shepherd" in the same verse can come from the same Hebrew word. Abram's herdsmen, Rachel, Moses and young David all did this daily work.

Scripture takes this ordinary work and makes it one of its central pictures of how God cares for His people and how He expects their leaders to treat them. Near the end of his life, Jacob called God the One "who has fed me all my life long to this day" (Genesis 48:15). David confessed, "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want" (Psalm 23:1). Israel's kings were called to shepherd God's people. When those leaders fed themselves and let the flock scatter, God pronounced judgment on them. He promised to shepherd His people Himself and to set one Shepherd from David's line over them. The New Testament shows Jesus taking up that role as the Good Shepherd who gives His life for the sheep. The same Hebrew word also carries a warning, because people can "feed" on what cannot nourish them, even ashes and wind.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Raising sheep and goats was a basic livelihood in ancient Israel. The patriarchs were herdsmen, and Joseph's brothers told Pharaoh plainly that they were shepherds (Genesis 46:32; Genesis 47:3). The work could be dangerous. David told Saul that he had defended his father's flock against both a lion and a bear (1 Samuel 17:34–36).

This study follows the STEPBible sense H7462B, "to pasture." It includes the related form roi (Strong's H7473), which STEPBible counts with the same sense. In the Leningrad Hebrew text, following its Qere readings (the readings marked in the margin for reading aloud), this sense occurs 167 times in 138 verses. Literal uses cluster in Genesis through Samuel. Figurative uses are concentrated in the Prophets, especially Jeremiah, Ezekiel 34 and Zechariah 11. Other Hebrew words with the same spelling mean "to accompany" or "to befriend." Those are separate words and are not part of this study.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic idea of raah is pasturing: taking a flock out to graze and tending it there. Individual passages add protection, searching or rescue when the context supplies those details. Its principal senses are:

1. To tend or pasture a flock. As a noun, the word means a shepherd or herdsman. Jacob agrees to go on tending Laban's flocks for wages (Genesis 30:31), and Jesse says of David, "there he is, keeping the sheep" (1 Samuel 16:11).

2. Of animals, to graze. In Pharaoh's dream the cows grazed in the meadow (Genesis 41:2). Isaiah pictures a renewed creation where the wolf and the lamb feed together (Isaiah 65:25).

3. Figuratively, to lead, care for or nourish people. God told David, "You shall shepherd My people Israel" (2 Samuel 5:2). Proverbs 10:21 applies the image to speech: the lips of the righteous feed many, which means they give others something that sustains them.

4. Figuratively, to feed on something, meaning to take it in and live by it. Proverbs 15:14 says the mouth of fools feeds on foolishness.

Senses 3 and 4 use the same word in opposite directions. In the first, a person nourishes others. In the second, a person consumes something.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Because the NKJV often renders the verb as "feed," the shepherd behind the word can disappear from view. Isaiah 40:11 reads, "He will feed His flock like a shepherd." In that verse, both "feed" and "shepherd" translate raah. When Jacob says God "has fed me" (Genesis 48:15), he is saying that God has been his shepherd.

One grammatical pattern helps the reader. The usual word for "shepherd" is a participle, a verb form that can work as a noun. It means "one who tends." The title therefore comes straight from the activity, and that wording sharpens Ezekiel's charge: "Woe to the shepherds of Israel who feed themselves! Should not the shepherds feed the flocks?" (Ezekiel 34:2). The verb that names these men's calling now describes them tending themselves. Their title points to the flock, but their care stops with their own bellies.

The subject of the verb changes how much weight the word carries. With a herdsman as the subject, raah describes a job. With a king, it describes the standard he will be measured by. With God, it describes His care for His people. The word can even be turned into a picture of dread: "Death shall feed on them" (Psalm 49:14). The verb there is raah, so Death is pictured as the shepherd of the foolish.

4. Theological Significance

God's rule over His people is the rule of a shepherd. Jacob had spent twenty years in Laban's household, enduring heat by day and cold by night while tending Laban's flocks (Genesis 31:38–41). Blessing Joseph's sons, he confessed that God had been shepherding him through all of it: "The God who has fed me all my life long to this day" (Genesis 48:15). Soon afterward, Jacob spoke of God as the Shepherd and Stone of Israel (Genesis 49:24). David, another working shepherd, said, "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want" (Psalm 23:1). Asaph prayed to the "Shepherd of Israel, You who lead Joseph like a flock," and in the same line called Him "You who dwell between the cherubim" (Psalm 80:1). The God enthroned above the ark is also the God who leads His flock.

Isaiah shows what that care looks like. Isaiah 40:10 announces the Lord GOD coming in strength, His arm ruling for Him. The next verse shows where that strength goes: "He will gather the lambs with His arm, And carry them in His bosom, And gently lead those who are with young" (Isaiah 40:11). The same arm that rules carries the lambs, and the Shepherd slows His pace for the ewes that are carrying young.

Because God shepherds His people this way, He treats their mistreatment as an offense against Himself. "Woe to the shepherds who destroy and scatter the sheep of My pasture!" (Jeremiah 23:1). They are His sheep, and the pasture is His. To the leaders who left the flock untended, He says, "Behold, I will attend to you for the evil of your doings" (Jeremiah 23:2). Jeremiah also describes judgment in shepherd language: "Wail, shepherds, and cry! Roll about in the ashes, You leaders of the flock!" (Jeremiah 25:34), "For the LORD has plundered their pasture" (Jeremiah 25:36). In this picture, the rulers lose what they had depended on, and they are left with no flock to rule and no place to feed it.

Psalm 49 sets God's shepherding against a darker one. Those who trust in their wealth are laid in the grave "Like sheep," and "Death shall feed on them" (Psalm 49:14). The psalmist then states his own hope: God will redeem his soul from the power of the grave and receive him (Psalm 49:15). The rich fool is led by Death into the grave. The one who trusts God is taken up by God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

David, from the sheepfold to the throne (2 Samuel 5:2; Psalm 78:70–72). When the tribes came to Hebron to make David king, they recalled God's word to him: "You shall shepherd My people Israel, and be ruler over Israel" (2 Samuel 5:2). The parallel account in 1 Chronicles 11:2 ends "and be ruler over My people Israel." In both accounts, Scripture describes kingship as shepherding. Psalm 78:70–72 describes the move from the fields to the throne. God took David from following the ewes that had young and brought him to shepherd Jacob His people, and David shepherded them with integrity of heart and guided them by the skill of his hands. After Jesse presented his older sons and the Lord chose none of them, Jesse sent for David, who was out tending the sheep, and God chose him (1 Samuel 16:10–12). The care once given to nursing ewes was now required for a nation.

God against the shepherds, and God as Shepherd (Ezekiel 34). No chapter uses raah more than Ezekiel 34. The charge against Israel's leaders is specific: "You eat the fat and clothe yourselves with the wool; you slaughter the fatlings, but you do not feed the flock" (Ezekiel 34:3). Ezekiel then lists what they neglected: "The weak you have not strengthened, nor have you healed those who were sick, nor bound up the broken, nor brought back what was driven away, nor sought what was lost; but with force and cruelty you have ruled them" (Ezekiel 34:4). As a result, the sheep "became food for all the beasts of the field when they were scattered" (Ezekiel 34:5), and "no one was seeking or searching for them" (Ezekiel 34:6).

God's answer begins with judgment on the leaders: "Behold, I am against the shepherds, and I will require My flock at their hand" (Ezekiel 34:10). Then He promises rescue: "I will deliver My flock from their mouths, that they may no longer be food for them" (Ezekiel 34:10). The rulers had become the predators. God announces that He Himself will search for His sheep, gather them from the lands where they were scattered, pasture them on the mountains of Israel and make them lie down (Ezekiel 34:11–15). He promises to do the very work the leaders refused to do. He will seek the lost, bring back the driven away, bind up the broken and strengthen the sick (Ezekiel 34:16). The future of the flock now depends on God's own care.

The chapter then adds a second promise. God will set up one shepherd over them, His servant David, who will feed them and be their shepherd (Ezekiel 34:23). Ezekiel places both promises side by side without explaining how they fit together. God will shepherd His flock, and God will appoint a son of David to shepherd it. The chapter closes with its own explanation of the image: "You are My flock, the flock of My pasture; you are men, and I am your God" (Ezekiel 34:31).

The worthless shepherd and the struck Shepherd (Zechariah 11; 13:7). Zechariah acts out Israel's history of failed leadership. God tells him, "Next, take for yourself the implements of a foolish shepherd" (Zechariah 11:15). The ruler this sign represents is one "who will not care for those who are cut off, nor seek the young, nor heal those that are broken, nor feed those that still stand," and instead "will eat the flesh of the fat and tear their hooves in pieces" (Zechariah 11:16). God pronounces, "Woe to the worthless shepherd, Who leaves the flock!" (Zechariah 11:17).

Two chapters later the picture changes. The LORD calls the sword to awake against His own Shepherd, the man who stands near Him as His companion. The Shepherd is struck, and the sheep are scattered. God then refines a remnant through fire, and they call on His name (Zechariah 13:7–9). Zechariah 11 shows a shepherd who abandons the flock. Zechariah 13 shows God's own Shepherd struck down, and through what follows God purifies a people for Himself.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets kept returning to the promise of a shepherd from David's line. Micah foretold a ruler from Bethlehem who would stand and shepherd his flock in the strength of the LORD, and whose greatness would reach the ends of the earth (Micah 5:2, 4). Ezekiel repeated the promise of one shepherd, David, over a reunited people (Ezekiel 37:24).

The New Testament, written in Greek, takes up this hope and shows Jesus claiming it. "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). Jesus speaks of knowing His own sheep and of other sheep He must bring, so that there will be one flock and one shepherd (John 10:14–16). That language matches Ezekiel's "one shepherd." In Jesus, the two promises of Ezekiel 34 meet. The Son of David shepherds the flock, and in doing so He does what God said He would do Himself. The Gospels show Him doing the seeking that Ezekiel 34:16 promised. In Jericho He called Zacchaeus down from a tree, went to his house and said that the Son of Man had come to seek and to save what was lost (Luke 19:1–10).

On the night of His arrest, Jesus quoted Zechariah 13:7 and applied it to Himself and His disciples: the Shepherd would be struck, and the sheep would be scattered (Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27). This is a stated fulfillment. Read alongside John 10:11, it shows that the Shepherd God appointed would save the flock by laying down His own life. The worthless shepherd of Zechariah 11 left the flock. The Good Shepherd gave Himself for it. Scripture's story ends with the Lamb at the center of the throne shepherding His people and leading them to living fountains of waters (Revelation 7:17).

7. Spirit & Life

Jacob's confession gives believers words for their own lives. His life included deception, exile, years of hard labor under Laban, and long grief over Joseph. Looking back over all of it, he named God as the One "who has fed me all my life long to this day" (Genesis 48:15). The confession covers his whole life, the dark years included. A believer can say the same about a hard history, trusting that the Shepherd was providing through it, even when that care was hard to see at the time.

Raah also exposes what people live on, because the word describes grazing as well as being shepherded. Isaiah pictures the idolater feeding on ashes. His deceived heart has led him astray, and he cannot stop to ask whether the thing in his right hand is a lie (Isaiah 44:20). Hosea describes Ephraim feeding on the wind as the nation chased agreements with Assyria and Egypt that could not save it (Hosea 12:1). People take in whatever they trust. Money, approval, anxious planning or self-reliance can become a steady diet of ashes. God's answer in Ezekiel 34 is a true Shepherd and good pasture on the mountains of Israel (Ezekiel 34:14). Those who are fed by Him can in turn feed others, as the lips of the righteous feed many (Proverbs 10:21).

For anyone who leads God's people, Ezekiel 34:4 reads as a job description written in the negative. Each failure in the verse names a duty: strengthen the weak, heal the sick, bind up the broken, bring back the strays and seek the lost. The apostle Peter carried the same charge forward when he urged elders to shepherd God's flock willingly and eagerly, not for dishonest gain (1 Peter 5:2). The flock belongs to God, and He says, "I will require My flock at their hand" (Ezekiel 34:10). That warning is sobering for leaders and a comfort to sheep who have been wounded by them. God has promised to rescue His flock from those who prey on it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 13:7 — "There was strife between the herdsmen of Abram’s livestock and the herdsmen of Lot’s livestock." The quarrel over grazing led to Abram and Lot separating, and Lot chose the plain near Sodom.

  • Genesis 29:9 — Rachel came with her father's sheep, for she tended them. Jacob met her at a well while she was doing this work.

  • Exodus 3:1 — Moses was tending the flock of Jethro, his father-in-law, when God called him at the burning bush.

  • Numbers 14:33 — God told the rebellious generation, "your sons shall be shepherds in the wilderness forty years, and bear the brunt of your infidelity." The children would share the long wandering that their parents' refusal to enter the land had brought on them.

  • Numbers 27:17 — Moses asked God for a successor so that the LORD's people would not be like sheep without a shepherd. Jesus was moved with compassion when He saw the crowds in that condition (Matthew 9:36).

  • 1 Kings 22:17 — Micaiah saw all Israel scattered on the mountains like sheep without a shepherd. The vision foretold King Ahab's death in battle.

  • Song of Solomon 2:16 — "My beloved is mine, and I am his. He feeds his flock among the lilies." The beloved is pictured at home among the lilies, and the verse pairs that image with a simple declaration of mutual belonging.
