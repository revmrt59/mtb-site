REU - (Rᵉʻûw, Strong's H7466)

Summary

Reu was the son of Peleg and the father of Serug, a descendant of Shem who lived in the generations after the Flood, in the era commonly associated with the scattering at Babel. His name probably means "friend" or "companion." It is generally connected with the Hebrew word for a friend or neighbor, though the derivation is not certain. Scripture records almost nothing about his life. Genesis 11:18–21 gives his birth, his age when Serug was born, the years he lived afterward, and the fact that he had other sons and daughters. First Chronicles 1:25 repeats his name in the line from Shem to Abram.

Reu still matters, because the family line that runs through him leads to Abraham and, in the end, to Jesus. Luke's genealogy names him among the ancestors of Christ (Luke 3:35). Reu lived in a world of shrinking lifespans and spreading idolatry, and the Bible records no word from God to him. God was nevertheless carrying His promise forward through ordinary generations, one father and son at a time, toward the day He would call Abram and, later, send His Son.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Reu is a single individual in the Hebrew Bible. He was the son of Peleg, the father of Serug, and the great-great-grandfather of Abram (Genesis 11:18–26). No other person in Scripture bears this name. He should not be confused with Reuel (H7467), a separate name with its own Strong's entry, although the two may share a related root.

The name Reu probably means "friend," "companion," or "friendship." It is usually linked to the Hebrew noun rea', meaning friend or neighbor, which is associated with the root ra'ah. That meaning is likely but not settled. Some popular sources render the name "friend of God," but the name contains no divine element such as El or Yah, so that rendering goes beyond the evidence. Scripture makes no wordplay on Reu's name, and nothing in the text ties its meaning to the shape of his life.

In the New Testament, Luke's genealogy includes him in Greek form, which NKJV renders "Reu" (Luke 3:35). This Greek name is indexed separately as Ragau (G4466). It refers to the same man, but its occurrence is not counted among the five Hebrew occurrences of H7466.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Reu's story is told in the plain rhythm of the Genesis 11 genealogy: "Peleg lived thirty years, and begot Reu" (Genesis 11:18). After that, "Reu lived thirty-two years, and begot Serug. After he begot Serug, Reu lived two hundred and seven years, and begot sons and daughters" (Genesis 11:20–21). By the Hebrew (Masoretic) figures, he lived 239 years. The Greek Septuagint, as in Brenton's translation, gives his age at Serug's birth as 132, which would make his lifespan 339 years and lengthen the period between the Flood and Abraham.

Reu's setting gives these bare numbers some weight. His father Peleg lived in the days when "the earth was divided" (Genesis 10:25, paraphrased), a period commonly associated with the scattering of the nations at Babel. Reu therefore belongs to the early generations after the Flood, though Scripture does not establish whether he was born after Babel or saw the division himself. His lifespan also reflects a sharp change in the genealogy. Eber lived 464 years, but Peleg and Reu each lived 239. Human life had been cut roughly in half within a few generations.

On the Masoretic figures, Reu was born while Noah was still alive, and he was still living when Abram was born. His life spanned the gap between the Flood survivors and the man God would call out of Ur.

First Chronicles 1:24–27 later lists Reu in the line from Shem to "Abram, who is Abraham." For the returned exiles, that record traced Israel's story back through these ancient fathers to the beginning.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Genesis 11:10–26 follows one line out of many. Each father "begot sons and daughters," and most entries name only one son until Genesis 11:26 names Terah's three sons, Abram, Nahor, and Haran. The line runs straight to Terah and Abram. Scripture states the line and its destination. It is a fair inference that God was preserving a particular family through whom He would bring His promised blessing to all the families of the earth (Genesis 12:3, paraphrased).

This line passed through a spiritually dark period. Joshua later reminded Israel: "Your fathers, including Terah, the father of Abraham and the father of Nahor, dwelt on the other side of the River in old times; and they served other gods" (Joshua 24:2). The text names Terah. Whether Reu himself worshiped the LORD or other gods, Scripture does not say. What is clear is that the family descended from him had slipped into idolatry by Abram's day. God did not wait for a faithful family to choose. He kept the line and then called Abram out of it by grace.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The connection to Christ here is explicit. Luke traces Jesus' ancestry backward: "the son of Serug, the son of Reu, the son of Peleg, the son of Eber, the son of Shelah" (Luke 3:35). Matthew begins his genealogy with Abraham. Luke continues past Abraham, through Reu and Shem, to Noah, and finally to Adam (Luke 3:34–38). Because of this, Reu appears only in Luke's list.

That longer reach fits Luke's emphasis that Jesus is a Savior for all people. He is a son of Abraham, and he is also a descendant of the post-Flood fathers who lived before Israel existed, early ancestors whom Genesis associates with the time the nations were divided. The one who would gather people "from every nation" has roots in the early generations associated with that division. This is a thematic resonance rather than a stated typology. The genealogical fact itself is plain: the line God kept through Reu led to Christ.

5. Spirit & Life

Reu lived 239 years, and Scripture gives him no recorded prayer, vision, or deed. God gave no recorded word to him. Yet God was at work in his generation, keeping a promise first spoken in Eden (Genesis 3:15) and moving it steadily toward Abraham and toward Bethlehem.

Most believers live lives that will never be written up in detail. Reu's place in Luke 3 shows that God's purposes are not limited to the generations that witness miracles. Paul says God sent His Son "when the fullness of the time had come" (Galatians 4:4, paraphrased). The quiet generations were part of how that time came to be fulfilled. God's faithfulness does not depend on whether we can see it working in our own day.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:25 – Places Reu's father Peleg in the time when the earth was divided.

  • Genesis 11:18–21 – Gives Reu's birth, fatherhood of Serug, other children, and lifespan.

  • Genesis 11:10–26 – Shows Reu within the narrowing line from Shem to Abram and the steep decline in lifespans.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:24–27 – Repeats Reu in the line from Shem to Abraham for post-exilic Israel.

  • Joshua 24:2 – Describes the idolatry of Abraham's ancestral family beyond the River.

  • Luke 3:34–36 – Names Reu among the ancestors of Jesus Christ, in a genealogy that reaches back to Adam.
