REUEL - (Rᵉʻûwʼêl, Strong's H7467)

Summary

Reuel is a Hebrew personal name generally understood to mean "friend of God," formed from a word for "friend" or "companion" joined to El, "God." Four different men carry it in Scripture, and they should not be confused with one another. The first is a son of Esau by Basemath, Ishmael's daughter, who became the father of several clan chiefs in Edom (Genesis 36). The second is the priest of Midian who took in Moses when he fled Egypt and gave him his daughter Zipporah in marriage (Exodus 2; Numbers 10:29). The third is the father of Eliasaph, leader of the tribe of Gad in the wilderness (Numbers 2:14), who is called Deuel elsewhere. The fourth is a Benjamite ancestor among the families who returned to Jerusalem after the exile (1 Chronicles 9:8).

The Midianite Reuel carries most of the name's biblical weight. Moses arrived at a well in Midian as a rejected man, a fugitive whose own people had refused him. This priest asked his daughters why they had left the stranger outside, invited him to eat, and gave him a home where he would spend the long years before God called him at the burning bush. The same household appears later, under the names Jethro and Hobab, blessing the LORD for Israel's deliverance and being urged to travel with Israel toward the promised land. The name "friend of God" cannot tell us what kind of man any of these four were. Their story, however, does show God at work beyond Israel's borders and sheltering the man He would use to deliver His people.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The name. Reuel is generally explained as a compound of the Hebrew word for "friend, companion" and El, the common Semitic word for God. That gives the meaning "friend of God," which some render as "God is a friend." The meaning is well supported. The exact grammatical relationship between the two parts can be expressed either way, but the sense stays the same. The name is confessional in form, and it uses the general name El rather than the personal name the LORD revealed to Moses. A name of this kind says something about God, but it does not by itself tell us the circumstances of the naming or what the family worshiped. It does not, by itself, tell us whether the child grew into what the name declares.

Four men named Reuel. Scripture distinguishes the following men:

  • Reuel the Edomite, son of Esau and Basemath (Genesis 36:4, 10, 13, 17; 1 Chronicles 1:35, 37).

  • Reuel the Midianite, the priest of Midian and father (or possibly grandfather) of Zipporah, Moses' wife (Exodus 2:18; Numbers 10:29).

  • Reuel the Gadite, father of Eliasaph (Numbers 2:14). The parallel lists call him Deuel (Numbers 1:14; 7:42, 47; 10:20), a separately indexed form (H1845).

  • Reuel the Benjamite, son of Ibnijah and grandfather of Meshullam, in the record of those living in Jerusalem after the exile (1 Chronicles 9:8).

The Midianite's other names. Exodus 2:18 calls Moses' host "Reuel their father," but Exodus 3:1 says Moses "was tending the flock of Jethro his father-in-law, the priest of Midian." Numbers 10:29 then speaks of "Hobab the son of Reuel the Midianite, Moses' father-in-law." Readers have proposed three main explanations. Reuel and Jethro may be two names for one man, with Jethro perhaps functioning as a title. Reuel may be Zipporah's grandfather, since Hebrew "father" can mean a more distant ancestor, which would make Jethro his son. Or Reuel may serve as a clan name. Numbers 10:29 adds a further question, because the phrase "Moses' father-in-law" can be read as describing either Reuel or Hobab. The text does not settle the matter, and none of these views should be pressed as certain. What is clear is that Reuel, Jethro, and Hobab belong to the one Midianite household into which Moses married.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Reuel the Edomite. Genesis 36 opens, "Now this is the genealogy of Esau, who is Edom" (36:1). Esau had taken wives "from the daughters of Canaan" (36:2), which grieved Isaac and Rebekah (26:34–35). He also married "Basemath, Ishmael's daughter, sister of Nebajoth" (36:3). (Genesis 28:9 calls Esau's Ishmaelite wife Mahalath, another instance of the varied naming of Esau's wives.) "Basemath bore Reuel" (36:4). Reuel's four sons, Nahath, Zerah, Shammah, and Mizzah, became chiefs in Edom (36:13, 17). The Chronicler repeats the line centuries later (1 Chronicles 1:35, 37). This places Reuel at the meeting point of two families that stood outside the line of promise: Esau, passed over for Jacob, and Ishmael, passed over for Isaac. Reuel's name honors God, and it is possible that this reflects some lingering knowledge of God from the patriarchs in these households. The record does not tell us why the name was given or what his parents believed. It does not mean that his descendants walked with God, and later Edom would often stand against Israel (Numbers 20:14–21; Obadiah).

Reuel the Midianite. The Midianites descended from Abraham through Keturah (Genesis 25:1–2). Moses came into their land as a fugitive after killing an Egyptian and being rebuffed by a fellow Hebrew (Exodus 2:11–15). He sat by a well. "Now the priest of Midian had seven daughters. And they came and drew water, and they filled the troughs to water their father's flock. Then the shepherds came and drove them away; but Moses stood up and helped them, and watered their flock" (2:16–17).

The daughters' early return surprised their father: "How is it that you have come so soon today?" (2:18). They told him, "An Egyptian delivered us from the hand of the shepherds" (2:19). His invitation follows: "And where is he? Why is it that you have left the man? Call him, that he may eat bread" (2:20). The result was a new life for Moses. "Then Moses was content to live with the man, and he gave Zipporah his daughter to Moses" (2:21). Moses named his first son Gershom, saying he had been a stranger in a foreign land (2:22). Stephen later recalls that Moses "became a dweller in the land of Midian" and that forty years passed before the angel appeared at the bush (Acts 7:29–30).

The household reappears in Moses' later story. Moses was keeping "the flock of Jethro his father-in-law" when he came to Horeb, "the mountain of God" (Exodus 3:1). After the exodus, Jethro brought Zipporah and her sons to Moses in the wilderness. When he heard all the LORD had done, he blessed the LORD, confessed that the LORD is greater than all the gods, and offered a burnt offering. Aaron and the elders ate bread with him before God, and he counseled Moses to share the burden of judging the people (Exodus 18:1–27). As Israel prepared to leave Sinai, Moses pleaded with "Hobab the son of Reuel the Midianite": "Come with us, and we will treat you well; for the LORD has promised good things to Israel" (Numbers 10:29). Hobab at first declined, and Moses urged him again (10:30–32). The text does not record his final answer, but later notes place his Kenite descendants among Israel (Judges 1:16; 4:11).

Reuel the Gadite. In the arrangement of the camp, "the leader of the children of Gad shall be Eliasaph the son of Reuel" (Numbers 2:14). Elsewhere the same father is "Deuel" (Numbers 1:14). The Hebrew letters for d and r look very similar, and the difference is commonly explained as an early copying variation. The person is clearly the same.

Reuel the Benjamite. First Chronicles 9 lists the people who lived in Jerusalem after the return from Babylon. Among the Benjamites is "Meshullam the son of Shephatiah, the son of Reuel, the son of Ibnijah" (9:8). Nothing more is said of him. His place in the list shows a family that kept its record through the exile and came back to the city.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God brought Moses into a Midianite priest's household and kept him there until the call at Horeb. It also records that this family later blessed the LORD and that Moses invited them to share in Israel's promised good. Several careful implications follow from these facts.

First, God prepared His deliverer outside Egypt and outside Israel. Moses learned shepherding in Midian, and it was while leading Jethro's flock that he came to the mountain of God (Exodus 3:1), the same mountain where Israel would later receive the law. The years in Reuel's household were part of God's plan for Moses, not a detour from it.

Second, the record is honest about what it does not say. Exodus 2 does not describe what Reuel's priesthood involved, and Exodus 18 presents Jethro's confession as something new: he came to know the LORD's greatness by hearing of the exodus. The family's story is best read as movement toward the LORD in response to His works, not as proof that Midian already held a complete faith.

Third, the genealogies keep the name in view across Israel's history: in Edom's beginnings, in the wilderness camp, and in Jerusalem after the exile. These quieter entries show that God's record keeps track of people whom history would otherwise forget.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Explicit New Testament development. Stephen retells Moses' story to show a pattern in how Israel treated those God sent. Moses' brothers asked, "Who made you a ruler and a judge?" (Acts 7:27, 35, paraphrased here). Moses went to Midian as a sojourner, yet "this Moses whom they rejected" was the one God sent as ruler and deliverer (Acts 7:35). Stephen ties this directly to the "Prophet like me" whom God would raise up (7:37) and to the Righteous One his hearers betrayed (7:52). Reuel's household is the place where the rejected deliverer was received and kept during that interval. This is the clearest Christ-centered thread connected to Reuel, and it comes through Stephen's reading of Moses, not through Reuel himself.

Canonical development. The meaning of the name points to a theme Scripture develops at length. Abraham is called God's friend (2 Chronicles 20:7; Isaiah 41:8; James 2:23). The LORD spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks with his friend (Exodus 33:11). Jesus told His disciples that He no longer called them servants but friends, because He had made known to them what He heard from the Father (John 15:14–15). Moses came to know friendship with God in a far deeper way than any name could claim, and he received the recorded call at the bush while keeping the flock of the household that bore it. Jethro's confession also anticipates the promise that the nations will come to know the LORD through what He does for His people.

Thematic resonance. Some readers compare Jethro to Melchizedek. Each was a priest from outside Israel's line who met a leader of God's people, shared a meal, and spoke of God's greatness (Genesis 14:18–20; Exodus 18:10–12). The comparison is worth noticing, but Scripture develops Melchizedek, not Jethro, as a pattern for Christ (Psalm 110:4; Hebrews 7), so it should remain a resonance rather than a claimed type.

5. Spirit & Life

Reuel's recorded reply includes a rebuke for missing hospitality: "Why is it that you have left the man? Call him, that he may eat bread" (Exodus 2:20). He welcomed the stranger without needing to know his future. He saw a man who had helped his family and would not let him stay outside. God used that ordinary table to shelter the man who would lead Israel out of Egypt. The New Testament urges believers not to forget to entertain strangers (Hebrews 13:2). Reuel's story shows why that matters, because faithful kindness is often how God carries His larger work forward without our knowing it.

The household's later story shows a God who makes Himself known. Jethro had heard of the LORD's acts, and when he heard the full account from Moses, he rejoiced and worshiped (Exodus 18:8–12). Moses then invited Hobab to share in "good things" the LORD had promised (Numbers 10:29). God's purpose for Israel was never meant to stop at Israel's edges. People outside the family of promise could hear what God had done, turn to Him, and join the journey.

The name also holds a warning together with a hope. Esau's son was called "friend of God," and Edom later became Israel's bitter enemy. A godly name, or a godly heritage, does not make anyone God's friend. That friendship comes from God's own initiative. He spoke with Moses as a friend, and in Christ He calls His people friends and tells them His Father's will (John 15:15).

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:1–4, 10, 13, 17 – Reuel the Edomite, son of Esau and Ishmael's daughter Basemath, and his sons who became chiefs.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:35, 37 – The Chronicler's repetition of the Edomite line.

  • Exodus 2:16–22 – The priest of Midian, named Reuel in 2:18, receives Moses, feeds him, and gives him Zipporah.

  • Exodus 3:1 – The same household under the name Jethro; Moses comes to Horeb while keeping its flock.

  • Exodus 18:1–27 – Jethro blesses the LORD, offers sacrifice, and counsels Moses.

  • Numbers 10:29–32 – Hobab, son of Reuel, invited to share in the LORD's promised good.

  • Numbers 2:14 (with 1:14) – Eliasaph son of Reuel/Deuel, leader of Gad.

  • 1 Chronicles 9:8 – Reuel the Benjamite among the families resettled in Jerusalem.

  • Acts 7:27–37 – Stephen's account of the rejected Moses in Midian, pointing to Christ.

  • Exodus 33:11; Isaiah 41:8; James 2:23; John 15:14–15 – The biblical theme of friendship with God.
