SHEPHERD - (rôʻîy, Strong's H7473)

Summary

The Hebrew word rôʻîy means a shepherd. It belongs to the family of the verb râʻâh (H7462), "to pasture, feed," the Bible's ordinary shepherding vocabulary. That family describes sheep and cattle grazing, the shepherd who leads them to pasture, and the person who does that work: Abel the keeper of sheep, Rachel the shepherdess, the boy David out with the flock. Israel's kings and leaders were called shepherds because they were meant to feed, guard, and gather God's people. The word can also turn grim. It describes enemies who "graze" a land bare and fools who feed on foolishness.

Scripture's richest uses belong to God Himself. "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want" (Psalm 23:1). When Israel's shepherds fed themselves and scattered the flock, God promised to shepherd His people personally through one Shepherd from David's line. Zechariah then foresaw the Lord ordering a sword against that Shepherd, "the Man who is My Companion." Jesus applied those words to Himself on the night He was betrayed. This word carries the story of God's care from the pastures of Genesis to the cross.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Israel's ancestors were herdsmen. Abraham's and Lot's herdsmen quarreled over grazing land (Genesis 13:7–8). Jacob tended Laban's flocks. Joseph was feeding the flock with his brothers when his story begins (Genesis 37:2), and his family later stood before Pharaoh as shepherds (Genesis 47:3). Moses was tending Jethro's flock when he saw the burning bush (Exodus 3:1).

Shepherding meant finding grass and water, keeping a scattered flock together, and fighting off lions, bears, and thieves. Across the ancient world kings were also called shepherds of their people. That made the word natural for national leaders, and the prophets use it heavily for that purpose. These wider word-family uses are especially frequent in Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Zechariah, including the shepherd discourse in Ezekiel 34.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core idea is pasturing: grazing animals, or leading them to food. Rôʻîy itself is a shepherd form, traditionally indexed at Isaiah 38:12 and Zechariah 11:17; the grazing and feeding uses below belong to the wider râʻâh word family to which it belongs. Context sets the nuance.

1. To graze or feed, said of animals. Pharaoh's fat cows "fed in the meadow" (Genesis 41:2), and in the coming kingdom "the cow and the bear shall graze" together (Isaiah 11:7).

2. To tend a flock, or the one who does it: a shepherd, herdsman, or keeper. Abel was "a keeper of sheep" (Genesis 4:2), and David was "keeping the sheep" when Samuel came (1 Samuel 16:11).

3. To lead and care for people as a shepherd. The NKJV sometimes renders this "rulers" (Jeremiah 2:8). The Lord told David he would shepherd His people Israel (2 Samuel 5:2).

4. To feed on or consume, sometimes destructively. The fool feeds on foolishness (Proverbs 15:14), and a wild beast devours Israel's vine (Psalm 80:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The same word names both the activity and the person who does it, so "feed" and "shepherd" often stand side by side. Jeremiah 3:15 promises "shepherds according to My heart, who will feed you." The pairing makes a point the English can hide: a shepherd is defined by feeding. A shepherd who does not feed the flock contradicts his own title.

The subject of the verb changes everything. When sheep are the subject, the word means grazing. When a shepherd is the subject, it means care. When enemies are the subject, it becomes stripping and devastation. Micah 5:6 speaks of laying waste the land of Assyria with the sword. The object matters too. Ezekiel's charge that Israel's shepherds feed themselves turns the word back on itself (Ezekiel 34:2).

4. Theological Significance

God refuses to be a distant ruler who manages His people through others. On his deathbed Jacob looked back on his life and called God the One who had fed him all his life (Genesis 48:15). He then named God the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel (Genesis 49:24). Asaph prays to Him as "O Shepherd of Israel, You who lead Joseph like a flock" (Psalm 80:1). Shepherding is something God does Himself.

That is why He takes the failure of human shepherds so personally. Leaders who exploit people are mistreating His flock, "the sheep of My pasture" (Jeremiah 23:1). Delegated authority is answerable to the Owner.

God's shepherding also reverses judgment. Jeremiah 31:10 says, "He who scattered Israel will gather him, And keep him as a shepherd does his flock." The same Lord who sent the exile brings His people home. He promises they will feed again on Carmel and Bashan (Jeremiah 50:19), and Micah prays, "Shepherd Your people with Your staff, The flock of Your heritage" (Micah 7:14). Restoration is described as being pastured again by God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 23:1 and Isaiah 40:11 — the Lord who shepherds personally. David knew the work from the inside, and he claims the Lord as "my shepherd." The result is "I shall not want." The sheep's sufficiency rests entirely on whose sheep it is. Isaiah 40 opens with the Lord God coming with strength and His arm ruling for Him. In the next breath that same arm gathers lambs: "He will feed His flock like a shepherd; He will gather the lambs with His arm, And carry them in His bosom, And gently lead those who are with young." The arm that rules nations carries lambs. The pace is set for the slowest, the nursing ewes.

Ezekiel 34 — false shepherds and the one Shepherd. Ezekiel indicts leaders who ate the fat and clothed themselves with the wool while the flock went hungry. They did not strengthen the weak, heal the sick, bind up the broken, or seek the lost, and the sheep were scattered and became food for wild beasts (34:2–8). God's answer comes in two moves. First He declares that He Himself will search for His sheep (34:11). Then He promises to set up one shepherd over them, His servant David, who will feed them (34:23). Ezekiel 37:24 repeats the promise: "they shall all have one shepherd." Micah adds that this ruler "shall stand and feed His flock In the strength of the LORD" (Micah 5:4). God says He will shepherd them Himself, and He also says a son of David will shepherd them. Both promises stand, and the prophets leave the reader to wonder how.

Zechariah 11–13 — the Shepherd struck. Zechariah acts out the role of a shepherd over a flock doomed to slaughter. He pronounces woe on the "worthless shepherd" who leaves the flock (11:17). Then comes the startling command: "Awake, O sword, against My Shepherd, Against the Man who is My Companion," Says the LORD of hosts. "Strike the Shepherd, And the sheep will be scattered" (13:7). This Shepherd is neither worthless nor rejected by God. He is God's own Companion, and the Lord Himself summons the sword against him. Elsewhere the good shepherd strikes the predator. David struck the lion and the bear to rescue his lambs (1 Samuel 17:34–35). Here the sword falls on the Shepherd, and through that striking the remnant is refined and comes to call on the Lord (13:9).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word follows a path from the pasture to the throne. David was taken from following the ewes to shepherd Jacob, and he "shepherded them according to the integrity of his heart" (Psalm 78:71–72). Yet David's line produced the very shepherds Jeremiah and Ezekiel condemned. The hope narrowed to one Shepherd.

The New Testament shows how God could shepherd His people Himself and also give them a Davidic Shepherd. Jesus is both God with us and the Son of David. "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). He exposes the hireling who "does not care about the sheep" (John 10:13), the false shepherds Ezekiel described. He also gathers "one flock and one shepherd" (John 10:16), which echoes Ezekiel 37:24.

On the way to Gethsemane Jesus quoted Zechariah directly: "I will strike the Shepherd, And the sheep will be scattered" (Mark 14:27). The scattering of the disciples that night was part of the plan, and the striking of the Shepherd was the Father's own purpose. Hebrews then calls the risen Jesus "that great Shepherd of the sheep," brought up from the dead "through the blood of the everlasting covenant" (Hebrews 13:20). The Shepherd who was struck now lives to shepherd.

7. Spirit & Life

Sheep never stop eating, and neither do people. The only question is what they feed on. Isaiah describes the man who carves an idol from the same log he burns for warmth: "He feeds on ashes; A deceived heart has turned him aside" (Isaiah 44:20). Hosea says Ephraim feeds on the wind and chases the east wind (Hosea 12:1), a picture of political schemes and idols that fill the mouth with nothing. "The mouth of fools feeds on foolishness" (Proverbs 15:14). A person who walks away from the true Shepherd keeps grazing, but on ashes and air.

The Shepherd's answer is real food. "The lips of the righteous feed many, But fools die for lack of wisdom" (Proverbs 10:21). God promises "shepherds according to My heart, who will feed you with knowledge and understanding" (Jeremiah 3:15). The words of the wise are "given by one Shepherd" (Ecclesiastes 12:11). Faithful teaching is how the Shepherd feeds His flock. Those who teach and lead in the church should measure their work by Jeremiah 3:15: are the people being fed with understanding, or are leaders feeding themselves?

For every believer, Psalm 23 says the Shepherd prepares a table "in the presence of my enemies." His people's peace does not depend on an empty wilderness. It depends on His presence in it. The same Lord who carries lambs and walks at the pace of the weakest is the One who let the sword fall on Himself rather than on the flock.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 4:2 — Abel is "a keeper of sheep." Scripture's first shepherd brought God an offering He accepted.

  • Exodus 3:1 — Moses was tending Jethro's flock when God called him to lead Israel out of Egypt.

  • Numbers 27:17 — Moses asks that the people not be like sheep without a shepherd. Jesus later felt the same compassion over the crowds (Mark 6:34).

  • 1 Kings 22:17 — Micaiah sees Israel scattered on the mountains as sheep without a shepherd, a sign of King Ahab's coming death.

  • Isaiah 44:28 — God calls the pagan king Cyrus "My shepherd," because Cyrus would carry out God's purpose to rebuild Jerusalem.

  • Isaiah 65:25 — In the new creation the wolf and the lamb feed together. Grazing becomes a picture of peace restored.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Jeremiah 3:15 interprets the image itself. For God's people, the pasture is "knowledge and understanding," and to feed them is to teach them God's truth. This does not mean that every literal grazing scene carries a hidden lesson.

Scripture repeatedly moves God's chosen leaders from the flock to leadership. Moses at Horeb and David in Bethlehem learned in the field the protective care that God required of rulers (2 Samuel 5:2). The image can also turn toward judgment. In Jeremiah 6:3 invading kings come like shepherds who pitch their tents around Jerusalem and graze it bare. Psalm 80:13 and Micah 5:6 use the same word for devouring and laying waste. Those passages describe consuming judgment and should not be confused with the Good Shepherd image.

Pastures often bordered dangerous wilderness, so the shepherd's presence marked the line between safety and danger. Ezekiel 34:25 promises that the flock will dwell safely in the wilderness and sleep in the woods, which supports reading Psalm 23's table "in the presence of my enemies" in that light. The movement from David striking the lion to the Lord striking His Shepherd (Section 5) is an interpretive pattern drawn from the passages. Jesus' own use of Zechariah 13:7 grounds the Christ-centered reading.
