MY LOVE, DARLING - (raʻyâh, Strong's H7474)

Summary

The Hebrew word raʻyâh is a tender term of endearment meaning darling, dear companion or beloved friend. It appears only nine times in the Bible, all in the Song of Solomon, and the NKJV translates it "my love" each time. It is the bridegroom's affectionate name for his bride in the Song's dialogue. He normally speaks it to her directly, sometimes in words she remembers; in Song of Solomon 2:2 he uses it to describe her as a lily among thorns. The word belongs to the same family as the common Hebrew word for friend or companion. That background matters: the bride is cherished as a woman he desires, and she is also his chosen partner in life.

The bridegroom uses this name soon after she asks not to be stared at because the sun has darkened her, when he calls her out to join him in spring, when he praises her beauty without reservation, when he knocks at her door at night, and when he speaks to her again after the poem's painful scene of separation. Read alongside Scripture's picture of marriage as a portrait of Christ and His church (Ephesians 5:25–32), raʻyâh shows a love that keeps speaking, keeps pursuing, and calls the beloved by a name of delight whatever she has said about herself.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Raʻyâh is the feminine form of the Hebrew noun rea' (H7453), the everyday word for a friend, companion, or neighbor. Rea' appears throughout the Old Testament, from the command to "love your neighbor" to the friendships of ordinary life. The feminine form, however, belongs to the Song of Solomon alone.

The Song is a collection of love poetry about a bride and bridegroom. It includes courtship, a wedding, longing, separation, and reunion. In this setting, raʻyâh serves as the bridegroom's affectionate name for the woman he loves. It is the language of a man speaking to someone he knows well and has chosen.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Raʻyâh means a female companion who is dearly loved. In practice it works like darling or my dear. It is an affectionate name, usually used in direct address, that combines romance with friendship. The word has one clear sense. Its warmth comes from its use. Eight of the nine are spoken directly to the bride, and the NKJV's "my love" reflects the possessive "my" that the bridegroom attaches to it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

All nine uses come in the bridegroom's own words, and eight address her directly. In Song of Solomon 2:2, a reply in the dialogue, he uses third-person wording, "So is my love among the daughters." This pattern explains much of the word's force. It names her, and in most uses it also speaks to her and reassures her.

The surrounding words change with the moment:

  • In courtship, the word comes with comparisons and praise (1:9, 1:15, 2:2).

  • In the spring song, it introduces an invitation (2:10, 2:13).

  • In the wedding poem, it accompanies a full description of her beauty (4:1, 4:7).

  • At the door at night, it stands among several other tender names (5:2).

  • After the separation, it opens his speech to her (6:4).

The bride has her own word for him, "my beloved" (5:2). Together the two names form the Song's language of mutual love.

4. Theological Significance

The friendship at the root of raʻyâh tells us something about the love God values. The Bible's language for the closest married love grows from the language of companionship. The bride is not a possession admired from a distance. She is someone he walks with, talks with, and wants beside him.

Scripture repeatedly presents God's relationship with His people as a marriage. Isaiah tells Zion, "you shall be called Hephzibah, and your land Beulah; For the LORD delights in you, And your land shall be married" (Isaiah 62:4). In the Song, the bridegroom's repeated "my love" works like such a name. He speaks over her what he delights in, much as the LORD speaks over Zion. Paul takes the marriage picture to its highest point when he writes, "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32).

Read in that light, raʻyâh reveals a God who wants to be known as a companion. He seeks fellowship with His people as well as obedience from them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:5–2:2: love that answers shame. The bride's first description of herself is "I am dark, but lovely" (1:5). She asks not to be stared at because the sun has darkened her skin, and she explains that her mother's sons were angry with her and made her the keeper of the vineyards, and she has not kept her own (1:6). Her words mix confidence with self-consciousness about how she looks. The bridegroom's words to her are all praise: "I have compared you, my love, To my filly among Pharaoh's chariots" (1:9), a picture of splendor. A few verses later he calls her "my love" again and tells her she is fair (1:15). Then he sets her apart from every other woman as a lily growing among thorns (2:2). The Song does not narrate a change in how she sees herself. It places his "my love" close beside her "Do not look upon me," and a reader can hear his delight as the answer to her self-consciousness. She is called beautiful by the one who loves her, with nothing said about her first becoming different.

Song of Solomon 4:1–7: the flawless one. The wedding poem begins, "Behold, you are fair, my love! Behold, you are fair!" (4:1). He then praises her eyes, hair, and features one by one. The poem ends where it began: he says that his love is entirely fair and that there is no spot in her (4:7). The word raʻyâh frames the whole description, so the poem opens and closes on the name of the companion he declares unblemished. Paul uses the same picture when he says Christ gave Himself for the church "that He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing, but that she should be holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27). The church's spotlessness does not come from within her. Christ secures it by giving Himself, and He presents her "holy, and blameless, and above reproach in His sight" (Colossians 1:22). The Song's poem offers a picture of this truth by analogy: a bridegroom looks at his bride and calls her flawless.

Song of Solomon 5:2–6:4: love that is not revoked. At night the bridegroom comes to the door: "Open for me, my sister, my love, My dove, my perfect one" (5:2). The bride has taken off her robe and washed her feet, and she makes excuses (5:3). When she opens the door, she finds him gone (5:5–6). She searches the city streets in sorrow (5:6–8). The poem then moves through her description of him (5:10–16) and her confidence that he has gone to his garden (6:1–3). The next words the bridegroom speaks in the Song are "O my love, you are as beautiful as Tirzah, Lovely as Jerusalem, Awesome as an army with banners!" (6:4). The Song does not narrate a reunion step by step or say how much time passes; it sets the two scenes side by side. Read together, they are striking. A reader might expect a rebuke or a cooling of affection. Instead he compares her to the royal city of Tirzah and to Jerusalem itself. He sets no probation and does not lower his praise. The name he used at the door is the name he uses in 6:4, and the full praise of the wedding poem returns.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never quotes the Song, but it tells the story the Song's marriage imagery points toward. Christ "loved the church and gave Himself for her, that He might sanctify and cleanse her with the washing of water by the word" (Ephesians 5:25–26). The bridegroom's repeated delight in the Song pictures, by analogy, the love and fellowship Christ has with His people. Paul states something further: Christ gives Himself to cleanse the church and present her to Himself without blemish (Ephesians 5:25–27).

Revelation does not cite the Song, but Christ's words to the lukewarm church in Laodicea recall the knock at the door in Song 5:2: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me" (Revelation 3:20). That church was spiritually sluggish, and Christ called it to turn back: "As many as I love, I rebuke and chasten. Therefore be zealous and repent" (Revelation 3:19). Yet He still came to it and offered fellowship at the table. The friendship at the root of raʻyâh also points to John 15:15: "No longer do I call you servants… but I have called you friends." The story Scripture tells ends in a wedding, with Christ and His people united as Bridegroom and bride and as friends.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride calls herself "dark, but lovely" and asks not to be looked upon (1:5–6); the bridegroom says, "Behold, you are fair, my love!" (1:15). The poem lets his voice stand beside hers. Many believers feel as she did in 1:6: uneasy about being seen after hard years have marked them, as the sun darkened her skin, and aware of the vineyard they have not kept. By analogy, the Song pictures how the Lord's word answers His people's shame: He keeps speaking until His word outweighs their verdict on themselves. Returning to what Christ says about His church can steady a believer's sense of who they are.

The spring call matters just as much: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away" (2:10). He calls her "my love" and asks her to come away with him, for winter is past and the vines are blossoming (2:11–13). His love invites her into shared life rather than leaving her where she is. By application, to be loved by Christ is to be invited to go somewhere with Him.

Finally, Song 5–6 speaks to anyone who has been slow to answer and then feared it was too late. The Song is a love poem rather than a lesson on repentance, but its scenes offer an analogy: the one who knocked while she hesitated next speaks to her with "O my love." Revelation states the truth plainly. Christ rebukes those He loves, calls them to repent, and promises to come in and share the table with anyone who opens the door (Revelation 3:19–20). Repentance brings the believer back into fellowship with the same Lord who loved him before.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All nine occurrences belong to one unfolding poem, so the significant uses have been discussed above. Song of Solomon 2:13 repeats the call of 2:10 almost word for word, "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away!" The repetition makes the invitation the frame of the whole spring scene, opening and closing his call.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7453 (rea'), friend, companion, neighbor. This is the masculine noun of which raʻyâh is the feminine form, so it is a true lexical relative. It shows that friendship lies at the root of the Song's word for darling.

  • Canonical pairing: H1730 (dod), "my beloved." The bride's name for the bridegroom (Song of Solomon 5:2) is a distinct Hebrew word. It answers his "my love" throughout the Song and gives the book its language of mutual love.
