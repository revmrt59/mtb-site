RAAMAH - (Raʻmâh, Strong's H7484)

Summary

Raamah was a son of Cush and a grandson of Ham, named in the Table of Nations after the Flood (Genesis 10:7; 1 Chronicles 1:9). His name later stands for the people and region descended from him, an Arabian trading nation that Ezekiel lists among the merchants who enriched the city of Tyre (Ezekiel 27:22). The meaning of the name is uncertain. Some connect it to a Hebrew root associated with thunder or trembling, but Raamah was not an Israelite name, and the Hebrew form may simply carry over a foreign, probably South Arabian, name. Scripture never builds anything on what the name means.

Raamah's biblical weight is small but real. He is the only son of Cush whose own sons, Sheba and Dedan, are named in the same breath, and those two names go on to appear far more often than his. By Ezekiel's day, Raamah's people were dealing in "the choicest spices, all kinds of precious stones, and gold," the same kinds of treasure God had once asked Israel to bring for His dwelling place. Raamah's wealth served a proud city that God judged. The prophets, however, look ahead to a day when the gold and incense of Arabia come to Zion to praise the Lord, and that hope gives this short entry its place in the larger biblical story.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Raamah is first a person: the fourth listed son of Cush, alongside Seba, Havilah, Sabtah, and Sabtechah. Cush was a son of Ham, so Raamah was Noah's great-grandson. Genesis then names Nimrod separately as another son of Cush (Genesis 10:8). Raamah's sons were Sheba and Dedan. In 1 Chronicles 1:9 the NKJV spells his name "Raama," but it is the same man in the same genealogy.

The name also identifies the people who came from him. In Ezekiel 27:22, "Raamah" is not an individual. It is a merchant people trading alongside Sheba. Scripture moves easily between an ancestor and his descendants, and the reader should do the same while keeping the two distinct. The five occurrences of the name fall in three verses: it appears twice each in Genesis 10:7 and 1 Chronicles 1:9 and once in Ezekiel 27:22.

The meaning of the name cannot be settled. Interpreters often link it to the Hebrew root ra'am, "to thunder" or "to tremble." Because the name belongs to a non-Israelite people, though, the Hebrew spelling may only reflect how Israel heard a foreign name, and in that case the Hebrew root tells us nothing about its original sense. The Bible makes no wordplay on it. The Septuagint renders the name Regma. Later ancient writers mention a Regma in Arabia, placed either in the southwest of the peninsula or near the Persian Gulf, so the exact location remains uncertain. An Arabian setting along the routes that carried spices, gems, and gold northward fits the biblical evidence well.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Genesis 10 describes the families of Noah's sons spreading across the earth after the Flood. Raamah appears in Ham's line through Cush: "The sons of Cush were Seba, Havilah, Sabtah, Raamah, and Sabtechah; and the sons of Raamah were Sheba and Dedan" (Genesis 10:7). Of the five brothers listed together, only Raamah has his descendants named in that verse. The record does this because Sheba and Dedan matter later, as peoples who will cross paths with Israel.

First Chronicles 1:9 repeats the genealogy for the community that returned from exile. Israel was rebuilding with a small, battered population, and the Chronicler opened its history by reminding it that the whole family of nations, Raamah included, stood within the story God was telling.

Centuries later, Raamah reappears as a commercial power. Ezekiel 27 is a lament over Tyre, which the prophet pictures as a magnificent ship loaded with the goods of the world. Among its trading partners he names Raamah: "The merchants of Sheba and Raamah were your merchants. They traded for your wares the choicest spices, all kinds of precious stones, and gold" (Ezekiel 27:22). Raamah's legacy in Scripture is economic. It is not remembered for war or for religion. It is remembered for supplying the luxuries that made Tyre glitter.

Genesis 25:3 also names a Sheba and a Dedan as sons of Jokshan, Abraham's son by Keturah, and Genesis 10:28 lists a Sheba among the descendants of Shem through Joktan. These names likely came to describe Arabian peoples and regions that took in more than one family line. Later references to "Sheba" and "Dedan" therefore cannot always be traced to Raamah alone. Both are separately indexed names.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states that Raamah belongs to the family of nations descended from Noah. Genesis 10 maps the known world before Genesis 12 turns to Abram, and the order matters. God chose one man and one people with all the others in view, and He promised Abram that "all the families of the earth" would be blessed in him (Genesis 12:3). Raamah, far off in Arabia, is one of those families. God knew him by name before Israel ever existed.

Ezekiel 27 shows what Raamah's people did with what they had. Their gold, gems, and spices flowed into Tyre, a city whose ruler said in his pride that he was a god (Ezekiel 28:2). The text does not condemn Raamah directly, but its goods fed a system God was about to bring down. A further point is an implication, not an explicit statement of the text: these were the same kinds of materials God had asked Israel to give for the tabernacle, including gold, precious stones for the high priest's breastpiece, and spices for the holy anointing oil and incense (Exodus 25:3–7; 28:17–20; 30:23–25). The goods themselves are good gifts from the Creator. What gives them meaning is whose glory they serve.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Raamah himself is not mentioned again after Ezekiel, but the treasures linked to his people keep appearing in Scripture. Psalm 72 describes the righteous King whose reign reaches the ends of the earth: "the gold of Sheba will be given to Him; prayer also will be made for Him continually" (Psalm 72:15). Isaiah sees the nations streaming to Zion's light: "All those from Sheba shall come; they shall bring gold and incense, and they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). Because the name Sheba gathers more than one line, these texts cannot be claimed for Raamah's son alone. Still, the merchant goods Raamah once sold to Tyre are the same goods the prophets see carried to God in worship.

The New Testament picks up these threads in two ways. Jesus explicitly points to the queen of the South, who came from Sheba to hear Solomon's wisdom (1 Kings 10:1–10), and says, "indeed a greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42, paraphrasing the NKJV wording). The Magi who bring gold and frankincense to the child Jesus (Matthew 2:11) are not said to come from Sheba, but their gifts echo Isaiah 60 so closely that the connection is a fitting thematic resonance, though Matthew does not present it as fulfillment.

Revelation carries the darker side of Ezekiel 27 forward. When Babylon falls, "the merchants of the earth" weep because no one buys their cargo of gold, precious stones, and spices such as cinnamon and incense (Revelation 18:11–13). The overlap with Raamah's trade is striking, and Revelation draws heavily on Ezekiel's lament over Tyre. Then Revelation 21 shows the holy city built of gold and precious stones, with the glory of the nations brought into it (Revelation 21:18–26). The Bible's story ends with the treasures of the earth in the city where God dwells with His people.

5. Spirit & Life

Raamah's few verses carry a plain lesson. Everything Raamah's people traded came from God's own creation, and the question was always where it would go. Tyre used such treasure to lift itself up, and God brought Tyre down. Zion receives it as an offering, and the nations who bring it "proclaim the praises of the LORD." Our own gifts, resources, and earnings face the same question. They can feed a self-made glory that will not last, or they can be laid before the King who is greater than Solomon.

There is grace here too. A man listed once among Ham's descendants was not forgotten. The God who named Raamah also promised that people from the far corners of Arabia would come to His light, and in Christ that promise reaches every family of the earth.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:7 establishes Raamah as a son of Cush and the father of Sheba and Dedan, the only one of his brothers whose sons are named.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:9 repeats the genealogy for Israel after the exile, with the spelling "Raama."

  • Ezekiel 27:22 presents Raamah as a merchant people supplying Tyre with spices, precious stones, and gold.

  • Genesis 25:3; 10:28 show other lines bearing the name Sheba, which requires care in tracing later references.

  • Psalm 72:15; Isaiah 60:6 look ahead to the gold and incense of Sheba being given to the King and to the Lord in worship.

  • Matthew 12:42 has Jesus pointing to the queen from Sheba as a Gentile who sought God-given wisdom.

  • Revelation 18:11–13; 21:18–26 contrast merchants mourning fallen Babylon with the nations bringing their glory into the holy city.
