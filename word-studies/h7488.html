GREEN, FRESH, FLOURISHING - (raʻănân, Strong's H7488)

Summary

The Hebrew word raʻănân describes a tree that is leafy, luxuriant and full of life. English Bibles usually translate it green, but the word describes vitality more than color. It also describes "fresh oil" (Psalm 92:10) and the righteous who stay fresh and flourishing in old age. The opposite of raʻănân is a branch that withers or a land struck by drought.

Most of the time Scripture uses this word for the lush trees where Israel worshiped idols. The repeated charge "under every green tree" shows people looking for life in what looks alive. Against that background, the Psalms and prophets show where real flourishing comes from. The person who trusts the Lord is like a tree whose leaf stays green "in the year of drought" (Jeremiah 17:8). In Hosea 14:8, God takes up the idolaters' own image and says, "I am like a green cypress tree; your fruit is found in Me." The life people kept seeking under the trees of the high places was found in God all along. That life comes to us fully in Jesus Christ, the true Vine.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Raʻănân is a Hebrew adjective found only in the Old Testament. It appears in three kinds of writing:

  • In the historical books and the prophets, it describes the places where Israel worshiped false gods.

  • In the Psalms and Job, it serves as a picture of a person's life, whether righteous or wicked.

  • In one love poem, it describes a lovers' leafy resting place.

In the hills of Canaan, a thick, shady tree stood out. It was a sign of water, fertility and life in a dry land. That setting helps explain why green trees became favored worship sites and why the prophets kept naming them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Raʻănân means flourishing with fresh, vigorous life. Two senses can be distinguished:

1. Leafy and luxuriant. This is the plain description of a healthy, full tree, as in "under every green tree" (Deuteronomy 12:2) or "a green olive tree" (Psalm 52:8).

2. Fresh and full of vigor. Here the word is applied by comparison to things that are not plants: "fresh oil" (Psalm 92:10), and the righteous who remain flourishing in old age (Psalm 92:14).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word stays steady, but its moral weight changes with what it describes. When raʻănân describes the trees of the high places, it marks a site of false worship. In wisdom poetry, it becomes a test of whether a life will last. The wicked can look "like a native green tree" (Psalm 37:35), and yet "his branch will not be green" in the end (Job 15:32). When the word describes God's people, it names a gift God gives and also a responsibility. When God applies the word to Himself in Hosea 14:8, it describes the source of all fruitfulness.

4. Theological Significance

This word shows that people want to flourish, and that God made them to want it. Creation itself grew lush at God's command, and "God saw that it was good" (Genesis 1:12). The problem in Scripture is never the desire for life. The problem is where people go to find it. Israel looked for fruitfulness from the gods worshiped under the green trees of the hills. The wicked looked for it in power and wealth. Both kinds of greenness proved temporary, because neither had its roots in God.

The word also shows that visible flourishing is not proof of God's approval. A wicked man can spread out like a healthy tree. A favored nation can lose its branches. What lasts is the greenness that comes from trusting the Lord. That kind of life keeps its leaf when every outward condition argues against it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

"Under every green tree" (Deuteronomy 12:2; Jeremiah 2:20; 1 Kings 14:23). Before Israel entered the land, God commanded them to "utterly destroy all the places where the nations… served their gods, on the high mountains and on the hills and under every green tree" (Deuteronomy 12:2). The nations served their gods beneath those trees. Hosea names one appeal, their good shade (Hosea 4:13), and the setting suggests that a thriving tree also looked like a place to seek fertility and blessing. Within a few generations, Judah "also built for themselves high places, sacred pillars, and wooden images on every high hill and under every green tree" (1 Kings 14:23).

Jeremiah calls this what it was. God had "broken your yoke and burst your bonds." Israel had promised, "I will not transgress." Yet "under every green tree you lay down, playing the harlot" (Jeremiah 2:20). God compares the people's false worship to a wife's unfaithfulness. A people He had freed from slavery gave themselves to false gods under trees that looked alive. Isaiah shows where this led: "inflaming yourselves with gods under every green tree, slaying the children in the valleys" (Isaiah 57:5). Ezekiel adds a grim irony. The bodies of the slain would lie among their idols "under every green tree" (Ezekiel 6:13). The place where they went looking for life became the place of their death.

Two trees in a drought (Jeremiah 17:5–8; Psalm 37:35). Jeremiah sets two people side by side. "Cursed is the man who trusts in man and makes flesh his strength." That man "shall be like a shrub in the desert" and "inhabit the parched places in the wilderness" (17:5–6). By contrast, "blessed is the man who trusts in the LORD." He is like a tree "which spreads out its roots by the river… its leaf will be green, and will not be anxious in the year of drought, nor will cease from yielding fruit" (17:7–8).

The difference between these two trees does not show in a good year. It shows in the heat. Psalm 37:35 explains why this matters: "I have seen the wicked in great power, and spreading himself like a native green tree." The next verse says he passed away and could not be found. Some greenness is only on the surface. The green that lasts depends on roots that reach the river.

The olive tree God named (Jeremiah 11:16; Psalm 52:8). In a chapter about Judah's broken covenant, Jeremiah reminds the nation what God once called them: "The LORD called your name, Green Olive Tree, Lovely and of Good Fruit." The same verse goes on: "He has kindled fire on it, and its branches are broken." Israel's flourishing had been God's gift and God's name for them. Because they gave it to idols, the gift was turned to ruin. David uses the same image very differently. In Psalm 52, a man trusts in his riches (Psalm 52:7), and David sets himself against that man: "But I am like a green olive tree in the house of God; I trust in the mercy of God forever and ever" (Psalm 52:8). The olive tree that burns and the olive tree that thrives differ in what they trust and where they are planted.

God, the green tree (Hosea 14:8). Hosea had spent his ministry charging Israel with sacrificing under oaks and terebinths "because their shade is good" (Hosea 4:13). Israel also credited Baal for the grain, wine and oil that God Himself had given (Hosea 2:8). At the book's close, a repentant Ephraim says, "What have I to do anymore with idols?" God answers: "I have heard and observed him. I am like a green cypress tree; your fruit is found in Me."

This is the only place where raʻănân describes God. He takes the very image tied to Israel's false worship and applies it to Himself. The shade they sought under trees on the hills, He provides. The fruit they credited to Baal was always His. God does not ask His people to give up their longing for life. He shows them where that longing was meant to lead.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hosea 14:8 points forward to Jesus. "I am the true vine," Jesus said, and "he who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing" (John 15:1, 5). Jesus does not quote Hosea, but the two passages speak to the same need. In Hosea, God says, "your fruit is found in Me." In John, Jesus says that branches bear fruit only by abiding in Him. The fruitfulness God promised a repentant Israel comes to His people through union with His Son.

Jeremiah's burned olive tree also has a New Testament echo. In Romans 11, Paul describes branches broken off God's olive tree because of unbelief. He also describes branches grafted in by faith. The image warns against pride and holds out hope that God can restore. The story ends at another tree. In Revelation 22:2, the tree of life grows beside the river, "each tree yielding its fruit every month," with leaves "for the healing of the nations." Revelation does not quote Jeremiah 17, but its picture parallels the tree planted by the waters: life that never withers.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 92 gives a picture of flourishing that does not fade. "I have been anointed with fresh oil," the psalmist says (Psalm 92:10). Later he says that those planted in the house of the Lord will still bear fruit in old age and be fresh and flourishing (Psalm 92:14). The same word describes the oil and the aging saint. The freshness comes from what God pours out, not from youth. Psalm 92 does not mention the Spirit, but Scripture ties anointing to Him. When Samuel anointed David, "the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward" (1 Samuel 16:13), and "God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power" (Acts 10:38). Believers can apply this psalm to the Spirit's work in a surrendered life. He keeps it from going dry, bitter or stale.

This word also exposes a quieter form of the green-tree temptation. Most believers will never worship at a shrine on a hill. Still, many people look for life in whatever looks alive: success, approval, comfort, momentum. Jeremiah's test applies here. In a dry year, does your leaf stay green? A person rooted in Christ can keep bearing fruit when circumstances give no reason to, because his life depends on the river and not on the weather. The same God who said "your fruit is found in Me" invites every believer to stop looking for shade anywhere else.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Kings 17:10 — Worship "under every green tree" appears in the explanation for the fall of the northern kingdom. The idolatry that tempted Israel led to its exile.

  • 2 Kings 16:4; 2 Chronicles 28:4 — King Ahaz "sacrificed and burned incense on the high places, on the hills, and under every green tree"; he "encouraged moral decline in Judah" (2 Chronicles 28:19).

  • Jeremiah 3:6, 13 — God calls backsliding Israel to acknowledge that she scattered her favors under every green tree. Repentance begins when people name the sin honestly.

  • Jeremiah 17:2 — Children remember the altars by the green trees. Idolatry passed from parents to children.

  • Song of Solomon 1:16 — "Also our bed is green." Here the word describes the lovers' leafy resting place and carries none of the shame of the shrine trees. The lushness that idolatry corrupted belongs to God's good gifts when it is enjoyed in its proper place.
