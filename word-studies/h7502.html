SPREAD, MAKE (A BED), REFRESH - (râphad, Strong's H7502)

Summary

The Hebrew verb râphad means to spread something out, especially to lay out a place to lie on, as when someone makes a bed or spreads a couch. It can also carry the related idea of propping someone up or supporting them on what has been spread beneath them. The word appears only three times in the Bible, all in poetry. Those few uses carry a surprising range of feeling.

In Job 17:13, Job pictures himself making his bed in the darkness of the grave. In Job 41:30, God describes Leviathan, whose sharp underside spreads pointed marks in the mire. In Song of Solomon 2:5, the bride, faint with love, asks to be refreshed or supported with apples. Together these verses set two pictures side by side: Job's bed in the dark, where hope seems to die, and the bride's plea for strength, where love supports a fainting soul. Between them stands Leviathan, a reminder that God alone rules what no one else can tame. Read in the light of Christ, who lay in a tomb and rose to prepare a place for His people, this small domestic word reaches from despair to communion.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Râphad is a Hebrew verb drawn from ordinary household life: spreading out bedding or a couch so someone can lie down. Nothing about the word is grand. It belongs to the bedroom, not the battlefield or the temple.

The biblical poets take this everyday action and use it to express extremes. Job uses it to describe the grave. God uses it to describe a terrifying sea creature. The Song uses it of a woman overcome by love. Because the verb is so rare and so concrete, each use stands out sharply against the other two.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To spread out or lay out a surface, especially a place to lie on: Job's "If I make my bed in the darkness" (Job 17:13) and Leviathan, who "spreads pointed marks in the mire" (Job 41:30).

2. To support or prop someone up by what is spread beneath or around them, as in the bride's plea, "Refresh me with apples" (Song of Solomon 2:5).

The NKJV's "refresh" is a fair rendering of the second sense, but the underlying picture is physical. The bride is asking to be braced and held up, rather than simply cheered.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes everything. When a suffering man spreads his bed, the action becomes a picture of giving up on life. When a monster's sharp underside spreads pointed marks in the mire, the same verb becomes part of a display of fearsome power. When a lover asks to be refreshed and supported with fruit, it becomes an image of being upheld in weakness.

The Song's use is a request. The bride does not hold herself up; she asks to be refreshed and supported. That detail matters. In Job 17:13 a man speaks of making a bed for himself. In the Song, the bride asks to receive support. The verse does not say who brings the apples, though the verses around it keep her beloved in view (2:3, 6).

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, the three uses of râphad trace where a person can finally lie down. Human strength, left to itself, ends in Job's bed in the darkness. Every created power, even the most fearsome, lies under God's hand, as Leviathan's description shows. Love that comes from outside ourselves holds up the fainting soul, as the Song pictures it.

These passages show a God who is both unapproachable in power and tender toward the weak. The One who describes Leviathan's fearsome power is the same God whose love, pictured in the Song, holds up the person who has no strength left. Job needed both truths. He needed to learn that God is far greater than his questions, and he needed to know that this great God does not leave His people lying in the dust.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Job 17:13 — A bed made in the dark. Job's words come at one of his lowest points: "My days are past, My purposes are broken off, Even the thoughts of my heart" (Job 17:11). He goes on, "They change the night into day; 'The light is near,' they say, in the face of darkness" (17:12). Many readers take "they" to be his friends with their cheerful predictions, though the verse does not name them. Whoever is meant, Job finds no comfort in talk of light while darkness surrounds him. Instead he speaks of making his bed in the grave: "If I wait for the grave as my house, If I make my bed in the darkness" (17:13). He goes on to call corruption his father and the worm his mother and sister (17:14), and then he asks, "Where then is my hope?" (17:15).

Making a bed is normally an act of hope. People prepare a bed because they expect to rest and to rise again. Job turns that ordinary act inside out. Everything he once had has been stripped away, and the only place left to lay his head is the dust (17:16). This is honest biblical language for how hopeless life looks to a person cut off from every visible comfort. Scripture does not hurry Job past this darkness. It lets him speak it.

Then, two chapters later, Job speaks the very opposite: "For I know that my Redeemer lives, And He shall stand at last on the earth" (19:25). He even says, "in my flesh I shall see God" (19:26). The man who spoke of making his bed in the darkness now expects to stand before God with his own eyes. His circumstances had not changed, and the book does not explain how he moved from one statement to the other. It simply sets them side by side: the bed in the darkness and the living Redeemer.

Job 41:30 — The marks in the mire. When God answers Job out of the whirlwind, He confronts him with Leviathan: "Can you draw out Leviathan with a hook, Or snare his tongue with a line which you lower?" (41:1). God's questions pile up: "Will he make a covenant with you? Will you take him as a servant forever?" (41:4). Late in the chapter comes our word: "His undersides are like sharp potsherds; He spreads pointed marks in the mire" (41:30).

The verb Job used for making his bed now describes the marks Leviathan leaves. Its underside is like sharp pieces of broken pottery, and it spreads pointed marks in the mire, as though a jagged tool had been dragged across the mud. Job cannot catch this creature, bargain with it, or even stand safely near it. That is God's point. If Job cannot contend with a creature God made, he is in no position to put the Creator on trial. The passage humbles Job by showing him how great God is, without ever explaining his suffering.

Song of Solomon 2:5 — Held up in weakness. The bride cries, "Sustain me with cakes of raisins, Refresh me with apples, For I am lovesick." She is faint, almost collapsing under the strength of her love. Râphad here keeps its physical sense: she asks to be refreshed and supported, braced and held up as her strength fails.

The apples matter. Just before this, in Song 2:3, she describes her beloved as an apple tree among the trees of the woods. She sits in his shade, and his fruit is sweet to her taste. The verse does not say who brings the apples, but naming apples so soon after 2:3 calls the beloved and his sweet fruit to mind. She is overwhelmed by love, and the remedy she asks for is the very fruit that reminds her of him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Job's bed in the darkness and the bride's plea for support stand at opposite ends of human experience, and the gospel joins them. Job could only make his bed in the grave. The Son of God went into that same darkness on purpose. After His death, Joseph of Arimathea took Jesus’ body and "laid it in his new tomb which he had hewn out of the rock" (Matthew 27:60). Jesus lay where Job feared to lie, and He did not stay there. The Redeemer Job longed for does live, and He did stand on the earth. For Christians, Christ's burial and resurrection are the answer to Job's question, "Where then is my hope?"

Because Christ rose, the believer's final resting place is no longer a bed spread in the dust. Jesus promised, "I go to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2). Job pictured a bed he had to make for himself in the grave. Christ promises a place He Himself prepares in His Father's house.

Leviathan also points ahead. Psalm 74:14 recalls God breaking Leviathan's heads, and Isaiah 27:1 looks forward to the day the Lord punishes Leviathan the twisting serpent. The creature Job could not touch is no match for God, and Scripture's story ends with every power of chaos defeated.

Christians have long read the Song's bride and beloved as a picture of Christ and His people. That reading is drawn from how Scripture as a whole describes God's people as a bride, rather than from an explicit statement in the Song itself. Read in that light, the apple tree whose sweet fruit the fainting bride remembers speaks of Christ, who is Himself the strength of those overwhelmed by His love.

7. Spirit & Life

Job's honesty gives suffering believers permission to say how dark things really are. He did not pretend the night was day. Yet the same book that records "If I make my bed in the darkness" also records "I know that my Redeemer lives." Faith does not require denying the darkness. Job's faith looked past it to a living Redeemer.

Leviathan teaches a different lesson. Sometimes God does not answer our "why." Instead He shows us how great He is. For a person who has demanded explanations, being shown God's greatness can be the beginning of rest. If God governs the creature whose sharp underside scores the mire, He can be trusted with what we cannot understand.

The bride's cry may be the most surprising of the three. She faints because of love, not because of suffering. Paul prays that believers would know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge (Ephesians 3:19), and a love that large is more than human strength can hold. The answer is not to pull back from that love. The bride asks for the apples that recall her beloved, and in the same way the person overwhelmed by Christ's love is held up by Christ Himself.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)
