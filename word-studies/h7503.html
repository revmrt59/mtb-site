SLACKEN, LET GO, LEAVE, BE STILL - (râphâh, Strong's H7503)

Summary

The Hebrew verb râphâh pictures a grip loosening and hands dropping. It can describe hands going limp with fear, workers growing slack, a person releasing what they held, or someone stopping what they were doing. English translations render it in many ways, including "feeble," "idle," "let go," "leave," "cease," "restrain," and "be still." Underneath every one of these renderings is the same picture of hands that let something go.

That picture carries some of the Bible's most important teaching. When God promises Israel and Joshua, "He will not leave you nor forsake you" (Deuteronomy 31:6), the word behind "leave" is râphâh. God is saying His hold on His people will never go slack. In Psalm 46:10, "Be still, and know that I am God," the same verb is God's command, spoken over a scene of war and His victory, to let the hands drop. In that setting it means, to put it in plain words, lay down your weapons and stop fighting. Human hands go limp under judgment, sag under discouragement, and grip anger too tightly. God's hands never loosen. Because He will not let go of us, we can let go of what we were never meant to hold.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Râphâh is an ordinary Hebrew verb of the body. It belongs to the language of hands, arms, belts, and wings, of things that sag, droop, or come loose. Its settings are just as down-to-earth. The word shows up on Egyptian work sites, on battlefields, at city walls under construction, in the wisdom sayings of Proverbs, and in the prophets' descriptions of nations collapsing in terror.

The breadth of English renderings comes from the many things a person can "let go of": strength, effort, a task, a person, a grudge, or a fight. Translators have to choose an English word that suits each setting. That is why the same Hebrew verb can sound weak in one verse ("feeble") and deliberate in another ("cease").

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to slacken or let drop. Context shows what is slackening and why.

1. To sink, droop, or grow weak. This is usually said of hands that go limp in fear or exhaustion, as in "every hand will be feeble" (Ezekiel 7:17). The word can also describe the living creatures letting down their wings (Ezekiel 1:24) and the day sinking toward evening (Judges 19:9).

2. To let go or release. The bride says of her beloved, "I held him and would not let him go" (Song of Songs 3:4). Elisha tells Gehazi to leave the grieving Shunammite woman alone as she clings to his feet (2 Kings 4:27).

3. To stop, hold back, or refrain. The command "Cease from anger" uses this sense (Psalm 37:8), and so does the Lord's order for the destroying angel to hold back his hand (2 Samuel 24:16).

4. To be slack or negligent. Pharaoh calls Israel "idle" (Exodus 5:17). Joshua asks the tribes how long they will "neglect" to possess the land (Joshua 18:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What changes most from one use to the next is the person whose hands are involved. When the subject is a frightened or defeated person, the word describes collapse. When it is a command, it describes a decision to stop or release. When it is negated and God is the subject, it becomes a promise of unbroken faithfulness: "He will not forsake you" (Deuteronomy 4:31).

One grammatical pattern matters for the reader. The verb can be used for making someone else's hands go slack, which means discouraging them. The princes accuse Jeremiah of exactly this: "he weakens the hands of the men of war" (Jeremiah 38:4). In Ezra 4:4, the people of the land "tried to discourage the people of Judah." The English word "discourage" stands for the Hebrew picture of weakening the builders' hands. In these passages, discouragement is something people deliberately do to others.

4. Theological Significance

The uses of râphâh set two kinds of hands side by side: human hands that keep giving way, and God's hands that never do.

The prophets return again and again to one image. When news of judgment arrives, hands go limp. Jeremiah describes Jerusalem hearing of the northern invader with hands grown "feeble" (Jeremiah 6:24). He uses the same image for Damascus (Jeremiah 49:24) and even for the king of Babylon (Jeremiah 50:43). Isaiah says all hands will be limp on the day of the Lord (Isaiah 13:7). Ezekiel says "every hand will be feeble" (Ezekiel 7:17; 21:7). Armies, kings, and capital cities all reach the same end: when God moves in judgment, human strength simply falls away.

Zephaniah reverses the image. Once the Lord has taken away Zion's judgments and dwells in her midst as a mighty Savior, the word to His people is "Do not fear; Zion, let not your hands be weak" (Zephaniah 3:16). The same God whose approach as Judge drains strength from every hand restores strength to His people by being present with them. Their hands will not hang limp because He is among them. Their own courage is not the reason.

This verb also shows God's freedom to hold back judgment, which is developed below. It shows His refusal to release those He has claimed as well. David's prayer rests on that refusal: "Do not forsake the works of Your hands" (Psalm 138:8). The prayer only makes sense because God's hands are the ones that formed him, and David is confident those hands will not let go.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 31:6–8; Joshua 1:5; 1 Chronicles 28:20 — the grip that does not loosen. On the edge of the conquest, Moses tells Israel, "Be strong and of good courage, do not fear nor be afraid of them; for the LORD your God, He is the One who goes with you. He will not leave you nor forsake you" (Deuteronomy 31:6). He repeats the promise to Joshua (Deuteronomy 31:8), and God then speaks it to Joshua directly (Joshua 1:5). In this promise, "leave" translates râphâh. "Forsake" translates a different Hebrew verb meaning to abandon. Put together, the two verbs say that God will not loosen His hold and He will not walk away. The order of the passage is important. Joshua is told to be strong because God's grip will not slacken, and he does not have to manufacture his strength first. Later, David gives the same assurance to Solomon. As Solomon takes on the building of the temple, David tells him that God will not leave him until all the work for the house of the Lord is finished (1 Chronicles 28:20). God's hold reaches all the way to the completion of what He assigns.

Psalm 46:10 — a command to stand down. "Be still, and know that I am God" is often read as an invitation to quiet devotion. The psalm itself is far louder than that. The earth gives way, the mountains slide into the sea, the waters roar (Psalm 46:2–3), and "the nations raged, the kingdoms were moved" (Psalm 46:6). Then God acts: "He makes wars cease to the end of the earth; He breaks the bow and cuts the spear in two; He burns the chariot in the fire" (Psalm 46:9). Only then comes the command to let the hands drop. Coming right after the bows are broken, "Be still" works like a battlefield order to stand down; an explanatory paraphrase would be drop your weapons. The command is most likely addressed first to the raging nations of verse 6, telling them to stop fighting God and acknowledge Him. As an application, God's people can hear in it a call to stop their own frantic efforts at self-defense and acknowledge who has already won: "I will be exalted among the nations." This stillness does involve quiet, but it is the quiet of a surrender.

2 Samuel 24:16 (1 Chronicles 21:15) — mercy that holds back the hand. After David's census, the plague spreads until the angel stretches out his hand over Jerusalem. The Lord then tells him it is enough and orders him to hold back his hand. The NKJV's "restrain" translates râphâh. Judgment had been justly set in motion, but it was not a mechanism running on its own. The Lord who sent the angel could also stop him, and He did so at the very threshold of His city. That stopping point became the place where David built the altar.

Ezra 4:4; Nehemiah 6:3, 9 — hands weakened in the work. Judah's opponents did not need to tear down the temple or the wall. They only needed the builders' hands to drop. Ezra records that "the people of the land tried to discourage the people of Judah. They troubled them in building." Nehemiah saw the strategy clearly: "they all were trying to make us afraid, saying, 'Their hands will be weakened in the work, and it will not be done.'" He refused an invitation to leave the work (6:3), and he answered the threats with a prayer that used the opposite picture: "Now therefore, O God, strengthen my hands" (6:9). Proverbs states the lesson plainly: "If you faint in the day of adversity, your strength is small" (Proverbs 24:10).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story told through râphâh is about human hands that keep failing. Pharaoh calls Israel idle. Soldiers' hands are weakened. Builders are discouraged. Whole nations go limp before judgment. Even Moses' arms grew heavy during the battle with Amalek and had to be held up by others (Exodus 17:12, using different wording). The human mediators in Israel's history could not keep their own grip.

Jesus Christ is the One whose hold never slackened. He set His face toward Jerusalem. In Gethsemane He did not let go of the Father's will. With His hands nailed to the cross, He did not release the people the Father had given Him. His own words echo the promise of Deuteronomy: no one will snatch His sheep out of His hand (John 10:28). The letter to the Hebrews takes Moses' promise to Joshua, that God will never leave or forsake His people, and gives it directly to Christian believers (Hebrews 13:5). The same letter takes up the prophetic image of drooping hands and calls the church to strengthen the hands that hang down (Hebrews 12:12, echoing Isaiah 35:3). Zephaniah's promise that the Lord in Zion's midst would end the weakness of her hands finds its fullest answer in Immanuel, God with us.

7. Spirit & Life

This verb teaches the believer to ask a searching question: what am I holding, and what should I let go?

Some things must be released. Psalm 37 speaks to someone watching the wicked prosper and feeling the heat rise: "Cease from anger, and forsake wrath; Do not fret—it only causes harm" (37:8). Anger is something we hold onto, like a weapon we expect to need later. God tells us to open our hands and leave our vindication with Him. Applied to believers, Psalm 46 points to the same kind of surrender on a larger scale. We are to put down the anxious, self-protective effort that acts as if the outcome depends on us.

Pharaoh's charge in Exodus 5 sheds light here. When Israel asked to go and worship, Pharaoh called them "idle" and added to their work (5:8, 17). To a system built on endless production, stopping for God looks like laziness. Yet the Lord who breaks the bow commands His people to let their hands drop and know Him. A believer who stops striving in order to seek God is not being slack. He is obeying.

Other things must never be released. Proverbs gives the direct instruction: "Take firm hold of instruction, do not let go" (Proverbs 4:13). Job says, "My righteousness I hold fast, and will not let it go" (Job 27:6). The bride in the Song, having found the one she loves, says, "I held him and would not let him go" (Song of Songs 3:4). Her words belong to a love poem, not a command, but by analogy they picture how a believer can cling to the Lord. Our grip on what God gives is meant to resemble His grip on us.

The two commands work together. We can let our anger, fear, and frantic self-reliance fall from our hands only because God has promised that His hands will never fall from us. When discouragement arrives, as it did for Nehemiah, the right response is his prayer: "O God, strengthen my hands."

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 9:14 — God says to Moses, "Let Me alone," so that He may destroy Israel. Moses does not let go. His intercession holds on, and God spares the people.

  • Joshua 18:3 — Joshua asks seven tribes how long they will "neglect" to possess the land God has given. The land was already theirs by God's gift, yet their slackness left it unclaimed while they delayed.

  • Proverbs 18:9 — The "slothful" worker is called a brother to the one who destroys. Slack hands do real damage.

  • Job 12:21 — God "disarms the mighty," loosening the strength of the powerful. No one's grip is secure against Him.

  • Judges 8:3 — After Gideon's gentle answer, the Ephraimites' anger "subsided." A soft word can loosen a tight fist.

  • 2 Samuel 4:1 — When Abner dies, Ishbosheth "lost heart," his hands going limp at the news. The account shows how quickly human confidence collapses.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Readers sometimes connect râphâh with the familiar Hebrew verb for healing, heard in the name "the LORD who heals" (Exodus 15:26). The two words look and sound alike, but they are different verbs. "Be still" in Psalm 46:10 does not mean "be healed." It is better to let each verb carry its own meaning.
