SUPPORT, BACK - (rᵉphîydâh, Strong's H7507)

Summary

The Hebrew word rᵉphîydâh names a supporting part of a royal carriage. Translators have understood it as the base, the back, or a railing, and the exact part cannot be fixed with certainty. The word appears only once in the entire Bible, in the Song of Solomon's description of King Solomon's wedding procession. The NKJV translates it "support": "He made its pillars of silver, Its support of gold, Its seat of purple" (Song of Solomon 3:10). Because it never appears anywhere else, everything we learn about the word comes from that one golden detail and the scene around it.

The scene gives the detail its meaning. A procession comes "out of the wilderness," guarded by sixty armed men. It carries a palanquin, a covered seat borne on poles, that the king built himself, and the passage ends on "the day of his wedding" (Song of Solomon 3:6–11). Its support is made of gold. Inside, the carriage is "paved with love." The picture is of a king who spared no cost on what he built for his wedding day. Many readers understand the carriage as bringing the bride to the king, though the verses do not say who rides in it. The materials also bring to mind the Tabernacle in the wilderness. Read as an analogy, alongside Scripture's direct promises that God carries His people, the scene points believers toward Christ, who brings His people home to His joy.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Rᵉphîydâh is a Hebrew noun that occurs only in Song of Solomon 3:10. A word used only once in the Bible is called a hapax legomenon. It belongs to the same word family as the verb raphad (H7502). That verb carries ideas of spreading out, making a bed, and supporting or refreshing someone. The family connection fits a word for the part of a seat or litter that a person rests against or sits upon.

The setting is a royal wedding procession. Song 3:6–11 moves from a question ("Who is this coming out of the wilderness...?") to a view of "Solomon's couch" with its armed escort, and then to a close description of the palanquin itself. The passage ends with the daughters of Zion invited to see the king crowned "on the day of his wedding." The verses do not plainly say who rides in the carriage. Many readers understand it as the bride being brought to the king, and the wedding setting supports that reading.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rᵉphîydâh means a support: a structural part of the palanquin, apparently one that held up or steadied whoever rode in it. English translations differ on exactly which part is meant. Older versions say "bottom," and others say "back." With only one occurrence, the precise architectural part cannot be fixed with certainty. Whether it is the base, the backrest, a railing, or the supporting frame, the word names a part that gave the structure support. In Song of Solomon 3:10, that support is made of gold.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Because the word appears only once, its usage is entirely a matter of its setting. Verse 10 presents it as one item in a list of four, each made of a different material:

  • pillars of silver

  • support of gold

  • seat of purple

  • interior "paved with love by the daughters of Jerusalem"

In the list, the support comes between the upright pillars and the seat. The verse does not say exactly where it sat on the carriage, whether it could be seen, or how much weight it carried. It does say the support was gold, a metal usually kept for display and honor.

The related verb appears earlier in the same book, when the bride cries, "Refresh me with apples, For I am lovesick" (Song 2:5). The noun and the verb are separate words, and the verb does not define the noun. Still, the shared family of meaning fits the scene. The language the book uses for bearing up a weary person is the same language family it uses for the support of the king's carriage.

4. Theological Significance

The golden support shows a king who spared no cost on what he built for his wedding day. Solomon "made himself a palanquin" (Song 3:9) of Lebanon wood, silver, gold, and purple, and he set warriors around it "because of fear in the night" (Song 3:8). The details suit a royal procession coming out of the wilderness: armed strength against threats, beauty fitting for a wedding, and a support made of gold.

As an analogy, that royal provision brings to mind how God provides for those He loves. He does not offer His people a frail arrangement and ask them to hold it together themselves. Scripture repeatedly pictures God carrying His people. He bore Israel "on eagles' wings" (Exodus 19:4), carried them through the wilderness as a father carries his son (Deuteronomy 1:31), and promised to carry them from birth to old age (Isaiah 46:3–4). Song 3 does not quote these passages or claim to describe God's care. The passages state directly what the Song only pictures: God Himself carries His people.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 3:6–11: the procession out of the wilderness. The scene opens with something rising "out of the wilderness Like pillars of smoke, Perfumed with myrrh and frankincense" (3:6). For any Israelite reader, those words would recall a particular history. Israel's own story began with God leading His people through the wilderness by a pillar of cloud and fire, and frankincense was burned in His worship. The carriage then appears with silver, gold, and purple.

Those are Tabernacle materials. When God told Moses to collect an offering for His dwelling place, it was "gold, silver, and bronze" (Exodus 25:3), followed by blue, purple, and scarlet cloth (Exodus 25:4). The Ark itself was overlaid with gold and carried on poles (Exodus 25:10–13). The Tabernacle was God's movable dwelling, carried through the desert so that His presence went with His people. Solomon's palanquin is also a carried structure of gold, silver, and purple, coming out of the wilderness.

The Song never calls the palanquin a sanctuary. The connection works as an echo in the imagery, and it does not tell us what the Hebrew word itself means. The echo is still worth hearing. In Exodus, God built a golden place where He could dwell among His people on the move. In the Song, a king builds a golden carriage for his wedding day. Both are costly structures carried out of the wilderness, and both are bound up with love and nearness.

Song of Solomon 3:10: a golden support, love within. The verse moves from the outside of the carriage to its center: pillars, support, seat, and finally an interior "paved with love." Love is the last and innermost item. The golden support is easy to pass over, yet it reveals the king's mind. He did not limit his gold to what onlookers would admire first. Even the support was made of gold, a metal that does not rust or decay.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament openly teaches that marriage points to Christ and His church. Christ loved the church, gave Himself for her, and will present her to Himself in glory (Ephesians 5:25–27), and history moves toward "the marriage of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:7). Jesus called Himself "greater than Solomon" (Matthew 12:42). The New Testament never cites Song 3 directly. Even so, a royal procession coming out of the wilderness on the king's wedding day fits closely with what the New Testament says about Christ.

The Tabernacle echo sharpens the connection. John writes that the Word became flesh and "dwelt" among us (John 1:14), using a word that recalls the tent where God's glory lived. The golden dwelling in the wilderness found its fulfillment in Christ Himself. The story ends in a city of pure gold where God dwells with His people forever (Revelation 21:3, 18). Read alongside these passages, the golden support in Song 3 becomes a small picture of the whole story. As an analogy, it suggests a King who has provided, at His own cost, everything needed to bring His people safely to the day of His gladness (Song of Solomon 3:11).

7. Spirit & Life

If the carriage in chapter 3 is bringing the bride, as many readers understand, then she does not walk out of the wilderness on her own strength. She is carried. Later in the Song a similar question is asked: "Who is this coming up from the wilderness?" This time the answer is that she comes leaning upon her beloved (Song of Solomon 8:5, paraphrased). The poem does not tell a story of moving from one scene to the other, but setting the two pictures side by side is instructive: in chapter 3 a golden carriage, in chapter 8 the beloved himself. Together they picture the Christian life: what carries us is finally a Person, not a structure. Moses said it plainly: "underneath are the everlasting arms" (Deuteronomy 33:27).

The placement of the gold also deserves attention. Many believers think of God's goodness in terms of what can be seen: answered prayers, open doors, visible blessing. Song of Solomon 3:10 puts precious metal even into the support, not only into the parts that catch the eye. Some of God's most costly care for His people works the same way. He holds them up through nights they barely survive, and they often see it only afterward. The weary believer who cries, like the bride, "Refresh me" (Song 2:5) is not asking a reluctant king. That believer is resting on care the King planned and paid for long before the need arose.

The order of the verse matters too. Silver, gold, and purple surround an interior "paved with love." The strength exists for the sake of the love. God's power, glory, and faithfulness are not cold attributes set apart from His affection. They form the frame that keeps His people safe inside His love.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Rᵉphîydâh occurs only in Song of Solomon 3:10, and that verse has been discussed above. The related verb raphad (H7502) appears in the bride's plea "Refresh me with apples" (Song 2:5). In Job 17:13, Job uses it when he speaks of making his bed in darkness (paraphrased). These are uses of a related word, not further occurrences of H7507.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7502 (raphad), lexical relative: the verb from the same word family, meaning to spread out, make a bed, or refresh. See Song of Solomon 2:5.

  • Thematic connection, the Tabernacle: the gold, silver, and purple of Exodus 25:3–4 share imagery with Song 3:10. This is a connection in imagery between different words, not a lexical relationship.
