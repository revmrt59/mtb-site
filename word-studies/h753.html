LENGTH, LONG - (ʼôrek, Strong's H0753)

Summary

The Hebrew noun ʼôrek means "length." Most often it gives the long measurement of something God told His people to build: Noah's ark, the tabernacle and its furniture, Solomon's temple, and the temple Ezekiel saw in vision. Again and again Scripture records "the length shall be..." because God was not vague about the places where He would save, meet, and dwell with His people. Holiness came with exact dimensions.

The same word also measures time. In the phrase "length of days" it describes a long life, and with a small preposition it can mean "forever." Moses tells Israel that the LORD "is your life and the length of your days" (Deuteronomy 30:20), and David says, "I will dwell in the house of the LORD Forever" (Psalm 23:6), literally "for length of days." The word that once measured the house of God comes to measure how long His people will live in it. In Proverbs it even measures patience, "long forbearance," which echoes God's own character as slow to anger.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼôrek is a common Hebrew noun from the word family meaning "to be long." Its home is the building passages of the Old Testament. It appears repeatedly in Exodus and Ezekiel, nearly always in descriptions of sacred structures and the land set apart around them. Outside those settings it appears in narrative for ordinary objects, like Ehud's dagger (Judges 3:16) or King Og's iron bed (Deuteronomy 3:11). In Psalms, Proverbs, Job, and Lamentations it turns almost entirely from space to time.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Physical length, the longer dimension of an object, building, or area, usually measured in cubits: "two and a half cubits shall be its length" for the ark of the testimony (Exodus 25:10).

2. Length of time, especially in the phrase "length of days," meaning a long life or, with the preposition "for," an enduring, unending duration: "Length of days forever and ever" (Psalm 21:4).

3. Extended restraint, used in the idiom "length of anger," which means patience or forbearance: "By long forbearance a ruler is persuaded" (Proverbs 25:15).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the building passages, ʼôrek usually comes in a set with width and height, and the numbers are commanded by God before they are carried out by craftsmen. Exodus 25–27 gives the instructions; Exodus 36–38 repeats the same measurements as the work is completed. The repetition shows careful obedience: what God specified is what Israel built. The word can even stretch to the vertical, as when the NKJV renders it "high" for the pillars of Solomon's temple (2 Chronicles 3:15). The pillars' long dimension happened to run upward.

The time sense depends on the phrase. "Length of days" describes a lifespan (Job 12:12; Proverbs 3:2). Add the preposition "for," and the phrase becomes "for length of days," which the NKJV translates "forever" (Psalm 23:6; 93:5) or "for so long a time" (Lamentations 5:20). Context decides whether a long earthly life or an unending duration is in view.

4. Theological Significance

This word shows a God who sets the terms of approach. He gave Noah the length of the ark and Moses the length of the altar. People did not design their own way into His presence. Hebrews 8:5 makes the reason plain: Moses was told, "See that you make all things according to the pattern shown you on the mountain." The measurements were a copy of heavenly realities, so they could not be adjusted to suit the builder.

The same God is also the source of life's duration. Length of days in Scripture is a gift tied to Him, His wisdom, and His presence, and never a mere biological fact. His patience is a kind of length as well. The God who measures His house carefully also extends His anger slowly, holding back judgment so that people may return.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The measured house of God (Genesis 6:15; Exodus 25–27; Ezekiel 40–43). The first time ʼôrek appears, God gives the length of the ark that will carry Noah's family through judgment (Genesis 6:15). Salvation had dimensions, and God drew them. The pattern continues through the tabernacle, where each piece, from the ark of the testimony to the mercy seat to the bronze altar, receives its length (Exodus 25:10, 17; 27:1). Ezekiel's vision carries the measuring into exile. After the temple had been burned, a man with a measuring rod walks Ezekiel through gateways, chambers, and courts, recording their lengths (Ezekiel 40–42). Once the measuring of the outer walls is complete, the glory of the LORD returns to the house (Ezekiel 43). God measured out a holy place before He filled it.

"He is your life and the length of your days" (Deuteronomy 30:20). Moses' final appeal sets life and death before Israel and calls them to love the LORD, obey His voice, and "cling to Him, for He is your life and the length of your days." Moses does not say the LORD gives length of days as a separate reward; he says the LORD is their length of days. Their future in the land was bound up with holding on to Him. Wisdom carries this forward. Proverbs 3 promises length of days to the one who keeps God's commands, and pictures wisdom holding it in her right hand (Proverbs 3:2, 16). Psalm 91 closes with God's own voice over the one who trusts Him: "With long life I will satisfy him, And show him My salvation" (Psalm 91:16). Long life and seeing God's salvation are promised together.

Dwelling in the house for length of days (Psalm 23:6; 93:5; Lamentations 5:20). This is where the word's two worlds meet. In Exodus and Ezekiel, ʼôrek measures the house of God. In Psalm 23, David uses the same word to measure his stay in it: "I will dwell in the house of the LORD Forever," literally "for length of days." The shepherd who led him through the valley brings him home to a house where he stays for good. Psalm 93:5 applies the phrase to the house itself: "Holiness adorns Your house, O LORD, forever." The holiness that the measurements once guarded becomes the lasting atmosphere of the place. Psalm 21:4 shows the generosity behind this, since the king asked for life and received "Length of days forever and ever."

Lamentations shows the other side. After Jerusalem fell and the temple lay in ruins, the people cry, "Why do You forget us forever, And forsake us for so long a time?" (Lamentations 5:20). It is the same phrase that promises dwelling forever in Psalm 23, now describing how long God's absence feels. The length that blesses when God is present becomes unbearable when He seems gone.

Measuring what God has promised (Genesis 13:17; Zechariah 2:2). God told Abram, "Arise, walk in the land through its length and its width, for I give it to you." Abram owned none of it yet, so walking its length was an act of faith in a gift already spoken. After the exile, Zechariah sees a man going "To measure Jerusalem, to see what is its width and what is its length" (Zechariah 2:2). Before he can finish, word comes that Jerusalem will be inhabited like towns without walls because of the multitude, and the LORD Himself will be a wall of fire around her and the glory in her midst (Zechariah 2:4–5, paraphrased). The surveyor's line was too small for what God intended to do.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The measured sanctuary was a copy and shadow (Hebrews 8:5), pointing ahead to the true meeting place of God with people, which the New Testament finds in Jesus Christ. Scripture closes with one more measuring. An angel with a gold reed measures the holy city, and "its length is as great as its breadth... Its length, breadth, and height are equal" (Revelation 21:15–16). The city has the shape of the Most Holy Place, and its people "shall see His face" and "reign forever and ever" (Revelation 22:4–5). The house David hoped to dwell in for length of days becomes a city where God's people live in His presence without end.

Deuteronomy's confession that the LORD is "your life and the length of your days" finds its fullest expression when Jesus says, "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in Me, though he may die, he shall live" (John 11:25), and "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). Length of days was always tied to clinging to God. In Christ, that life outlasts death.

7. Spirit & Life

The measuring chapters of Exodus and Ezekiel are easy to skim, yet they teach something worth slowing down for. God cared about every cubit of the place where sinful people would draw near to Him. Our access to God is also on His terms, through the sacrifice and Person He provided, and that is a mercy. We do not have to guess how to approach Him.

The Psalms show what those careful boundaries were for. God did not measure His house to keep David out, but to make a place where David could live. Psalm 27:4 shows the same longing: "That I may dwell in the house of the LORD All the days of my life, To behold the beauty of the LORD." The believer's hope is more than a long life. It is a long life spent near Him, and the honesty of Lamentations 5:20 gives permission to say how hard it is when He feels far away.

Proverbs 25:15 adds a quieter kind of length: "By long forbearance a ruler is persuaded, And a gentle tongue breaks a bone." The Hebrew pictures a long-held anger, slow to flare. The same idiom, built with a related adjective, describes the LORD Himself as slow to anger when He proclaims His name to Moses (Exodus 34:6). A patient person is reflecting something of God's own heart. Gentleness and restraint can accomplish what force cannot, and the believer learns this by first being on the receiving end of God's long patience.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 12:12 — "Wisdom is with aged men, And with length of days, understanding." Job gives the common expectation that years bring insight, though the book goes on to show that true wisdom belongs to God.

  • Judges 3:16 — Ehud makes a dagger a cubit in length, hidden on his right thigh. The word's ordinary physical sense serves an unlikely deliverance.

  • 2 Chronicles 6:13 — Solomon stands on a bronze platform five cubits long to pray at the temple's dedication, a measured place for a king to kneel before God.

  • Ezekiel 31:7 — Pharaoh, pictured as a great cedar, is beautiful "in the length of its branches," yet his height and pride come before his fall.

  • Ezekiel 48:8–10 — The holy district of the restored land is laid out by length, with the sanctuary at its center, so that the whole land is arranged around God's dwelling.

  • Zechariah 5:2 — The flying scroll of judgment is twenty cubits long, a curse measured as precisely as the sanctuary.
