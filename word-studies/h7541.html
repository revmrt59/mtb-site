TEMPLE - (raqqâh, Strong's H7541)

Summary

The Hebrew word raqqâh names the temple, the soft, flat place on the side of the head between the eye and the ear. It appears only five times in the Old Testament, in two very different scenes. In Judges it marks the exact spot where Jael drove a tent peg into Sisera, the commander of the army of Jabin, the Canaanite king who had harshly oppressed Israel for twenty years. In the Song of Solomon it describes the bride's face, as the bridegroom tells her, "Your temples behind your veil are like a piece of pomegranate."

The word likely comes from a Hebrew root meaning thin, and the temple is in fact a vulnerable spot on the head. In Judges that vulnerable place is where a powerful enemy of God's people falls, a scene that fits the pattern of the promise that the woman's Seed would bruise the serpent's head. In the Song the bridegroom compares his bride's temples to a piece of pomegranate, a picture that suggests rich color and beauty. The same part of the body appears in a scene of God's judgment on His enemy and in a husband's praise of his wife. Read in the light of the whole Bible, both pictures point to Christ, who crushes the enemy and delights in His bride.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Raqqâh is a Hebrew word for a part of the body. Standard lexicons connect it with raq (H7534), a word meaning thin or lean, and Strong's explains raqqâh as properly thinness, used for the side of the head. The word itself simply names that part of the head. Notice also what the word does not do. Hebrew does not use it as a figure for the mind or for thinking; the Old Testament usually speaks of the "heart" for that. Raqqâh stays a physical place on the head.

All five uses fall in Judges (4:21, 4:22, 5:26) and the Song of Solomon (4:3; 6:7). Both settings are private: a woman's tent and a bridal scene of love poetry. The NKJV reads "temple" in Judges, where one blow is described, and "temples" in the Song, where the bride's face is described.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Raqqâh has one meaning: the temple of the human head. That one meaning appears in two very different settings.

1. In a story of judgment, the temple is where Sisera receives a fatal blow (Judges 4:21; 5:26).

2. In love poetry, the bride's temples are praised as part of her beautiful face and compared to a piece of pomegranate (Song of Solomon 4:3; 6:7).

Some modern translations render the Song's verses as "cheeks," because the poem is describing the visible part of the face beside the eyes. The Hebrew word is the same in both books.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Judges the word is precise. The prose account says Jael "drove the peg into his temple, and it went down into the ground" (Judges 4:21). Barak then finds Sisera "dead with the peg in his temple" (4:22). Deborah's song retells the moment in poetry and pairs the temple with the head: "She pounded Sisera, she pierced his head, She split and struck through his temple" (5:26). The poem moves from the general "head" to the exact "temple," the way a witness narrows down to the decisive detail.

In the Song the word belongs to a simile. The bridegroom is describing his bride's face from the top down, from eyes and hair to teeth, lips, and mouth. When he reaches her temples, he compares them to a "piece of pomegranate." The phrase probably pictures a cut section of the fruit, and the comparison suggests color, perhaps the red or rosy tone of her face. The Song does not explain exactly what the likeness is. The setting has moved from a battlefield story to a wedding song, yet the word still names the same part of the head.

4. Theological Significance

The Judges passages show God keeping His word in a way that humbles human pride. Deborah had told Barak, "the LORD will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman" (Judges 4:9). King Jabin had nine hundred chariots of iron (4:3), and Sisera, the commander of his army, led them into battle (4:2, 13). God brought him down through a woman's hand, a tent peg, and a blow to his temple. The word raqqâh names the place where all his strength turned out to be useless. God does not need to overpower His enemies on their terms. He can find the weak point their strength cannot protect.

The Song passages reveal another side of the same God, who made the human body and delights in the love between a husband and his wife. The part of the head where Sisera received his death blow is, in the bride's face, a feature her husband praises. The God who judged the oppressor is the God who made the human body and gave the love that delights in it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Judges 4:17–22 and 5:24–27: The enemy's head struck. Sisera fled on foot to Jael's tent because there was peace between his king and her husband's clan (Judges 4:17). Jael welcomed him with "Turn aside, my lord, turn aside to me; do not fear," and covered him with a blanket (4:18). Then, while "he was fast asleep and weary," she drove the peg into his temple, "and it went down into the ground" (4:21). The tent that seemed to offer him refuge became the place of his death. The detail "into the ground" carries weight. The commander of the army of Jabin, who had "harshly oppressed" Israel for twenty years (4:2–3), was pinned to the ground.

The scene fits a pattern that begins in Genesis 3:15, where God tells the serpent that the woman's Seed "shall bruise your head." The serpent had already been told, "you shall eat dust All the days of your life" (Genesis 3:14). Judges does not call Jael's act a fulfillment of that promise. Still, the pattern recurs: more than once an enemy falls when his head is struck, often by an unlikely hand. A woman drops a millstone and "crushed his skull" (Judges 9:53). A shepherd boy's stone "sank into his forehead" (1 Samuel 17:49). Deborah's song lingers over Sisera's temple, and the place of the blow matters. The enemy fell at the point he could not defend.

Song of Solomon 4:3 and 6:7: The bride's veiled temples. The bridegroom says, "Your temples behind your veil Are like a piece of pomegranate" (Song of Solomon 4:3). The simile suggests rich color and beauty, though the Song does not explain its exact basis. The veil is mentioned too. The Song does not say what the veil adds to the picture, but it does place her beauty in an intimate setting: he is the one who sees her this way. Sisera's temple was struck in judgment; the bride's temples are praised by the one who loves her.

Where the line repeats also matters. Between chapters 4 and 6 comes the painful night of chapter 5, when she hesitates at the door, he withdraws, and she searches the city for him. When he speaks to her again in 6:4–7, he repeats in 6:5–7 the same images as before, hair, teeth, and temples: "Like a piece of pomegranate Are your temples behind your veil" (Song of Solomon 6:7). The estrangement has not lowered his view of her. His delight is unchanged.

The pomegranate also has a sacred history. Pomegranates of "blue, purple, and scarlet" hung on the hem of the high priest's robe (Exodus 28:33), and rows of pomegranates crowned the bronze pillars of Solomon's temple (1 Kings 7:18). That temple was a building, the house of the LORD. English uses the same word for it and for the side of the head, but the Hebrew words are different; raqqâh names only the part of the head. These sanctuary uses are also separate from what the Song says about the woman, which is simply that her temples look like a piece of pomegranate. An Israelite hearer who knew the pomegranate from the sanctuary might also hear in the comparison a note of holy beauty, but that is a connection readers draw, not something the Song states.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pattern that runs from Genesis 3:15 through Jael, the woman of Thebez, and David reaches its goal in Jesus Christ. Like Sisera, the enemy who oppressed humanity looked unbeatable. Christ defeated him through what looked like weakness. By His death He destroyed "him who had the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14), and on the cross He disarmed the powers that stood against us (Colossians 2:15). Paul also promises the church that "the God of peace will crush Satan under your feet shortly" (Romans 16:20). Jael's hammer is a small, sharp picture of that larger victory: God choosing the unexpected instrument and striking the head.

The Song's portrait points the other way, toward Christ as Bridegroom. Scripture presents the church as His bride (Ephesians 5:25–27; Revelation 19:7). The same Lord who brings the oppressor down to the dust looks on His people with delight. Read together, the two settings of raqqâh hold both pictures, and both are true of Christ. He is fatal to the enemy and tender toward His bride.

7. Spirit & Life

Sisera's story can be read as a warning against false peace. He accepted a welcome that seemed safe, let Jael cover him with a blanket, asked her to stand at the tent door, and slept (Judges 4:18–21). His enemy was not in front of him. The tent looked like a refuge, yet the danger was inside it with him. People still find their rest in places God has not given, and the world's peace can leave the most important part of a life uncovered. Real safety comes only from belonging to the Lord.

For those who do belong to Him, the Song's picture brings real comfort. The bridegroom looks at his bride's face and praises what he sees there. Believers often hide their weakness from God, as though fragility disqualified them. In Christ, the places where we are most exposed are covered, and the Bridegroom who sees what others do not see still speaks with delight. The Song's repeated praise after the bride's hesitation at the door (Song of Solomon 5:2–6; 6:5–7) pictures a love that is not withdrawn after a painful night. The line itself is a love poem, not a promise about Christ, but the picture fits what the New Testament says plainly: nothing "shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:39).

God also works through ordinary things. Jael took a tent peg and a hammer, ordinary implements in the scene (Judges 4:21; 5:26), and the pomegranate was common fruit. Faithfulness in small, everyday tasks is not wasted, because the Lord uses ordinary hands and ordinary things for His purposes.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Raqqâh occurs only five times, and its most important uses have been discussed above. One verse adds a brief confirmation:

  • Judges 4:22 — "there lay Sisera, dead with the peg in his temple." Barak would not go to battle without Deborah (4:8). The LORD routed Sisera's army before Barak, and Barak pursued it (4:15–16); when he came after Sisera to Jael's tent, he found him already dead. He became a witness that God's word in Judges 4:9 came true exactly as spoken.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7534, raq (thin) — Lexical relative. Standard lexicons give it as the root behind raqqâh.

  • Canonical pairing with H7218 (rosh), "head" — Judges 5:26 sets "head" and "temple" in parallel lines, linking Sisera's death to the broader biblical theme of the enemy's head being struck (Genesis 3:15).

  • Thematic connection with H7416 (rimmon), the pomegranate — In Song of Solomon 4:3 and 6:7 the temple is compared to a pomegranate, a fruit also used to decorate the priest's robe (Exodus 28:33) and Solomon's temple (1 Kings 7:18).
