SPICED - (reqach, Strong's H7544)

Summary

The Hebrew noun reqach means spice. It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 8:2, where the bride says to the one she loves, "I would cause you to drink of spiced wine, Of the juice of my pomegranate." The NKJV renders it with the single word "spiced." Spiced wine was not everyday wine. It was ordinary wine blended with costly aromatic ingredients, a drink fit for a special guest, and the bride names it as the gift she would offer if her longing were fulfilled.

Because the word is used only once, its significance comes from that one verse and the biblical pictures around it. In the Song, the bride is not only receiving love. She longs to offer something rich for her beloved's enjoyment. Read in light of the whole Bible, the scene points to a truth many believers overlook. The Lord takes real delight in His people, and the love they bring Him can be prepared, enriched and offered with the care of someone mixing spiced wine for a king.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Reqach is a Hebrew noun. It belongs to a family of words built on the verb raqach (H7543), which means to mix, compound or blend aromatic ingredients. That word family appears in Exodus 30, where God instructs Moses about the holy anointing oil: "an ointment compounded according to the art of the perfumer" (Exodus 30:25). The same kind of skilled blending is used for the sacred incense later in that chapter.

The word's family history tells us what kind of activity lies behind it. It does not decide what reqach means in Song 8:2. In the ancient world, wine was often mixed with spices for feasts and honored guests. Spices were expensive imports, so a spiced drink signaled generosity and value. The Song does not say which spices she has in mind. It tells us only that she would give her beloved spiced wine to drink.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Reqach means spice or spiced preparation, something aromatic blended into another substance to enrich it. In its one use, the word describes wine. "Spiced wine" in Song 8:2 is wine improved by the deliberate addition of fragrant ingredients. The single sense is enough. Reqach names an enrichment that someone adds by intention and skill. The wine does not develop it by itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting of the word's one use matters. In Song 8:1 the bride wishes her beloved were like a brother, so she could show him affection openly without anyone despising her. Then she imagines bringing him home: "I would lead you and bring you Into the house of my mother, She who used to instruct me" (8:2). The spiced wine belongs to that longed-for homecoming. It is the hospitality she would offer once he was finally welcomed into the place that shaped her.

The verse pairs the spiced wine with "the juice of my pomegranate," and the word "my" is worth noticing. Earlier the bridegroom described her as "a garden enclosed," whose "plants are an orchard of pomegranates" (Song 4:12–13). The garden is his picture of her, not a literal garden she harvests. Still, when she speaks in 8:2 of "my pomegranate," the imagery suggests that the gift she imagines comes from what she has and who she is.

4. Theological Significance

The central truth in this word's setting is that love in Scripture flows both ways. The Lord gives, and His people are also able to bring Him joy. Zephaniah says, "He will rejoice over you with gladness, He will quiet you with His love, He will rejoice over you with singing" (Zephaniah 3:17). Psalm 45, a royal wedding psalm, tells the bride, "So the King will greatly desire your beauty; Because He is your Lord, worship Him" (Psalm 45:11). God is not a reluctant host putting up with His people. He finds gladness in them.

The bride's spiced wine gives that truth a concrete shape. Much of the Song is about what the beloved means to her: "your love is better than wine" (Song 1:2). In 8:2 the movement reverses. She longs to give him wine and cause him to drink it. Faith that thinks only about what it receives from God has missed half of this picture. The Lord who gives everything also desires the love His people offer back to Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 8:2 — Love that has been prepared. Ordinary wine already takes crushing, pressing and time. Spiced wine takes one step more. Someone has to gather costly ingredients and blend them with skill and purpose. The bride speaks of this drink in the language of longing: "I would cause you to drink." The Song does not say she has already mixed it or set it aside. Still, the drink she names is telling. Spiced wine is a gift that takes forethought, and her wish is to give her beloved the best she can offer. That makes the image a fitting picture of love that is cultivated and ready to be given.

Proverbs 9 — Two women, two tables. Proverbs pictures Wisdom as a woman preparing a feast: "She has slaughtered her meat, She has mixed her wine, She has also furnished her table" (Proverbs 9:2). The Hebrew word for "mixed" there is a different word from reqach, but the image is the same: a drink deliberately prepared for guests. The chapter then sets the foolish woman beside her. That woman calls out that "Stolen water is sweet, And bread eaten in secret is pleasant," and her guest "does not know that the dead are there" (Proverbs 9:17–18). Folly offers stolen pleasure that leads to death. Wisdom offers a prepared table that leads to life. The bride of the Song stands with Wisdom. The spiced wine she would offer belongs within faithful love, in the house where she was taught. Scripture also warns about people "who go in search of mixed wine" for its own sake (Proverbs 23:30). Rich drink is a blessing only when it serves faithful love and is not pursued as an indulgence.

Exodus 30 — Blending for the sanctuary. The word family behind reqach describes how the holy anointing oil was made, "compounded according to the art of the perfumer" (Exodus 30:25). The resemblance is one of language, not of definition, since the bride's wine is not temple oil. Still, the same idea of careful, costly blending appears both in the sanctuary and in the bride's gift. That shared language fits the larger biblical pattern in which love for God and worship of God are never far apart.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament calls Christ the Bridegroom (Matthew 9:15) and the church His bride. That framework has led Christians across the centuries to read the Song as a picture of love between Christ and His people. The Song does not name Christ, but the canonical pattern is clear enough to give the bride's offering lasting meaning.

Revelation shows the story's destination: "the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7). The next verse adds, "And to her it was granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright, for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints" (Revelation 19:8). Her readiness is something she has done, and her clothing is something given to her. Paul says the same from the other side: Christ gives Himself to present the church "holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27). The spiced wine of Song 8:2 offers an analogy here. The bride in the Song longs to have something rich ready for her beloved, and the church's readiness for the Lamb is the larger reality that picture can illustrate.

The feast itself is promised long before it arrives. Isaiah foresees the LORD making "a feast of wines on the lees, ... Of well-refined wines on the lees" for all people (Isaiah 25:6). At the Last Supper Jesus said, "I will not drink of this fruit of the vine from now on until that day when I drink it new with you in My Father's kingdom" (Matthew 26:29). Revelation announces, "Blessed are those who are called to the marriage supper of the Lamb!" (Revelation 19:9). The bride's wish to welcome her beloved home with spiced wine is a fitting analogy for that coming table, though Revelation itself does not describe the bride serving wine there.

One more cup belongs in this story. Psalm 75:8 describes a cup in the LORD's hand: "the wine is red; It is fully mixed," and the wicked must drain it. In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed about the cup set before Him, and then accepted it: "Shall I not drink the cup which My Father has given Me?" (Matthew 26:39; John 18:11). Many readers connect that cup with the cup of judgment in passages like Psalm 75. These are real scenes of judgment and suffering, while the bride offering wine is an image from the Song. Set side by side, they form a contrast: Christ drank the cup of judgment so that His people might share the joy of His wedding feast and bring Him the love the bride of the Song longs to offer.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers live as if the Christian life were only about receiving: forgiveness, help, strength and answers. Those gifts are real and constant. Song 8:2 opens another window. The King can be delighted by you. Zephaniah's picture of the Lord singing over His people is not poetic exaggeration. It shows the heart of a God who wants fellowship with His people and enjoys it.

The spiced wine also shows what kind of love pleases Him. At Bethany a woman, whom Mark does not name, came to Jesus with "an alabaster flask of very costly oil of spikenard," broke it and poured it on His head (Mark 14:3). John tells of Mary of Bethany, who "anointed the feet of Jesus, and wiped His feet with her hair," until "the house was filled with the fragrance of the oil" (John 12:3). Many readers take these as accounts of the same evening, though each Gospel gives its own details. In both, something very costly was spent on Jesus. Devotion grows the same way in a believer's life. Trials endured with faith, obedience chosen when it costs something and prayer that keeps going when it feels dry are the spices blended into a life over time. Revelation pictures the prayers of the saints as incense held before the Lamb (Revelation 5:8). Paul says believers are "to God the fragrance of Christ" (2 Corinthians 2:15). What is cultivated in private with the Lord rises to Him as something He values.

Notice, too, that the bride speaks of "my pomegranate," and the bridegroom has pictured her as a garden (Song 4:12–13). You do not have to invent some gift you lack. The Lord asks for the love, trust and worship He Himself has planted in you, prepared and given back to Him. The question Song 8:2 leaves with every believer is a hopeful one: when the Bridegroom comes, what will you have ready to offer Him?

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Reqach occurs only in Song of Solomon 8:2, which is developed above. The following verses use different Hebrew words, but they supply the closest surrounding imagery:

  • Song of Solomon 4:10 — "How much better than wine is your love, And the scent of your perfumes Than all spices!" The bridegroom finds the bride's love richer than wine and spice. This sets up her offering of spiced wine in 8:2.

  • Song of Solomon 4:14 — The bridegroom's picture of the bride as a garden includes "Spikenard and saffron, Calamus and cinnamon ... With all the chief spices." The one who later speaks of offering spiced wine is herself pictured as full of costly fragrance.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H7543 (raqach), the verb meaning to mix, compound or perfume. It is part of the language describing the holy anointing oil, "compounded according to the art of the perfumer" (Exodus 30:25).

  • Canonical pairing: In Song of Solomon 8:2, "spiced wine" is paired with "the juice of my pomegranate" (H6071, asis, juice; H7416, rimmon, pomegranate). Both are gifts the bride longs to give her beloved.

  • Thematic connection: Prepared or mixed wine appears in Wisdom's feast (Proverbs 9:2), the cup of judgment (Psalm 75:8) and the LORD's final feast (Isaiah 25:6). These passages use different Hebrew words, but they belong to the same family of images.
