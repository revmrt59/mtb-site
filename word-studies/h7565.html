FLASH, FLAME, PESTILENCE - (resheph, Strong's H7565)

Summary

The Hebrew word resheph is a rare poetic noun for a flash of fire, and for things that strike suddenly the way a flash does. It appears seven times, in six verses. Its uses fall into three main kinds: a flame or spark (Job 5:7; Song of Solomon 8:6), a burning sickness such as plague or fever (Deuteronomy 32:24; Habakkuk 3:5), and a flashing bolt such as lightning or arrows (Psalm 78:48; Psalm 76:3). In their settings these images picture the troubles of human life, the instruments of God's judgment, and the weapons God breaks to protect His people.

The word's most striking use comes in the Song of Solomon. There the same word that elsewhere means plague and lightning describes the "flames" of love that is "as strong as death" (Song of Solomon 8:6). Outside the Bible, Resheph was also the name of a feared plague god in the ancient world. Habakkuk 3:5 may echo that background when it pictures fever following at the Lord's feet, but the verse itself personifies fever. It does not name a god or say that the prophet was arguing against one. Set side by side, the word's uses offer a striking comparison. The same fire-language serves God's judgment, the fierce love of the Song, and the arrows God breaks to protect Zion. Together these passages show that the God whose holiness burns against rebellion is the God whose love holds on to His own.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Resheph is a Hebrew noun that appears only in poetry: the Song of Moses, Job, two psalms, the Song of Solomon, and Habakkuk's prayer. Poets chose it for vivid, compact images. It is a different word from 'esh, the ordinary Hebrew word for fire. Resheph carries the added idea of flashing, flying, or striking heat.

Israel's neighbors also knew the word as a name. Texts from across the ancient Near East speak of Resheph, a god of plague, war, and sudden death, often pictured striking with arrows. Scripture itself never uses resheph as the name of a god. In the Bible the word describes fire, disease, and weapons, all of which stand under God's authority.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is a sudden, burning flash. Context decides what kind of flash is meant:

1. A flame or spark: the visible flashes of a fire, as with sparks flying upward (Job 5:7) or the "flames" of love (Song of Solomon 8:6).

2. A burning sickness: fever, plague, or the wasting heat of disease, as in "fever followed at His feet" (Habakkuk 3:5) and "pestilence" (Deuteronomy 32:24).

3. A flashing bolt: something that streaks through the air and strikes, whether lightning (Psalm 78:48) or the swift, fiery "arrows of the bow" (Psalm 76:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

English translations use six different words for resheph, but the Hebrew holds these ideas together. To an ancient reader, lightning from the sky, an arrow from an unseen archer, and a fever that struck overnight were all sudden blows from above. In each case there was no warning and no defense. Deuteronomy 32 shows this way of thinking. In verse 23 God says He will spend His arrows on a rebellious people, and in verse 24 those arrows take the form of hunger and burning plague.

The setting decides whose fire it is. In Psalm 76 the flashing arrows belong to Zion's enemies. In Psalm 78 and Habakkuk 3 the burning heat serves God's justice. In Job 5:7 the sparks picture ordinary human trouble. In the Song of Solomon, the fire is love.

4. Theological Significance

Resheph teaches that nothing in creation acts independently of God, including what people fear most. The Lord sends burning plague against covenant-breaking Israel (Deuteronomy 32:24) and fiery lightning against Egypt's flocks (Psalm 78:48). These are not random disasters. They are the active, burning edge of a holy God's justice against rebellion and oppression. Isaiah states the principle directly: "I make peace and create calamity; I, the LORD, do all these things" (Isaiah 45:7).

Read beside other passages, the word also offers a comparison between God's holiness and His love. Moses says, "the LORD your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God" (Deuteronomy 4:24). The same pairing of fire and jealousy appears in Song of Solomon 8:6. The Song is describing human love, but its language echoes the way Moses speaks of God. The comparison shows that God's holiness and His devoted love belong together.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 8:6 — the flames of love. The bride pleads, "Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm." A seal marked ownership and identity, and she asks to be bound to her beloved that closely. Her reason follows: "love is as strong as death, Jealousy as cruel as the grave." Death never lets go of its prey, and the grave never gives back what it holds. Love is just as tenacious. The jealousy here is the fierce devotion that refuses to share the beloved with any rival, not petty suspicion. Then the poet uses resheph twice: "Its flames are flames of fire." The word that elsewhere names plague and lightning now describes love's unstoppable heat.

The line closes with "A most vehement flame." In Hebrew this phrase ends with a short form of God's name, so many readers understand it as the flame of the LORD. Others take it as a way of saying a mighty flame. Either way, the verse presents love's fire at its highest intensity, and the next verse adds that floodwaters cannot put it out. The Song celebrates the love between a husband and wife. Yet the poet describes that love with the strongest fire-language in the Bible, language that recalls how Scripture speaks of God's own jealous holiness.

Habakkuk 3:5 — fever at the Lord's feet. Habakkuk sees the Lord marching out in majesty like a conquering king: "Before Him went pestilence, And fever followed at His feet." "Pestilence" here is a different Hebrew word. "Fever" is resheph. The verse personifies fever as an attendant in the Lord's royal procession. It does not lead; it follows at His feet. Readers who know that Israel's neighbors feared a plague god named Resheph may hear an echo here, but Habakkuk names no god and does not say he is arguing against one. What the verse plainly shows is that the burning sickness people dread moves at the Lord's command. This vision did not crush Habakkuk. It brought him to the end of his prayer, where he says that even if the crops fail, he will still rejoice in the God of his salvation (Habakkuk 3:17–18).

Psalm 76:3 — flashing arrows broken. The psalm celebrates God's dwelling in Salem and Zion: "There He broke the arrows of the bow, The shield and sword of battle." Literally, the Hebrew speaks of the flashes of the bow, the fiery shafts an army sent streaking toward a city. Arrows struck from a distance, often before anyone saw them coming. Yet where God lives among His people, those arrows lie snapped like twigs. Psalm 46:9 makes the same claim: "He breaks the bow and cuts the spear in two."

Job 5:7 — sparks of trouble. Eliphaz tells Job, "Yet man is born to trouble, As the sparks fly upward." The Hebrew calls the sparks sons of resheph, children of the flame. Sparks rise from a fire by their nature, and trouble comes to human beings in a fallen world just as naturally. God later rebukes Eliphaz and his friends for having "not spoken of Me what is right" (Job 42:7), a verdict on their speech about God, not on this proverb in particular. Still, the image itself is honest about life. Trouble is real, and it rises from the ground of ordinary human existence.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Read side by side, the uses of resheph suggest a movement. Fiery trouble rises from human life (Job). Burning judgment falls on rebellion (Deuteronomy, Psalm 78). God breaks the fiery weapons of His enemies (Psalm 76). Finally the same word describes the fire of love (the Song of Solomon).

That movement reaches its goal in Jesus Christ. The New Testament still says, "our God is a consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:29). At the cross Christ took the judgment that sin deserved and, "having disarmed principalities and powers, He made a public spectacle of them" (Colossians 2:15). Then God raised Him up, "because it was not possible that He should be held by" death (Acts 2:24). Paul uses a related image when he tells believers that the shield of faith can "quench all the fiery darts of the wicked one" (Ephesians 6:16). The Greek word is different, but the picture recalls the flashing arrows broken in Zion.

The church is called Christ's bride, so the Song's plea to be set as a seal on the beloved's heart finds a fitting echo in His people. The Song describes human love and does not announce this as prophecy, but the bride's plea supplies a vivid comparison to Christ's steadfast love for His people. The love that holds Christ's people is stronger than death because He went through death and came out of it.

7. Spirit & Life

The Bible never calls God's fire safe, and the believer should not either. The God who sent fever behind His feet in Habakkuk is the God Moses called a consuming fire, a jealous God. The good news is that the God of this fire is also the God of steadfast love, and the Song's fire helps believers understand His jealousy for them as a love that refuses to let them go. When the bride asks to be a seal on the heart, she wants to be held by a love fierce enough to fight death for her.

This changes how a believer faces whatever flies at them. Trouble still rises like sparks, as Job learned. Sickness still burns, and arrows still fly. None of these is a god, and none acts outside the Lord's authority. Psalm 76 shows Him breaking the weapons raised against His people, and Habakkuk shows that trouble He does not remove still does not have the last word. Habakkuk saw fever walking behind his God and still rejoiced, because he knew who led the procession.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 32:24 — "Devoured by pestilence and bitter destruction." In the Song of Moses, burning plague is among the arrows God warns He will spend on an unfaithful Israel.

  • Psalm 78:48 — "And their flocks to fiery lightning." The psalmist recalls the Egyptian plagues, when fire came down with the hail (Exodus 9:23–24), as God's judgment on an oppressor.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1698 (deber), pestilence — a distinct word that goes before the Lord as resheph follows (Habakkuk 3:5).

  • H0784 ('esh), fire — the ordinary word for fire, joined to resheph in "flames of fire" (Song 8:6).
