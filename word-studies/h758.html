ARAM - (ʼĂrâm, Strong's H0758)

Summary

Aram is the Hebrew name for the Arameans and their homeland, the cluster of kingdoms north and northeast of Israel centered on Damascus. The NKJV usually translates it "Syria." The same name also belongs to three individuals: a son of Shem in the table of nations, a grandson of Abraham's brother Nahor, and a little-known man of the tribe of Asher. The name is traditionally understood to mean "high" or "exalted," from a root meaning to rise or be lifted up. That meaning is a traditional explanation rather than a settled one, and Scripture never builds a wordplay or a spiritual message on it.

Aram matters because of what God did in Israel's history through this nation. David subdued its kingdoms, and after Solomon's decline Aram rose again as Israel's most persistent northern rival. Kings of Israel and Judah bribed it, fought it, spared it, and finally worshiped its gods. God used Aram to discipline His wayward people, and He also used it to show His mercy and His rule over all the earth. An Aramean general, Naaman, was healed and confessed that Israel's God is the only God. Elisha fed a captured Aramean army and sent it home. In the crisis of Aram's alliance against Jerusalem, God gave Ahaz the sign of Immanuel. Through Amos, God declared that He had guided Aram's migrations just as He brought Israel out of Egypt. When Jesus stood in the Nazareth synagogue, He pointed to "Naaman the Syrian" as proof that God's grace reaches outsiders who receive it (Luke 4:27).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The name and its meaning. ʼĂrâm (אֲרָם) is traditionally understood to mean "high" or "exalted," from a root that has to do with rising or being elevated. This explanation is common, but it should be held loosely. No biblical writer explains the name, and no passage plays on its sound or sense. The name's meaning also should not be read as a description of the nation's character or destiny. Aram's spiritual significance comes from the history Scripture records, not from its etymology.

Translation. The NKJV, like many older English versions, normally renders the nation and land of Aram as "Syria" and its people as "Syrians." Biblical Aram should not be equated with the borders of modern Syria. It was a network of Aramean city-states and kingdoms, including Damascus, Zobah, Beth Rehob, and Maachah. Their territory covered the region around the Damascus oasis and the Anti-Lebanon mountains and reached eastward toward the Euphrates. Damascus became the leading power among them.

People and nation named Aram. The name belongs to three men as well as the Aramean nation and its land:

1. Aram, son of Shem (Gen 10:22–23; 1 Chr 1:17). He appears in the table of nations as a grandson of Noah and is traditionally regarded as the ancestor whose name the Aramean people bear. Genesis lists Uz, Hul, Gether, and Mash as his sons. First Chronicles 1:17 lists the same names, with "Meshech" for Mash, in one continuous line after Shem's sons without repeating the phrase "sons of Aram." Chronicles is following a compressed genealogical convention here and does not contradict Genesis.

2. Aram, son of Kemuel (Gen 22:21). He was a grandson of Nahor, Abraham's brother. His family belongs to the same household that produced Rebekah. Scripture does not say whether he was connected with the later Aramean peoples, so that connection should not be treated as fact.

3. Aram the Asherite (1 Chr 7:34). He was a son of Shemer (Shomer) in the genealogy of Asher. He was an Israelite, and nothing more is recorded about him.

4. Aram the nation and land (most occurrences, from Numbers through Amos).

Related and separately indexed names. Several closely related terms carry their own Strong's numbers and are not counted as occurrences of H0758. They still shed light on it. Paddan Aram (H6307), the region of the patriarchs' relatives. Aram Naharaim (H0763), "Aram of the two rivers," usually rendered "Mesopotamia." Aram-Zobah, a specific Aramean kingdom. The gentilic Arammi (H0761), "Aramean" or "Syrian," which appears in Israel's confession, "My father was a Syrian [Aramean]" (Deut 26:5), and in the description of Laban as "the Syrian" (Gen 25:20). The Aramaic language also takes its name from Aram.

One English-only confusion should be avoided. Older versions such as the KJV read "Aram" in Matthew 1:3–4, but that name is the Greek form of Ram, son of Hezron, in the line of Judah. The NKJV reads "Ram." He has no connection to this entry.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Origins and the Patriarchal Background

Aram first appears among the descendants of Shem (Gen 10:22–23), which places the Arameans within the family of nations that God scattered after Babel. The patriarchal stories are tied closely to Aramean country, though most of those passages use the related names Paddan Aram and "the Syrian." Hosea 12:12, which uses this name, looks back to that era. It recalls that Jacob "fled to the country of Syria" and served there for a wife, tending sheep. In context, Hosea uses this memory to humble Israel. The nation's father was once a fugitive and a hired shepherd in Aram, and the LORD brought Israel out of Egypt by a prophet (Hos 12:13).

Aram in the Days of Moses and the Judges

Balaam, the hired seer, came from Aram. His first oracle begins, "Balak the king of Moab has brought me from Aram, From the mountains of the east. 'Come, curse Jacob for me, And come, denounce Israel!'" (Num 23:7). In this wilderness encounter, a seer brought from Aram was hired to curse God's people, and God turned the curse into blessing.

In Judges, an oppressor-king from Aram, Cushan-Rishathaim, ruled Israel for eight years until the Spirit of the LORD came upon Othniel, who delivered Israel (Judg 3:8–10). Aram also became a source of Israel's idolatry. In a dark summary of the period, "the children of Israel again did evil in the sight of the LORD, and served the Baals and the Ashtoreths, the gods of Syria, the gods of Sidon... and they forsook the LORD and did not serve Him" (Judg 10:6). First Chronicles 2:23 adds that Geshur and Aram took towns from the eastern clans of Manasseh.

David's Victories over Aram

Under David, Aram came under Israel's control. David defeated Hadadezer king of Zobah. When the Arameans of Damascus came to help Hadadezer, David struck them down and placed garrisons in Syria of Damascus, and the Arameans became his servants and brought tribute (2 Sam 8:5–6; 1 Chr 18:5–6). The narrator explains the success plainly: the LORD preserved David wherever he went (2 Sam 8:6). David dedicated the silver and gold taken from Aram to the LORD (2 Sam 8:11–12).

A second campaign followed when the Ammonites insulted David's envoys and hired Aramean troops from Beth Rehob, Zobah, and Maachah (2 Sam 10:6). Joab faced enemies on two fronts and divided the army with his brother Abishai. He urged courage and left the outcome with the LORD (2 Sam 10:9–12). The Arameans fled. Hadadezer regrouped with forces from beyond the Euphrates, and David defeated them again. The kings serving Hadadezer made peace with Israel, and the Arameans were afraid to help Ammon anymore (2 Sam 10:15–19; 1 Chr 19). The title of Psalm 60 connects that psalm with these wars. Even in this period of strength, Aram remained a place of refuge for David's troubled family. Absalom made his vow while living in Geshur in Aram (2 Sam 15:8).

Rezon and the Rise of Damascus

Solomon traded horses and chariots with the kings of Aram (1 Kgs 10:29; 2 Chr 1:17). But as Solomon turned from the LORD, God raised up adversaries. Rezon seized Damascus, reigned over Aram, and "was an adversary of Israel all the days of Solomon" (1 Kgs 11:23–25, paraphrased). From then on, Damascus was an independent and often hostile power.

Bribes, Wars, and a Spared Enemy

When Baasha of Israel pressed against Judah, King Asa took the silver and gold left in the temple treasuries and sent them to Ben-Hadad of Aram to break his treaty with Israel (1 Kgs 15:18–20; 2 Chr 16:2–4). The plan worked politically, but the prophet Hanani confronted Asa. Because he relied on the king of Aram rather than on the LORD, the army of the king of Aram had escaped from his hand (2 Chr 16:7). Hanani reminded him that the eyes of the LORD search the whole earth to show Himself strong for those whose hearts are loyal to Him (2 Chr 16:9).

In Ahab's day, a later Ben-Hadad besieged Samaria with thirty-two allied kings and was routed (1 Kgs 20:1–21). His advisers concluded that Israel's gods were gods of the hills and urged him to fight on the plains (20:23). God answered that theology directly. Because the Arameans said the LORD is God of the hills but not of the valleys, He would give this great multitude into Israel's hand, "and you shall know that I am the LORD" (20:28, paraphrased). Israel won at Aphek. Then Ahab spared Ben-Hadad, called him "my brother," and made a treaty with him (20:32–34). A prophet condemned Ahab for letting go a man the LORD had appointed to destruction (20:42).

Three years later Ahab and Jehoshaphat went to recover Ramoth Gilead from Aram, against the warning of Micaiah (1 Kgs 22; 2 Chr 18). The king of Aram ordered his chariot commanders to fight only the king of Israel (1 Kgs 22:31). Ahab disguised himself, but an arrow drawn "at random" struck him, and he died propped up in his chariot facing the Arameans (22:34–35). His disguise could not hide him from the word of the LORD.

Aram in the Ministry of Elijah and Elisha

At Horeb, the LORD gave Elijah a striking commission: "Go, return on your way to the Wilderness of Damascus; and when you arrive, anoint Hazael as king over Syria" (1 Kgs 19:15). A prophet of Israel was sent to designate the king of a foreign and hostile nation.

The Elisha narratives bring Aram into the center of the story. Naaman was "commander of the army of the king of Syria," a great and honored man, because "by him the LORD had given victory to Syria" (2 Kgs 5:1, paraphrased). The text says the LORD gave victory to Aram. Naaman was also a leper. A young Israelite girl, taken captive in an Aramean raid, told her mistress about the prophet in Samaria (5:2–3). After Naaman's pride was humbled and he washed seven times in the Jordan, his flesh was restored like a little child's. He returned to Elisha and confessed that there is no God in all the earth except in Israel (5:14–15). He asked for two mule-loads of Israelite soil so he could worship the LORD back in Aram (5:17).

The king of Aram then sent raiding parties against Israel, but Elisha kept warning the king of Israel where they would strike (2 Kgs 6:8–12). The Aramean king surrounded Dothan to capture the prophet. Elisha's servant panicked. Elisha prayed, and the young man's eyes were opened to see the mountain full of horses and chariots of fire (6:15–17). The LORD then struck the Aramean soldiers with blindness, and Elisha led them into Samaria. The king of Israel asked, "My father, shall I kill them? Shall I kill them?" Elisha answered, "You shall not kill them. Would you kill those whom you have taken captive with your sword and your bow? Set food and water before them, that they may eat and drink and go to their master." The king prepared a great feast, sent them home, "So the bands of Syrian raiders came no more into the land of Israel" (6:21–23).

Later Ben-Hadad besieged Samaria, and the famine became so severe that mothers ate their children (2 Kgs 6:24–29). Elisha promised relief within a day (7:1). Four lepers, deciding they had nothing to lose, walked out to the Aramean camp and found it empty. The LORD had made the Arameans hear the sound of chariots and horses and a great army. They fled, convinced Israel had hired the Hittites and Egyptians against them (7:3–7). The siege was broken without Israel striking a blow, and Elisha's word came true to the letter (7:16).

When Ben-Hadad lay sick in Damascus, he sent Hazael to inquire of Elisha (2 Kgs 8:7–9). Elisha stared at Hazael until he was ashamed, then wept. He knew the evil Hazael would do to Israel: burning strongholds and killing young men, children, and pregnant women (8:11–12). Hazael asked how a "dog" like him could do such a thing, and Elisha told him the LORD had shown him that Hazael would be king over Aram (8:13). Hazael murdered Ben-Hadad the next day and took the throne (8:15).

Hazael's Oppression and God's Compassion

Hazael fought Israel at Ramoth Gilead, where Joram was wounded (2 Kgs 8:28–29; 9:14–15). He also marched south, took Gath, and turned toward Jerusalem. Joash of Judah bought him off with the sacred treasures of the temple and palace (2 Kgs 12:17–18). Chronicles adds that a small Aramean force defeated Judah's much larger army, because the LORD delivered them into Aram's hand when Judah had forsaken Him (2 Chr 24:23–24).

In the north, the LORD's anger burned against Israel, and He delivered them into the hand of Hazael and his son Ben-Hadad (2 Kgs 13:3). Jehoahaz's army was reduced to fifty horsemen, ten chariots, and ten thousand foot soldiers, "like the dust at threshing" (13:7, paraphrased). Then Jehoahaz pleaded with the LORD, and the LORD listened, "for He saw the oppression of Israel," and gave Israel a deliverer (13:4–5, paraphrased). On his deathbed, Elisha had King Joash shoot "the arrow of the LORD's deliverance" from Aram and then strike the ground with arrows. Joash struck three times and stopped. Elisha was angry, because five or six strikes would have meant the complete defeat of Aram (13:14–19). The section closes with God's patience: the LORD was gracious to Israel and had compassion on them because of His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and Joash recovered cities from Aram (13:22–25).

Rezin, Ahaz, and the Fall of Damascus

In the last generation of Aram's independence, the LORD began to send Rezin king of Aram and Pekah of Israel against Judah (2 Kgs 15:37). They besieged Jerusalem to remove Ahaz (2 Kgs 16:5; Isa 7:1), and Rezin recovered Elath (2 Kgs 16:6). Chronicles records that God delivered Ahaz into the hand of the king of Aram, who carried away many captives to Damascus (2 Chr 28:5).

Isaiah describes the moment the news reached Jerusalem: the heart of Ahaz and his people shook as trees shake in the wind (Isa 7:2). God sent Isaiah to meet Ahaz with a message. He told Ahaz not to fear these two kings, whom He called smoldering stubs of firewood (7:4). Their plans would not stand, because the head of Aram is only Damascus and the head of Damascus is only Rezin, mere men (7:5–8). Isaiah warned that unless Ahaz believed, he would not be established (7:9). In that setting, God offered a sign: the virgin would conceive and bear a Son and call His name Immanuel. Before the child knew to choose good, the land of the two feared kings would be forsaken (7:14–16).

Ahaz did not trust. He sent temple silver and gold to Tiglath-Pileser of Assyria, calling himself the Assyrian king's servant and son (2 Kgs 16:7–8). Assyria took Damascus, carried its people captive to Kir, and killed Rezin (16:9). Ahaz then went further. He copied an altar from Damascus for the temple (16:10–16), and according to Chronicles, "he sacrificed to the gods of Damascus which had defeated him, saying, 'Because the gods of the kings of Syria help them, I will sacrifice to them that they may help me.' But they were the ruin of him and of all Israel" (2 Chr 28:23).

The Prophets on Aram

The prophets placed Aram firmly under the LORD's judgment and rule. Amos opened his oracles against the nations with Damascus: "I will also break the gate bar of Damascus... The people of Syria shall go captive to Kir," says the LORD (Amos 1:5). Assyria's deportation of Damascus to Kir (2 Kgs 16:9) fulfilled this word. Isaiah announced that Damascus would cease to be a city and the remnant of Aram would fade like the glory of Israel (Isa 17:1–3). He also described Aram and Philistia devouring Israel from east and west, and repeated, "For all this His anger is not turned away" (Isa 9:12, paraphrased).

Aram still appears in later passages. The Rechabites came into Jerusalem for fear of the Chaldean and Aramean armies (Jer 35:11). The LORD sent Aramean raiders, with others, against Jehoiakim in fulfillment of His word (2 Kgs 24:2). Ezekiel mentions the "daughters of Syria" among those who scorned Jerusalem (Ezek 16:57) and lists Aram among Tyre's trading partners (Ezek 27:16). Finally, Amos 9:7 reframes the whole history: "Did I not bring up Israel from the land of Egypt, The Philistines from Caphtor, And the Syrians from Kir?"

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God governed Aram's history. The LORD gave victory to Aram through Naaman (2 Kgs 5:1). He sent Elijah to anoint Hazael (1 Kgs 19:15). He delivered Israel into Hazael's hand (2 Kgs 13:3) and Judah into Aram's hand (2 Chr 24:24; 28:5). He began to send Rezin against Judah (2 Kgs 15:37), announced Aram's exile to Kir (Amos 1:5), and claimed credit for Aram's earlier migration from Kir (Amos 9:7). Aram did not live outside God's jurisdiction. Its kings rose and fell at His word.

Amos 9:7 deserves particular attention. Israel had come to treat the exodus as a guarantee that God would always be on their side, whatever they did. Through Amos, God asks whether Israel is any different to Him than the people of Ethiopia, and He places Israel's exodus beside the movements of the Philistines and the Arameans. This does not erase Israel's calling, which Amos affirms elsewhere (Amos 3:2). It removes Israel's presumption. God is the Lord of every nation's story, including the stories of Israel's enemies. The details of Aram's history support this point. Amos 1:5 says Aram will go captive to Kir, Amos 9:7 says God brought Aram up from Kir, and 2 Kings 16:9 records the deportation. When Aram turned against God's purposes, God sent the nation back to the place He had brought it from. The text does not spell this out as a lesson, but the pattern is plain in the record.

Aram tested the faith of Israel's and Judah's kings. This is a careful inference from repeated episodes, and it is well supported. Asa trusted a bribe and was rebuked (2 Chr 16:7). Ahab showed misplaced kindness to an enemy God had condemned (1 Kgs 20:42). Joash of Judah emptied the temple to buy off Hazael (2 Kgs 12:18). Joash of Israel struck the ground three times when God offered complete victory (2 Kgs 13:19). Ahaz trembled before Rezin, hired Assyria, and then worshiped the gods of Damascus. Each time, Aram exposed what the king actually trusted.

Aram also displayed God's supremacy over the gods of the nations. The Arameans believed in local, territorial gods. Their advisers thought Israel's God ruled the hills but not the valleys, and God defeated them to correct that error (1 Kgs 20:23, 28). Naaman, once healed, confessed that there is no God anywhere but the God of Israel (2 Kgs 5:15). The tragedy of Ahaz is that a king of David's line adopted the Aramean logic that Naaman had abandoned. He reasoned that whoever wins the battle has the stronger gods (2 Chr 28:23). An Aramean general came to see the truth while a Judean king walked away from it.

God's discipline through Aram was real, and so was His compassion. Hazael's cruelty was terrible, and Elisha wept to foresee it (2 Kgs 8:11–12). God's sovereignty over Hazael did not make Hazael's evil good or God indifferent to suffering. Yet in the same chapters, God heard Jehoahaz's plea and saw Israel's oppression (2 Kgs 13:4). He refused to destroy His people because of His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (13:23). Aram was God's rod, but the rod was never the last word.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Immanuel sign came in the Aramean crisis (explicit New Testament fulfillment). The promise of Isaiah 7:14 was spoken to a king terrified of Rezin of Aram and Pekah of Israel. Matthew 1:22–23 declares that the birth of Jesus fulfilled this word. The setting matters. God answered fear of a hostile alliance with a promise of His own presence: "God with us." Ahaz refused to believe, but the promise stood, and its fulfillment in Christ shows that God's purposes do not depend on the faith of a frightened king. Isaiah's warning in the same chapter—unless you believe, you will not be established (Isa 7:9)—remains the call that Christ's coming presses on everyone who hears it.

Jesus named Naaman (explicit New Testament use). In Nazareth Jesus said, "And many lepers were in Israel in the time of Elisha the prophet, and none of them was cleansed except Naaman the Syrian" (Luke 4:27). Jesus used Aram's general to show that God's grace had reached a receptive outsider while many in Israel were left unhealed. The synagogue crowd was so enraged that they tried to throw Him off a cliff (Luke 4:28–29). Naaman's story became part of Jesus' own description of His mission. The Gospel would go to those who humble themselves and receive it, whatever their nation.

God's lordship over the nations (strong canonical development). The God who sent Elijah to anoint Hazael and who brought Aram from Kir is the same God whose salvation in Christ is announced to all nations. Amos 9:7 already undercuts the idea that God's concern stops at Israel's borders. The New Testament carries this concern forward when Gentiles are brought into the people of God through faith in Christ. The connection is a development of a theme, not a direct prophecy, but the theme is genuinely biblical.

Feeding the enemy (thematic resonance). Elisha's feast for the blinded Aramean army anticipates the teaching of Scripture that the godly should feed a hungry enemy (Prov 25:21; Rom 12:20) and Jesus' command to love one's enemies (Matt 5:44). The New Testament does not cite 2 Kings 6, so this is best treated as a strong resonance rather than a stated fulfillment. Still, the parallel is clear. Israel showed mercy to its enemies at the prophet's command, and the raids stopped (2 Kgs 6:23). Mercy accomplished what the sword had not.

Blindness and sight (thematic resonance). In the same chapter, God opened the eyes of Elisha's servant to see heaven's armies and closed the eyes of the Aramean soldiers (2 Kgs 6:17–18). The kings of Israel and Judah, meanwhile, repeatedly misread Aram, overestimating it when they should have trusted God and underestimating it when they spared Ben-Hadad. Scripture's larger pattern of God giving sight to the humble and exposing the blindness of the proud (John 9:39) fits naturally alongside these stories.

The language of Aram (historical resonance). The Aramaic language, named after Aram, eventually spread far beyond its homeland. Portions of Ezra and Daniel are written in it, and the Gospels preserve Aramaic words from Jesus' own lips, such as "Talitha, cumi" (Mark 5:41) and His cry from the cross (Mark 15:34). This is a historical observation rather than a theological claim. Still, it is a striking turn of history that the speech of Israel's old northern enemy became a language in which the Son of God spoke words of life.

5. Spirit & Life

God is not a local deity. The Arameans believed gods ruled particular hills, plains, and nations, and they fought accordingly. God's answer was to win in the valley so they would know that He is the LORD (1 Kgs 20:28). Naaman learned the same lesson more personally. He asked for two mule-loads of Israelite soil, promising to offer sacrifice to no other god but the LORD (2 Kgs 5:17). Scripture does not explain the request. Many readers take it as a sign that he still thought in local terms even as he confessed the one true God, but that is an interpretation rather than a stated motive. Either way, Elisha sent him away in peace (5:19). Believers today are tempted in subtler ways to treat God as Lord of Sunday but not of the workplace, or of private devotion but not of politics and money. Aram's story calls that thinking what it is: a small view of God.

Idolatry worships what seems to work. Ahaz's reasoning in 2 Chronicles 28:23 shows how idolatry operates. He did not reject religion. He changed gods because he thought Aram's gods had a better record. He misread God's discipline as the strength of another god and turned toward the very thing that ruined him. The same temptation appears whenever people measure truth by results and abandon God in seasons of loss. Ahaz missed what Hanani had told Asa a century earlier: the LORD's eyes search the earth for those whose hearts are loyal to Him (2 Chr 16:9).

Fear of people is answered by God's word and presence. Isaiah 7 is written for people whose hearts shake like trees in the wind. God did not deny that Rezin and Pekah were real or that their armies were real. He shrank them to their true size: smoldering stubs, mere men with capital cities. He also offered the promise of Immanuel. The fitting response is the one Ahaz refused, which is to stand firm by believing God. Turning to Assyria solved one problem and created a worse one.

God's mercy reaches enemies, and it can stop hostility. Elisha's command to feed the Aramean soldiers was not weakness. Israel held them captive, the king was eager to kill them, and the prophet chose a feast instead. The text records the result plainly: the Aramean raiding bands stopped coming (2 Kgs 6:23). God's way of dealing with enemies is not naïve. He is the God who also judged Damascus. But in this moment He showed that mercy can end what force only prolongs. The parallel with Naaman is worth noticing. The God who healed an Aramean commander also fed an Aramean army.

God hears His people even under discipline. Hazael's oppression was the LORD's judgment, yet when Jehoahaz pleaded, God listened, because He saw the oppression of Israel (2 Kgs 13:4). Discipline does not mean God has stopped listening. The chapter closes by saying He would not destroy His people or cast them from His presence (13:23). Those who are under God's correction can still cry out to Him and expect Him to hear.

Half-hearted obedience leaves victories unclaimed. Joash struck the ground three times and stopped, and Elisha's anger shows that God had offered more than the king was willing to take (2 Kgs 13:18–19). The lesson is not mechanical, as though God's blessing depends on counting. It concerns the heart. Scripture does not tell us what was in Joash's mind, but stopping at three looks like a response that settled for less than God was offering, and he received partial deliverance.

God turns outsiders into witnesses. In the wilderness, Aram's part in Israel's story included Balaam brought "from Aram" to curse Israel (Num 23:7), and that story reaches its spiritual high point with Naaman confessing Israel's God. Between those two figures stands a nameless captive girl who cared enough about her enemy master to point him to the prophet (2 Kgs 5:2–3). God's grace crossed enemy lines through an unlikely messenger. Jesus later held up that same story as a picture of how the gospel would travel (Luke 4:27).

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:22–23; 1 Chronicles 1:17 — Aram, son of Shem, in the table of nations.

  • Genesis 22:21; 1 Chronicles 7:34 — The two other individuals named Aram: Nahor's grandson and the Asherite.

  • Numbers 23:7 — Balaam brought from Aram to curse Israel.

  • Judges 10:6 — Israel serves "the gods of Syria" and forsakes the LORD.

  • 2 Samuel 8:5–6; 10:6–19 — David subdues the Aramean kingdoms; the LORD preserves him.

  • 1 Kings 11:23–25 — Rezon founds independent Aram-Damascus as an adversary.

  • 2 Chronicles 16:2–9 — Asa hires Aram and is rebuked for relying on a foreign king rather than the LORD.

  • 1 Kings 20:23, 28 — Aram's belief in territorial gods is corrected by the LORD.

  • 1 Kings 19:15 — Elijah commissioned to anoint Hazael; God's authority over foreign thrones.

  • 2 Kings 5:1–19 — Naaman healed and confessing the God of Israel.

  • 2 Kings 6:8–23 — Eyes opened and closed; Elisha feeds the Aramean army.

  • 2 Kings 7:3–16 — The LORD routs Aram's army and breaks the siege of Samaria.

  • 2 Kings 8:7–15; 13:3–5, 14–25 — Hazael's rise and oppression; God hears Israel and has compassion.

  • Isaiah 7:1–16 — The Aramean-Israelite alliance, the call to believe, and the Immanuel sign (fulfilled in Matt 1:22–23).

  • 2 Kings 16:5–9; 2 Chronicles 28:5, 23 — Ahaz hires Assyria, Damascus falls, and Ahaz worships Aram's gods to his ruin.

  • Amos 1:5; Isaiah 17:1–3 — Prophetic judgment on Damascus and Aram.

  • Amos 9:7 — God brought Aram from Kir as He brought Israel from Egypt.

  • Hosea 12:12 — Jacob's flight to Aram recalled.

  • Luke 4:27 — Jesus cites Naaman the Syrian.
