SHEOL, GRAVE, HELL, PIT - (shᵉʼôwl, Strong's H7585)

Summary

Sheol is the Old Testament's name for the realm of the dead, and often for the power of death itself. Righteous and wicked alike speak of going down to it. Jacob expected to go there grieving for Joseph, psalmists cry out when its cords tighten around them, and prophets announce that proud kings and nations will be brought down to it in judgment. English Bibles render the word "grave," "hell," "pit," or simply "Sheol." In the Old Testament, every NKJV use of "hell" translates Sheol, usually where God's judgment is in view. It means the realm of death, not the place of final punishment that the New Testament calls Gehenna. Poets and prophets picture Sheol as a living thing. It has a mouth that is never full, gates and bars that hold its prisoners, and cords that reach up to drag the living down.

What makes this dark word worth studying is what Scripture says about God in relation to it. Sheol is "naked before Him" (Job 26:6). David says, "If I make my bed in hell, behold, You are there" (Psalm 139:8). From that conviction grow promises that God will not leave His Holy One in Sheol, that He will redeem His people from its power, and that He will be the grave's destruction. The New Testament hears in these promises the resurrection of Jesus, who now holds "the keys of Hades and of Death" (Revelation 1:18).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shᵉʼôwl is a Hebrew noun used throughout the Old Testament, most often in Job, Psalms, Proverbs, and the prophets. These are the books where people wrestle most openly with mortality, judgment, and rescue. The word does not name a particular burial site. Hebrew has another word, qeber, for a physical tomb. Sheol is the unseen realm or condition of death that lies behind every tomb.

The NKJV's changing translations reflect context rather than different words. When grieving parents or aging kings speak of going down, the translators usually write "grave." When judgment on the wicked is in view, they often write "hell." Readers should not import the full New Testament teaching on final punishment into every use, because righteous Jacob also expected to go to Sheol. Neither should they ignore the passages where Sheol stands for God's judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of Sheol is the realm or state of the dead. Two other uses build on that meaning, and it helps to keep the three apart:

1. The realm of the dead. This is the basic sense: the place or condition into which a person goes down at death. Laments and narratives use it this way for the righteous as well as the wicked. Jacob says he will go down to his son in grief (Genesis 37:35).

2. Death pictured as a consuming power. This is not a different meaning of the word but a poetic way of speaking about the realm of death, giving it a mouth, an appetite, gates, and snares: "Sheol has enlarged itself and opened its mouth beyond measure" (Isaiah 5:14).

3. Sheol in passages of judgment. Here Sheol is the lowest depth, the opposite of heaven (Amos 9:2), and God brings the wicked down to it: "The wicked shall be turned into hell, and all the nations that forget God" (Psalm 9:17). The word still means the realm of death. What these passages teach, beyond the word itself, is that God's judgment sends the wicked down to death.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the narratives, Sheol is mostly the plain end of a life. Jacob's grief, repeated four times in Genesis, is the sorrow of an old man who expects to die mourning. David tells Solomon not to let Joab's "gray hair go down to the grave in peace" (1 Kings 2:6), meaning that justice must reach Joab before death does.

Poetry gives Sheol a body. In Numbers 16 the earth opens its mouth, and Korah's rebels "go down alive into the pit" (Numbers 16:30). That literal swallowing becomes the image the wicked borrow in Proverbs 1:12: "Let us swallow them alive like Sheol." Sheol also has architecture. Job asks, "Will they go down to the gates of Sheol?" (Job 17:16, where some translations read "bars"), and Hezekiah fears he "shall go to the gates of Sheol" in the prime of life (Isaiah 38:10). Sheol also hunts. David remembers that "the sorrows of Sheol surrounded me; the snares of death confronted me" (Psalm 18:5), which is the language of a trapper's cords and nets. In these pictures death does more than wait for people. It reaches up into the land of the living and pulls them down.

Psalms and Isaiah also describe Sheol by what is missing there: "In the grave who will give You thanks?" (Psalm 6:5). Heman feels already counted among those "cut off from Your hand" (Psalm 88:5).

4. Theological Significance

The most important truth this word carries is that Sheol lies within God's rule. Many of Israel's neighbors imagined the underworld as a separate kingdom beyond the reach of heaven's gods. Scripture leaves no such gap. "Sheol is naked before Him, and Destruction has no covering" (Job 26:6). Proverbs reasons from this fact to the human heart: "Hell and Destruction are before the LORD; so how much more the hearts of the sons of men" (Proverbs 15:11). If the deepest place in creation is open to God, no secret motive is hidden from Him.

That same reach makes God's judgment inescapable. "Though they dig into hell, from there My hand shall take them; though they climb up to heaven, from there I will bring them down" (Amos 9:2). Sheol is the lowest point a person can imagine and heaven the highest, yet God is present at both ends. For the believer, the same truth becomes comfort in Psalm 139:8.

This explains how Psalm 88 and Psalm 139 can stand in the same Bible. Psalm 88 describes the experience of death: no praise, no fellowship with God's people, a sense of being forgotten. Psalm 139 declares the reality that God's presence and authority do not stop at the grave. The anguish is honest, and the hope rests on God Himself.

Isaiah 28 shows the folly of seeking safety anywhere else. Jerusalem's leaders boast, "We have made a covenant with death, and with Sheol we are in agreement," confident that disaster will pass them by. They are trusting political arrangements and "lies" as their refuge (Isaiah 28:15). God answers that their agreement with death will be annulled (Isaiah 28:18). Death keeps no bargains with people, because it answers only to the Lord.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 16:10 and Psalm 49:15. David writes, "For You will not leave my soul in Sheol, nor will You allow Your Holy One to see corruption." On Pentecost, Peter points out that David "is both dead and buried, and his tomb is with us to this day" (Acts 2:29). David did see decay. So Peter concludes that David, "being a prophet," spoke "concerning the resurrection of the Christ, that His soul was not left in Hades" (Acts 2:30–31). The promise outgrew the man who first prayed it.

Psalm 49 takes the hope further. The arrogant rich are pictured as sheep laid in the grave, with Death tending them: "Death shall feed on them" (Psalm 49:14). Then the psalmist turns: "But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave, for He shall receive me" (Psalm 49:15). Wealth cannot buy a way out of Sheol, but God can redeem a person from it and take him to Himself.

Hosea 13:14. "I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death. O Death, I will be your plagues! O Grave, I will be your destruction!" In Hosea the line sits inside a severe judgment on Ephraim, and it closes with "Pity is hidden from My eyes," so its first force is debated. Yet God names Himself as the one who has authority to ransom from Sheol and to destroy it. Paul takes up these words at the climax of his resurrection chapter: "O Death, where is your sting? O Hades, where is your victory?" (1 Corinthians 15:55). The enemy addressed in Hosea is the enemy taunted after Christ's victory.

Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 31–32. When the king of Babylon falls, "Hell from beneath is excited about you, to meet you at your coming; it stirs up the dead for you." It has "raised up from their thrones all the kings of the nations" to greet him (Isaiah 14:9). This is the king who boasted he would rise above the stars and be like the Most High (Isaiah 14:13–14). Instead he is brought down to Sheol, to the depths of the pit (Isaiah 14:15). Ezekiel pictures Egypt and Assyria as a towering cedar that went down to hell while the nations mourned (Ezekiel 31:15–17), joining fallen warriors already there (Ezekiel 32:21, 27). Sheol levels every throne and makes the proudest ruler as weak as the dead he once despised.

Jonah 2:2. Inside the fish, Jonah says he cried out of the belly of Sheol, and God heard his voice. He was not dead, but he was as near to death as a living man can be. Psalms 18, 30, 86, and 116 use Sheol the same way, for the place where every human resource has run out. Each of these prayers turns to praise because God answered from beyond that edge.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament builds a problem that only resurrection can solve. Sheol claims one generation after another (Psalm 89:48), holds its prisoners behind gates and bars, and silences their praise. Job, in agony, asks God to hide him in Sheol until His wrath has passed, and then wonders whether a man can live again (Job 14:13–14). The promises of Psalm 16, Psalm 49, and Hosea 13 point toward an answer that the Old Testament did not yet fully explain.

Jesus gives that answer in His own body. He pointed to Jonah's three days in the fish as the sign of the Son of Man in the heart of the earth (Matthew 12:40). Jonah's figurative descent became Christ's actual death. Peter states that Psalm 16 was fulfilled when God raised Jesus (Acts 2:31). The risen Lord then says, "I am He who lives, and was dead... and I have the keys of Hades and of Death" (Revelation 1:18). Having died and risen, He now holds authority over death and the realm of the dead. Earlier in His ministry Jesus had used a different picture. He promised that "the gates of Hades shall not prevail against" the church He would build (Matthew 16:18). There the gates stand for the power of death, which will not overcome His church. Matthew's gates and Revelation's keys are two separate sayings, but they rest on the same hope: the One who conquered death holds His people safe from it.

The devouring image is also reversed. Sheol opened its mouth "beyond measure" (Isaiah 5:14), but Isaiah foresaw the Lord swallowing up death forever (Isaiah 25:8). Paul names the moment: "Death is swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). The one that swallowed every generation is itself swallowed.

7. Spirit & Life

Sheol's appetite exposes the human heart. "Hell and Destruction are never full; so the eyes of man are never satisfied" (Proverbs 27:20). Habakkuk says the proud Babylonian "enlarges his desire as hell, and he is like death, and cannot be satisfied" (Habakkuk 2:5). Greed, lust, and pride behave like the grave. They take in more and more and never say "enough." When a person notices that hunger in himself, Scripture identifies its source: such desire belongs to death, not to life. Only God, who redeems from Sheol, can give a soul what it was made to receive.

Proverbs turns this into a choice of direction. The adulteress's "house is the way to hell, descending to the chambers of death" (Proverbs 7:27). Proverbs 9 ends with a similar warning about Folly, the foolish woman who calls to the simple. The man who accepts her invitation "does not know that the dead are there, that her guests are in the depths of hell" (Proverbs 9:18). Her guests are still eating and drinking, but they are headed for death. The poem shows their destination as if it were already in view. By contrast, the way of life winds upward for the wise, so that he turns away from Sheol below (Proverbs 15:24). Even a parent's loving correction is described as delivering a child's soul from Sheol (Proverbs 23:14). Sin feels like freedom, but Proverbs shows it running downhill, and God's wisdom and discipline are His means of turning a life upward.

For believers in distress, the psalms of Sheol provide words for the worst hours. David did not pretend when the cords tightened, and Heman did not hide his sense of abandonment. They prayed from the depths to a God who is present even there. Because Christ has the keys, a Christian can grieve at a grave without fearing it as the final word.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 37:35 — Jacob refuses comfort and says he will go down to Sheol mourning for Joseph. This is the first use of the word, and it describes the sorrow of a righteous man.

  • Numbers 16:30, 33 — Korah's rebels "go down alive into the pit," a judgment that proves they "have rejected the LORD."

  • Ecclesiastes 9:10 — The Preacher urges wholehearted work now, because there is no work or wisdom in the grave where we are going.

  • Song of Solomon 8:6 — Jealous love is described as cruel as the grave, as relentless in its grip as Sheol itself.

  • Psalm 9:17 — "The wicked shall be turned into hell, and all the nations that forget God." In context, the nations have sunk into the pit they made, caught in their own net (Psalm 9:15–16). The verse warns that forgetting God leads to His judgment and to death. It does not say that only those who forget God go down to Sheol.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6913 (qeber), tomb or burial place. This is a distinct Hebrew word for a physical grave. It is useful as a contrast to Sheol, which is the unseen realm of the dead.

  • H7845 (shachath), pit or corruption. This is a related Hebrew image of the pit, thematically linked to Sheol in passages about death and decay.

  • G0086 (Hades). This is the Greek word the New Testament uses where it quotes Old Testament Sheol passages, as in Acts 2:27 (from Psalm 16:10) and 1 Corinthians 15:55 (echoing Hosea 13:14). It is a canonical pairing across languages.

  • G1067 (Gehenna). This is a thematic connection only. It is the New Testament word for final punishment and should not be read automatically into every use of Sheol.
