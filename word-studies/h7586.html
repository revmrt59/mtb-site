SAUL - (Shâʼûwl, Strong's H7586)

Summary

Saul was the first king of Israel, a son of Kish from the tribe of Benjamin, whose reign marks the troubled passage from the days of the judges to the house of David. His name means "asked for." The Hebrew is drawn from the ordinary verb "to ask," and the meaning is woven into the story itself. Israel asked for a king like all the nations (1 Samuel 8), and God gave them a man whose very name said "asked for." Paul sums it up centuries later: "And afterward they asked for a king; so God gave them Saul the son of Kish, a man of the tribe of Benjamin, for forty years" (Acts 13:21). Saul began with real gifts. He was tall and impressive, the Spirit of God came upon him, and he rescued the town of Jabesh Gilead. Yet impatience at Gilgal, a reckless vow, and half-hearted obedience against Amalek led God to reject him as king. He spent his later years tormented, jealous, and hunting David. At the end he turned to a medium, and he died on Mount Gilboa. The Chronicler's verdict is blunt: "So Saul died for his unfaithfulness which he had committed against the LORD, because he did not keep the word of the LORD" (1 Chronicles 10:13).

Saul's story matters far beyond his own lifetime. He shows what happens when God's people want leadership measured by outward strength. He shows that God wants obedience more than religious display, and that a person who keeps refusing God's word may one day find heaven silent: "the LORD did not answer him, either by dreams or by Urim or by the prophets" (1 Samuel 28:6). His failure prepares the way for David, the king God chose, and through David for Jesus, the King whose obedience never wavered. The same Hebrew name belongs to three minor figures: an early king of Edom, a son of Simeon, and a Levite (rendered Shaul in the NKJV for the latter two). In the New Testament the name returns in Saul of Tarsus, another Benjamite who persecuted the Lord's anointed. Where King Saul met judgment, Saul of Tarsus met the risen Christ and received mercy.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The name Shâʼûwl (שָׁאוּל) is a passive form of the common Hebrew verb shâʼal, "to ask, to inquire." It means "asked for" or "one who was asked about." This meaning is well established, and it is more than a dictionary fact. The writer of 1 Samuel builds on it deliberately. The people who "asked him for a king" (1 Samuel 8:10) receive a king named "asked for." Later Samuel calls their demand wickedness "in asking a king for yourselves" (1 Samuel 12:17, paraphrased). The verb of asking also echoes through the opening chapter. There Hannah asked the Lord for a son and then gave him back to the Lord (1 Samuel 1:20, 27–28). So the book sets two "asked-for" figures side by side. Samuel was asked from God in faith and given back to Him. Saul was asked for by a people who wanted to be like the nations around them. The wordplay comes from the text itself. Still, the name does not explain every part of Saul's life, and it should not be stretched into a symbol for every scene.

Four people in the Old Testament bear this name, and they must be kept distinct.

Saul, first king of Israel, is the main subject of this study. He was the son of Kish, of the tribe of Benjamin (1 Samuel 9:1–2). He was linked with Gibeah, which later Scripture calls "Gibeah of Saul" (Isaiah 10:29). His wife was Ahinoam, and his children included Jonathan, Michal, Merab, and Ishbosheth (1 Samuel 14:49–51; 2 Samuel 2:8). The Chronicles genealogies give Ner as father of Kish (1 Chronicles 8:33; 9:39), while 1 Samuel 14:51 names Ner and Kish as sons of Abiel. Readers should treat the exact family links in that generation with some care. Chronicles also lists a son named Esh-Baal, generally identified with Ishbosheth.

Saul of Rehoboth-by-the-River was one of the kings who ruled Edom "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31, 37–38; 1 Chronicles 1:48–49). He followed Samlah and was followed by Baal-Hanan. Nothing more is recorded of him.

Shaul the Simeonite was a son of Simeon, called "the son of a Canaanite woman" (Genesis 46:10; Exodus 6:15). He was ancestor of the clan of the Shaulites (Numbers 26:13; 1 Chronicles 4:24).

Shaul the Levite appears in the Kohathite line as the son of Uzziah (1 Chronicles 6:24). He is known only from this genealogy.

In the New Testament, the Greek forms of the name are separately indexed words, not occurrences of H7586. They refer back to King Saul in Acts 13:21. They also belong to Saul of Tarsus, the apostle Paul, who describes himself as "of the tribe of Benjamin" (Philippians 3:5).

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

The minor namesakes

The three minor figures named Saul appear only in lists. The Edomite king belongs to a genealogy that quietly notes Edom had kings before Israel did (Genesis 36:31). It is fair to observe that Israel's later demand for a king "like all the nations" (1 Samuel 8:5) was a request to have what their neighbors already had. Scripture itself does not connect the two Sauls, and we should not press the link. The Simeonite Saul shows that Jacob's family took in outsiders, including a Canaanite mother, from the earliest generations. The Levite Saul is a single name in a Kohathite, Levitical line traced through Korah (1 Chronicles 6:22–24).

A reluctant beginning (1 Samuel 9–11)

King Saul enters the story looking for his father's lost donkeys. He is the most handsome young man in Israel, taller than everyone from the shoulders up (1 Samuel 9:2). The search brings him to Samuel, whom God has already told to expect him and to anoint him as ruler (9:15–17). Saul's first response is humble. He says that Benjamin is the smallest tribe and his family the least in it (9:21). After his anointing, the Spirit of God comes upon him, and he prophesies among a band of prophets. The onlookers are so surprised that a saying arises, asking whether Saul is also among the prophets (10:10–12). When the lot falls on him in public at Mizpah, he is found hiding among the baggage (10:21–22).

His first test reveals real courage. Nahash the Ammonite threatens to gouge out the right eyes of the men of Jabesh Gilead. The Spirit of God comes upon Saul, and he rallies all Israel with a startling summons: he cuts up a yoke of oxen and sends the pieces through the land (11:6–7). The rescue succeeds. Saul refuses to execute the men who had scorned him, and he gives the credit to the Lord (11:13). The kingdom is renewed at Gilgal with rejoicing (11:15). Samuel then warns the nation that king and people alike will prosper only if they fear the Lord and obey Him (1 Samuel 12).

The fractures: Gilgal, Michmash, and Amalek (1 Samuel 13–15)

The first crack appears at Gilgal. The Philistines gather in huge numbers, Saul's troops begin to slip away, and Samuel has not yet arrived when Saul's patience fails. Saul offers the burnt offering himself. Samuel arrives just as he finishes. Saul explains that he saw the people scattering, complains that Samuel "did not come within the days appointed," and says he felt compelled to act (13:11–12). Samuel tells him that he has acted foolishly, that his kingdom will not continue, and that the Lord has sought a man after His own heart (13:13–14). The issue was not a minor slip in ritual. Under pressure, Saul trusted what he could see more than the word God had given through His prophet.

Chapter 14 shows the strain growing. Jonathan's faith-filled attack at Michmash sets off a Philistine rout. Saul, meanwhile, puts his army under an oath not to eat until evening (14:24). The oath weakens the troops and drives them to eat meat with the blood, against God's law (14:32–33). When Saul asks God whether to pursue the Philistines, God does not answer him that day (14:37). This is the first silence in his story. Saul learns that Jonathan unknowingly broke the oath, and he is ready to put his own son to death. The people refuse and rescue Jonathan (14:43–45). The chapter closes with a summary of Saul's wars on every side and his family (14:47–52). He had real military achievements, but the problems underneath were serious.

The decisive break comes in chapter 15. God commands Saul to destroy Amalek completely. Saul attacks, but he spares King Agag and the best of the livestock (15:9). He then goes to Carmel and sets up a monument for himself (15:12). When Samuel arrives, Saul greets him by claiming he has carried out the Lord's command (15:13). The bleating of the sheep says otherwise. Saul blames the people and claims the animals were saved for sacrifice. Samuel answers with words that echo through the rest of Scripture: obeying is better than sacrifice, and paying attention is better than the fat of rams. He adds that rebellion is like the sin of witchcraft and stubbornness like idolatry (15:22–23, paraphrased). Saul finally admits the root of it: he feared the people and obeyed their voice (15:24). Even his confession turns toward his image. He asks Samuel to honor him before the elders (15:30). As Samuel turns away, Saul grabs his robe and it tears. Samuel declares that the Lord has torn the kingdom from Saul that day (15:27–28). The chapter ends on a note of grief. Samuel mourned for Saul, and the Lord regretted that He had made Saul king (15:35).

The king and the shepherd (1 Samuel 16–27)

After David is anointed, the Spirit of the Lord departs from Saul, and a distressing spirit from the Lord troubles him (16:14). Interpreters debate exactly what this spirit was. The text is clear, though, that it came under God's rule and as part of His judgment. In an irony only God could arrange, David is brought to court to play the harp and ease the king's torment (16:19–23).

When Goliath defies Israel, Saul and all Israel are dismayed and greatly afraid (17:11). The tallest man in Israel, the one who should have faced the giant, does not go out to face him. He offers his armor to a shepherd boy, and David sets it aside because he cannot fight in it (17:38–39). The king who once nearly killed his son over an oath now leaves the challenge to a shepherd boy while the living God is mocked, though he does send David with a blessing (17:37).

David's victory turns into Saul's poison. The women sing that Saul has slain his thousands and David his ten thousands (18:7). From that day Saul eyes David with suspicion (18:9). He makes repeated spear attacks on David: in chapter 18 David escapes his presence twice (18:10–11), and chapter 19 records a later attack (19:10). Scripture names the deeper reason: Saul was afraid of David because the Lord was with David and had departed from Saul (18:12). He offers his daughters as traps. For Michal's hand he demands a hundred Philistine foreskins, hoping David will fall (18:25). His own children take David's side. Jonathan pleads for him (19:4–6), and Michal helps him escape through a window (19:11–17). When Jonathan defends David at the new-moon feast, Saul throws a spear at his own son (20:33).

At Naioth in Ramah, Saul sends three groups of messengers to seize David, and each group begins to prophesy. Then Saul goes himself. The Spirit of God comes upon him as well. He strips off his clothes and lies down before Samuel all that day and night, and the old saying returns: Is Saul also among the prophets? (19:20–24). This is the second episode linked with the proverb, and it is very different from chapter 10. There the Spirit's coming marked the start of his calling. Here it stops him in his tracks and humbles him before the prophet he has defied. A dramatic spiritual experience is no proof of a right heart toward God.

The worst crime follows. Doeg the Edomite reports that the priest Ahimelech helped David. Saul has eighty-five priests of Nob killed, and the whole town with them (22:9–19). Only Abiathar escapes, and he goes to David carrying the ephod (22:20; 23:6). The contrast is pointed. From then on David asks the Lord for guidance and receives answers (23:2, 9–12). Saul has cut down the very priesthood through which he might have sought God.

The pursuit through the wilderness of Ziph and En Gedi gives David two chances to kill Saul. Both times he refuses. He will not stretch out his hand against the Lord's anointed (24:6; 26:9–11). In the cave David cuts off only a corner of Saul's robe, and even that troubles his conscience (24:4–5). Saul weeps and admits that David is more righteous than he is, that David will surely be king, and he asks David to swear not to wipe out his descendants (24:16–22). At their last meeting Saul confesses that he has played the fool (26:21). Yet his remorse never leads to repentance. Near the end of the pursuit, David flees to Philistine territory, and Saul stops looking for him (27:1–4).

The silence and the end (1 Samuel 28–31)

Samuel is dead. The Philistines gather at Shunem, and Saul is terrified. Then comes the most sobering verse of his life: "And when Saul inquired of the LORD, the LORD did not answer him, either by dreams or by Urim or by the prophets" (28:6). In earlier years Saul had expelled the mediums from the land (28:3). Now, in disguise, he seeks one out at En Dor. He even swears by the Lord to protect her (28:10). Interpreters have long debated whether Samuel truly appeared. Many conservative commentators conclude that God allowed Samuel to come, since the medium herself is shocked by what she sees. Whatever view is taken, the message is unmistakable. The Lord has departed from Saul and become his enemy, because Saul did not obey Him concerning Amalek, and tomorrow Saul and his sons will be with Samuel (28:16–19). Saul falls full length on the ground, drained of strength. In a strange touch of compassion, the medium makes him eat before he walks out into the night (28:20–25).

On Mount Gilboa, the Philistines kill Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua. Saul is badly wounded by archers. His armorbearer refuses to kill him, so Saul falls on his own sword (31:2–5). The Philistines cut off his head, put his armor in the temple of the Ashtoreths, and fasten his body to the wall of Beth Shan (31:9–10). Then the men of Jabesh Gilead march all night, recover the bodies, and bury the bones under a tamarisk tree (31:11–13). They had not forgotten that his first act as king was to rescue their town.

The long shadow (2 Samuel; 1 Chronicles; Psalms; Isaiah)

Saul's story does not end at his death. A young Amalekite comes to David with Saul's crown, claiming he finished the king off (2 Samuel 1:1–10). It is a bitter irony: Saul's death is announced by a member of the people he refused to destroy. David does not celebrate. He executes the messenger and composes a lament for Saul and Jonathan. He calls them beloved and pleasant in their lives, and he calls the daughters of Israel to weep over Saul (1:17–27).

Saul's house fights on. Abner makes Ishbosheth king, and a long war follows between the house of Saul and the house of David (2 Samuel 2:8–10; 3:1). Then Abner defects and Ishbosheth is murdered (2 Samuel 3–4). The tribes later remind David that even while Saul was king, it was David who led Israel (5:2). Michal, Saul's daughter, despises David as he dances before the ark, and she remains childless (6:16–23). Chronicles adds that under Saul, Israel had neglected the ark altogether (1 Chronicles 13:3).

Two later episodes show both mercy and judgment reaching Saul's family. David asks whether anyone is left of Saul's house to whom he can show kindness for Jonathan's sake. He brings Jonathan's lame son Mephibosheth to his table and restores to him all the land of Saul (2 Samuel 9:1–7). Years later a three-year famine comes "because of Saul and his bloodthirsty house," because Saul in his zeal had tried to wipe out the Gibeonites. That violated the oath Israel had sworn to them in Joshua's day (2 Samuel 21:1–2; Joshua 9). Seven of Saul's descendants are handed over. Rizpah keeps watch over their bodies. David finally buries the bones of Saul and Jonathan in the tomb of Kish, and after that God heeds prayer for the land (21:8–14). Once again Saul's zeal had been aimed at the wrong target.

Chronicles opens its history of the kingdom with Saul's death and gives the theological summary in plain words: "But he did not inquire of the LORD; therefore He killed him, and turned the kingdom over to David the son of Jesse" (1 Chronicles 10:14). This might seem to contradict 1 Samuel 28:6, where Saul did inquire. The tension clears up when we see that the Chronicler is judging whether Saul truly sought the Lord. His panicked question came late, after years of disregarding God's word, and he turned to a medium. Scripture does not count that as seeking God. Chronicles also records men, including Saul's own Benjamite kinsmen, who left him to join David (1 Chronicles 12:1–2, 19, 23–29).

Saul's pursuit also left its mark on Israel's worship. The superscriptions of Psalms 18, 52, 54, 57, and 59 tie David's prayers for deliverance to his years of flight from Saul. Psalm 18, also found as 2 Samuel 22, is David's song of thanks when the Lord delivered him from all his enemies and from the hand of Saul. Isaiah later names "Gibeah of Saul" on the route of an invading army (Isaiah 10:29). By then Saul's name had become simply a marker on the map of Benjamin.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states plainly that Israel's demand for a king was a rejection of God as their King (1 Samuel 8:7). Yet God granted the request and chose Saul Himself (9:15–17; 10:24). The text also states why Saul fell: he did not keep the word of the Lord (1 Samuel 13:13; 15:23; 1 Chronicles 10:13). From those facts we can carefully draw a larger point. Saul's reign was a hard lesson God allowed. Israel got a king who looked like the kings of the nations, and they learned that height, good looks, and military success cannot hold up a kingdom when the king will not listen to God.

Saul's failure also prepared the stage for God's own choice. Samuel told Saul that the Lord had sought a man after His own heart (13:14). When Samuel came to Jesse's house, God warned him not to look at appearance or height, because the Lord looks at the heart (16:7). That warning reads like a direct comment on Saul, who had been introduced by his stature. In the promise to David, God draws the contrast Himself. He says His mercy will not depart from David's son as He took it from Saul (2 Samuel 7:15). With these words Saul becomes the dark background against which God's faithful love to David's line shines. That love is what later Scripture calls the Davidic covenant.

Saul's life also displays God's justice and patience together. Judgment was announced long before it fell. Saul reigned for years after his rejection, and he received repeated warnings, the example of David's mercy in sparing his life, and moments of tears and confession. Yet he would not turn. God's verdict was slow, but it came.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

Explicit New Testament interpretation. When Paul preaches in Pisidian Antioch, he moves from Saul's forty-year reign to God's removal of Saul and the raising up of David. He then goes straight from David's offspring to Jesus, the Savior God promised (Acts 13:21–23). In other words, the apostle himself places Saul at the start of the line of kings that leads to Christ, as the king who had to be set aside. 2 Samuel 7:15 makes the same contrast from the Old Testament side. The steadfast love God removed from Saul is the steadfast love He promised never to remove from David's Son. Jesus is the King in whom that promise holds forever.

Strongly supported canonical development. Saul's great failure was offering sacrifice in place of obedience (1 Samuel 15:22). The Psalms take up the same theme. In Psalm 40:6–8 David says God did not desire sacrifice and offering, and that he delights to do God's will. Hebrews 10:5–10 places those words in the mouth of Christ and says that by His willing obedience we are made holy through His offering. Where Saul tried to cover disobedience with sacrifice, Jesus offered Himself as the sacrifice precisely by obeying the Father completely. The king Israel asked for could not obey. The King God gave did.

There is also a clear pattern in Saul's hostility toward David. Saul hunted the Lord's anointed, the innocent man God had chosen, using the full power of the throne. David refused to defend himself by killing Saul and waited for God to vindicate him. In Acts 4:25–27 the early church applies Psalm 2, the rulers taking counsel against the Lord and His Anointed, to the opposition against Jesus. The text does not call Saul a type of those rulers, but the resonance is real. David suffered rejection from an established king before he received the throne. Christ went to His throne by the same road, through suffering at the hands of those in power.

Thematic resonance: the two Sauls. The New Testament never explicitly compares the apostle to the king. Still, the parallels are striking, and Acts 13 brings them together: the preacher introduced as "Saul, who also is called Paul" (Acts 13:9) recounts the reign of King Saul. Both men were Benjamites (Philippians 3:5). Both carried a zeal that turned violent. King Saul killed the Gibeonites in his zeal (2 Samuel 21:2). Paul describes his own former zeal as persecuting the church (Philippians 3:6, paraphrased). Both hunted the Lord's anointed. Saul of Tarsus heard the risen Jesus ask why Saul was persecuting Him (Acts 9:4). In that moment Jesus identified Himself with His people. The endings could not be more different. King Saul's final encounter with a word from beyond the grave announced his death. Saul of Tarsus's encounter with the risen Christ brought him to repentance, forgiveness, and a lifelong commission. Offered as a pattern rather than a stated type, this contrast shows the gospel plainly: the same God who judged one Saul showed mercy to the other.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Saul's story does not mainly warn about weakness. It warns about strength that will not bow. He had gifts, opportunity, prophetic guidance, and early help from the Spirit of God. What he lacked was a willing heart. At Gilgal he reasoned that circumstances forced him to act. At Amalek he renamed disobedience as worship. At each step he was sure he was doing something religious. Samuel's answer still searches us: God is not impressed by offerings made from what we kept back from Him. He wants our attention to His word and our obedience to it.

The root Saul finally admitted was the fear of people (1 Samuel 15:24). He watched the troops scatter, listened to the soldiers' wishes, and asked to be honored before the elders. A heart ruled by the opinion of others will always obey the loudest voice in the room. The cure is not a stronger personality but the fear of the Lord, which Samuel had urged on king and people from the beginning (1 Samuel 12:14, 24).

His fierce zeal also deserves attention. Saul was ready to kill his son over a rash oath and ready to wipe out the Gibeonites. He hunted David across the wilderness with three thousand chosen men. Yet when Goliath mocked the living God, he did not go out to face him. Zeal that rises up to defend our own decisions, our reputation, or our position, while staying quiet about God's honor, is not zeal for God. The jealousy that began over one victory song grew until it consumed his reign. Refusing to rejoice in what God was doing through someone else opened the door to everything that followed.

Saul also shows that spiritual experience is not the same as knowing God. He prophesied more than once (1 Samuel 10:10; 18:10; 19:23–24), and the second episode linked with the proverb was a humbling interruption, not a sign of God's approval. In Saul's era the Spirit came upon him for the work of kingship, and when God rejected his kingship, the Spirit departed from him, though he kept the throne for years. Believers in Christ have a different promise: the Spirit is given to abide with them forever (John 14:16). Saul's story should not drive a trusting believer into fear. It should give pause to anyone who treats past spiritual moments as a substitute for present obedience.

The silence of 1 Samuel 28:6 is the hardest word in Saul's life. After years of setting aside God's word, he wanted a word from God and heard nothing. He had even destroyed the priests through whom he might have inquired. God's patience is long, but we are never told to presume on it. Hebrews 3 warns its readers about this very thing: today, if you hear His voice, do not harden your heart.

Yet the last word over Saul's house is not silence. David sought out a lame grandson of Saul, brought him to the king's table, and restored his inheritance for Jonathan's sake (2 Samuel 9). Centuries later the risen Christ stopped a persecuting Benjamite named Saul and made him an apostle of grace. Mephibosheth received mercy because of his father Jonathan. Saul of Tarsus received mercy because of Christ. Those who had every reason to expect judgment found a place at the King's table. The God who rejected the king Israel asked for still gives grace to those who will receive the King He has chosen.
