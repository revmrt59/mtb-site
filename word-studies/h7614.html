SHEBA - (Shᵉbâʼ, Strong's H7614)

Summary

Sheba is the name most closely associated with a wealthy trading people of southern Arabia, the Sabeans, though not every biblical Sheba group can simply be identified with that southern kingdom. Scripture traces the name through three separate genealogical lines: a descendant of Ham through Cush, a descendant of Shem through Joktan, and a grandson of Abraham through Keturah. The meaning of the name itself is uncertain. Scripture offers no explanation or wordplay on it, so the study rests on what the Bible says about Sheba, not on an etymology. Readers should also keep this name separate from two others that look similar in English. Seba is a different name, and Sheba the son of Bichri, who rebelled against David, is a different Hebrew name with its own Strong's number. In Job, Sabeans appear as raiders who strike Job's household. They are also known as caravan traders in gold, spices, frankincense, and precious stones. Sheba's most famous moment comes when its queen travels to Jerusalem to test Solomon's wisdom. She hears his answers, sees his house and the way he went up to the house of the LORD, and blesses the LORD who set him on Israel's throne "to do justice and righteousness" (1 Kings 10:9).

The prophets and psalmists then take Sheba further. Psalm 72 sees the kings of Sheba bringing gifts to God's righteous King. Isaiah 60:6 promises that "all those from Sheba shall come" bearing gold and incense to "proclaim the praises of the LORD." Jeremiah gives a sober warning in the other direction: frankincense from Sheba means nothing to God when His people's hearts are far from Him (Jeremiah 6:20). Jesus Himself recalls the queen's long journey. He says that the "queen of the South" will rise in the judgment to condemn a generation that refused to listen, because "a greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42). Sheba becomes a biblical picture of distant nations drawn to the wisdom and glory of God's King, and a standing rebuke to anyone who has that King close at hand and will not come to Him.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name Shᵉbâʼ (Strong's H7614) appears 23 times in the Old Testament. It refers both to individuals in the genealogies and to the people and land that bore their name, the Sabeans of southern Arabia. That region is generally associated with modern Yemen, and its people controlled the overland trade in incense and spices. Ezekiel calls them merchants who traded "the choicest spices, all kinds of precious stones, and gold" (Ezekiel 27:22).

The genealogies name three men called Sheba:

  • Sheba son of Raamah, in the line of Ham through Cush (Genesis 10:7; 1 Chronicles 1:9). The NKJV reads, "the sons of Raamah were Sheba and Dedan."

  • Sheba son of Joktan, in the line of Shem through Eber (Genesis 10:28; 1 Chronicles 1:22). He is listed with brothers such as Ophir and Havilah.

  • Sheba son of Jokshan, a grandson of Abraham through Keturah (Genesis 25:3; 1 Chronicles 1:32). Here again Sheba is paired with a brother named Dedan.

Scripture does not explain how these three relate to the later kingdom of Sheba. They may be three distinct ancestors whose descendants mixed along the Arabian trade routes. They may also be one people group whose ties to several branches of humanity are recorded from different angles. The text leaves the question open, and so should we. Even so, the pairing of Sheba with Dedan in both the Cushite and the Abrahamic lines is striking.

The meaning of the name Sheba is uncertain. It appears to be a foreign name of uncertain derivation, and the Bible offers no wordplay or comment on it. Two similar-sounding names must be kept apart from this entry. Seba, a son of Cush (Genesis 10:7), appears next to Sheba in Psalm 72:10 ("the kings of Sheba and Seba") but is a separately indexed name. Sheba son of Bichri (2 Samuel 20) is written differently in Hebrew and is indexed separately as H7652. Neither belongs to the history traced here.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Raiders on the Horizon (Job)

In Job, Sheba first appears as a threat. A messenger reports that while the oxen were plowing, "the Sabeans raided them and took them away—indeed they have killed the servants with the edge of the sword" (Job 1:15). The Sabeans are the first of several disasters that strip Job of everything in a single day. Behind the scene, Scripture has already shown that the LORD set limits on what Satan could touch (Job 1:12). The raiders acted freely and wickedly, yet they were not outside God's rule. Later, Job compares his unreliable friends to dry streams that fail desert caravans, and he names "the travelers of Sheba" among those who hoped for water and were disappointed (Job 6:19, paraphrased). So the book knows Sheba as both raiders and traders.

The date of Job's setting is debated. Many readers place it in the patriarchal age, which would make this the earliest scene involving Sheba in biblical history, but the book does not state its date.

The Queen Who Came to Test Solomon (1 Kings 10; 2 Chronicles 9)

Sheba's great moment in Scripture comes during Solomon's reign. "Now when the queen of Sheba heard of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD, she came to test him with hard questions" (1 Kings 10:1). The report that reached her was about the LORD's name as well as Solomon's cleverness. She arrived "with a very great retinue, with camels that bore spices, very much gold, and precious stones," and she "spoke with him about all that was in her heart" (1 Kings 10:2). Solomon held nothing back: "there was nothing so difficult for the king that he could not explain it to her" (1 Kings 10:3).

What she saw overwhelmed her. Scripture lists Solomon's house, "the food on his table, the seating of his servants, the service of his waiters and their apparel, his cupbearers, and his entryway by which he went up to the house of the LORD." Then it adds, "there was no more spirit in her" (1 Kings 10:5). The list moves from the king's table to the king's way of worship, and she took in the whole as one ordered life under God. She confessed that the report she had heard was true and that she had not been told even half (1 Kings 10:6–7, paraphrased). She called Solomon's servants happy to stand before him and hear his wisdom (1 Kings 10:8). Then she spoke words that look beyond Solomon himself: "Blessed be the LORD your God, who delighted in you, setting you on the throne of Israel! Because the LORD has loved Israel forever, therefore He made you king, to do justice and righteousness" (1 Kings 10:9).

She gave the king a great gift of gold, spices, and precious stones (1 Kings 10:10). Solomon in turn gave her "all she desired" beyond what he gave from his royal bounty, and she returned to her own land (1 Kings 10:13, paraphrased). Chronicles retells the visit in nearly the same words (2 Chronicles 9:1–12), so the event is preserved twice in Israel's history. Scripture does not say she abandoned her gods or joined Israel. It records that she heard, saw, and blessed the LORD. We should neither shrink that confession nor stretch it beyond what the text says.

The Gold of Sheba in a Royal Psalm (Psalm 72)

Psalm 72, titled "of Solomon," prays for a king who will judge the poor with righteousness. Its vision grows larger than any one reign: "The kings of Tarshish and of the isles Will bring presents; The kings of Sheba and Seba Will offer gifts" (Psalm 72:10). Later the psalm prays that "the gold of Sheba" will be given to this king (Psalm 72:15, paraphrased). The psalm goes on to picture all nations serving him (72:11) and all nations calling him blessed (72:17). What the queen did once, the psalm expects kings to do as a matter of course for God's righteous King.

Frankincense That God Refused (Jeremiah 6)

Jeremiah turns Sheba's most famous export against a disobedient Judah. "For what purpose to Me Comes frankincense from Sheba, And sweet cane from a far country? Your burnt offerings are not acceptable, Nor your sacrifices sweet to Me" (Jeremiah 6:20). The people were importing the finest incense money could buy while refusing God's words and His law (6:19). God would not be impressed by the price of their offerings.

Merchants and Onlookers (Ezekiel 27, 38)

In Ezekiel's lament over Tyre, Sheba belongs to the vast trading network that made Tyre rich (Ezekiel 27:22–23). Later, when Gog marches against restored Israel, "Sheba, Dedan, the merchants of Tarshish, and all their young lions" ask, "Have you come to take plunder? … to carry away silver and gold, to take away livestock and goods, to take great plunder?" (Ezekiel 38:13). The traders read the invasion in commercial terms. Their question may be a protest or the interest of rivals in the spoils, but either way they stand at a distance while God acts to defend His people and make Himself known (Ezekiel 38:16, 23). The related word for the Sabeans, indexed separately as H7615, adds a darker trade role in Joel 3:8: in a judgment on those who sold Judah's people, the LORD says their children will be sold "to the Sabeans, To a people far off," so the Sabeans there are distant buyers of captives.

Sheba's Coming in Glory (Isaiah 60)

Isaiah gives Sheba its brightest promise. When the LORD's glory rises on Zion and the nations come to her light (Isaiah 60:1–3), "The multitude of camels shall cover your land, The dromedaries of Midian and Ephah; All those from Sheba shall come; They shall bring gold and incense, And they shall proclaim the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). The camels, gold, and spices recall the queen's caravan, but now a whole people comes, and they come to praise the LORD.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Several things are stated plainly in Scripture. Sheba was a real, distant, wealthy people. Its queen came because she heard of Solomon's fame "concerning the name of the LORD." She traced Solomon's throne to the LORD's love for Israel and to His purpose of justice and righteousness. The prophets expected Sheba's kings and people to bring gifts and praise to God's King and to Zion. And God rejected Sheba's incense when it was offered without obedience.

From these statements we can draw careful implications. The queen's visit shows what Israel's kingdom was meant to be at its best. When God's king ruled with wisdom and justice, the nations noticed, and the glory went back to the LORD. This fits God's promise that all the families of the earth would be blessed through Abraham's offspring (Genesis 12:3), and Psalm 72:17 echoes that promise when it speaks of all nations blessed in the king. The fact that Scripture links the name Sheba to Ham, Shem, and Abraham makes it a fitting picture of the many-branched Gentile world. That is an observation from the genealogies, not a claim Scripture makes outright.

Sheba's path across the canon also moves in a hopeful direction. The name first appears in the genealogies of Genesis, and its later portrayals range widely: raiders with swords and stolen oxen in Job, merchants and buyers of captives in Ezekiel and Joel, and camels laden with gifts and mouths full of praise in Isaiah 60. These portrayals do not form an established chronological transformation, and they may not all concern the same branch of Sheba. Still, the contrast between raiders and worshipers lets us see that God means to bring even distant and hostile peoples to praise Him.

Jeremiah keeps that hope from becoming shallow. Sheba's wealth cannot buy God's favor. In Isaiah 60, the gifts follow the praise and flow from it. In Jeremiah 6, the gifts are an attempt to replace obedience. The same frankincense can be either worship or an insult, depending on the heart that offers it.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jesus' explicit use of the queen. The clearest connection comes from Jesus Himself. "The queen of the South will rise up in the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and indeed a greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42; see Luke 11:31). The mention of Solomon makes plain that this queen is the queen of Sheba. Jesus pairs her with the repentant Ninevites (Matthew 12:41). Both were Gentiles who responded to far less light than His hearers had. She crossed the desert to hear a wise king. Jesus' generation had One greater than Solomon standing among them, and many would not cross the street. Her journey becomes evidence in God's court. Jesus also claims, without hesitation, to surpass Israel's wisest king.

The royal psalm and the greater King. Psalm 72 begins with Solomon, but its vision of endless dominion, worldwide service, and nations blessed in the king reaches beyond any son of David who has died. The New Testament does not quote this psalm directly. Even so, the wider witness of Scripture to a Davidic King whose reign has no end (2 Samuel 7:12–16; Luke 1:32–33) gives strong grounds for reading its fullest meaning in Christ. On this reading, the kings of Sheba bringing gifts look forward to the nations honoring Jesus.

The Magi. Matthew records that the wise men from the East "fell down and worshiped Him. And when they had opened their treasures, they presented gifts to Him: gold, frankincense, and myrrh" (Matthew 2:11). Matthew does not name Sheba or cite Isaiah 60. But the scene resonates clearly with Isaiah's promise of nations bringing "gold and incense" and with Psalm 72's kings bearing gifts. It is best read as a meaningful canonical echo, not a stated fulfillment. Gentiles arrive from afar with treasure, as Sheba once did, and they bow before the newborn Son of David.

The New Jerusalem. Revelation draws on Isaiah 60 when it describes the holy city: "the kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into it. Its gates shall not be shut at all by day (there shall be no night there). And they shall bring the glory and the honor of the nations into it" (Revelation 21:24–26). Isaiah had promised gates that stay open so that the wealth of the nations may enter (Isaiah 60:11). Sheba is not named in Revelation, but the chapter of Isaiah that promised "all those from Sheba shall come" finds its final horizon here. The queen's single visit to Solomon's Jerusalem becomes a picture of what the nations will one day do forever before God and the Lamb.

5. Spirit & Life

The queen of Sheba took God's reputation seriously. She heard a report about Solomon "concerning the name of the LORD," and she did not settle for rumor. She paid dearly in distance, time, and treasure to find out for herself. She brought "all that was in her heart" to the king, and he answered every question. Jesus held her up because her effort exposed the indifference of people who had far more than she did. Many of us have Bibles within reach and the gospel of a greater Solomon preached in plain words. The danger is not that Christ is too far away. The danger is that He is close and we do not bother to come.

What the queen received points to something greater. Solomon gave her all she desired and sent her home. Christ gives more than answers to hard questions. He "became for us wisdom from God—and righteousness and sanctification and redemption" (1 Corinthians 1:30, paraphrased). She left Jerusalem breathless at a king's glory. Those who come to Jesus are not sent away. They are welcomed into His kingdom.

Jeremiah 6:20 deserves to be taken to heart. Judah had the best incense on the market and hearts that refused to listen. God asked, in effect, "What is this to Me?" Expensive devotion, polished worship, and generous giving cannot stand in for a heart that hears and obeys Him. The remedy is not cheaper incense. It is a returning heart, and God gladly receives every gift that flows from one.

Sheba's story across the canon also gives hope about God's dealings with the nations. Job knew Sabeans as a band of raiders who killed his servants. Isaiah saw caravans from Sheba coming to Zion to proclaim the praises of the LORD. The two portrayals may not concern the same branch, but together they show the range of God's purpose. God's rule reaches people who start as enemies and turns their wealth, skill, and strength into offerings of praise. The church's mission to the ends of the earth rests on that promise. Those who were once far off are brought near in Christ (Ephesians 2:13).

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:7, 28; 25:3; 1 Chronicles 1:9, 22, 32 — The three genealogical lines of Sheba through Ham, Shem, and Abraham.

  • Job 1:15; 6:19 — Sabeans as violent raiders and as desert caravan travelers.

  • 1 Kings 10:1–13; 2 Chronicles 9:1–12 — The queen of Sheba's visit to Solomon. It shows her quest, her amazement, her blessing of the LORD, and her exchange of gifts.

  • Psalm 72:10, 15 — The kings and gold of Sheba brought to God's righteous King.

  • Isaiah 60:6 — The prophetic promise that all from Sheba will come with gold and incense to praise the LORD.

  • Jeremiah 6:20 — God rejects frankincense from Sheba offered without obedience.

  • Ezekiel 27:22–23 — Sheba's merchants in Tyre's trade in spices, stones, and gold.

  • Ezekiel 38:13 — Sheba among the trading nations watching Gog's invasion.

  • Joel 3:8 — The related name for the Sabeans (H7615, indexed separately), distant buyers of captives in a judgment oracle.

  • Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31 — Jesus names the queen of the South as a witness against unbelief and declares Himself greater than Solomon.

  • Matthew 2:11 — The Magi's gold and frankincense, an echo of Isaiah 60 and Psalm 72.

  • Revelation 21:24–26 — The glory and honor of the nations brought into the New Jerusalem, the final horizon of Isaiah 60.
