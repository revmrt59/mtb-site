SWEAR, OATH, CHARGE - (shâbaʻ, Strong's H7650)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shâbaʻ means to swear, or to bind oneself with an oath. It can also mean to put someone else under oath, which the NKJV often translates "charge." In Scripture, swearing has nothing to do with foul language. It is a solemn affirmation or commitment, the most serious guarantee a person can give that his word is true or that he will do what he has promised. The words can be very brief: when Abimelech asks him to swear by God, Abraham answers simply, "I will swear" (Genesis 21:23–24). Often an oath calls on God as witness, and some oaths spell out a curse. In Numbers 5:19–22 a wife suspected of unfaithfulness is put under an oath whose curse would fall on her only if she had actually done what was alleged. Abraham swears to Abimelech at Beersheba. Jonathan and David bind their friendship by oath. Israel is commanded to take oaths only in the LORD's name. The prophets condemn people who swear by Baal, or who swear by the LORD while lying.

The word reaches its greatest depth when God Himself is the One who swears. No one is greater than God, so He swears by Himself. His own life and holiness stand behind His word. He swore the land to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and Moses pleaded that oath when Israel deserved destruction. He swore a throne to David's descendant and an eternal priesthood to the King of Psalm 110. With equal certainty, He swore that an unbelieving generation would not enter His rest. Hebrews draws all this together for believers in Christ. Our hope rests on God's promise and His oath, "two immutable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie" (Hebrews 6:18).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâbaʻ belongs to every part of Israel's life. It appears in treaties between clans, in the charges dying fathers gave their sons, in courtroom procedure (Leviticus 6:3–5; Numbers 5:19–21), in arguments over royal succession (1 Kings 1:13–30), in public worship, and in prophetic warnings. The word is heavily concentrated in Deuteronomy. There Moses keeps returning to "the land which the LORD swore to your fathers."

In form, the verb closely resembles the Hebrew word for seven. Genesis 21 plays on that likeness. Abimelech asks Abraham to "swear to me by God" (Genesis 21:23), and Abraham sets apart seven ewe lambs, "that they may be my witness that I have dug this well" (Genesis 21:30). "Therefore he called that place Beersheba, because the two of them swore an oath there" (Genesis 21:31). The name can be heard as well of the oath or well of seven. Many scholars connect the two words historically, though the exact link is uncertain. Either way, the scene shows how seriously an oath was taken. Alongside the spoken oath, Abraham's claim to the well was confirmed with visible witnesses that anyone could count.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To swear or take an oath. A person solemnly binds himself to tell the truth or keep a promise, often by calling on God as witness. For example, "And Abraham said, 'I will swear'" (Genesis 21:24).

2. To put someone under oath; to charge or adjure. A person makes another solemnly bind himself. Abraham makes his servant swear by the LORD not to take a Canaanite wife for Isaac (Genesis 24:3). During the battle with the Philistines, Saul "strictly charged the people with an oath" not to eat (1 Samuel 14:28). The NKJV's "charge," "charged," and "made swear" usually reflect this second sense.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When people swear, the most important detail is whose name they swear by. To "take oaths in His name" (Deuteronomy 6:13; 10:20) means naming the LORD, and no other god, as the One who witnesses and stands behind your word. That is why Joshua warns Israel not to "cause anyone to swear by" the gods of Canaan (Joshua 23:7). Zephaniah condemns people who swear by the LORD and also by Milcom (Zephaniah 1:5).

When God swears, Scripture often tells us what He swears by. He swears "by Myself" (Isaiah 45:23) and "by My holiness" (Psalm 89:35). In Amos 6:8 and Jeremiah 51:14, the NKJV says He "has sworn by Himself"; the Hebrew says literally by His soul, meaning by His own life. Each phrase names what God swears by. Psalm 95:11 is different. There God says, "So I swore in My wrath." The phrase "in My wrath" does not name something God swears by. It describes the anger in which He swore that oath against the rebellious generation.

The "charge" sense allows a striking poetic use. In the Song of Songs, the bride puts the daughters of Jerusalem under oath "by the gazelles or by the does of the field" not to "stir up nor awaken love until it pleases" (Song 2:7; 3:5). In Song 8:4 she repeats the charge without naming the gazelles or the does. The animals she names are shy and easily startled, and they suit her point well: love cannot be forced or hurried.

4. Theological Significance

The oath God swore to the patriarchs is the foundation of Israel's existence. Moses tells Israel that the LORD did not choose them because they were many. He chose them "because the LORD loves you, and because He would keep the oath which He swore to your fathers" (Deuteronomy 7:8). Moses repeats the point in Deuteronomy 9:5: Israel was not taking the land because of its own righteousness. The same truth gave Moses his strongest plea after the golden calf. Israel had broken faith at the foot of Sinai, and Moses had nothing in Israel's favor to offer. So he reminded God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, to whom He had sworn by His own self (Exodus 32:13). Moses' plea rested on God's oath to the fathers, not on anything in Israel itself.

The oath is also where allegiance shows. The one a person swears by is the one he treats as the guardian of truth. That is why swearing by Baal amounted to treason against Israel's true King: it gave Baal the honor that belonged to the LORD alone. Jeremiah 12:16 turns this into a promise for the nations. If they learn "to swear by My name, 'As the LORD lives,' as they taught My people to swear by Baal, then they shall be established in the midst of My people." Even former enemies who learn to swear by the LORD can be welcomed among His people.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

God swears by Himself (Genesis 22:16; Hebrews 6:13–18). Abraham did not complete the sacrifice. When he showed that he would not withhold Isaac, the Angel of the LORD stopped his hand, and Abraham offered a ram in his son's place (Genesis 22:12–13). Then the LORD declared that He had sworn by Himself to bless Abraham and multiply his descendants (Genesis 22:16). Hebrews explains why God swore this way: "because He could swear by no one greater, He swore by Himself" (Hebrews 6:13). Among people, "an oath for confirmation is for them an end of all dispute" (v. 16). God's word was already enough. Yet He added an oath, "determining to show more abundantly to the heirs of promise the immutability of His counsel" (v. 17). He added the oath for our sake, so that by two unchangeable things, His promise and His oath, "we might have strong consolation" (v. 18). That oath was built on God Himself. He swore by Himself because there was no one greater, and the oath is sure because His purpose does not change and it is "impossible for God to lie" (v. 18). He swore by Himself in judgment as well: "The Lord GOD has sworn by Himself" against the pride of Jacob (Amos 6:8), and against Babylon (Jeremiah 51:14).

The oath that closed the door (Numbers 14:23; Psalm 95:11; Hebrews 3:11–16). When Israel refused to enter Canaan, God declared, "they certainly shall not see the land of which I swore to their fathers" (Numbers 14:23). Two different oaths meet in that sentence. The oath God swore to the fathers had promised the land, and that promise still stood: Caleb, Joshua and the children of the rebels would enter it (Numbers 14:24, 30–31). In His anger God now swore a separate oath against the men who had refused Him, that none of them would see that land (Numbers 32:10–11; Deuteronomy 1:34–35). The promise did not change, but that unbelieving generation was shut out of it. Psalm 95:11 gives God's own words about that second oath: "So I swore in My wrath, 'They shall not enter My rest.'" His wrath was the occasion of the oath, the anger in which He swore it. Hebrews repeats that verse to believers and warns them against "an evil heart of unbelief" (Hebrews 3:12). It also urges them to "exhort one another daily, while it is called 'Today'" (Hebrews 3:13). God's judgment is as certain as His blessing, and He backed it with an oath just as firm.

Keeping an oath that hurts (Joshua 9:15–20; Psalm 15:4). The Gibeonites tricked Joshua into a peace treaty, and Israel's leaders swore to them by the LORD God of Israel. When the trick came to light, the leaders refused to strike the Gibeonites. They had sworn by the LORD God of Israel, and they said that breaking that oath would bring wrath on them (Joshua 9:18–20). Generations later, Saul broke that oath, and the land suffered for it (2 Samuel 21:1–2). Psalm 15:4 describes the person who may dwell on God's holy hill as one who swears to his own hurt and does not change. The worshiper God welcomes keeps his word as God keeps His.

The curse that comes home (Zechariah 5:3–4). Zechariah sees a flying scroll, "the curse that goes out over the face of the whole earth." It is aimed at the thief and at "the one who swears falsely by My name." The curse "shall remain in the midst of his house and consume it, with its timber and stones." Swearing falsely by God's name placed a person under God's judgment, and some oaths spelled out a curse that would fall only if the charge named in the oath was true (Numbers 5:19–22). In Zechariah's vision, the curse on the false swearer arrives. The liar who used God's name as cover finds God's name turned against him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

God's oaths carry Israel's hope forward from the land to a Person. God swore to David that his descendant would hold the throne forever. Psalm 89 records God saying He had sworn by His holiness and would not lie to David (Psalm 89:3, 35). Psalm 132:11 adds that the LORD swore in truth to David and will not turn from that oath. On the day of Pentecost, Peter appealed to this oath as part of his case for the resurrection. David knew that God had sworn to seat one of his descendants on his throne, and he foresaw the Christ raised from the dead (Acts 2:30–31).

God also sealed the priesthood with an oath. In Psalm 110:4, the LORD swears to David's Lord and will not change His mind: He is a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. Hebrews 7:20–22 points out the difference this makes. Aaron's sons became priests without an oath, but Jesus became priest with one, and by it He "has become a surety of a better covenant." The two great offices of the Messiah, King and Priest, both rest on God's sworn word, but in different ways. The kingship comes through ancestry: God's oath to David concerns his descendant, so the King must be David's Son. The priesthood is secured by an oath apart from ancestry. Jesus came from the tribe of Judah, not from Aaron's family (Hebrews 7:14), yet God's oath made Him priest forever. Neither office depends on changing circumstances. Paul adds that "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). The Law's curse fell on everyone who failed to keep what it commanded (Galatians 3:10), and that failure includes the false swearing the Law forbade (Leviticus 19:12).

The patriarchal oaths may point the same way. Abraham's servant swore with his hand under Abraham's thigh (Genesis 24:2–3), and Joseph swore the same way to Jacob (Genesis 47:29–31). Scripture speaks of descendants as coming from a man's thigh (Exodus 1:5). These oaths concerned a bride for Isaac and a burial in the promised land, so the gesture may point to the promised offspring through whom God would keep His word. Genesis does not explain the gesture, but the connection fits the whole Abraham story.

The story ends with the oath God swore in Isaiah 45:23: "That to Me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall take an oath." Paul draws on this verse twice. In Romans 14:10–11, where he reminds believers that all will stand before the judgment seat of Christ, he quotes it as "every tongue shall confess to God." In Philippians 2:10–11 he echoes it: at the name of Jesus "every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father." Isaiah speaks of an oath and Paul of confession. Together they picture history ending with every tongue acknowledging God and confessing Jesus Christ as Lord.

7. Spirit & Life

A believer's security rests on God's oath to us, not on our vows to Him. Israel learned this at Sinai, where Moses could plead nothing in Israel's favor and appealed instead to what God had sworn (Exodus 32:13). Hebrews says we "have fled for refuge to lay hold of the hope set before us" (Hebrews 6:18). The picture is a person running for safety and grabbing hold of something that will not give way. On the days when our own faithfulness looks thin, God's word to Abraham has not changed. He has also given us more than His word: He has sworn, by Himself.

The same God sealed with an oath that unbelief would not enter His rest. His certainty is a mercy and a warning together. Hebrews does not turn that oath into a threat to drive believers to despair. It turns the oath into a call to encourage one another "while it is called 'Today.'"

God keeps His oaths, so His people learn to keep their word. Jesus carried this further than the Law did. He reminded His hearers of the command to "perform your oaths to the Lord," and then said, "do not swear at all," whether by heaven, by the earth, by Jerusalem or by one's own head. Instead, "let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No'" (Matthew 5:33–37). A disciple's plain word should be so reliable that it needs no oath to back it up. That is how Psalm 15 and Joshua 9 still speak to Christians. They do not teach believers to multiply vows. They show what truthful, reliable speech looks like. Psalm 15 describes a person who honors his word when keeping it costs him, and Joshua kept faith with the Gibeonites even though they had deceived him. Integrity like that reflects the character of the God we worship. Scripture also shows oaths of renewal. In Asa's day, Judah swore to the LORD "with a loud voice," and "all Judah rejoiced at the oath," for they had sought Him with all their heart and He was found by them (2 Chronicles 15:14–15). In that scene, commitment made in response to God's faithfulness brought joy, not bondage.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 50:24–25; Exodus 13:19. Joseph made Israel swear to carry his bones out of Egypt, trusting the land God had sworn to Abraham. Moses kept that oath generations later.

  • Joshua 21:43–45. The LORD gave Israel all the land He had sworn to their fathers, and not one of His good promises failed.

  • 1 Samuel 20:42. Jonathan and David parted after swearing friendship in the LORD's name, and the oath included their descendants after them.

  • Isaiah 54:9. God compares His oath not to be angry with Zion to His oath after the flood never again to cover the earth with water.

  • Micah 7:20. The book ends with God giving truth to Jacob and mercy to Abraham, which He had sworn to the fathers long ago.

  • Malachi 3:5. God promises to come near in judgment as a swift witness against perjurers, along with those who exploit wage earners, widows and orphans.
