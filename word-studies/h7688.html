GAZE, LOOK - (shâgach, Strong's H7688)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shâgach means to gaze, to look intently, or to peer. It describes an attentive look at something. How long the look lasts and what it leads to come from each passage, not from the verb alone. The verb appears only three times in the Old Testament, and each time a different person is looking. In Psalm 33:14 the LORD looks from His heavenly dwelling "On all the inhabitants of the earth." In Song of Solomon 2:9 the beloved stands outside the house; he "is looking through the windows," and the parallel line adds that he is "Gazing through the lattice" at the one he loves. In Isaiah 14:16 the nations stare at the fallen king of Babylon and can hardly believe what they see.

These three passages show three kinds of attention: God's sovereign attention to every human heart, a lover's eager attention to his beloved, and the stunned attention of people watching pride brought down. Together they teach that nothing is hidden from God's eyes. The God who searches every heart also seeks His people in love, and He exposes every proud power that sets itself against Him. For the believer, His gaze is more than an inspection to fear. He knows us fully and still calls us to come near.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâgach is a rare Hebrew verb. All three of its uses come in poetry or prophecy: a psalm of praise, a love song, and a prophet's taunt song over a fallen empire. Ordinary seeing in Hebrew is expressed by other, far more common words. Shâgach puts the act of looking itself in view. In two of the three passages, the person looks across a distance or through something: from heaven to earth, or through a window and a lattice. The lattice was a crisscrossed wooden screen over a window opening, and it let light and air in while partly blocking the view.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one basic sense:

1. To gaze, look intently, or peer at something; to direct one's eyes attentively toward something (Psalm 33:14; Song of Solomon 2:9; Isaiah 14:16).

The word itself does not say whether the look is kind or hostile. It tells us that someone is looking attentively. The setting and the one doing the looking show whether that look means watchful sovereignty, longing love, or astonished scorn.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The three uses change according to who is looking. When God is the subject (Psalm 33:14), the gaze is comprehensive. It covers "all the inhabitants of the earth," and the next verse carries it down into the human heart. When the beloved is the subject (Song of Solomon 2:9), the gaze is personal and full of desire, directed toward one person behind a wall. When the nations are the subject (Isaiah 14:16), the gaze becomes a disbelieving stare at a ruined ruler.

In two passages the NKJV places the gaze beside the word "consider." In Psalm 33:15 the LORD "considers all their works." In Isaiah 14:16 the onlookers "gaze at you, And consider you." In those two places the parallel word, not the verb itself, adds the idea of weighing what is seen. In Psalm 33 the LORD's look goes with His knowledge of every heart; in Isaiah 14 the onlookers' look goes with their astonished questions.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, these passages reveal a God who sees. His attention is not general or distracted. He looks at the whole earth and at each heart within it. That truth humbles human pride, because no king, army, or empire acts outside His view.

The same attention also carries tenderness. Scripture pictures God's people as the object of a gaze that longs and pursues as well as one that evaluates. The God who sees everything is also the God who wants to be with His people.

The third passage completes the picture. Pride that once demanded the world's terrified attention ends up as a spectacle. God's judgment brings the truth about such pride into the open, and people stare at what has been exposed. Across the three passages, being seen is either a comfort or a ruin. The outcome depends on whether a person is humbled before God or exalts himself against Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 33:13–18 — The Creator who looks into hearts. The psalmist builds his point in steps. "The LORD looks from heaven; He sees all the sons of men" (Psalm 33:13). Then comes shâgach: "From the place of His dwelling He looks On all the inhabitants of the earth" (Psalm 33:14). Verse 15 explains why His gaze goes so deep: "He fashions their hearts individually; He considers all their works." God sees the heart because He formed it. He does not look at humanity as an outsider studying strangers. He looks as the Maker who knows what He made.

The psalm then turns to kings and armies. Verses 16–17 say that no king is saved by a large army and that a warhorse cannot deliver anyone. Human strength looks impressive from the ground, but it is weak under heaven's gaze. Verse 18 then points that same divine attention in a gentler direction: the eye of the LORD rests on those who fear Him and hope in His mercy. The gaze that sees through human self-reliance becomes watchful care over those who trust Him.

Song of Solomon 2:8–10 — The lover at the lattice. The scene opens with sound: "The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes Leaping upon the mountains, Skipping upon the hills" (Song of Solomon 2:8). He hurries over hills like a gazelle and then stops at the house: "Behold, he stands behind our wall; He is looking through the windows, Gazing through the lattice" (Song of Solomon 2:9). The word studied here stands behind "looking" through the windows; "Gazing through the lattice" is the parallel line, using a different expression. Together the two lines picture the wall and the lattice standing between them while his eyes find her anyway. In this scene, with his hurrying approach and his call, his looking is eager and searching. He has come for her.

The gaze leads straight to an invitation. In verse 10 he speaks and calls his love to rise up and come away with him. Later in the chapter (Song of Solomon 2:14) he asks to see her face and hear her voice, calling her his dove hidden in the clefts of the rock. Looking, longing, and calling belong together in this scene. He looks at her because he wants her with him.

Isaiah 14:12–17 — The stare at fallen pride. Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon recalls his boast: "I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God... I will be like the Most High" (Isaiah 14:13–14). Then comes the reversal: "Yet you shall be brought down to Sheol, To the lowest depths of the Pit" (Isaiah 14:15). Sheol is the realm of the dead. Those who see him now "gaze at you, And consider you, saying: 'Is this the man who made the earth tremble, Who shook kingdoms, Who made the world as a wilderness And destroyed its cities, Who did not open the house of his prisoners?'" (Isaiah 14:16–17).

The man who once demanded that the world look up at him in fear is now looked at in disbelief. The setting turns their look into a puzzled stare, as people try to reconcile the terror he once caused with the weakness in front of them. Many Christian readers have also heard in this boast the pride of Satan behind earthly tyranny. That reading is an application beyond the passage's direct subject, the king of Babylon. Even so, the pattern is clear: whoever reaches for God's throne is brought low in full view of others.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The three gazes follow the larger shape of Scripture. Jesus displayed the heart-searching sight of Psalm 33. John records that He knew what was in man and needed no one to tell Him (John 2:25). His knowing was also loving. When the rich young ruler came to Him, Mark says that Jesus looked at him and loved him (Mark 10:21). After Peter's denial, "the Lord turned and looked at Peter. Then Peter remembered the word of the Lord," and he "went out and wept bitterly" (Luke 22:61–62). It is fair to see in that look the beginning of Peter's way back, but Luke does not say so; the restoration itself comes when the risen Jesus asks him three times, "Do you love Me?" and tells him to feed His sheep (John 21:15–17). These Gospel passages use different words in Greek, but they show the God of Psalm 33 present in a human face.

The Church has long read the Song of Solomon as a picture of Christ and His people. The New Testament supports the image by calling Christ the Bridegroom and the Church His bride (Ephesians 5:25–32; Revelation 19:7). The connection is a resonance, not a direct prophecy. Still, the lover who comes over the hills, looks through the lattice, and calls "come away" fits the Savior who came looking for the lost (Luke 19:10).

Isaiah's stare at fallen Babylon points ahead as well. Jesus spoke of seeing Satan fall like lightning from heaven (Luke 10:18). Revelation pictures kings and merchants standing at a distance, watching the smoke of the great city Babylon as she burns (Revelation 18:9–10). The story ends with every proud power exposed and the Lamb on the throne.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 33 removes the illusion that anyone lives unseen. The God who formed each heart looks into each heart. That truth can frighten those who trust in their own strength. To those who fear Him, the same psalm says His eye rests on them to keep them. Being fully known is a mercy, because the One who sees everything is the One who formed us and keeps us.

The Song adds what the psalm alone might not make clear: God's attention is affectionate. The beloved at the lattice does not peer in to find fault. He looks because he longs for the one inside, and he speaks to call her out. Many believers hide behind their own walls of shame, busyness, or fear, as the bride in verse 14 hides in the clefts of the rock. His gaze is not a reason to stay hidden. It is the reason to come out, because the One looking in wants to see your face and hear your voice.

Isaiah 14 is a sober warning against self-exaltation. Every boast of "I will ascend" ends with someone else's astonished stare. Humility before God is the safest place to stand. The proud end up as a spectacle, while the humble live under the eye of the LORD.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Shâgach occurs only three times, and all three (Psalm 33:14; Song of Solomon 2:9; Isaiah 14:16) are developed in Section 5. For related biblical teaching on God's watchful eye, Psalm 33:18 in the same psalm uses a different expression but carries the thought of verse 14 toward God's care for those who fear Him.
