BREAST, BOSOM - (shad, Strong's H7699)

Summary

The Hebrew word shad means breast, most often a woman's breast as the place where a child is nursed. Scripture uses the word in a small number of passages, but it reaches across a wide range of human experience. It appears in a newborn's first trust, a young woman's coming of age, the delight of married love, the cruelty of famine and siege, and the warmth of a mother's comfort. The Song of Solomon accounts for a third of its uses, where it celebrates the body and marital love as good gifts of God.

The prophets take this ordinary word and give it unusual force. Ezekiel and Hosea use it to expose Israel's idolatry as intimate unfaithfulness to God. Hosea and Lamentations describe judgment as dry breasts and mothers who no longer nurse. Isaiah, however, ends with God's people nursing at the "bosom" of a restored Jerusalem, and the LORD Himself promises to comfort them as a mother comforts her child. In Psalm 22, whose opening cry Jesus quoted at the cross, the sufferer traces his trust in God back to his mother's breasts. The word shows that God's care reaches the most helpless stage of life and that His final purpose for His people is deep, satisfying comfort.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shad is a plain Hebrew body word. It usually appears in the plural "breasts," and the NKJV renders it "breasts," "breast," once "bosom" (Isaiah 66:11), and once "nursing" (Joel 2:16, literally those who suck the breasts). Its settings fall into two groups. In poetry and wisdom writing (Genesis 49, Job, Psalm 22, and the Song of Solomon), it belongs to birth, nursing, beauty, and love. In the prophets (Isaiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, Hosea, and Joel), it becomes an image for Israel's faithfulness, her betrayal of God, her judgment, and her restoration.

In the ancient world, a mother's milk was a child's only reliable food. A woman who could not nurse could lose her baby. That reality helps explain why the image carries so much weight in Scripture.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The nursing breast, the source of an infant's food and closeness to its mother (Job 3:12; Psalm 22:9). Lamentations 4:3 applies the word to animals nursing their young.

2. The breast as part of a woman's mature body, marking her growth into womanhood and serving as an object of a husband's love (Ezekiel 16:7; Song of Solomon 8:8; 1:13).

3. A figurative source of nourishment and comfort, applied to a city or to nations (Isaiah 66:11; 60:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines the tone of the word. In the Song of Solomon, the word appears in love poetry spoken between bride and groom and carries no hint of shame. In Ezekiel 23, the same word describes the shameful conduct of two sisters who represent Samaria and Jerusalem. The image itself stays the same while the moral setting changes completely.

The prophets also use the word with gestures. Isaiah 32:12 says, "People shall mourn upon their breasts For the pleasant fields, for the fruitful vine." The picture is the mourner's gesture of striking the chest. Women strike the very part of the body that nourishes life because the fields and vines that nourish the land are gone. In Lamentations 4:3 the contrast is with animals: "Even the jackals present their breasts To nurse their young; But the daughter of my people is cruel." Siege and famine had pushed Jerusalem below the instincts of wild animals.

4. Theological Significance

God is the giver of life's first provision. In Jacob's blessing, Joseph receives blessings "By the Almighty who will bless you... Blessings of the breasts and of the womb" (Genesis 49:25). A mother's ability to carry and nurse a child is counted among the blessings of heaven and the deep. Readers have long noticed that "Almighty" (Shaddai) and "breasts" (shad) sound alike in this verse. Scholars dispute whether the two words are actually related, so the link should not be built into the meaning of God's name. Still, the verse plainly places the Almighty beside the nursing mother as the One who sustains life.

Judgment reverses that blessing. Hosea prays over rebellious Ephraim, "Give them a miscarrying womb And dry breasts!" (Hosea 9:14). The blessing Jacob spoke over Joseph, whose son Ephraim gave the tribe its name, is withdrawn from Joseph's own descendants. Lamentations shows where that withdrawal finally leads, to a city where mothers fail their children. When God's blessing departs, the most basic tenderness of human life can collapse with it.

Idolatry, in this imagery, wounds God personally. The prophets could have described Israel's sin as broken law alone. Instead they chose the most intimate language available, and the use of shad in these passages makes the betrayal physical and personal (Section 5).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 22:9–10: trust learned at the breast. The psalm opens with the cry Jesus spoke from the cross (Matthew 27:46). In the middle of that sense of abandonment, the sufferer remembers how his life began: "But You are He who took Me out of the womb; You made Me trust while on My mother's breasts." Verse 10 adds, "From My mother's womb You have been My God." He does not appeal to his own record of faithfulness. He appeals to a trust God Himself produced in him before he could speak. A nursing infant does nothing except receive, and the psalmist says God was already teaching him to rest in Him then. Matthew records Jesus crying out the psalm's first line, not these verses. Still, the whole psalm is a rich setting for hearing that cry, and Christians rightly hear it against this memory of a trust in God that began before the sufferer could speak.

Ezekiel 16:7 and Song of Solomon 8:8–10: readiness for love. Ezekiel pictures Jerusalem as an abandoned baby whom the LORD rescued and raised: "Your breasts were formed, your hair grew, but you were naked and bare." In the next verse God spreads His garment over her and enters a covenant of marriage with her. Here the word marks the moment she becomes ready for that relationship. The Song uses the same marker. Her brothers say, "We have a little sister, And she has no breasts. What shall we do for our sister In the day when she is spoken for?" Later the sister replies that she is a wall and her breasts are like towers, and that she has found peace in her beloved's eyes. She has grown up, and she has kept herself for him. Together these passages honor the body's maturing as part of God's design and connect it with faithfulness.

Ezekiel 23 and Hosea 2:2: unfaithfulness at the heart. Ezekiel describes the sisters Oholah and Oholibah giving themselves away in Egypt in their youth (Ezekiel 23:3), and Jerusalem later longing for that old lewdness (Ezekiel 23:21). For Israel, this meant trusting Egypt and Assyria for security instead of the LORD. The judgment is that she will drink the cup of desolation and tear at her own breasts (Ezekiel 23:34). The body that was given away becomes the body that suffers. Hosea speaks in the voice of a wronged husband: "Let her put away her harlotries from her sight, And her adulteries from between her breasts" (Hosea 2:2). The sin has settled into the most intimate place. These passages show that God experiences idolatry as a husband experiences betrayal.

Isaiah 28:9: refusing to grow up. The words that follow are most plausibly the mocking reply of Ephraim's scoffers to Isaiah's message, though the verse does not name its speaker: "Whom will he teach knowledge?... Those just weaned from milk? Those just drawn from the breasts?" To them, Isaiah's plain warnings sounded like teaching for toddlers. God's answer is that because they would not listen to simple words, He would speak to them through foreign invaders with a strange language (Isaiah 28:11). People who scorn the simple word of God are not ready for anything deeper.

Isaiah 60:16 and 66:11: nursed at Zion's breast. Isaiah's final chapters reverse the picture of dry breasts. Restored Zion will "drink the milk of the Gentiles, And milk the breast of kings." The nations that once crushed Israel will end up feeding her, and the result is knowledge of God: "You shall know that I, the LORD, am your Savior." In chapter 66, Jerusalem becomes the nursing mother, so that her children "may feed and be satisfied With the consolation of her bosom." Then the LORD speaks directly: "As one whom his mother comforts, So I will comfort you; And you shall be comforted in Jerusalem" (Isaiah 66:13; see 66:12). Two pictures stand side by side in the passage. Jerusalem is pictured nourishing her children, and the LORD promises in His own voice to comfort them.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word follows the outline of the whole biblical story. Genesis and the Song present the nursing mother and married love as good gifts. Ezekiel and Hosea show Israel giving that intimacy to false gods. Hosea and Lamentations describe judgment as breasts that no longer nourish. Isaiah promises a mother-city where God's people will be fed and satisfied.

Jesus stands at the center of this movement. He entered the full helplessness of infancy, and from the cross He cried out the opening words of Psalm 22, the psalm that also remembers trust learned at the breast. On the road to Golgotha He told the weeping women that days were coming when people would call the barren blessed, along with breasts that never nursed (Luke 23:29). His words recall the reversal Hosea had announced (Hosea 9:14), though Luke does not say He was drawing on Hosea. He then bore the judgment He foretold, so that Isaiah's promise could stand. Paul calls the Jerusalem above "the mother of us all" (Galatians 4:26), and Revelation closes with the holy city where God wipes away every tear. That city is where Isaiah's nursing comfort reaches its fulfillment.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 22 teaches that faith begins with God. The psalmist's trust started before he could understand it, and he returns to that trust when he feels most abandoned. A believer in a dark season can do the same, remembering the God who "took Me out of the womb" and has carried him since.

Isaiah 28 and the New Testament speak about growth together. Paul told the Corinthians, "I fed you with milk and not with solid food" (1 Corinthians 3:2), and Hebrews says that "everyone who partakes only of milk is unskilled in the word of righteousness, for he is a babe" (Hebrews 5:13). The writer of Hebrews describes maturity as senses "exercised to discern both good and evil" (Hebrews 5:14). Growing up in Christ does not mean despising simple truth, as Ephraim did. It means receiving God's word and putting it to use until it becomes strength.

Isaiah 66 also keeps maturity from becoming self-sufficiency. The same God who calls His people to solid food promises that they will "drink deeply and be delighted" in restored Jerusalem and that He Himself will comfort them (Isaiah 66:11, 13). Mature believers still need to be comforted by God, and He is glad to comfort them.

The Song and Ezekiel together give the body its rightful honor. Physical beauty and married love are God's gifts. Their misuse is a serious sin because the gift is so good.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 3:12 — "Why did the knees receive me? Or why the breasts, that I should nurse?" Job's anguish makes him wish he had never been fed and kept alive as a baby.

  • Job 24:9 — "Some snatch the fatherless from the breast." Job names this as one of the worst cruelties of the wicked: taking the most defenseless child from its only source of food.

  • Joel 2:16 — The call to repent includes "the children and nursing babes." No one in the community is too small to be included when God's people return to Him.

  • Song of Solomon 1:13 — "A bundle of myrrh is my beloved to me, That lies all night between my breasts." The bride holds her beloved close, like a sachet of fragrant myrrh.

  • Song of Solomon 4:5 (compare 7:3) — "Your two breasts are like two fawns, Twins of a gazelle, Which feed among the lilies." The comparison suggests gentleness and peace as well as beauty.
