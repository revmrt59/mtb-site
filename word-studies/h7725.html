RETURN, TURN BACK, RESTORE - (shûwb, Strong's H7725)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shûwb means to turn back or return. In daily life it describes a traveler heading home, a dove flying back to the ark, or a lender handing back a borrowed cloak. It can also mean to bring something back: to restore stolen property, recover captives, or give back an answer. From these ordinary uses the Old Testament draws one of its central pictures of life with God. Sin is a turning away from Him, and repentance is turning around and coming home.

What makes the word so rich is that God uses it of Himself. He calls, "Return to Me, and I will return to you" (Malachi 3:7). He turns from His fierce anger when His people turn from their sin, and He restores what sin has ruined: captives, fortunes, and even the soul (Psalm 23:3). The prophets also see that people cannot sustain this return on their own, so God promises to give them a heart that will return to Him. That promise leads to Jesus Christ, who calls people to turn and be healed and who brings home the scattered people of God.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shûwb is a common, everyday Hebrew verb, and it appears in nearly every book of the Old Testament. It runs through family stories in Genesis, property laws in Leviticus, battle reports in Kings, proverbs about speech, and laments over exile. Its heaviest use comes in the prophets, especially Jeremiah, where Israel's history of turning away from God meets His repeated call to come back.

Because the verb is so ordinary, its spiritual uses never lose their physical feel. When a prophet says "return," his hearers would picture someone stopping on a road, turning around, and walking back the way they came.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core idea is a change of direction that leads back toward a starting point, an owner, or a former condition. Context decides which of several senses is meant:

1. To turn back or return physically. The word describes going back to a place. Noah's dove "returned into the ark to him" (Genesis 8:9).

2. To bring back, restore, or repay. Here someone causes something to return. A man must return a neighbor's pledged garment "before the sun goes down" (Exodus 22:26), and a thief "shall restore what he has stolen" (Leviticus 6:4). The same sense covers God restoring Job's losses (Job 42:10).

3. To turn toward or away from God. The word is used for both apostasy and repentance, depending on direction: "O Israel, return to the LORD your God" (Hosea 14:1), or "if you or your sons at all turn from following Me" (1 Kings 9:6).

4. To return a word, that is, to answer. "He who answers a matter before he hears it" (Proverbs 18:13) uses the verb in this sense.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The same verb can describe movement in opposite directions. Context tells whether someone is turning away from God or turning back to Him. Ezekiel describes a righteous man who "turns away" from righteousness, and in the same chapter he describes a wicked man who turns from wickedness and lives (Ezekiel 18:24, 27). Jeremiah sees the pattern in Jerusalem. He asks whether someone who turns away does not turn back again, and then grieves that the city refuses to return (Jeremiah 8:4–5, paraphrased).

The subject of the verb also changes its weight. When people return, the word speaks of repentance. When God is the one who returns, it speaks of renewed favor, His presence restored, or His anger turned aside.

Two grammatical patterns matter. The first is the command "Return!", which God speaks again and again through the prophets. This form shows that repentance answers His initiative. The second is a reciprocal pairing in which the verb appears twice: "Return to Me, and I will return to you" (Zechariah 1:3; Malachi 3:7). With the same word on both sides, people and God move toward each other.

4. Theological Significance

The way Scripture uses shûwb treats sin as more than breaking a rule. Sin turns a person's back on God to face something else. Solomon was warned against turning "from following Me" and going to "serve other gods" (1 Kings 9:6). Because sin is a change of direction, the remedy must be one too. Regret that keeps walking the same road is not a return.

The word also shows how God's anger works. Moses pleaded with the Lord to turn from His fierce wrath after the golden calf (Exodus 32:12). Nineveh's king hoped God would turn from His anger (Jonah 3:9), and in Hosea God promises that His anger has turned away from His repentant people (Hosea 14:4). God's wrath is a righteous response to sin, and it is answered by repentance and by a mediator who stands in the gap. Even before His people return, God says through Jeremiah, "I will not remain angry forever" (Jeremiah 3:12).

When God brings back, He undoes loss. "When the LORD brought back the captivity of Zion, We were like those who dream" (Psalm 126:1). Through Jeremiah He promises, "I will bring back from captivity My people Israel and Judah" (Jeremiah 30:3). Exile, poverty, and grief are not the final word over those He restores.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Zechariah 1:3; Malachi 3:7; Joel 2:12–14 — returning to a Person. In these passages repentance means coming back to Someone. God does not say "return to My standards" so much as "Return to Me," and He promises to return in turn. Joel shows that this turning must reach the heart: "So rend your heart, and not your garments; Return to the LORD your God, For He is gracious and merciful." The ground of the call is God's character. Hosea adds a tender detail when he tells Israel, "Take words with you, And return to the LORD" (Hosea 14:2), and then supplies the very words: "Take away all iniquity; Receive us graciously." Jeremiah gives the reason God keeps calling: "Return, O backsliding children... for I am married to you" (Jeremiah 3:14). Israel's question in Malachi, "In what way shall we return?", shows how far people can drift without noticing. God answers that question by naming specific sins, and He does not withdraw the invitation.

Deuteronomy 30:1–10 and Jeremiah 24:7 — God gives the heart to return. Moses foresees Israel scattered under judgment and promises that when they return to the Lord, He will bring them back from captivity (Deuteronomy 30:2–3). In the same passage the Lord promises to circumcise their hearts so that they will love Him (Deuteronomy 30:6, supporting context). Israel's later history proves that a people commanded to return could not keep doing so in their own strength. Jeremiah makes God's initiative explicit: He will give His people a heart to know Him, and they will return to Him with their whole heart (Jeremiah 24:7, paraphrased). The command and the gift are joined. The God who says "Return" also supplies the heart that obeys.

Isaiah 55:10–11 — the word that does not come back empty. Isaiah uses shûwb in two directions within two verses. Rain and snow do not return to the sky without watering the earth and making it bear fruit. In the same way God's word goes out from His mouth and will not return to Him empty; it accomplishes what He pleases (paraphrased). Human promises often come back unfulfilled. God's word works like a messenger who never reports back until the errand is done. This passage stands right after the call to "return to the LORD" (Isaiah 55:7), so the reliability of His word assures the returning sinner that His promise of mercy will hold.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of shûwb moves from Eden to exile and toward a homecoming. Adam hears that he will return to dust (Genesis 3:19), Israel repeatedly turns away, and the prophets promise a return that God Himself will bring about.

The New Testament takes up these very verses. Isaiah 6:10 speaks of a people who will not turn and be healed. Jesus quotes it to explain the hardness that met His teaching (Matthew 13:15), so the long story of refusing to return reaches its crisis in His ministry. Malachi's final promise that God would turn the hearts of fathers to their children (Malachi 4:6) is applied to John the Baptist, who prepared the way for Christ (Luke 1:17). Isaiah's Servant is sent to bring Jacob back and restore the preserved of Israel, and also to be a light to the nations (Isaiah 49:5–6). Paul and Barnabas take that light-to-the-nations commission as their own in Acts 13:47. Paul also reads Isaiah 59:20 as a promise that the Deliverer will turn ungodliness away from Jacob (Romans 11:26).

Moses pleading for God to turn from His wrath foreshadows a greater Mediator. Christ bears the judgment so that God's anger is turned away from those who belong to Him. The new heart promised in Deuteronomy and Jeremiah is given through the new covenant in His blood. The New Testament's call to "repent," in Greek, carries forward the prophets' call to turn around and come home to God.

7. Spirit & Life

"He restores my soul" (Psalm 23:3) uses the same verb as the prophets' call to return. The Shepherd brings back a wandering, weary soul, much as a shepherd carries a strayed sheep back to the fold. Someone who feels too far gone to turn around can take heart that the One calling them home is also the One who brings them home.

God's invitation does not wait for His people to have perfect words. Hosea tells them to bring words, and then he gives them the words to bring. Malachi's people ask, half-defensively, "In what way shall we return?", and God takes the question seriously enough to answer it. Honest confusion about how to come back is not a barrier to Him.

Restoration also flows outward. Job's losses were restored "when he prayed for his friends" (Job 42:10), the same friends who had wounded him. Isaiah calls those who care for the hungry and afflicted the restorer of streets to dwell in (Isaiah 58:12). People God has brought back become people through whom He brings others back.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 3:19 — Humanity will return to the dust it came from. This is the first and darkest return in Scripture, the one every later restoration must answer.

  • Ruth 1 and 4:15 — Naomi returns to Bethlehem empty, and at the end the women call her grandson a restorer of life. Her homecoming becomes the setting for God's kindness.

  • Job 33:25 — "He shall return to the days of his youth." Elihu pictures God's restoring mercy reaching even a failing body.

  • Proverbs 15:1 — A gentle answer turns away wrath. Ordinary speech can reverse a quarrel's direction.

  • Psalm 51:12 — David asks God to restore the joy of His salvation, a prayer for what sin had taken.

  • Lamentations 5:21 — Jerusalem's ruined survivors ask God to turn them back to Himself so they may be restored. They see that even their return must come from Him.
