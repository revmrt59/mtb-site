SHUAH - (Shûwach, Strong's H7744)

Summary

Shuah was a son of Abraham by Keturah, Abraham's wife, also called a concubine. Genesis introduces her after Sarah's death; the traditional reading places the marriage then, though the text does not settle its timing. He is named last among her six sons in Genesis 25:2 and again in 1 Chronicles 1:32. His name is probably related to a Hebrew verb meaning "to sink down" or "to bow down," which suggests a sense such as "depression" in the sense of a low place, or "lowliness." That derivation is likely but not certain, and Scripture makes no wordplay on it. Shuah never speaks in the Bible, and no deed of his is recorded. His one appearance in a story is a parting: Abraham gave gifts to the sons of his concubines and "sent them eastward, away from Isaac his son, to the country of the east" (Genesis 25:6).

That parting shows something important about God's ways. Shuah was truly Abraham's son, and his birth belongs to God's promise that Abraham would father many nations. But the line of promise ran through Isaac alone. Later, the book of Job introduces "Bildad the Shuhite" (Job 2:11). Most interpreters take "Shuhite" to point back to Shuah's descendants in the East, though the Bible never states the link outright. If the connection holds, the family Abraham sent away reappears generations later still speaking about God, defending His justice with ancestral tradition. Bildad's errors do not show that all of Shuah's descendants lacked revelation because they were sent away. In the end God corrected Bildad and his friends, and He also provided for them: Job prayed for them, they obeyed, and the LORD accepted Job (Job 42:7–9).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Shuah (H7744) is one person: the son of Abraham and Keturah. Genesis 25:1 calls Keturah Abraham's "wife." First Chronicles 1:32 calls her "Abraham's concubine," and Genesis 25:6 groups her sons among "the sons of the concubines." These descriptions fit together. She was a lawful partner of secondary rank, and her children did not stand on equal footing with Isaac. Shuah's brothers were Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, and Ishbak. Because the lists place him last, he is often called the youngest, though the text names the sons without stating their birth order.

He must not be confused with two others spelled similarly in English. Shua (H7770) was the Canaanite father of Judah's wife (Genesis 38:2, 12; 1 Chronicles 2:3). Shua (H7774), a separate name, was a daughter of Heber in the tribe of Asher (1 Chronicles 7:32). The English spellings are close, but the Hebrew names and the people are different.

The name Shûwach is generally connected with the root shuach (H7743), which carries the idea of sinking down, bowing low, or being humbled. A related noun can refer to a pit or ditch. So the name likely means something like "depression" or "sinking," but this rests on derivation rather than on any explanation in Scripture. The Bible draws no lesson from it, and Shuah's life should not be read through it.

A related, separately indexed term is the gentilic "Shuhite" (H7747), used of Bildad in Job 2:11; 8:1; 18:1; 25:1; and 42:9. Those verses are not occurrences of H7744, but they are the main reason Shuah's name carries weight beyond a genealogy. Many scholars also link Shuah with Suhu, a region on the middle Euphrates known from ancient Near Eastern inscriptions. That is a reasonable scholarly proposal, not a biblical statement.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Genesis 25:1–4 records Abraham's later family through Keturah, including grandsons such as Sheba and Dedan and the sons of Midian. Then comes the decisive sentence: "And Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac" (Genesis 25:5). Abraham did not leave his other sons empty-handed. He gave them gifts, and while he was still alive he sent them east, "away from Isaac his son, to the country of the east" (Genesis 25:6). Settling the matter in his lifetime protected Isaac's inheritance from later dispute.

Centuries later, the Chronicler repeats the same six names (1 Chronicles 1:32) in his record of Abraham's descendants. Even sons who stepped off the main stage of the story kept their place in Israel's memory.

The book of Job, set among the peoples of the East, then introduces Bildad the Shuhite as one of three friends who "made an appointment together to come and mourn with him, and to comfort him" (Job 2:11). Bildad's speeches (Job 8, 18, 25) defend God's justice and appeal to the wisdom of earlier generations: "inquire, please, of the former age, And consider the things discovered by their fathers" (Job 8:8).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Abraham gave everything to Isaac and sent Keturah's sons away. Elsewhere it states that God promised to make Abraham a father of many nations (Genesis 17:4–5) and that Abraham's chosen seed would be named through Isaac (Genesis 21:12). Shuah sits where these two promises meet. His birth and his descendants show God multiplying Abraham beyond Israel. His sending away shows that the chosen messianic inheritance did not pass down simply by natural birth. It is not a verdict on Shuah's personal salvation or access to God. Paul later builds on the Isaac pattern, though he does not name Shuah, teaching that "the children of the promise" are counted as the seed, not merely the children of the flesh (Romans 9:7–8, paraphrased).

Abraham's gifts also matter. The sons who did not inherit the promise were still provided for. Beyond that, we cannot say what Abraham passed on to them about the Lord. It is a fair inference, though not a stated fact, that some knowledge of the one true God went east with them.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never mentions Shuah, and he is not a type of Christ. The clearest canonical development comes by inference from the line Paul does discuss. Paul does not name Shuah in Romans 9 or Galatians 3, but his teaching on Isaac's line suggests that Shuah stood outside the chosen line of promise that leads to Christ, the true Seed of Abraham (Galatians 3:16, paraphrased). Shuah's story is one of the early places where God makes that distinction visible.

There is also a thematic resonance, not a stated fulfillment. Abraham sent his other sons to "the country of the east," and Matthew records wise men "from the East" coming to worship the newborn King of the Jews (Matthew 2:1–2, paraphrased). Scripture does not connect the Magi to Keturah's family, but the picture fits God's larger purpose: peoples once sent away from the promise are drawn back to it in Christ.

5. Spirit & Life

Genesis 25:5–6 shows that loving every child did not require Abraham to give every child the same calling. He gave gifts generously, yet he honored God's choice of Isaac and settled it while he still could. Faithfulness to God's word sometimes means drawing lines that natural affection would rather blur.

Bildad shows the limits of inherited tradition. He believed God was just and could ask, "Does God subvert judgment? Or does the Almighty pervert justice?" (Job 8:3). But he applied that truth harshly to Job's grieving heart: "If your sons have sinned against Him, He has cast them away for their transgression" (Job 8:4). Sound-sounding tradition was not enough. God Himself rebuked the friends for not speaking what was right about Him, and then provided a way for them through Job's prayer (Job 42:7–9). Inherited religion, even with true elements, still needs the living word of God.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 25:1–2 introduces Keturah and names Shuah among her sons.

  • Genesis 25:5–6 records Isaac's sole inheritance and the sending east of Keturah's sons with gifts.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:32 repeats Shuah's name in Israel's genealogical record and identifies Keturah as a concubine.

  • Genesis 17:4–5; 21:12 set out the promise of many nations alongside the choice of Isaac.

  • Romans 9:7–8 gives Paul's interpretation of the Isaac pattern: the children of promise are the counted seed.

  • Job 2:11; 8:1–10; 42:7–9 introduce Bildad the Shuhite, record his appeal to ancestral tradition, and show God's correction of the friends.
