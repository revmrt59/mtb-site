ARPHAXAD - (ʼArpakshad, Strong's H0775)

Summary

Arphaxad (also spelled Arpachshad) was a son of Shem and a grandson of Noah. He was born "two years after the flood" (Genesis 11:10), so he belongs to the first generation of the world that came out of the ark. The meaning of his name is uncertain. It may not be Hebrew in origin. Its last letters resemble Kesed, the root behind the Hebrew name for the Chaldeans, so some scholars have suggested a link to Chaldea or to a region near Assyria. That proposal remains unproven. Scripture never explains the name, and nothing in his story depends on it.

Arphaxad appears only in genealogies: twice in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:22, 24), four times in the family record of Shem (Genesis 11:10–13), three times in 1 Chronicles 1, and once in Luke's genealogy of Jesus (Luke 3:36). Genesis records no words or deeds of his. It gives only his birth, his son Salah (Shelah), and his 438 years. Even so, the line that leads from Noah to Abram passes through him, and Luke names him among the ancestors of Jesus. He is a reminder that God carried His promise forward through ordinary generations whose names we would otherwise never know.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Arphaxad is a single individual, the son of Shem listed between Asshur and Lud: "The sons of Shem were Elam, Asshur, Arphaxad, Lud, and Aram" (Genesis 10:22). He was the father of Salah (Shelah) and an ancestor of Eber, Terah, and Abram. The Hebrew form is אַרְפַּכְשַׁד (ʼArpakshad). In Luke 3:36 the same man appears in Greek as Arphaxad, which is a separately indexed Strong's entry (G0742). The Arphaxad called a king of the Medes in the apocryphal book of Judith is a different figure and has no connection to the Genesis patriarch.

No one knows for certain what the name means. It is unusual for a Hebrew name and may have been borrowed from another language. One common proposal notes that the final three consonants (k-sh-d) match Kesed, the root of Kasdim, "Chaldeans." On that basis some scholars have suggested a meaning such as "boundary of Chaldea." Others connect the name to Arrapachitis, an ancient district east of the Tigris near Assyria. Both ideas are scholarly conjectures. The Bible itself offers no meaning and plays on the name nowhere.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Genesis tells Arphaxad's story twice, and each telling has its own purpose. In Genesis 10 he stands among the peoples who descend from Noah's sons. His brothers' names became the names of known peoples: Elam, Asshur (Assyria), and Aram. This setting places Arphaxad's descendants in the Mesopotamian world, and Genesis 10:24 traces his line through Salah to Eber. Shem is already called "the father of all the children of Eber" (Genesis 10:21), so Arphaxad's branch is the one that leads to the Hebrews.

Genesis 11 then returns to Shem's family and follows a single line from Shem to Abram: "This is the genealogy of Shem: Shem was one hundred years old, and begot Arphaxad two years after the flood" (Genesis 11:10). The record continues with the steady rhythm of these genealogies: "Arphaxad lived thirty-five years, and begot Salah. After he begot Salah, Arphaxad lived four hundred and three years, and begot sons and daughters" (Genesis 11:12–13).

Those numbers mark a turning point in human life spans. Noah lived 950 years (Genesis 9:29) and Shem 600 (Genesis 11:10–11), but Arphaxad lived 438. His generation began the steep decline in longevity that continues through the rest of Genesis 11. If the Masoretic figures are read as an unbroken sequence, Arphaxad would have outlived several of his own descendants and would still have been alive during Abram's early life. That reading assumes the genealogy has no gaps, and biblical genealogies sometimes skip generations. Even with that caution, Arphaxad clearly links people who remembered the world before the flood to the age of the patriarchs.

The Chronicler repeats the line centuries later (1 Chronicles 1:17, 18, 24), beginning Israel's history with Adam and tracing it through Shem, Arphaxad, and Shelah to Abram. For a people rebuilding after exile, this list showed that their story ran back without a break to the beginning.

One textual difference deserves mention. The Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) places a man named Cainan between Arphaxad and Salah, and Luke follows that tradition: "the son of Cainan, the son of Arphaxad, the son of Shem, the son of Noah, the son of Lamech" (Luke 3:36). Interpreters explain this in different ways. Some see an early addition to the Greek text. Others see a generation that the Hebrew genealogy did not record, since biblical genealogies often compress their lists. The question is real, but it does not change Arphaxad's place in the line.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states that the family record of Shem runs through Arphaxad to Abram (Genesis 11:10–26). It also records that Shem's line received Noah's blessing, "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Shem" (Genesis 9:26). The structure of Genesis suggests something further. Genesis 5 carries ten generations from Adam to Noah, and Genesis 11 carries ten from Shem to Abram. The book is slowly narrowing its focus from all humanity to one family. Arphaxad stands at the start of that second narrowing. His name sits in the Table of Nations alongside Elam and Asshur, and then the story leaves those peoples and follows him.

Genesis 10:22 lists Arphaxad third among Shem's sons. List order does not always reflect birth order, though, and Genesis 11:10 dates his birth very early after the flood, so it cannot be said for certain that he was a younger son. What is clear is that God's line did not run through Asshur, whose descendants built the Assyrian empire. Throughout Genesis the promise moves through the people God chooses: Seth, Shem, Isaac, Jacob, Judah. Human strength and prominence do not decide it.

A sober note belongs here as well. Joshua later reminded Israel, "Your fathers, including Terah, the father of Abraham and the father of Nahor, dwelt on the other side of the River in old times; and they served other gods" (Joshua 24:2). Within a few generations of Arphaxad, his own descendants had turned to idols. A godly family line did not keep faith alive on its own. God had to call Abram out from Ur of the Chaldeans (Genesis 11:31; 15:7). If the proposed link between Arphaxad's name and the Chaldeans is correct, the point becomes sharper, because Abram would have been called away from his own wider kin. Even without that link, Joshua 24:2 makes the point directly.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Luke names Arphaxad explicitly among the ancestors of Jesus (Luke 3:36). Matthew's genealogy begins with Abraham, so Arphaxad appears only in Luke, whose list runs all the way back to "Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38). This matters to Luke's purpose. Jesus is presented as the Savior of the whole human race, and His family tree passes through the generation that rebuilt the world after the flood.

There is also a strongly supported canonical pattern here. In Genesis 10 Arphaxad is listed among the nations. In Genesis 11 his line leads to Abram, through whom God promised that "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3, referenced). God followed one line out of the nations in order to bring blessing back to the nations, and Luke shows that purpose reaching its goal in Christ. Arphaxad is not a type of Christ. He is one of the real, datable people through whom God kept His word.

5. Spirit & Life

Arphaxad performed no recorded miracles and spoke no recorded words. Genesis tells us only that he was born, became a father, and lived out his years. Yet Luke writes his name in the ancestry of the Son of God. God's purposes do not move only through dramatic moments. They also move through quiet generations who live, raise children, and pass on what they have received.

His story also teaches honesty about faith and family. The line through Arphaxad carried the promise, but Joshua 24:2 shows that it did not keep its descendants from idols. Being in a privileged line saved no one. God Himself had to call Abram by grace. That remains true for every family and every church. We are responsible to hand down the knowledge of God faithfully, and we must also depend on God to awaken faith in each new generation.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:21–24 – Places Arphaxad among Shem's sons and traces his line to Eber.

  • Genesis 11:10–13 – Dates his birth two years after the flood, names Salah, and gives his life span of 438 years.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:17–18, 24 – Reaffirms his place in Israel's line from Adam to Abram.

  • Genesis 11:26–31; 15:7 – Shows his line reaching Abram in Ur of the Chaldeans.

  • Joshua 24:2 – Records that Abram's fathers served other gods.

  • Luke 3:36 – Names Arphaxad (G0742) in the genealogy of Jesus, with Cainan between him and Shelah.
