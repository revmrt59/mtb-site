SHULAMITE - (Shûwlammîyth, Strong's H7759)

Summary

"Shulamite" is the name given to the bride in the Song of Solomon. It appears only twice in the Bible, and both times in the same verse, Song of Solomon 6:13, at a high point of the poem: "Return, return, O Shulamite; Return, return, that we may look upon you!" It reads more like a title than a personal name, and its origin is debated. It may point to a home region, much like "Shunammite." It may also be a feminine form that matches the name Solomon, since both names sound like the Hebrew word for peace, shalom. On that reading, she is the woman of peace who belongs to the king of peace. The Song itself seems to invite the connection when she later says, "Then I became in his eyes as one who found peace" (Song of Solomon 8:10).

The verse calls her by this title at a moment when others long to see her. Early in the Song she begged, "Do not look upon me, because I am dark" (Song of Solomon 1:6). Now onlookers plead with her to turn so they can gaze at her. The verse then compares her to "the dance of the two camps," without saying who speaks those words. The phrase may recall Mahanaim, the place where Jacob met the angels of God, though the Song does not say so. For Christian readers, this name gives a picture of what Christ does for His people. He takes those who are ashamed to be seen and makes them a glorious bride whose peace comes from Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shûwlammîyth is a Hebrew word found only in Song of Solomon 6:13. The Song is a series of love poems in which the bride, her beloved, and a group called the daughters of Jerusalem speak in turn. The speakers are not always labeled, so readers have to follow the voices from the content.

There are two main explanations for where the name comes from. The first connects it to Shunem, a town in northern Israel. Abishag, the young woman brought to King David in his old age, is called "Abishag the Shunammite" (1 Kings 1:3), and "Shulamite" could be a local spelling of the same word. The second treats it as a feminine counterpart to the name Solomon. Both names resemble shalom, the Hebrew word for peace. Scholars have not settled the question. The link to peace is still widely recognized, and the use of "peace" in Song of Solomon 8:10 gives it real support.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means "the Shulamite," a woman identified as belonging to a particular people, place, or name. Song of Solomon 6:13 uses it for the bride. It works as a title for her rather than a description of her appearance or character. If the peace connection holds, the title carries the sense the woman of peace or Solomon's lady, a name that matches the king's own name. If it is a place designation instead, it identifies where she came from.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The two uses come in quick succession. First, the speakers address her directly: "Return, return, O Shulamite." The fourfold "return" sounds urgent. The verse does not say where she is going or why; it simply records the onlookers' plea for her to turn "that we may look upon you!"

Second, a question follows: "What would you see in the Shulamite—As it were, the dance of the two camps?" The question may come from the bride herself, modestly asking why anyone would want to look at her, or from another voice. Either way, the answer is a comparison. The Hebrew phrase translated "the two camps" is the same as the place name Mahanaim. To look at the Shulamite is like watching a great company of people moving together.

4. Theological Significance

If the title is formed to match Solomon's name, it ties the bride's identity to the king. If it is a place designation, it does not show that the king gave her his name. Either way, Song of Solomon 8:10 connects her with peace in poetic terms: she "became in his eyes as one who found peace." In that verse the peace she speaks of is found in his eyes, in how he sees her.

This shows us how God relates to people. Throughout Scripture, God gives names that say who someone now is because of Him: Abram becomes Abraham, Jacob becomes Israel. The Song does not say who gave the Shulamite her title, so she cannot simply be added to that list. What the Song does show is a woman whose dignity is received through being loved, not earned or put on for display.

Scripture also contrasts this kind of beauty with a darker one. In Ezekiel 16, God tells Jerusalem that her beauty "was perfect through My splendor which I had bestowed on you" (Ezekiel 16:14). Then comes the tragedy: "But you trusted in your own beauty, played the harlot because of your fame" (Ezekiel 16:15). Beauty given by God turned into self-worship. The Shulamite shows the other path. Others marvel at her, and she stays devoted to the one who loves her. Hosea 2 shows God winning back His unfaithful wife and promising, "I will betroth you to Me forever" (Hosea 2:19). That restored bride, who once again calls Him "My Husband," is close to the picture of the bride that the Shulamite gives.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:6 and 6:13: from "Do not look" to "that we may look." The bride's first words about herself hold both confidence and shame. She says she is "dark, but lovely" (Song of Solomon 1:5), yet she pleads, "Do not look upon me, because I am dark, because the sun has tanned me." Her brothers had been angry with her and put her to work in the vineyards, and she admits, "my own vineyard I have not kept." She knows she has been weathered by hard work, and she does not want to be stared at. By chapter 6 the scene is reversed. The two verses do not repeat the same Hebrew verb for looking, but the contrast between them is striking even in English. Now others cry, "Return, return... that we may look upon you!" The poem does not explain why the onlookers want to see her, and it does not say the title is newly given. Read across the whole Song, though, the woman who once shrank from being seen is now one whom others long to see, and between those scenes stands the king's open delight in her.

Song of Solomon 6:13 and Genesis 32:1–2: the dance of Mahanaim. When Jacob was on his way home to face his brother Esau, "the angels of God met him." Jacob said, "This is God's camp," and named the place Mahanaim (Genesis 32:1–2). Only after that encounter did his messengers report that Esau was coming with four hundred men, and then "Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed" (Genesis 32:6–7). The Hebrew phrase behind "the two camps" matches the place name, so the comparison may recall heaven's company meeting Jacob on the road, just before the news that made him afraid. That is a possible resonance, not something the Song states; the poet may simply be picturing a stirring festival dance. Either way, the image changes how she is viewed. In chapter 1 she asked not to be looked at, though even then she called herself lovely. Now she is compared to something majestic, ordered, and full of movement. If her title speaks of peace, her beauty is still described with the language of camps and companies.

Song of Solomon 8:10: "as one who found peace." Near the end of the Song, the bride describes herself as "a wall" with towers, an image of strength and maturity, and then says, "Then I became in his eyes as one who found peace." This verse is the strongest support inside the Song for hearing shalom in the name Shulamite. Her peace is found "in his eyes," in the way the king regards her.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song of Solomon is first a celebration of love between a man and a woman. Paul, though, teaches that marriage itself points beyond itself: "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). That gives Christian readers solid ground to see the Shulamite as a picture of the people Christ loves, even though the Song never names Him.

The pattern fits the larger story. Isaiah promised a Child whose name would be called "Prince of Peace" (Isaiah 9:6), and Paul says of Jesus, "He Himself is our peace" (Ephesians 2:14). Whatever the exact origin of the Shulamite's title, the New Testament plainly teaches that Christ is His people's peace, and His bride receives her peace from Him. Christ "loved the church and gave Himself for her," so that "He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle" (Ephesians 5:25, 27). For Christian readers, the movement from the vineyard keeper of chapter 1 to the Shulamite of chapter 6 mirrors the movement of the gospel.

The story ends at a wedding. "The marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7), and "to her it was granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright" (Revelation 19:8). She makes herself ready, yet her clothing is granted to her. Taken together, the two statements suggest that even her readiness rests on what she has been given. Paul adds that when Christ comes, He will come "to be glorified in His saints and to be admired among all those who believe" (2 Thessalonians 1:10). Read in that light, the onlookers' cry, "that we may look upon you," finds its fullest answer on that day. The bride's glory is His glory seen in her.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers live with the bride's plea: "Do not look upon me." They are aware of what the sun and the years and their own neglect have done, and they assume that if God looks closely, He will look away. The Song answers that fear with the king's delight. It never shows the bride fixing herself before he delights in her, and in Song of Solomon 8:10 her peace is described as something found "in his eyes." The person who knows Christ can stop trying to manufacture a sense of worth and start receiving the way He sees them.

The image of the dance of the two camps may also suggest that the bride's peace is not fragile, especially if it recalls the angels of God meeting Jacob on his way home, just before the report of Esau's approach that made him afraid (Genesis 32:1–7). That link is uncertain, but the New Testament teaches the point plainly. Jesus told His disciples, "In the world you will have tribulation; but be of good cheer, I have overcome the world," and He spoke so "that in Me you may have peace" (John 16:33). Paul promises that "the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:7). The peace Christ gives holds under pressure, because it rests on Him rather than on calm circumstances. Paul also describes believers as fighting with weapons that are "mighty in God" (2 Corinthians 10:4). Read alongside such verses, the dance of the two camps fits a peace that is strong rather than fragile.

Finally, the Shulamite does not campaign for attention. It is the onlookers who call, "Return, return." Jesus said, "A city that is set on a hill cannot be hidden," and told His disciples to let their light shine so that others "may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven" (Matthew 5:14, 16). A church that is truly at peace in Christ's love tends to draw attention it does not have to chase. Ezekiel 16 warns about the other possibility: a people who begin to trust in the beauty God gave them can lose it. The Shulamite's safety lies in remembering whose love she rests in.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

"Shulamite" occurs only in Song of Solomon 6:13, where it appears twice, and both uses have been discussed above. The related passages in this study, such as Song of Solomon 1:6, Song of Solomon 8:10, and Genesis 32:1–2, give context to the word but do not contain it.
