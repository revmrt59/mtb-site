EARTH, LAND, GROUND, COUNTRY - (ʼerets, Strong's H0776)

Summary

ʼErets is the ordinary Hebrew word for the earth beneath our feet. It can mean the whole created world, a particular country such as Egypt or Canaan, or simply the ground where someone bows or falls. It is one of the most common nouns in the Old Testament. It opens the Bible, where God created the heavens and the earth, and it carries the story through Abraham's call, Israel's inheritance, exile, and the promise of a new earth.

The word's biblical weight comes from whose earth it is. The earth belongs to the Lord, so no nation owns its land outright and no people can treat it as a place where God is absent. The Promised Land was given as a gift and held on God's terms. When Israel defiled it, the land itself bore the damage, and in the end the people were expelled from it. The prophets still looked past judgment to a restored land and a renewed earth, and Psalm 37 promises that the meek will inherit it. Jesus repeats that promise, and through Him God's people wait for "a new heaven and a new earth" (Revelation 21:1).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼErets is a common Hebrew noun found in every part of the Old Testament. It appears in creation accounts, family stories, laws about property and worship, battle reports, royal records, psalms, wisdom sayings, and prophetic oracles. It clusters most heavily where the story concerns the land of promise. In Genesis God calls Abraham into a land. Deuteronomy sets that land before Israel, and Joshua divides it among the tribes. Jeremiah and Ezekiel describe its loss and promise its restoration.

The NKJV renders the word most often as "land" and "earth," and less often as "country," "ground," "countries," or "world." It occasionally appears in idioms, such as "a little distance" (Genesis 35:16) or "the common people," literally "the people of the land" (Leviticus 4:27).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

ʼErets refers to the terrestrial realm: the solid ground and inhabited world, as distinct from the sky above and the sea. Context decides how large the area is.

1. The earth as a whole: the created world and everything living on it, often paired with "the heavens" to describe all of creation (Genesis 1:1; Psalm 24:1).

2. A land or country: a bounded territory belonging to a people, such as the land of Egypt, the land of Canaan, or "your country" (Genesis 12:1; 15:18).

3. The ground: the surface where people stand, fall, or bow (Genesis 18:2; 33:3).

In the prophets it can be hard to tell whether the word means "the land" of Judah or "the earth" as a whole (Isaiah 24). Sometimes the local judgment serves as a preview of a worldwide one, and translators have to choose one English word where the Hebrew allows both.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of a passage tells you which sense is in view. When God creates, rules, or judges, ʼerets usually means the whole world: Abraham calls Him "the Judge of all the earth" (Genesis 18:25). When the subject is promise, conquest, or exile, it usually means the land given to Israel. In personal scenes it shrinks to the ground. Abraham bows to his visitors with his face to the ground (Genesis 18:2), Jacob bows seven times before Esau (Genesis 33:3), and a defeated psalmist says his body clings to the ground (Psalm 44:25).

Two recurring phrases are worth knowing. "Heaven and earth" together express the whole of creation, which is why Moses can call both as witnesses (Deuteronomy 4:26). "The land of Egypt" and "the land of Canaan" are common geographical phrases, but in Exodus and Deuteronomy they also stand for slavery and promise. Leaving one land and entering the other is the shape of Israel's redemption.

4. Theological Significance

The first truth the word carries is that God owns the earth. He made it, so it is His. At Sinai He grounds His choice of Israel in that ownership: He takes them as His special treasure because all the earth is His (Exodus 19:5). David says the same thing in a psalm: "The earth is the LORD's, and all its fullness" (Psalm 24:1). The land law applies it directly. Land in Israel could not be sold permanently, because the land belonged to God and the people lived on it as strangers and sojourners with Him (Leviticus 25:23). The God of Israel was never a local deity tied to one region. When He gave Canaan to His people, He gave them part of what was already His.

A second truth follows from the first. God's attention to one land served His purpose for the whole earth. In Abraham all the nations of the earth would be blessed (Genesis 18:18; 22:18). The psalms call the whole earth to worship: "Let all the earth fear the LORD" (Psalm 33:8), "Sing to the LORD, all the earth" (Psalm 96:1). Isaiah hears God appeal to all the ends of the earth to turn to Him and be saved (Isaiah 45:22), and he foresees a day when the earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord as the waters cover the sea (Isaiah 11:9; compare Habakkuk 2:14). Israel's land was meant to be the starting point from which God would reclaim the earth.

Scripture also gives the land a voice in God's dealings with His people. Heaven and earth are called as witnesses to His covenant with Israel and to His case against them (Deuteronomy 4:26; 32:1; Isaiah 1:2; Micah 6:2). The earth was there before Israel and outlasts every generation, so it can testify to their vows and their unfaithfulness. Isaiah pictures the land as a bride: Zion's land will no longer be termed Desolate but Beulah, "For the LORD delights in you, And your land shall be married" (Isaiah 62:4). The condition of the ground mirrors the relationship, desolate when God has turned away and fruitful when He delights in His people again. The same earth is called to be glad before the LORD, "For He is coming to judge the earth" (Psalm 96:11–13).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 12:1 — called out of one land into another. God's first word to Abram uses ʼerets twice. He tells Abram to leave his country, which is ʼerets, and go to a land He will show him, which is also ʼerets. Abram's faith begins by giving up the ground he knows for ground he has not yet seen. God later confirms the land by promise and oath (Genesis 15:18; 17:8). Even so, Abram owned nothing in Canaan except a burial cave (Genesis 23). The land was promised to him long before it was possessed.

Leviticus 18:25–28; 26:34; Numbers 35:33 — a land that reacts to sin. Scripture describes the land as morally sensitive. Because the Canaanites defiled it, the land vomited out its inhabitants, and God warns Israel that it will do the same to them if they practice the same abominations (Leviticus 18:25, 28). The land was also owed a Sabbath every seventh year. If Israel refused it that rest, exile would empty the land so it could enjoy the Sabbaths it had been denied (Leviticus 26:34). The Chronicler later sees exactly this in the Babylonian exile (2 Chronicles 36:21). Bloodshed pollutes the land, and nothing cleanses it except the blood of the one who shed it (Numbers 35:33).

These laws teach that sin never stays inside the sinner. It stains the ground, wears out the soil, and calls for judgment from the earth itself. The prophets extend the picture: the land mourns and dries up over its people's sin (Isaiah 24:4; Jeremiah 4:28; Hosea 4:3). The ground can even become an instrument of judgment. In Numbers 16:32 "the earth opened its mouth and swallowed" Korah's rebels, fully under God's command rather than acting as an independent power. Paul draws on the same reality when he says that "the whole creation groans and labors with birth pangs together until now" (Romans 8:22).

Psalm 37 — the meek will inherit. This psalm repeats one promise in several forms. The wicked will be cut off, but "those who wait on the LORD, they shall inherit the earth" (v. 9). "The meek shall inherit the earth, and shall delight themselves in the abundance of peace" (v. 11). The righteous will inherit the land and dwell in it forever (vv. 22, 29). David wrote to people tempted to fret when the ruthless seemed to own everything. His answer is that the ground goes in the end to those who trust and wait, not to those who grab.

Ezekiel 36:24–35 — a cleansed land and a new heart. Ezekiel uses ʼerets for the "countries" from which God will gather His scattered people (v. 24) and for the desolate "land" that is farmed again (v. 34). The return comes together with inward renewal: clean water, a new heart, and God's Spirit within them, so that they walk in His statutes and dwell in the land He gave their fathers (vv. 25–28). Ground that had been desolate is farmed again, until passersby say it has become like the garden of Eden (vv. 34–35). The land was defiled when the people's hearts went astray, and it is restored when their hearts are made new.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of ʼerets runs from a good creation, through a promised land gained and then lost, to a renewed earth. Christ stands at the turning point of that story.

Psalm 2 promises God's Anointed: "Ask of Me, and I will give You the nations for Your inheritance, and the ends of the earth for Your possession" (v. 8). The heir of the whole earth, however, first came as Isaiah's Servant, growing up "as a root out of dry ground" and then "cut off from the land of the living" (Isaiah 53:2, 8). Both phrases use ʼerets. Luke records that Philip used this passage to tell an Ethiopian official about Jesus (Acts 8:32–35). The One to whom the earth was promised was buried in it, and He rose to claim it.

Jesus applies Psalm 37:11 directly to His disciples: the meek will inherit the earth (Matthew 5:5). Hebrews explains that Joshua's conquest did not give the final rest, since God later spoke of "another day" (Hebrews 4:8). Hebrews 3–4 treats Israel's entry into Canaan as a picture of entering God's rest through faith. That reading does not erase the land's history. Canaan was real ground given to a real people, and God's faithfulness in giving it is part of what makes the larger promise believable. The patriarchs understood this. They "confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth," desiring "a better, that is, a heavenly country" (Hebrews 11:13, 16). That hope does not leave the earth behind. Isaiah promised new heavens and a new earth (Isaiah 65:17; 66:22), and John saw them: "a new heaven and a new earth" (Revelation 21:1). Canaan served as a down payment on that larger inheritance, which comes to God's people through Christ.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 24:1 means that there is no neutral ground. The office, the kitchen, the farm, and the nation all belong to God, and believers live on His property as guests. Leviticus 25:23 makes the point carefully: Israel was to live as sojourners with God. That phrase describes nearness to Him as well as temporary residence.

The land laws also take away the idea of private sin. Leviticus and Numbers show that wrongdoing pollutes what it touches and that rest denied to God's creation is eventually reclaimed. A believer who takes this seriously will treat the creation, ordinary work, and other people's lives as matters God watches over.

Psalm 37 gives a reason to stop fretting. Those who grasp at the earth will lose it, and those who wait on the Lord will receive it. Christ's meekness proved this, and His resurrection guaranteed it. A Christian can therefore hold possessions loosely and still love the world God made, because the meek will inherit a renewed earth.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 18:25 — Abraham appeals to the Judge of all the earth to do right. His intercession for Sodom rests on God's rule over the whole ʼerets.

  • Psalm 8:1, 9 — The psalm opens and closes by praising God's excellent name in all the earth, framing a meditation on humanity's small but honored place within creation.

  • 2 Chronicles 7:14 — God promises to heal the land of a people who humble themselves, pray, and turn. Restoration of the people and of the ground go together, as in Leviticus.

  • Isaiah 6:3 — The seraphim declare that the whole earth is full of His glory, even while Judah's land is heading toward judgment.

  • Jeremiah 4:23 — Jeremiah sees the earth without form and void. In this vision of coming judgment, the land returns to the emptiness that existed before creation.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H8064, shamayim ("heavens") — Canonical pairing. "The heavens and the earth" together express the whole of creation (Genesis 1:1), and both are summoned as witnesses (Deuteronomy 4:26; Isaiah 1:2).

  • G1093, gē ("earth, land") — Cross-language equivalent used where the New Testament carries forward the inheritance theme, as in Matthew 5:5 (quoting Psalm 37:11) and Hebrews 11:13.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Exploratory associations. Bowing with one's face to the ground (Genesis 18:2; 33:3) can reasonably be read as acknowledging human origin from the dust and dependence on God and others. Psalm 139:15 speaks of being skillfully wrought "in the lowest parts of the earth," a picture of the hidden origin of human life that also recalls that people are made from dust. Neither association is stated as the point of these passages, so both should be held loosely.
