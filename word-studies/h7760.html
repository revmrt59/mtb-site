PUT, SET, PLACE, MAKE, APPOINT - (sûwm, Strong's H7760)

Summary

The Hebrew verb sûwm means to put, set, or place something where it belongs. It can also mean to make, appoint, or establish. It is one of the Old Testament's everyday working verbs. People use it when they put food on a table, set a stone upright, put on sackcloth, or appoint a king. Because the word is so ordinary, its weightier uses can be easy to miss. When God is the one doing the putting, the word describes Him arranging reality according to His purpose. He sets the sand as the boundary of the sea. He puts His name in the place He chooses and on the people He blesses. He puts His Spirit upon His servants. In Isaiah 53:10 the Servant's soul is made an offering for sin, and the surrounding lines show that His suffering took place within God's own purpose.

The word also reaches inward. In Hebrew, putting the heart on something means paying close, deliberate attention to it. God uses this expression when He calls His people to "consider" their ways and to "take it to heart." Scripture treats both God's placements and ours as purposeful acts. God's placements carry His authority, and what we place in our hearts and before our eyes will either draw us to Him or trip us up.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Sûwm is a common Hebrew verb, and it appears from Genesis through Malachi. Its English translations show how broad it is. The NKJV most often renders it "put," "set," "make," or "made," and also uses "laid," "appointed," "consider," "issue," and dozens of other words. The verb appears in household scenes, building projects, battles, royal courts, worship, and prophetic poetry.

Two settings stand out. The first is Israel's worship. In Exodus 40 the verb appears again and again as Moses puts, sets, and inserts each piece of the tabernacle exactly as the LORD commanded. The second is the prophets. There God "makes" cities into ruins or deserts into pools, and He calls people to set their hearts on what He is saying.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, sûwm means to cause something to be in a particular position or condition. Context determines which of these senses is in view:

1. To put or place physically: setting an object, a hand, or a person in a location. Jacob set up the stone he had slept on as a pillar (Genesis 28:18).

2. To make or turn into, so that one thing becomes another. God "made the depths of the sea a road" (Isaiah 51:10).

3. To appoint or establish: placing someone in an office, or setting a law, boundary, or decree in force. Israel was to set a king over themselves whom the LORD would choose (Deuteronomy 17:15), and Moses was given "the judgments which you shall set before them" (Exodus 21:1).

4. To set the heart or mind on something, meaning to consider carefully or take seriously. The LORD asks Satan, "Have you considered My servant Job?" (Job 1:8).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The subject of the verb changes its weight. When servants put grain in sacks, nothing more than a household chore is in view. When the LORD "set a mark on Cain, lest anyone finding him should kill him" (Genesis 4:15), the placement carries divine authority. It is a protective sign, and no one may overrule it.

The thing placed also matters. Hands placed on a head carry blessing (Genesis 48:18). Words placed in a mouth carry prophecy. A name placed on a house or a people carries ownership.

One idiom deserves particular attention. Several English phrases, including "consider," "take it to heart," and "purposed in his heart," translate a Hebrew expression that literally means to put the heart. The English loses the vivid wording, but the idea is clear. Attention is an act of placing, because a person deliberately sets the center of his inner life on something.

4. Theological Significance

The God who puts things in place is the God who orders creation. Through Jeremiah He asks, "Do you not fear Me?… Who have placed the sand as the bound of the sea, by a perpetual decree, that it cannot pass beyond it?" (Jeremiah 5:22). The psalmist says the same: "You have set a boundary that they may not pass over" (Psalm 104:9). The roaring sea was the ancient picture of chaos, yet its limit is a line God has placed. The same verb describes God setting judgments before Israel (Exodus 21:1). The boundaries on the sea and the boundaries in His law come from the same hand, and both protect life.

God also rearranges what He has placed, whether to save or to judge. He "made the depths of the sea a road for the redeemed to cross over" (Isaiah 51:10). "He turns a wilderness into pools of water" (Psalm 107:35), and He also turns "rivers into a wilderness," a fruitful land into barrenness, "For the wickedness of those who dwell in it" (Psalm 107:33–34). Human beings build fortified cities to make themselves permanent, but the prophet says to the LORD, "You have made a city a ruin" (Isaiah 25:2). Nothing stays where it stands unless God keeps it there. For the redeemed this is a deep comfort. No obstacle is too fixed for Him to turn into a road.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 53:10: The Servant made an offering for sin. The fourth Servant Song reaches its climax when it speaks of the Servant's soul becoming an offering for sin. The NKJV reads, "When You make His soul an offering for sin," a conditional clause spoken to the LORD. Sûwm is the verb behind "make," but the verb on its own does not settle who makes the offering. The surrounding lines carry the theological weight. The verse begins, "Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise Him; He has put Him to grief," and earlier the prophet says, "the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6). Two verses later the Servant Himself "poured out His soul unto death" (Isaiah 53:12). The offering named here is the guilt offering, the sacrifice Leviticus required when a wrong had to be paid for (Leviticus 5:15). Human cruelty was real, as the chapter's oppression and injustice show. Yet the Servant's suffering stood within God's purpose. The New Testament says the same: Jesus was "delivered by the determined purpose and foreknowledge of God" (Acts 2:23), and He "offered Himself without spot to God" (Hebrews 9:14). The cross was not a plan that collapsed. God put it in place, and the Son gave Himself willingly.

Deuteronomy 12:5, 1 Kings 9:3, and Numbers 6:27: God's name placed. Israel was to seek the place the LORD would choose to put His name as His dwelling (Deuteronomy 12:5, 21). When Solomon finished the temple, God answered, "I have consecrated this house which you have built to put My name there forever, and My eyes and My heart will be there perpetually" (1 Kings 9:3). The God whom heaven cannot contain chose to make Himself reachable at a place on earth. In this setting, His name stands for His revealed presence.

That name was placed on people as well as on a building. After the priestly blessing, the LORD said that the priests would put His name on the children of Israel and He would bless them (Numbers 6:27). The blessing does more than wish the people well. It marks them as His own.

Haggai 1:5, 7, Malachi 2:2, and Job 1:8: putting the heart. The returned exiles lived in paneled houses while the LORD's house lay in ruins. Twice God says, "Consider your ways" (Haggai 1:5, 7), which literally means to set their hearts on their ways. They had been working hard and gaining nothing, and God wanted them to stop long enough to see why. Malachi presses the same idiom against careless priests: "If you will not take it to heart, to give glory to My name… I will send a curse upon you" (Malachi 2:2). God was not looking for more ritual from them. He was looking for hearts deliberately turned toward His glory.

In Job 1:8 the idiom describes the attention God asks Satan to give: "Have you considered My servant Job?" God's own verdict follows in the same verse: "there is none like him on the earth, a blameless and upright man, one who fears God and shuns evil." Job knew nothing of this conversation. Before any trouble came, God had already spoken of him in these terms.

Genesis 28:18 and 1 Samuel 7:12: stones set as witnesses. After his dream at Bethel, Jacob "took the stone that he had put at his head, set it up as a pillar, and poured oil on top of it" (Genesis 28:18). The same verb appears twice in that verse. The stone that had been a pillow became a witness that God had met him there. Generations later, Samuel "took a stone and set it up between Mizpah and Shen, and called its name Ebenezer, saying, 'Thus far the LORD has helped us'" (1 Samuel 7:12). In both scenes an ordinary stone, deliberately placed, became a permanent testimony to God's help.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament identifies Isaiah's suffering Servant with Jesus (Acts 8:32–35). Paul states the same reality in the language of the gospel: "He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Paul writes in Greek, so this is not the same Hebrew word, but the pattern is the same. God placed the sinless One where sinners stood.

Jesus Himself pointed to a placement in Numbers. When the people were dying from the serpents' bites, God told Moses to make a serpent and "set" it on a pole, and Moses "put" it there (Numbers 21:8–9). Anyone who looked lived. Jesus said, "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have eternal life" (John 3:14–15). The dying Israelites could not cure themselves. God provided the remedy and placed it where everyone could see it.

The Servant also cooperates with the Father's purpose. In Isaiah 50:7 He says that He has set His face like a flint, and Luke later describes Jesus steadfastly setting His face to go to Jerusalem (Luke 9:51). Luke describes this in Greek rather than Hebrew, but the shared image shows a willing Son.

God's placing of His name moves in the same direction. The name God put in the temple came to dwell among people in person: "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory" (John 1:14). The name the priests placed on Israel reaches its final form in the new creation: "They shall see His face, and His name shall be on their foreheads" (Revelation 22:4).

The Spirit follows the same path. God told Moses, "I will take of the Spirit that is upon you and will put the same upon them; and they shall bear the burden of the people with you" (Numbers 11:17). Isaiah 59:21 speaks of a lasting gift in two parts: "My Spirit who is upon you," and "My words which I have put in your mouth." The Spirit is described as already upon the one addressed; it is the words that God says He has put in the mouth. Both are promised to remain "from this time and forevermore." At Pentecost God declared, "I will pour out of My Spirit on all flesh" (Acts 2:17). What He once placed on seventy elders He now gives to all His people.

7. Spirit & Life

Every one of us is always placing things. Ezekiel 14:4 and 14:7 describe elders who came to inquire of the LORD while they had set up idols in their hearts. The prophet adds that each of them put before his own face what caused him to stumble into iniquity, and sûwm is the verb for that second action. Nobody tripped these men. They put the stumbling block there themselves and then came to God as though their path were clear. God's answer was to deal with the heart directly (Ezekiel 14:5), and He later promised a new heart (Ezekiel 36:26). The searching question for us is what we have set before our own eyes. Daniel shows the other way: he "purposed in his heart," literally set his heart, not to defile himself (Daniel 1:8). Holiness began with where he chose to place his heart.

Power for service is also something God places on a person. Moses was being crushed by the burden of leadership, and God did not tell him to try harder. He put His Spirit on others to share the load (Numbers 11:17). The strength to carry God's work comes as His gift, given from outside ourselves.

The same verb that sets boundaries on the sea also appears in one of the tenderest prayers in Scripture: "You number my wanderings; put my tears into Your bottle; are they not in Your book?" (Psalm 56:8). The picture is poetic, but the confidence behind it is solid. God gives careful attention to the tears of His people, just as He does to the boundaries of the sea. The God who spoke so highly of Job before his trials keeps watch over our sorrow too.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 45:7–8: Joseph tells his brothers that God sent him ahead "to preserve" a remnant and "made" him a father to Pharaoh. God's placement of Joseph worked through the brothers' betrayal.

  • Genesis 48:18: Joseph protests, "put your right hand on his head," but Jacob deliberately places the greater blessing on the younger son, a recurring pattern of God's surprising choices.

  • Exodus 40:18–30: Moses "set up" and "put" each part of the tabernacle in its place. Faithful worship followed God's arrangement rather than human invention.

  • Isaiah 42:4: The Servant will not fail "till He has established justice in the earth." His mission is to set God's justice firmly in place.

  • Song of Solomon 8:6: "Set me as a seal upon your heart." Love asks to be placed where it cannot be removed.
