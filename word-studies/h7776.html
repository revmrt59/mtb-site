FOX, JACKAL - (shûwʻâl, Strong's H7776)

Summary

The Hebrew word shûwʻâl names the fox. It also covers the jackal, the fox's larger cousin that runs in packs and feeds on carrion. The word appears only seven times, in six verses of the Old Testament (Song of Solomon 2:15 uses it twice), and in nearly every case the animal turns up where something valuable is left unguarded or has already been lost. Foxes slip into a vineyard to spoil the young grapes. They prowl over the rubble of a fallen city. Jackals feed on the bodies of the defeated. One of Israel's enemies even makes the fox a joke, sneering that the weight of one fox would topple the wall Nehemiah's builders were raising.

Read together, these passages make the fox a way of measuring the condition of what God gives His people to guard. Where love is tended, the little foxes are caught before they ruin the fruit (Song of Solomon 2:15). Where people rebuild in faith, mockery cannot bring their wall down (Nehemiah 4:3). Where God's people turn from Him, foxes walk freely over Zion (Lamentations 5:18), and false prophets behave like jackals feeding in the ruins when they should be repairing the broken wall (Ezekiel 13:4–5). Jesus draws on the same picture when He calls Herod "that fox" (Luke 13:32). Whatever shade of meaning Jesus intended, Herod's threat could not turn the true King from His road to Jerusalem.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shûwʻâl is a Hebrew noun for the wild dogs that lived in Israel's hills, fields and wastelands. Ancient Hebrew grouped animals by how they looked and lived, without the categories of modern biology. As a result, one word served for both the solitary fox and the pack-running, scavenging jackal. The NKJV usually translates it "fox" or "foxes." At Psalm 63:10 it uses "jackals," because the scene there is of animals feeding on the fallen.

The word appears in narrative (Judges, Nehemiah), poetry (Psalms, Song of Solomon), and lament and prophecy (Lamentations, Ezekiel). Farmers knew these animals as raiders of vineyards. City dwellers knew them as the creatures that move in once people move out.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one basic meaning: a wild canine, the fox or the jackal. The context shows which animal, and which habits, the writer has in mind.

1. Fox: a small, quick, light-footed animal that slips into cultivated ground and spoils it, as in "the little foxes that spoil the vines" (Song of Solomon 2:15).

2. Jackal: a scavenger of desolate places and of unburied bodies, as in "They shall be a portion for jackals" (Psalm 63:10).

The two pictures overlap, and some passages, such as Lamentations 5:18, could suit either animal.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the word in four main ways.

  • Living animals put to use. Samson catches the animals and turns them loose as firebrands against the Philistines (Judges 15:4).

  • A picture of weakness. Tobiah mocks the Jews' work by saying a fox could break it (Nehemiah 4:3).

  • A threat to growing fruit. The foxes in the Song endanger "tender grapes" (Song of Solomon 2:15).

  • A sign of ruin and judgment. The animals roam a desolate Zion (Lamentations 5:18), feed on fallen enemies (Psalm 63:10), and serve as a picture of false prophets (Ezekiel 13:4).

Two small details of wording affect the meaning. In the Song, the word "little" carries the warning: the danger comes from animals small enough to be overlooked. In Ezekiel, the prophets are "like foxes." That comparison judges their character. It describes what kind of men they are.

4. Theological Significance

Across these passages, the fox appears wherever the boundaries God has given His people are being tested, breached, or erased. Vineyards, walls and cities are places of fruitfulness and safety. The fox is the creature that finds the weak point.

That pattern ends in judgment. In Psalm 63, David is writing in the wilderness while men hunt for his life. He says those who seek to destroy him "shall go into the lower parts of the earth" (Psalm 63:9). Then he adds, "They shall fall by the sword; They shall be a portion for jackals" (Psalm 63:10). In Israel's world, an unburied body left for scavengers was the most shameful end a person could suffer. The law names it among the curses that fall on those who reject the LORD (Deuteronomy 28:26). The psalm then turns to the king who rejoices in God (Psalm 63:11). People who set themselves against God's anointed do not end in glory. They are left in the open country for the jackals.

The same ruin can come to God's own people when they abandon Him. Then the jackal's wasteland is Zion itself. God does not take judgment lightly, and He does not hide what is at stake: what He guards flourishes, and what He gives up becomes a home for scavengers.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:15 — The little foxes. The request comes in the middle of a spring song of love. Winter is past, the flowers are blooming, and the beloved calls his bride to come away. Then comes the practical plea: "Catch us the foxes, The little foxes that spoil the vines, For our vines have tender grapes." Notice the words "us" and "our." The vineyard is shared, so guarding it is a shared task. The grapes are still tender, which means the young fruit can be lost before it ever ripens. The very next line explains why the vineyard is worth guarding: "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (Song of Solomon 2:16).

These foxes are not lions or bears. They are small, quick and hard to catch, and they do their damage quietly. In marriage, in a church, and in a believer's walk with Christ, what is growing is seldom destroyed by one open catastrophe. Small things get in instead: a grudge allowed to stay, a careless habit of speech, prayer quietly dropped. Love that is growing has to be protected on purpose.

Nehemiah 4:3 with Lamentations 5:18 — The fox on the wall. Tobiah the Ammonite watches the returned exiles rebuild Jerusalem and jeers: "Whatever they build, if even a fox goes up on it, he will break down their stone wall." The insult works because a fox is light on its feet. Tobiah is saying the wall is so flimsy that the lightest animal could knock it over.

Read alongside Lamentations, his mockery has a painful echo. Lamentations had mourned "Mount Zion which is desolate, With foxes walking about on it." Foxes really had walked over Jerusalem's ruins. Nehemiah does not say that Tobiah had that lament in mind, but the parallel is striking: the animal of Zion's ruin has become the animal of an enemy's taunt. Nehemiah answered with prayer instead of argument (4:4–5). Then he records, "So we built the wall, and the entire wall was joined together up to half its height, for the people had a mind to work" (4:6). The wall the world called fragile held, because God was building it. The fox of Lamentations would not have the last word over Zion.

Ezekiel 13:4–5 — Jackals in the ruins. God tells Israel, "your prophets are like foxes in the deserts." A fox in a ruined place does not rebuild anything. It searches the rubble to feed itself. The next verse shows what God expected of His prophets instead: "You have not gone up into the gaps to build a wall for the house of Israel to stand in battle on the day of the LORD." A true prophet climbs into the breach and repairs it. A false prophet slips through the breach and lives off the damage. These men also smeared whitewash over a crumbling wall, promising peace when there was none (Ezekiel 13:10–11). God names them as the opposite of a shepherd: they used His people's desolation for their own benefit.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

When the Pharisees warned Jesus that Herod wanted to kill Him, Jesus answered, "Go, tell that fox, 'Behold, I cast out demons and perform cures today and tomorrow, and the third day I shall be perfected'" (Luke 13:32). The Greek word is different, but the picture is close to the one Israel's Scriptures had already painted. Jesus may have meant that Herod was crafty, or destructive, or a lesser figure than he imagined himself to be; some later Jewish writings contrast the fox with the lion. Any of these nuances is possible. What the passage makes certain is Jesus' resolve. Herod's threat would not change His course: "Nevertheless I must journey today, tomorrow, and the day following; for it cannot be that a prophet should perish outside of Jerusalem" (Luke 13:33). He would keep doing His Father's work and go on to Jerusalem, where He would lay down His life. Revelation later names Him "the Lion of the tribe of Judah" (Revelation 5:5).

Christ also stands where Ezekiel's prophets refused to go. They would not go up into the gaps. God had searched for someone to stand in the gap and found no one (Ezekiel 22:30). Jesus took that place. As the Good Shepherd, He lays down His life for the sheep, while the hired man runs away (John 10:11–13). The foxes ruined the vineyard, mocked the wall, and fed in the rubble. Christ guards His bride's vineyard, builds His church on a foundation that cannot be shaken, and brings the ruined city back to life.

7. Spirit & Life

The Song's plea, "Catch us the foxes," fits life with Christ. Many believers can name the moment they were drawn to Him. Fewer notice the slow damage afterward, as their first love fades through small things they never dealt with. The little foxes are rarely dramatic sins, which is exactly why they survive. Ask the Lord to show you what has slipped into the vineyard, and deal with it while the fruit is still tender.

Ezekiel puts a sharp question to anyone who leads, teaches or simply influences others: when people around you are broken, do you build or do you scavenge? It is possible to use a struggling church, family or friend for your own comfort or reputation. The calling of Christ's servants is to go up into the gap, pray, and help rebuild.

Nehemiah's wall speaks to anyone whose obedience is being mocked as too weak to last. Tobiah's taunt sounded clever, but it changed nothing. Pray as Nehemiah did, keep building, and trust God to hold up what He has told you to raise.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Judges 15:4–5. "Samson went and caught three hundred foxes" (Judges 15:4), tied them tail to tail with torches, and let them go into the Philistines' standing grain. The fire "burned up both the shocks and the standing grain, as well as the vineyards and olive groves" (Judges 15:5). Jackals, which travel in packs, may be the likelier animals here. In this scene, the creatures that elsewhere ruin Israel's vineyards are turned against the harvest of Israel's oppressors. The episode suggests that God can use instruments of ruin to judge His enemies.

The remaining occurrences (Nehemiah 4:3; Psalm 63:10; Song of Solomon 2:15, twice; Lamentations 5:18; Ezekiel 13:4) are discussed in Sections 4 and 5.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G0258 (alōpēx), fox. This is the Greek word for fox. It is a parallel term in another language, not a Hebrew relative of shûwʻâl. Jesus uses it for Herod in Luke 13:32, and the Old Testament fox imagery traced in this study helps explain His meaning.
