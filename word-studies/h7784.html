STREET - (shûwq, Strong's H7784)

Summary

The Hebrew word shûwq means street, the public lanes of a town where people walk, trade, meet, and pass one another. It appears only four times in the Old Testament, all in the poetry of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Solomon. In each passage the street is shared public space, outside the private shelter of the home, and each passage gives it its own image.

In Proverbs 7 a young man devoid of understanding passes along the street near a woman's corner and takes the path to her house, toward the one who will ruin him. In the Song of Solomon a young woman leaves her bed at night and searches the streets for the one she loves. In Ecclesiastes 12 the street marks the edge of life itself: as old age closes in, the doors to the street are shut, and at death the mourners walk through it. The same lanes test the heart's loves and measure the shortness of life. Scripture's last street, in the New Jerusalem, is paved with gold, its gates never shut, with the tree of life growing in its middle.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shûwq is a Hebrew noun for a street or lane inside a town. A related word in other Semitic languages means a market or bazaar, which suggests the busy, crowded passages where ordinary business took place. That connection fits the biblical uses, but the four verses themselves simply present the street as public space.

Ancient towns were tight clusters of houses with narrow lanes running between them. Hebrew had other words for public places, including the broad open square near the gate. In Song of Solomon 3:2, shûwq appears alongside that word for an open square: "in the streets and in the squares." Every use of shûwq comes from Israel's poetry and wisdom writing, so the word is always carrying an image as well as naming a place.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shûwq means a street: a public way through a town, outside the private shelter of a house. It has one sense throughout. The meanings that grow around it in Scripture, such as danger, longing, and mortality, come from the passages where it appears.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The street appears in three settings. In Proverbs 7:8 it is the route of a foolish young man at dusk. In Ecclesiastes 12:4–5 it belongs to a poem about growing old and dying. In Song of Solomon 3:2 it is the scene of a lover's night search. In every case, the street matters because of who is in it and what happens there. The young man without understanding takes the path to the wrong house. The old man is cut off from it. The mourners fill it. The bride crosses it on purpose. The word never changes, but each setting gives the street its own meaning.

4. Theological Significance

In Proverbs, public space is not morally empty. Wisdom "raises her voice in the open squares" and "cries out in the chief concourses" (Proverbs 1:20–21). The adulteress is "at times in the open square, lurking at every corner" (Proverbs 7:12). God does not leave the world's crowded places to folly alone. His wisdom calls out in the same lanes where temptation waits, and Proverbs pictures the person walking there as hearing two voices and answering one of them.

The street also sets two night walkers side by side. The young man in Proverbs 7 and the bride in the Song of Solomon both walk public lanes in the dark. Proverbs does not disclose what the young man intended; it reports his route, past her corner and onto the path to her house (Proverbs 7:8). The bride, by contrast, says plainly why she goes out: she is seeking the one she loves. The adulteress tells the young man, "I came out to meet you, diligently to seek your face, and I have found you" (Proverbs 7:15). Set beside the Song, her words sound like a counterfeit of the bride's search. The bride says, "I will seek the one I love," and then, "I sought him, but I did not find him" (Song of Solomon 3:2). The adulteress finds her prey easily. The bride's search costs her, and she keeps looking anyway.

Ecclesiastes adds a sober truth. However busy the street may be, the day comes when the mourners go about its streets for each person who once walked there. God made human life to be active, social, and public, and He also set limits on it that no one can bargain away.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 7:6–27: the path to her house. The father watches from his window as a young man "devoid of understanding" walks by at twilight, as evening turns to dark night (7:7, 9, paraphrased). He is "passing along the street near her corner; and he took the path to her house" (7:8). Proverbs does not tell us what was in his mind as he set out. It tells us where he walked: near her corner, and then along the path to her house. He is "devoid of understanding," and his route exposes his lack of judgment. The woman, by contrast, has a plan. Her "feet would not stay at home" (7:11), and she comes dressed, prepared, and religious-sounding, claiming she has paid her vows (7:14). The young man follows "as an ox goes to the slaughter... he did not know it would cost his life" (Proverbs 7:22–23). Walking where he should not have gone gave her an opening, and the chapter closes with the father's plea: "Do not stray into her paths" (Proverbs 7:25).

Ecclesiastes 12:4–5: the street at the end of life. The Preacher urges the young to remember their Creator before the hard days come (12:1), and then he describes old age in a series of images. "The doors are shut in the streets, and the sound of grinding is low" (12:4). Interpreters differ on what each image pictures. Some see failing lips, ears, or teeth; others read the lines differently, and the poem leaves its details open. The overall movement is clear, though: the busy world of the street recedes as life draws toward its close. Then comes the last use: "man goes to his eternal home, and the mourners go about the streets" (12:5). The streets where he once came and went now hold the people grieving for him, and the dust returns to the earth while the spirit returns to God who gave it (12:7, paraphrased).

Song of Solomon 3:1–4: seeking love in the streets. At night on her bed the young woman seeks the one she loves and does not find him (3:1, paraphrased). So she resolves: "I will rise now," I said, "And go about the city; in the streets and in the squares I will seek the one I love" (3:2). She leaves her bed for the dark city lanes, and she searches everywhere, in both the narrow streets and the open squares. The city watchmen find her (3:3). Only after she has passed them does she find him, and she holds him and will not let him go (3:4, paraphrased). The street becomes the path of costly, determined love. Her search does not succeed at once, and the delay is part of what the passage shows. Her love keeps searching through the night.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Bible's story of the street ends in a city. In the New Jerusalem, "the street of the city was pure gold, like transparent glass" (Revelation 21:21), and "its gates shall not be shut at all by day" (21:25). Revelation uses a Greek word for street, so this is a canonical contrast and not another use of shûwq. The contrast is still striking. In Ecclesiastes, doors are shut in the streets and mourners walk through them. In the city of God, no gate is shut, God wipes away every tear, and there is no more death or mourning (Revelation 21:4, paraphrased). In Proverbs, the street is where a young man walks toward his death. In the New Jerusalem, "in the middle of its street... was the tree of life," whose leaves are "for the healing of the nations" (22:2).

The Gospels add another movement. Wisdom cried out in the public places of Proverbs, and Jesus, who is the wisdom of God, taught in the open lanes of Israel's towns. In His parable of the great supper, the master sends his servant into "the streets and lanes of the city" to bring the poor and the lame to his table (Luke 14:21, paraphrased). The bride of the Song searched the streets for her beloved. Many Christians have read the Song as a picture of Christ and His people, a reading that Ephesians 5 makes fitting even though the Song never names Him. In the gospel, the Lord sends His invitation into the streets, and the Son of Man Himself has come "to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10).

7. Spirit & Life

Believers cannot stay indoors. Work, relationships, and ordinary errands take us into public life, and Proverbs 7 shows that the danger often begins with where we choose to walk. Proverbs does not say what the young man intended, but it shows his route: near her corner, at dusk, and then onto the path to her house. Lacking understanding, he put himself where temptation was waiting. A believer who pays no attention to where his steps are taking him leaves room for someone else to choose the destination.

The bride shows the opposite. She walked the same kind of streets because she was looking for someone, and that purpose carried her through the dark city and past the watchmen. Set beside Proverbs 7, her search suggests that the strongest safeguard against the street's pull is a stronger love, more than better caution. When a heart is set on seeking Christ, the world's lanes become a route to Him, and a temptation that speaks smoothly has less to work with.

Ecclesiastes adds urgency. The street will not always be open to us, because the doors will close and the mourners will come. The Preacher's answer is to remember the Creator now, while the street is still noisy and our strength still holds. Revelation then gives the hope that sustains that remembering. The streets of this age are temporary, and those who seek the Lord in them are walking toward a city whose gates are never shut.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Shûwq occurs only four times, in Proverbs 7:8, Ecclesiastes 12:4, Ecclesiastes 12:5, and Song of Solomon 3:2. All four have been discussed above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

Canonical pairing (a distinct word used alongside shûwq, not a lexical relative):

  • H7339 (rechôb), open square, broad place: paired with shûwq in Song of Solomon 3:2 ("in the streets and in the squares"). It also appears in Proverbs 1:20 and 7:12, where shûwq itself does not occur; those verses are thematic comparisons.

Thematic connections (comparisons from other verses, not words used alongside shûwq):

  • H2351 (chûwts), outside, street: describes the adulteress "outside" in Proverbs 7:12 and Wisdom calling "outside" in Proverbs 1:20. Shûwq does not occur in these verses, so this is a thematic comparison rather than a pairing.

  • G4113 (plateia), street: the golden street of the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:21; 22:2).
