LEG, THIGH, HIP - (shôwq, Strong's H7785)

Summary

The Hebrew word shôwq means leg. It can mean the upper leg, or thigh, of an animal, or the legs of a person. In the laws of worship it names the right thigh of the peace offering, the portion God claimed for Himself and gave to His priests "by a statute forever" (Leviticus 7:34). The same word appears when Samuel had a specially reserved thigh served to Saul at a feast, the day before he anointed him (1 Samuel 9:24; 10:1). In these passages the thigh is food that carries honor. God feeds those who serve Him from what comes to His altar.

Outside the sanctuary, the human leg stands for a person's own strength: the power to stand, march, fight and walk. Scripture repeatedly warns God's people against trusting it. The Lord "takes no pleasure in the legs of a man" (Psalm 147:10). Folly leaves wisdom hanging like lame legs (Proverbs 26:7). Judgment strikes the legs of faithless Israel and exposes those of proud Babylon (Deuteronomy 28:35; Isaiah 47:2). Against these failing legs, the Song of Solomon pictures the beloved's legs as "pillars of marble set on bases of fine gold" (Song of Solomon 5:15). Taken together, the word's uses point away from the strength we stand on and toward the provision God supplies from His altar and, as Christians have long read the Song, toward the strength found in Christ, the beloved King.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shôwq is an ordinary Hebrew word for a body part. Most of its uses come from the instructions for Israel's priests in Exodus, Leviticus and Numbers, where it names a cut of meat from a sacrificed animal. The rest appear in narrative, poetry, wisdom and prophecy, where it refers to human legs.

Readers should know that English Bibles also use "thigh" for a different Hebrew word, yarek. That word appears in Abraham's servant swearing an oath with his hand under Abraham's thigh, and in Jacob's injured hip after he wrestled with God. Those passages belong to yarek. Shôwq speaks of the leg as meat, as strength and as the means of walking.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The thigh or upper leg of a sacrificial animal, especially the right thigh of the peace offering, which was set apart as the priest's portion (Leviticus 7:32).

2. The leg of a person, seen as the part that bears weight and carries a person forward. It can be strong, afflicted, lame or uncovered (Psalm 147:10; Proverbs 26:7; Isaiah 47:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the sanctuary passages the word is almost always paired with "the breast." Together the breast and thigh are the priests' share of the peace offering. The breast was "waved" before the Lord, and the thigh was a "heave offering," which means something lifted up and set apart to Him. The repeated word "right" matters. The right side often marks honor in Scripture, and this specific portion was the one God named for His priests.

When the word describes people, the context changes what the leg represents. In Judges 15:8 Samson "attacked them hip and thigh with a great slaughter." The phrase pairs shôwq with yarek and works as an idiom for crushing, thorough defeat. In wisdom and prophecy the leg shows how strong or how helpless a person really is.

4. Theological Significance

Through this word, Scripture places two kinds of strength side by side. One is the strength God gives from His altar. The other is the strength people trust in their own bodies.

At the altar, God is the giver. Leviticus 7:34 puts the verbs in His own mouth: "the thigh of the heave offering I have taken from the children of Israel... and I have given them to Aaron the priest and to his sons." The thigh passed through God's hands first. It was His portion before it became theirs. Their support was not meant to depend on bargaining with worshipers; they ate from the Lord's table. Leviticus 10:14–15 extends this portion to the priests' sons and daughters, so the whole household lived on what God provided.

In daily life, by contrast, God takes no pleasure in the strength of a man's legs (Psalm 147:10). In Deuteronomy 28 and Isaiah 47 His judgment strikes or exposes the very limbs people relied on. Together these uses point to dependence. God honors and sustains those who draw their life from Him, and He humbles those who stand on their own.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 7:32–34 with Exodus 29 — the thigh God takes and gives. "Also the right thigh you shall give to the priest as a heave offering from the sacrifices of your peace offerings" (Leviticus 7:32). The next verse names who received it: the priest "who offers the blood of the peace offering and the fat" (Leviticus 7:33). The man who did the holy work ate this portion. The ordination of Aaron adds a striking detail. The right thigh of "a ram of consecration" (Exodus 29:22) was not eaten. God's instructions direct that it be burned on the altar (Exodus 29:25), and Leviticus 8:25–28 records that Moses did so at the ceremony. The same instructions also give Aaron and his sons the thigh from Israel's future peace offerings "by a statute forever" (Exodus 29:27–28). That particular thigh went wholly to the altar, while the thigh of ordinary peace offerings became the priests' regular portion. So when a priest ate the thigh, he was eating from what belonged to the Lord. The peace offering was a meal of fellowship, and God's servants sat at it as His guests. Numbers 6:20 applies the same provision when a Nazirite completed his vow, and Numbers 18:18 compares the priests' share of the firstborn animals to "the right thigh."

1 Samuel 9:23–24 — honor at the table. On the day before Saul's anointing, Samuel told the cook to bring the portion he had ordered set apart, and the cook set "the thigh with its upper part" before Saul. Samuel said, "It was set apart for you. Eat; for until this time it has been kept for you." This was a feast portion Samuel had reserved for his guest; the narrative does not identify it with the priests' right thigh of the peace offering. The young man from Benjamin, who had come out only to look for lost donkeys, found the place of honor already reserved for him. The anointing itself came the next morning, when Samuel poured oil on his head (1 Samuel 10:1). The specially reserved serving honored Saul before Samuel anointed him. Read in light of what the LORD had told Samuel the day before Saul arrived (1 Samuel 9:15–16), Samuel's deliberate honor can also be seen as a quiet sign that Saul was the man God had chosen.

Psalm 147:10–11 — the legs God does not delight in. "He takes no pleasure in the legs of a man." Strong legs carried the soldier, the runner and the laborer. They stood for everything a man could accomplish on his own. Verse 11 tells us where God's pleasure actually rests: "The LORD takes pleasure in those who fear Him, in those who hope in His mercy." Psalm 33:16–17 says the same about armies and horses. Isaiah 40:30–31 adds that even young men fall, while "those who wait on the LORD... shall walk and not faint." Strength that lasts is renewed strength, and it comes from waiting on God.

Isaiah 47:2 with Deuteronomy 28:35 — strength stripped and struck. Babylon had once been called tender and delicate (Isaiah 47:1). Now she hears: "Take the millstones and grind meal. Remove your veil, take off the skirt, uncover the thigh, pass through the rivers." The proud queen is reduced to a slave grinding grain and wading across rivers with her legs bare and her shame exposed. Deuteronomy 28:35 warns faithless Israel that "the LORD will strike you in the knees and on the legs with severe boils which cannot be healed." In both passages God's judgment reaches the very place where people stand and walk.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The priests' portion carries into the New Testament. Paul draws directly on it: "Do you not know that those who minister the holy things eat of the things of the temple, and those who serve at the altar partake of the offerings of the altar? Even so the Lord has commanded that those who preach the gospel should live from the gospel" (1 Corinthians 9:13–14). The God who fed Aaron's household still provides for those who serve His Word.

The New Testament also calls Christ our peace and our sacrifice. Hebrews says believers have an altar from which they eat (Hebrews 13:10). Read in this light, the peace offering, shared between God and His priests, finds its deepest meaning in Christ, whose self-offering brings us to God's table. Believers are now called a priesthood, and they are fed by the sacrifice of the One who made peace.

Song of Solomon 5:15 belongs to a love poem, and its first meaning concerns a bridegroom praised by his bride. Christians have long read the Song in light of Christ and His church, and this verse rewards that reading. "His legs are pillars of marble set on bases of fine gold" uses the language of a grand building with precious foundations. Psalm 147 says God takes no pleasure in a man's legs, yet the King's legs are glorious and do not give way. John's vision of the risen Christ carries a similar picture: "His feet were like fine brass, as if refined in a furnace" (Revelation 1:15). This is a connection of imagery rather than stated fulfillment, but it holds together. The One we lean on does not stumble.

A related image appears at the cross, though the Greek word for leg there is different. The soldiers broke the legs of the men crucified with Jesus, but they did not break His. John saw in this the fulfillment of Scripture that not one of His bones would be broken (John 19:31–36). The Lamb of God went to His death whole.

7. Spirit & Life

These passages expose a habit most of us have. We like to stand on our own legs. We trust our stamina, our competence and our ability to keep going. Psalm 147 does not say God despises bodily strength. It says His pleasure is somewhere else, with people who hope in His mercy. That turns the question of how strong we are into the question of whom we are hoping in.

Proverbs 26:7 adds a searching picture: "Like the legs of the lame that hang limp is a proverb in the mouth of fools." The legs are there, but they cannot carry weight. A fool can quote wise words without being able to walk in them. Knowing the right verse is not the same as living it, and God means His wisdom to move us forward.

The priestly portion suggests where that strength comes from. The priest who served at the altar was fed from the altar, and his portion came from what God had first claimed as His own. Eating the thigh did not pass the animal's strength to him, and Leviticus never suggests it did. As a pastoral analogy, though, the pattern fits the gospel. Believers do not walk in holiness by gripping their own resolve. They are sustained by feeding on Christ, the sacrifice who made peace. God feeds His servants from His own portion. People who have been humbled out of trusting their own legs can stand on the One whose legs are pillars of marble.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 29:27 — God directs Moses to consecrate the breast and thigh from the ram of consecration, and the next verse makes these the priests' lasting portion from Israel's peace offerings.

  • Leviticus 9:21 — On the eighth day, when Aaron first offers sacrifices as priest, he waves the breasts and right thigh before the Lord as Moses commanded.

  • Leviticus 10:14–15 — Right after the deaths of Nadab and Abihu, Moses confirms, as the Lord had commanded, that the breast and thigh belong to Aaron's family, to be eaten in a clean place. His provision continues even in a time of grief.

  • Numbers 6:20 — The thigh of the heave offering is "holy for the priest" when a Nazirite completes his vow.

  • Judges 15:8 — Samson strikes the Philistines "hip and thigh," a picture of complete defeat.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3409 (yarek, thigh, loins) — A distinct Hebrew word that English often also translates "thigh." It is paired with shôwq in the idiom of Judges 15:8. It is the word used in oath-taking and in Jacob's wrestling, and its uses should not be merged with this word.
