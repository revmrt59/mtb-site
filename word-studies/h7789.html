BEHOLD, SEE, PERCEIVE, LURK - (shûwr, Strong's H7789)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shûwr means to see. More exactly, it means to gaze, watch closely, or keep someone or something in view. It is a rare and mostly poetic word. Most of its uses come from the book of Job, with the rest in Balaam's oracles, the Song of Solomon, Jeremiah, and Hosea. The NKJV renders it in several ways: "behold," "see," "perceive," "regard," "observed," and even "lurk." That range shows how much depends on who is watching and why.

Three uses stand out. Balaam, a hired diviner, is given a sight far beyond his own: "I behold Him, but not near; A Star shall come out of Jacob" (Numbers 24:17). In Job, suffering people struggle to see God at work, and Elihu answers that "justice is before Him, and you must wait for Him" (Job 35:14). In Hosea, God uses this same verb for His own watching. First He lurks like a leopard against Israel's idolatry. Then He tenderly observes repentant Ephraim. Read together, these passages teach that our sight is limited, God's sight never fails, and what anyone truly sees of God's purposes comes as His gift.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shûwr is a Hebrew verb of sight. It appears almost entirely in poetry and prophecy, where Hebrew writers often place two lines side by side to say related things. In those paired lines, shûwr often stands next to the common verb for seeing, ra'ah. In Numbers 23:9, for example, Balaam says, "I see him, And from the hills I behold him." The second line, which uses shûwr, usually adds focus and weight to the first. This is why the word suits Job. That book is full of people straining to see God, being seen by Him, and passing from the sight of others in death.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning is to look at with attention. Context shows what kind of attention is meant:

1. To behold or gaze on something, near or far. Balaam beholds Israel's camp from the hilltops (Numbers 23:9).

2. To perceive or take notice. This sense refers to grasping something with the mind as well as the eyes: "Yet man does not perceive it" (Job 33:14).

3. To watch with intent, lie in wait, or lurk. This is the watching of a hunter or ambusher: "Like a leopard by the road I will lurk" (Hosea 13:7).

4. To regard or attend to. Here the word describes giving notice or a hearing: "Nor will the Almighty regard it" (Job 35:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The person doing the watching determines much of the word's force. When the wicked watch, shûwr describes predators. In Jeremiah 5:26 men "lie in wait as one who sets snares," and in Job 24:15 the adulterer waits for twilight, saying, "No eye will see me." When humans try to see God, the word often appears with a negative: man does "not perceive," and no one can "see Him" when He hides His face (Job 34:29). When God is the one watching, the same intensity can bring judgment or care. Job also uses the word to describe death. The dying person passes out of view, and the eye that sees him "will see me no more" (Job 7:8).

4. Theological Significance

These passages reveal an imbalance. Human sight is narrow, blurred by pain, pride, and mortality. God's sight is complete. The adulterer comforts himself that "No eye will see me" (Job 24:15), yet the whole book assumes that God sees what people hide. Even when people cannot see God, God is still watching them.

The word also shows that seeing God's purposes depends on His revealing them. Balaam had no spiritual standing of his own, yet he beheld the coming King. Elihu tells Job that God speaks in many ways "yet man does not perceive it" (Job 33:14). True perception of God's ways is received from Him, not achieved by human effort.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Numbers 23:9; 24:17 — Balaam beholds the Star. Balak hired Balaam to curse Israel. Standing on the rocks, Balaam "beheld" the camp below: "There! A people dwelling alone." By the fourth oracle, his gaze has moved far past the tents in front of him. He calls himself "the man whose eyes are opened... Who sees the vision of the Almighty" (Numbers 24:15–16), then says, "I see Him, but not now; I behold Him, but not near; A Star shall come out of Jacob; A Scepter shall rise out of Israel" (Numbers 24:17). The same verb that took in a desert campsite now reaches across centuries to a royal ruler. Balaam's will had not changed, since he had come to curse. God simply opened his eyes and made him a witness to a purpose no payment or curse could overturn.

Job 33:14; 34:29; 35:14 — When God cannot be seen. Elihu uses shûwr six times in his speeches (Job 33:14, 27; 34:29; 35:5, 13, 14). Three of those uses face the problem at the center of Job's suffering. God speaks, "Yet man does not perceive it" (33:14). When God "hides His face, who then can see Him" (34:29)? Then Elihu answers Job's complaint directly: "Although you say you do not see Him, Yet justice is before Him, and you must wait for Him" (35:14). Elihu does not promise that Job will understand his suffering soon. He points instead to what remains true while Job cannot see it. God's justice has not left His presence, and the right response to God's hiddenness is to wait.

Hosea 13:7; 14:8 — The leopard and the cypress. Hosea uses shûwr for God's own watching, and the contrast is startling. Against Israel's stubborn idolatry God says, "Like a leopard by the road I will lurk" (13:7). He pictures Himself as a predator waiting beside the path His people walk. One chapter later Ephraim renounces idols, and the same verb takes on a tender meaning: "I have heard and observed him. I am like a green cypress tree; Your fruit is found in Me" (14:8). God's attention to His people stays just as close in both passages. What changes is their response to Him, which turns that attention from a threat into watchful care.

Job 33:27 — The restored man faces others. In Elihu's picture of a sinner brought back to God, the man prays, is restored, and then "he looks at men and says, 'I have sinned, and perverted what was right, And it did not profit me'" (33:27). In the NKJV's reading, the man no longer hides. He looks others in the face and confesses openly. Some translators read the verb here as "sings" before men instead of "looks," so the exact image is uncertain. In either reading, the man restored by God declares his sin and God's mercy in front of other people.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Balaam's Star is the clearest line in this word's story. A Star and a Scepter from Jacob point to a coming royal ruler. Israel's hope later took that ruler to be the Messiah. Matthew tells of wise men following a star to the newborn King of the Jews. At the close of Scripture Jesus names Himself "the Root and the Offspring of David, the Bright and Morning Star" (Revelation 22:16). What Balaam beheld "not near," the church now knows by name.

Job's problem of the hidden God also finds its answer in Christ. Elihu could only say "wait for Him." The New Testament announces that the unseen God has made Himself known in His Son (John 1:18). Hosea's promise, "Your fruit is found in Me," looks ahead to Jesus' words about the vine and the branches. Fruitfulness comes from abiding in Him (John 15:4–5). The God who watches over His repentant people becomes the source of their life.

7. Spirit & Life

Job 35:14 describes a real condition of believers: they cannot see what God is doing. Elihu does not shame Job for that. He moves Job's attention from what Job cannot see to what remains true. Faith, as Hebrews 11:1 says, is "the evidence of things not seen." Waiting in this sense is not passive resignation. It is trust in God's character during the time before His purposes come into view.

Hosea teaches us to take God's attention seriously. His gaze rests on His people. To someone clinging to idols, that closeness is dangerous. To someone who says, "What have I to do anymore with idols?" the same closeness becomes the shade of an evergreen tree and a source of lasting fruit. Psalm 121:4 assures us that the One who keeps Israel "shall neither slumber nor sleep."

The Song of Solomon adds a picture of seeing in company with the beloved. The bridegroom calls his bride to come with him from Lebanon. He asks her to look from the peaks of Amana, Senir, and Hermon, "from the lions' dens" and "the mountains of the leopards" (Song of Solomon 4:8, paraphrased). Some translations read the verb as "depart" from those heights, which adds a sense of coming away from danger. On either reading the verse is an invitation. The bridegroom calls her to come with him, and the poem does not narrate their arrival or describe where she ends up. As an analogy, that call to come away with the beloved and look from a new height fits Paul's plain teaching: "If then you were raised with Christ, seek those things which are above, where Christ is" (Colossians 3:1). "Set your mind on things above, not on things on the earth" (3:2). Paul does not cite the Song there, so this is an analogy, not a stated fulfillment. What Colossians states outright is that believers have been raised with Christ and are to fix their minds where He is.

Finally, the restored man of Job 33:27 shows that grace changes how we face others. A person forgiven by God no longer has to avoid other people's eyes. He can look at them and tell the truth about his sin and God's mercy.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 7:8 — "The eye of him who sees me will see me no more." Job feels how quickly a life disappears from human sight.

  • Job 17:15 — Job asks who can see his hope. Despair has hidden it even from himself (paraphrased).

  • Job 20:9 — Zophar says the wicked man's place will not behold him anymore. Death removes him from the place he once filled (paraphrased).

  • Job 35:5 — Elihu tells Job to look at the heavens and behold the clouds, which are higher than he is. The point is how far God exceeds human reach (paraphrased).

  • Job 35:13 — "Nor will the Almighty regard it." God gives no hearing to empty talk.

  • Jeremiah 5:26 — Wicked men "lie in wait as one who sets snares." The word's predatory sense describes people who hunt their neighbors.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7200 (ra'ah, to see) — a canonical pairing, not a word-family relative. Ra'ah stands parallel to shûwr in Balaam's oracles, as in "I see Him, but not now; I behold Him, but not near" (Numbers 24:17).
