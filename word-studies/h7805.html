SEE, CATCH SIGHT OF, TAN - (shâzaph, Strong's H7805)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shâzaph means to catch sight of, look on, or gaze at something. It is one of the Bible's rarest words for seeing. It appears only three times, all in poetry, and each time a different created eye is looking. In Job 20:9 a human eye sees a proud man prospering and then cannot find him. In Job 28:7 the falcon, a bird famous for its keen sight, has never seen the hidden path the miner cuts to buried treasure. In Song of Solomon 1:6 the sun itself is the one gazing, and its stare has darkened a young woman's skin. The NKJV translates that last use as "tanned."

Together these uses teach something important about sight. The sharpest eyes in creation still have limits. Human eyes are fooled by the apparent permanence of the wicked. The falcon cannot see what the miner uncovers, and yet the miner, who brings hidden things to light, still has to ask where wisdom can be found. The sun's gaze leaves its mark on a young woman's skin, but it cannot decide her worth. Scripture sets these limited eyes beside the God who "understands its way" (Job 28:23) and who "looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). Real wisdom, real worth, and final justice are known only as God reveals them.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâzaph is a Hebrew verb found only in Job and the Song of Solomon. Both books are poetry, and Hebrew poets often chose an uncommon word to give a line more color. Standard Hebrew lexicons describe its sense as catching sight of something or scanning it with a searching look. Its rarity means each occurrence carries weight, and the setting of each verse matters greatly for how it should be read.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word describes a look that catches sight of something or rests on it intently. Two senses can be distinguished:

1. To catch sight of or see something, used of a person or a creature looking at what lies before it. Job 20:9 speaks of "the eye that saw him."

2. By poetic extension, to gaze on something so as to scorch or darken it, used of the sun. "The sun has tanned me" (Song of Solomon 1:6) pictures the sun looking on the woman until its gaze has marked her skin.

The second sense grows naturally from the first. The poet treats the sun as an eye whose stare leaves a mark.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Each occurrence has a different subject. In Job 20:9 the one looking is a human eye. In Job 28:7 it is the falcon, the creature whose sight was most admired. In Song of Solomon 1:6 the sun is pictured as looking. The word moves from human sight, to the keenest animal sight, to the sun high above the earth, and each step shows the limits of what created eyes can do.

The opening plea of Song of Solomon 1:6, "Do not look upon me," uses a more ordinary Hebrew verb for looking. She asks the others not to look at her; the text does not say how they had looked. The sun's gaze is the one described with shâzaph, and its gaze has actually changed her.

4. Theological Significance

Read together, the three uses show that created sight is real but limited. People and animals see, and the sun's heat has real effects, yet none of these eyes reaches what matters most. The human eye cannot hold on to the prosperity of the wicked. The falcon's eye cannot see the miner's hidden path, and even the miner who sees it cannot find wisdom there. The sun's eye can darken a person's skin, but it cannot see her worth.

Scripture sets these limits beside the sight of God. Job 28 turns from the falcon to the Creator: "God understands its way, And He knows its place" (Job 28:23). Samuel, looking at Jesse's sons, learned the same truth from a different side: "the LORD does not see as man sees; for man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). God's sight is complete where ours is partial, and it is also gracious. He sees the way of wisdom and shows it to us. He sees the person beneath the marks of labor, and He sees the end of the wicked while they still look secure.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Job 28:7–12, the falcon, the miner and the question of wisdom. Job 28 opens with a celebration of human skill. Miners find silver and gold. They bring up iron and copper from the ground. "Man puts an end to darkness, And searches every recess For ore in the darkness and the shadow of death" (Job 28:3). They dig shafts "in places forgotten by feet," hanging on ropes and swinging "to and fro" (Job 28:4). Underneath the fields that grow bread, they turn up sapphires and gold dust (Job 28:5–6). People can reach treasure no one else has seen.

The miner's path is hidden even from the sharpest creatures: "That path no bird knows, Nor has the falcon's eye seen it. The proud lions have not trodden it" (Job 28:7–8). Yet the miner reaches it. He overturns mountains, and "his eye sees every precious thing" and "What is hidden he brings forth to light" (Job 28:10–11). Human skill sees what the falcon cannot. Then the poem turns with its great question: "But where can wisdom be found? And where is the place of understanding?" (Job 28:12). That progression gives the passage its force. People can uncover what no animal sees, and still they cannot dig wisdom out of the earth. Wisdom is not one more hidden ore that a sharper eye or a deeper shaft will eventually reach. Scripture places wisdom with God, who "understands its way" (Job 28:23). He then speaks to us what we could never discover: "Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom, And to depart from evil is understanding" (Job 28:28). Wisdom comes to people as a word given by God. It is received through reverent trust and a turning from evil. No amount of human brilliance can find it on its own.

Song of Solomon 1:6, the sun's scorching gaze. The young woman says, "Do not look upon me, because I am dark, Because the sun has tanned me." She explains why: "My mother's sons were angry with me; They made me the keeper of the vineyards, But my own vineyard I have not kept." Her darkened skin records hard outdoor work she did not choose. The sun has looked on her for long days, and its look has marked her.

The verse before shows how she sees herself. "I am dark, but lovely, O daughters of Jerusalem" (Song of Solomon 1:5). She refuses to let the looks of others or the marks of the sun define her. Soon her beloved, the king of the Song, answers with his own delight, telling her she is fair (Song of Solomon 1:15). The sun's gaze darkened her, and she asked the daughters of Jerusalem not to look at her. Her beloved looks with love, and he sees beauty.

Job 20:9, the glance that finds nothing. Zophar speaks of the wicked man whose "haughtiness mounts up to the heavens" (Job 20:6). Zophar says such a man will vanish "like a dream" (Job 20:8), until "The eye that saw him will see him no more, Nor will his place behold him anymore" (Job 20:9). People caught sight of his power and assumed it would last. One day they look again, and he is gone.

Zophar used this truth wrongly. He applied it to Job as though every sufferer must be wicked, and Job rejects that conclusion. Yet the picture itself agrees with wider Scripture. The psalmist writes, "I have seen the wicked in great power, And spreading himself like a native green tree. Yet he passed away, and behold, he was no more; Indeed I sought him, but he could not be found" (Psalm 37:35–36). The human eye sees only a passing moment. God sees how the story ends.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The wisdom that no miner could dig up, though he saw what the falcon never saw, is finally revealed in a Person. The New Testament calls Christ "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24) and says that in Him all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden (Colossians 2:3). Job 28 leaves wisdom with God, who alone knows its place. The gospel shows God giving that wisdom to us in His Son.

The other two uses also point toward Christ. Those who labor under the sun receive a promise. The Lord keeps His people so that the sun will not strike them by day (Psalm 121:6), and in the new creation the sun will no longer beat down on those the Lamb shepherds (Revelation 7:16). The beloved's delight in the woman of the Song offers a picture of Christ's love for His church. Paul writes that Christ "loved the church and gave Himself for her," that He might present her to Himself "holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:25–27). His love does not wait for her to become flawless. He loves her and makes her clean. As for the wicked who appear permanent, Christ's return will settle what the human eye could never judge with certainty, since nothing is hidden from the eyes of the One to whom we must give account (Hebrews 4:13).

7. Spirit & Life

Most of us trust what we can see. We admire sharp minds and new discoveries. We also feel the heat of other people's stares, and we are troubled when proud people seem to prosper. Shâzaph speaks to each of these.

The falcon in Job 28 does not need to be humbled, but we do. Research, skill, and insight are real gifts, and Job 28 admires them. The miner sees what the falcon never saw, yet the way to wisdom is not found by looking harder. It is received when God speaks and we bow before Him in reverent fear. Bringing our questions to His word is not giving up on thinking. It is going to the One who knows where wisdom lives.

The woman in the vineyard says something many believers need to say: "I am dark, but lovely." Hard years leave marks, and some of those marks came from other people's anger, as hers did from her brothers. The sun's gaze is not the last word, and neither are the looks she dreaded from others. The One who looks at the heart sees what others miss, and Christ delights in those He has made His own.

When the wicked seem to flourish, Job 20:9 and Psalm 37 steady us. Our eyes catch only a moment of the story. God sees the whole of it, and we can rest our sense of justice in His sight instead of in our own.
