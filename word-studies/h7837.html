DAWN, MORNING, DAYBREAK - (shachar, Strong's H7837)

Summary

The Hebrew word shachar means the dawn: the first light breaking over the horizon as night gives way to day. Most English Bibles translate it as "morning," "day," "dawn," or "daybreak." In the narrative books, the word often marks the moment when a long night ends and God's purpose moves forward. The angels hurry Lot out of Sodom at dawn. Jacob wrestles until the day breaks and leaves with a new name. Israel rises at daybreak to march around Jericho on the seventh day.

The poets and prophets enlarge the picture. Dawn is a vast force that only God commands (Job 38:12). It is a picture of His sure coming to His people: "His going forth is established as the morning" (Hosea 6:3). It is the light that breaks out over those who show mercy (Isaiah 58:8). It is the rising beauty of the bride (Song of Solomon 6:10). The same sudden daybreak that brings rescue also brings judgment on the proud, and Babylon's king, mocked as "son of the morning," is thrown down (Isaiah 14:12). David refuses to wait passively for daylight and sings, "I will awaken the dawn" (Psalm 57:8). The New Testament takes up the image of dawn for Jesus Himself, the "Dayspring from on high" (Luke 1:78) and "the Bright and Morning Star" (Revelation 22:16).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shachar is a Hebrew noun for the earliest light of day. It is narrower than the more common Hebrew word for "morning" (boqer), which can refer to the whole morning period. Shachar names the threshold itself, the edge of the night where light first appears. It occurs in historical narrative, in Job and the Psalms, in the Song of Solomon, and throughout the prophets.

Hebrew poetry sometimes speaks of the dawn as though it were a living figure. The dawn has "eyelids." Job 41:18 says Leviathan's eyes "are like the eyelids of the morning," and Job 3:9 uses the same Hebrew phrase, which the NKJV renders "the dawning of the day." The dawn also has a "son" (Isaiah 14:12). These are poetic pictures, and they never present the dawn as a god. In Scripture the dawn is a creature that waits for its orders.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. The literal dawn or daybreak. This is the moment first light appears and night turns to day. Joshua 6:15 records that Israel "rose early, about the dawning of the day."

2. Dawn as a figure of light, rising, or beginning. The word can stand for illumination or for the point where something starts. Isaiah 8:20 says of false teachers that "there is no light in them." Isaiah 47:11 uses the word for the hidden start of a disaster: "You shall not know from where it arises."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

A turning point in the story. In the narrative books, dawn often marks the moment when delay ends and action begins. When the morning dawned over Sodom, the angels pressed Lot to hurry, and judgment followed within hours (Genesis 19:15–24). At Jericho, the seventh day began at daybreak, and it was the day of victory (Joshua 6:15).

A force under God's control. In poetry, the dawn is great and wide. It spreads over mountains and reaches the ends of the earth. Yet it is never independent of God. Amos describes the LORD as the One "who... makes the morning darkness" (Amos 4:13).

A picture of rising beauty. Song of Solomon 6:10 asks, "Who is she who looks forth as the morning, Fair as the moon, Clear as the sun, Awesome as an army with banners?" The images grow brighter as the verse moves: first dawn, then moon, then full sun. The bride's beauty appears the way morning does, first faintly and then until it cannot be missed.

An ironic use for judgment. Isaiah 47:11 turns the usual expectation upside down. In Scripture, morning is often the time when what happened in the night is uncovered. Babylon, however, will face a ruin whose "dawn" no one can see coming. In effect, it will be a dark dawn, sudden but without warning light.

One meaningful construction. In Psalm 57:8 and Psalm 108:2, the dawn is the object of the singer's action: "I will awaken the dawn." Normally the morning wakes people up. Here David speaks as though his praise will wake the morning.

4. Theological Significance

The dawn shows that God rules creation, including time itself. No person can delay the sunrise or hurry it, but God can, and the dawn keeps its place because He has assigned it one. His presence also reaches farther than the dawn's light. David imagines taking "the wings of the morning" (Psalm 139:9), traveling as fast as first light streaks across the sky to the farthest sea. Even there, he finds that God's hand is already present to lead and hold him (Psalm 139:10).

Because God governs the dawn, its certainty becomes a way of speaking about His certainty in both mercy and judgment. Joel describes the day of the LORD coming "Like the morning clouds spread over the mountains" (Joel 2:2). The Hebrew pictures dawn racing across the hills, which no one can hold back. Hosea announces, "At dawn the king of Israel Shall be cut off utterly" (Hosea 10:15). For Bethel, the morning that should have brought light brought final ruin.

The same daybreak means rescue for some people and exposure for others. In the end, the dawn reveals what was hidden in the night.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 32:24–30 — Jacob wrestles until daybreak. Jacob spent his life grasping. He seized his brother's birthright, deceived his father, and outmaneuvered Laban. Alone by the Jabbok, a Man wrestled with him "until the breaking of day." As dawn came, the Man asked to be released. Jacob clung to Him and refused to let go without a blessing. At daybreak Jacob received a new name, Israel, and an injured hip. He named the place Peniel, because he had seen God face to face and lived. His night of struggle ended at dawn with both blessing and weakness. "The sun rose on him, and he limped on his hip" (Genesis 32:31). The text does not say how long the limp lasted, but as the sun rose Jacob carried blessing and weakness together.

Hosea 6:1–4 and Isaiah 58:6–8 — God's sure coming and light that breaks out. In Hosea 6, Israel calls itself to return: "Let us pursue the knowledge of the LORD. His going forth is established as the morning." The statement about God is true. He comes as reliably as daybreak, and like rain on dry ground. God's reply in verse 4, however, exposes the people. Their faithfulness is like a morning cloud and early dew that disappear. The contrast is sharp and instructive. God's faithfulness is as fixed as the dawn, while Israel's loyalty evaporates by midmorning.

Isaiah 58 shows what genuine return looks like. Israel was fasting while ignoring the oppressed. God calls them instead to loose the bonds of wickedness, share bread with the hungry, and take in the poor (Isaiah 58:6–7). Then comes the promise: "Then your light shall break forth like the morning, Your healing shall spring forth speedily, And your righteousness shall go before you; The glory of the LORD shall be your rear guard." When God's people turn toward mercy, His light breaks over them as surely as the sunrise.

Job 38:12–15 — The dawn under orders. God asks Job, "Have you commanded the morning since your days began, And caused the dawn to know its place, That it might take hold of the ends of the earth, And the wicked be shaken out of it?" God pictures the earth as a blanket. Each morning the dawn takes it by the edges and shakes out the wicked who worked under cover of night. Under the light, the earth "takes on form like clay under a seal." Every ridge and valley shows clearly again. Job had demanded answers, and God pointed him to a daily event that Job could neither order nor explain.

Isaiah 14:12 — The false son of the dawn. "How you are fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!" The chapter is a taunt song that Israel is to raise "against the king of Babylon" (Isaiah 14:4). The NKJV's "Lucifer" comes from the Latin word for light-bearer, an old name for the morning star. The Hebrew calls the king a shining one, literally son of dawn, using this word. Isaiah's taunt gives him that title to mock him as a bright morning star. A morning star shines brilliantly before daybreak and then fades when the true day arrives. The taunt pictures the king saying in his heart that he would ascend above the stars of God (Isaiah 14:13–14). Instead he was brought down to Sheol. Light that people claim for themselves does not last. Isaiah 8:20 makes a similar judgment about spiritual sources that people consult apart from God: "If they do not speak according to this word, it is because there is no light in them."

Psalm 57:8 — Worship that wakes the morning. The psalm's title links it to the time David "fled from Saul into the cave." He describes himself surrounded by people like lions and by men whose teeth are spears (Psalm 57:4). Still, he declares that his heart is steadfast, and he calls out: "Awake, my glory! Awake, lute and harp! I will awaken the dawn." The psalm does not record the hour of David's singing or the moment of his rescue. In poetry, though, he pictures his praise rousing the dawn itself, so that worship comes first and the morning follows. His praise rests on God's mercy, which reaches to the heavens (Psalm 57:10), rather than on any change in his circumstances.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

In many Old Testament passages, dawn is the moment God acts. The New Testament announces the dawn God's people had waited for. Zacharias praises "the tender mercy of our God, With which the Dayspring from on high has visited us" (Luke 1:78). He uses a Greek word that means a rising, like the sunrise, and he applies it to the coming of Messiah, who brings light to those sitting in darkness.

Isaiah's taunt called Babylon's king "son of the morning" in mockery, and he fell. Set beside that passage, Revelation gives the morning star title to Jesus in truth: "I am the Root and the Offspring of David, the Bright and Morning Star" (Revelation 22:16). The false star grasped at heaven and was cast down. The true Morning Star humbled Himself and was exalted.

Psalm 22, the psalm Jesus quoted from the cross, carries the title "The Deer of the Dawn." The meaning of that musical direction is uncertain. Even so, the psalm moves in a way that fits its title. It begins with a hunted sufferer crying through the night, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (22:1–2). It ends with the nations turning to the LORD. The Gospels place the empty tomb at early dawn on the first day of the week.

Psalm 110:3 uses a closely related Hebrew word and pictures the Messiah-King's people as volunteers who appear "from the womb of the morning." They are fresh and countless, like dew born at daybreak.

The New Testament also places believers between night and morning. Paul writes, "The night is far spent, the day is at hand" (Romans 13:12). Peter tells believers to heed the prophetic word "as a light that shines in a dark place, until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts" (2 Peter 1:19). In light of these passages, a sunrise can remind believers of the final morning when Christ returns.

7. Spirit & Life

The dawn obeys God, so it can teach us to trust Him. No night has ever kept the sun from rising, and Hosea says God's coming to His people is just as fixed. That certainty brings comfort, and it also searches the heart. The problem in Hosea 6 was not God's faithfulness. It was Israel's devotion, which disappeared like early mist. A believer can rest in the sure dawn of God's mercy while asking Him for a love that lasts past the first heat of the day.

Jacob's night shows that some blessings arrive only at the end of a long struggle. Many believers know the experience of wrestling with God through the dark hours over a sin, a fear, or a loss. Jacob teaches us to hold on until daybreak. He also teaches us that we may walk into the morning limping, more dependent than before, and more blessed.

Isaiah 58 connects the breaking of the dawn to feeding the hungry and loosening burdens. People who wait for God's light while ignoring the oppressed may find the morning slow to come. Mercy shown to others is where God promises His light will break out.

Psalm 57 shows what faith does in the dark. It praises God before circumstances change. Worship in the dark does not deny the darkness. It declares who rules the morning. Peter's picture gives us our place in God's timeline: we hold the lamp of Scripture in a dark place and watch for the Morning Star.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 19:25 — The Levite's concubine was abused all night and released only as the day began to break. In this verse, dawn exposes one of Israel's darkest nights, when everyone did what was right in his own eyes.

  • 1 Samuel 9:26 — Samuel called Saul at daybreak, and that morning led to Saul's anointing as Israel's first king. The day of his calling began at dawn.

  • Nehemiah 4:21 — The builders worked on the wall from daybreak until the stars appeared, with half of them holding spears. Their faithful labor filled the whole day.

  • Jonah 4:7 — "But as morning dawned the next day God prepared a worm." God controls even the timing of the dawn as He teaches His reluctant prophet about mercy.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H1242 (boqer), morning — This is a distinct word paired with shachar in Job 38:12: "Have you commanded the morning since your days began, And caused the dawn to know its place." There "morning" renders boqer and "dawn" renders shachar. Boqer covers the broader morning, while shachar names its first breaking.

  • H0216 (or), light — This is a distinct word paired with shachar in Isaiah 58:8: "Then your light shall break forth like the morning." There "light" renders or and "morning" renders shachar.

  • H4891 (mishchar), a related Hebrew form for "dawn" — Psalm 110:3 uses a related form in "the womb of the morning." This is a lexical relative of shachar, not an occurrence of H7837 itself.
