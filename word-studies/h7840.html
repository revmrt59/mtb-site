DARK, BLACKISH - (shᵉcharchôreth, Strong's H7840)

Summary

The Hebrew word shᵉcharchôreth means dark or dark in complexion. It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 1:6. There a young woman stands before the "daughters of Jerusalem" and pleads, "Do not look upon me, because I am dark, Because the sun has tanned me." She explains her dark skin by the sun and by the outdoor work her angry brothers forced on her in the vineyards. Meanwhile, as she puts it, "my own vineyard I have not kept."

Because the word occurs only once, its whole meaning lives in that one scene, and the scene says a great deal. The woman feels exposed and asks not to be looked at. Yet the king has already drawn her near, and her beloved soon calls her beautiful. Christians have long read this as a picture of grace: Christ delights in people weathered and worn by life in a fallen world, and His love outweighs their fear of being seen. The verse can also serve as a gentle warning against meeting everyone else's demands while neglecting our own souls.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shᵉcharchôreth is a Hebrew adjective in the feminine form, used here of a woman. It belongs to the same family as shachor, the ordinary Hebrew word for "black" or "dark." Shachor appears one verse earlier, where she says, "I am dark, but lovely" (Song of Solomon 1:5).

In shᵉcharchôreth, part of the root is doubled. Hebrew sometimes uses this kind of doubling to soften a quality, giving blackish or somewhat dark. Some scholars read it as intensifying the quality instead, giving very dark. The context settles what matters most for this verse: the speaker herself names the sun as the cause of her darkness. That explanation belongs to the passage, not to the word's meaning.

In the ancient world, a sheltered, untanned complexion suggested a life of leisure and privilege. Sun-darkened skin marked someone who worked outdoors. Her words to the women of Jerusalem seem to carry that social contrast between the field worker and the royal court.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shᵉcharchôreth has one sense:

1. Dark, or dark in complexion. In Song of Solomon 1:6 it describes a woman who goes on to explain her dark skin by the sun and her forced labor in the vineyards.

The word itself carries no moral meaning. It does not mean "sinful" or "defiled." It names a visible physical condition, and the poetry around it supplies its emotional and spiritual weight.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word sits inside a carefully built pair of verses. In Song of Solomon 1:5 she makes a bold claim: "I am dark, but lovely." She compares herself to "the tents of Kedar," which were known for dark goat-hair coverings, and to "the curtains of Solomon," which were royal finery. The verse does not say which quality each image represents.

Song of Solomon 1:6 moves from confidence to explanation, and her tone becomes more self-conscious: "Do not look upon me." Shᵉcharchôreth is the word she uses to describe herself as she shrinks from the women's gaze.

The Hebrew verb behind "tanned" may add a further touch. That verb elsewhere means to catch sight of (Job 28:7). If that sense carries over here, her words amount to something like Do not look at me, because the sun has looked at me. That is an explanatory rendering, not the NKJV's wording, but it captures the wordplay. She has already been gazed at long by something harsh, and she does not want to be examined again.

4. Theological Significance

This single word opens a window onto how God's love meets human shame. The woman is conscious of what hard work has left on her, and she shrinks from being looked at. The Song sets her plea between welcome and praise: the king has already brought her in, and her beloved soon calls her beautiful. This pattern, drawn from love poetry, fits what Scripture shows elsewhere of God's way with His people. His love is not reserved for those who have lived sheltered, unmarked lives. It reaches those who bear the heat of the day.

The verse also reveals something about the damage done by other people's demands. Her brothers' anger put her in the vineyards, and the sun did the rest. Scripture is honest that people can be worn down by burdens others place on them. The darkened skin is the visible result of an imbalance she did not choose. God sees that kind of weariness with compassion, not contempt.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 1:5–6: The marks of the vineyard. The full verse gives the setting in which the word is used: "Do not look upon me, because I am dark, Because the sun has tanned me. My mother's sons were angry with me; They made me the keeper of the vineyards, But my own vineyard I have not kept."

She attributes her complexion to field labor under the sun. That labor was imposed by "my mother's sons," who "were angry with me." The verse does not explain their anger, but it makes clear that the work was laid on her by her own family.

The final line, "my own vineyard I have not kept," is a play on words. Literally, she tended the vineyards her brothers put her in charge of, not her own. Figuratively, her "own vineyard" is most naturally read as herself: her person, her beauty, her life. Keeping other people's vineyards cost her the care of her own, and her sun-darkened skin is the sign she points to.

Song of Solomon 1:4 and 1:15: Welcomed and praised. Her shame does not have the last word. Just before this, she has said the king has brought her into his chambers (Song of Solomon 1:4). So her plea "Do not look upon me" comes from someone who has already been welcomed. A few verses later, her beloved tells her that she is fair, that she is beautiful (Song of Solomon 1:15). He delights in her as she is. The same woman who asked not to be looked at is looked at with delight.

The order matters. She is received first, and the declaration of her beauty follows. Her fear of being looked at is real, but the beloved's words show how he actually sees her.

Song of Solomon 8:11–12: The vineyard image returns. Near the end of the Song, the vineyard image comes back. Solomon's vineyard is leased to keepers, but the speaker, most often read as the woman, says, "My own vineyard is before me" (Song of Solomon 8:12). The Song does not say what happened between these two scenes. Set beside 1:6, though, the two verses make a striking literary comparison: the woman who once said she had not kept her own vineyard is answered near the close by a voice that speaks of "my own vineyard" as "before me," within a secure love.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament does not quote Song of Solomon 1:6. Still, Scripture's larger picture of Christ as Bridegroom and His people as bride makes this scene a fitting echo. Paul writes that "Christ also loved the church and gave Himself for her, that He might sanctify and cleanse her with the washing of water by the word, that He might present her to Himself a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing, but that she should be holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:25–27).

The two passages illuminate each other, but they speak of different things. The Shulamite's dark skin is not a flaw; the Song calls her lovely as she is. The spots and wrinkles in Ephesians are a picture of sin, and Christ deals with them by giving Himself for His bride, cleansing her, and presenting her to Himself glorious.

The two passages share the Bridegroom's love, but each has its own order. In the Song, the woman has already been brought into the king's chambers (Song of Solomon 1:4) before her beloved speaks his praise (Song of Solomon 1:15). In Ephesians, Christ first gives Himself for the church, and her cleansing and presentation follow. As a theological application, a believer's fear of being seen is answered by Christ's love, shown in His self-giving.

7. Spirit & Life

Many believers know the Shulamite's plea from the inside. Years of hard circumstances, other people's anger, failure, and sheer fatigue leave marks. When we come near to God, the instinct is to say, "Do not look upon me." Applied to Christ, the Song's pattern of welcome and praise shows that instinct to be a misreading of Him. In Christ we have been brought near (Ephesians 2:13), and He looks at us with love. Coming to Christ means letting His love outweigh our fear.

The vineyard line carries a second, quieter lesson. "They made me the keeper of the vineyards, But my own vineyard I have not kept." One can work hard for others, even in God's service, while one's own soul grows dry.

Martha's story shows this. She "was distracted with much serving," and Jesus answered her, "Martha, Martha, you are worried and troubled about many things. But one thing is needed, and Mary has chosen that good part" (Luke 10:40–42). Martha's serving was not wrong. But it had crowded out sitting at His feet. A weathered soul often comes from the same imbalance: tending every vineyard but the one entrusted to us.

The Song also speaks of small things that spoil vines: "Catch us the foxes, The little foxes that spoil the vines" (Song of Solomon 2:15). An unkept heart is rarely ruined in a day. Keeping our own vineyard means returning, again and again, to the Beloved who first drew us in. His love is where we find rest from the glare.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Shᵉcharchôreth occurs only in Song of Solomon 1:6, which is discussed fully above. The closely related word for "dark" in Song of Solomon 1:5 belongs to the same family and is treated in Sections 1, 3, and 9.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7838 (shachor), black or dark: a lexical relative from the same root. It is used in the verse just before, "I am dark, but lovely" (Song of Solomon 1:5), and together the two words frame her self-description.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The word describes dark skin, which the woman explains by the sun and her labor. It says nothing about ethnicity or about any group of people, and it should never be used to attach moral value to skin color. The Shulamite herself says she is "dark, but lovely." The Song affirms her beauty; by her account, her darkness marks her forced work.
