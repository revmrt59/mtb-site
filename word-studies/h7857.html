OVERFLOW, RINSE, WASH AWAY - (shâṭaph, Strong's H7857)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shâṭaph describes water in motion that carries things away. In ordinary life it meant rinsing: a bronze pot or a wooden bowl was rinsed in water, and a bloody chariot was washed at a pool. Its larger meaning is the rushing flood that overflows its banks and sweeps everything before it. The prophets used that flood to picture invading armies and the judgment of God. The psalmists used it for trouble rising until it threatens to close over their heads.

Scripture also turns this word in surprising directions. God promises redeemed Israel that the rivers "shall not overflow" them as they pass through (Isaiah 43:2). The Song of Solomon declares that floods cannot "drown" love (8:7). In Ezekiel 16, God first tells an abandoned infant who pictures Jerusalem to live, and after she has grown and He has bound Himself to her, He washes the blood from her Himself (Ezekiel 16:6–9), and He promises to send peace into His city "like a flowing stream" (Isaiah 66:12). The force that destroys in one passage cleanses, preserves, and blesses in another, and in each case the outcome depends on whose hand directs the water.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâṭaph is a vivid water word from a land of dry riverbeds that could turn into roaring torrents within an hour of a storm. Its earliest uses are domestic and ceremonial. Leviticus requires that a bronze pot used for the sin offering "be both scoured and rinsed in water" (Leviticus 6:28), and that a wooden vessel touched by an unclean person "be rinsed in water" (Leviticus 15:12). An earthen vessel in the same situation had to be broken. Rinsing assumes that the defilement can be carried off by water.

Most of the word's uses appear in poetry and prophecy. More than half come from Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel, where the flash flood becomes a picture of power that nothing can resist.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic idea is water moving with force over or across something. Context determines whether that force is gentle or destructive.

1. To rinse or wash off: to remove something by running water over it, as with vessels and hands (Leviticus 15:11) or the blood on Ahab's chariot (1 Kings 22:38).

2. To overflow, flood, or sweep away: the surging of rivers, rain, or torrents, often used for armies and overwhelming disaster (Isaiah 8:8; Job 14:19).

3. To rush headlong: forceful forward movement without water, as when Jeremiah pictures a horse that "rushes" into battle (Jeremiah 8:6).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the subject is a person washing something, the word is quiet and practical. When the subject is a river, a storm, or an army, the same word becomes menacing. Daniel 11 uses it repeatedly for kings whose forces "overwhelm and pass through" (Daniel 11:10), describing military campaigns as rising waters.

In Ezekiel 13:11–13 the word describes the "flooding rain" God sends against a flimsy wall plastered with "untempered mortar." The wall stands for the false peace that lying prophets had promised. When God's storm hits it, the falseness of the peace becomes plain.

The psalms move the image inside. "I have come into deep waters, Where the floods overflow me," David cries (Psalm 69:2). There the flood describes the experience of a person going under.

4. Theological Significance

The prophets never present these floods as random. Isaiah 10:22 states that the destruction God has decreed will overflow with righteousness. The judgment sweeping over Israel is the measured outworking of God's justice, and Isaiah adds that a remnant will return through it. Isaiah 28:17 makes the same point with a builder's tools: "I will make justice the measuring line, And righteousness the plummet." The waters that overflow the hiding place of liars are released by a God who measures exactly.

The God who sends floods of judgment also sends floods of blessing. Psalm 78:20 remembers the rock in the wilderness: "the waters gushed out, And the streams overflowed." In that dry land the overflow meant survival. Isaiah 66:12 looks ahead to a restored Jerusalem: "I will extend peace to her like a river, And the glory of the Gentiles like a flowing stream." Isaiah 8 had described the nations coming as a flood to drown Judah. In Isaiah 66 the nations' glory streams into the city as a gift. God promises more than to end the destructive flood; He reverses its direction. Isaiah 48:18 shows what Israel had refused: "your peace would have been like a river." God's intended overflow was always peace.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Isaiah 8:8 and 28:15–18 — the flood no one can bargain with. Ahaz trusted Assyria instead of the Lord, so Isaiah warned that the king of Assyria would come like the Euphrates in flood: "He will overflow and pass over, He will reach up to the neck." A person in water to the neck is one surge from drowning. Twenty chapters later, Jerusalem's leaders boast that they have made a "covenant with death." They claim that when the overflowing scourge passes through, it will not reach them (Isaiah 28:15). God answers that hail will sweep away their refuge of lies, their agreement will be cancelled, and the scourge will trample them (28:17–18). The flood exposes every shelter except the one God provides.

Ezekiel 16:9 — washing the outcast. Ezekiel portrays Jerusalem as a newborn thrown into an open field. Her cord is uncut, she is unwashed, and she lies in her own blood (16:4–6). No one pitied her. God passes by and says to her, "Live!" (16:6). He makes her thrive, and she grows up (16:7). Then He passes by again, spreads His wing over her and enters into a covenant with her, so that she becomes His (16:8). Only then comes the washing: "Then I washed you in water; yes, I thoroughly washed off your blood, and I anointed you with oil" (16:9). God takes the initiative in both scenes. He gives the helpless infant life, and later He washes and anoints the young woman He has made His own. The ordinary rinsing verb of Leviticus becomes God's own hands at work. He does not wait for her to make herself presentable. He stoops to the filth and removes it Himself.

Psalms 69 and 124 with Isaiah 43:2 — preserved in the flood. The psalmists know the terror of the flood from the inside. David pleads, "Let not the floodwater overflow me" (Psalm 69:15). Psalm 124 looks back on a rescue and admits that if the Lord had not been on Israel's side, "Then the waters would have overwhelmed us, The stream would have gone over our soul" (Psalm 124:4). Isaiah 43 answers that fear directly. The promise is addressed to Israel, the people God created, formed and redeemed: "Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; You are Mine" (Isaiah 43:1). Then God says: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you; And through the rivers, they shall not overflow you" (43:2), and He grounds it in who He is, "the Holy One of Israel, your Savior" (43:3).

God says when, not if. He does not promise Israel a life without rivers. Israel crossed the Red Sea and the Jordan, and the rivers remained, but the waters did not prevail because God was there. The promise is His presence in the crossing, and His presence keeps the flood from doing its usual work. Believers rightly hear the pastoral force of this promise as a picture of God's presence with His people in trials. Their assurance, though, rests on the New Testament's own words. Christ promises, "I am with you always" (Matthew 28:20), and Paul says that nothing, not even tribulation, persecution or sword, "shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:39). Paul does not promise that no harm will come. He lists the very harms believers may suffer and says they cannot cut them off from Christ's love (Romans 8:35–37).

Song of Solomon 8:7 — what the floods cannot carry off. "Many waters cannot quench love, Nor can the floods drown it." Across Scripture, floods take away crops, soil, walls, armies, and hope. Here, in a song that has just called love "as strong as death" (8:6), love is named as something the flood cannot sweep away. The verse goes on to say that love cannot be bought: a man who offered all his wealth for it "would be utterly despised." Love that cannot be purchased also cannot be washed away.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Assyrian flood in Isaiah 8:8 fills "the breadth of Your land, O Immanuel." Even while Judah is going under, the land belongs to Immanuel, "God with us." The New Testament identifies Immanuel as Jesus (Matthew 1:23). Isaiah 43:2's promise to Israel, "I will be with you," finds its fullest expression in Immanuel, God with His people in Person.

In Isaiah 28, God's answer to the refuge of lies is a "precious cornerstone" laid in Zion, so that whoever believes will not act hastily (28:16). Peter and Paul apply this stone to Christ (Romans 9:33; 1 Peter 2:6). Jesus Himself told of two houses struck by the same flood. The house built on the rock stood, and the house on sand fell (Matthew 7:24–27). Jesus' parable uses Greek, not this Hebrew word, and it does not claim to summarize the prophets, but it runs strongly parallel to Isaiah 28 and Ezekiel 13. The flood will come, and only the foundation God has laid will survive it.

Psalm 69, with its overflowing floods, is one of the psalms the New Testament most often applies to Jesus (John 2:17; 15:25; Romans 15:3). The Son entered the deep waters that threatened David and went under them in His death. Because He went down into those waters for His people, He can promise that the waters will not overwhelm those who belong to Him. Paul also quotes Isaiah 10:22 to show that God preserves a remnant through judgment (Romans 9:27–28). In the cross, God's righteous judgment and His saving mercy meet in one Person.

7. Spirit & Life

Jeremiah 8:6 uses this word without any water at all. God listened for someone to say, "What have I done?" but each person turned to his own course like a horse that rushes into battle. A warhorse charging into the fight does not stop to consider its danger. Sin works with that kind of momentum, carrying a person forward and drowning out every warning. In the next verse, God notes that the stork knows its seasons while His people do not know His judgment (8:7). Repentance means pulling up in the middle of the charge and asking the question God was waiting to hear.

For those who have stopped and turned to Him, the word comes back full of comfort. Every flood a believer meets, whether grief, illness, persecution, or a crisis that rises "up to the neck," is a river through which Christ has promised to be with them. That promise does not mean the water will never hurt. The fear of drowning is real, and the psalmists say so openly. But a believer's safety rests on His presence and His unbreakable love, and the depth of the water does not change that.

Ezekiel 16:9 also corrects the instinct to clean ourselves up before coming to God. The infant could do nothing about her own blood. God spoke life to her when no one pitied her, and later He did the washing Himself. That is how grace still meets people, and it is why the love of God in Christ is the one thing the floods cannot drown.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 14:19 — "As water wears away stones, And as torrents wash away the soil of the earth; So You destroy the hope of man." Job speaks of slow erosion, a quieter kind of sweeping away that wears down a person over time.

  • 1 Kings 22:38 — "someone washed the chariot at a pool in Samaria, and the dogs licked up his blood." Rinsing Ahab's chariot fulfilled the word of the Lord exactly as Elijah had spoken it.

  • 2 Chronicles 32:4 — Hezekiah's people stopped "the brook that ran through the land" so Assyria's army would find no water. Here the word describes a running stream, and cutting off its flow became part of Jerusalem's defense.

  • Jeremiah 47:2 — Waters rising from the north become an overflowing flood over the Philistines. God's judgment by flood reaches beyond Israel to the surrounding nations.

  • Ezekiel 38:22 — God judges Gog with "flooding rain," hail, fire, and brimstone. The storm imagery of Ezekiel 13 returns on an international scale.

  • Daniel 11:22 — Forces are "swept away" before a coming ruler. Daniel shows the flood rising and falling with empires, all within the limits God has set.
