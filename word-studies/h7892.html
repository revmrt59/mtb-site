SONG, MUSIC - (shîyr, Strong's H7892)

Summary

The Hebrew word shîyr means "song." It can name a particular song, the act of singing, or the organized music of Israel's worship. The word itself is ordinary. Laban uses it for the farewell party he says Jacob denied him, and Isaiah uses it for a harlot's tune. Yet the Bible's most important uses gather around what God has done. Israel's first song to the LORD rises on the far shore of the Red Sea. Moses writes a song so that God's warnings will outlive his generation. The temple song begins when the burnt offering begins, and the psalmists sing a "new song" when God shows fresh mercy (Psalm 40:3).

The word also carries a warning. God refuses songs that cover injustice, and He silences them in judgment. The captives in Babylon cannot bring themselves to sing the LORD's song for their mockers. Across Scripture, a true song answers salvation, carries memory, and sustains pilgrims in the night. The story reaches its end in Revelation, when the four living creatures and the twenty-four elders sing a new song to the slain Lamb, who redeemed people to God by His blood.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shîyr is a common Hebrew noun related to the verb "to sing." It appears in law, history, wisdom, prophecy, and especially the Psalms, where it often stands in the titles above the psalms. Fifteen of those titles read "A Song of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134). In Chronicles and Nehemiah the NKJV often translates the word "music," "singing," or "musical," because it describes the trained Levitical ministry that David set up for the house of God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A song or poem meant to be sung, whether it celebrates, teaches, or mourns (Exodus 15:1; Deuteronomy 31:19).

2. Singing or music as an activity, including the organized music of worship and the instruments used for it, which the NKJV renders "musical instruments" (1 Chronicles 15:16; 2 Chronicles 5:13).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting decides what kind of song is meant. Some songs are celebration, such as the "songs" of Genesis 31:27. Some are love poetry: Song of Songs 1:1 calls its book "The song of songs, which is Solomon's," meaning the finest song of all. Some are liturgy, the scheduled Levitical service of 1 Chronicles 25:6–7. Some are testimony, such as David's song of deliverance in 2 Samuel 22:1.

The word can also turn dark. Isaiah 23:15–16 compares ruined Tyre to a forgotten harlot who takes up the harp and sings "many songs" to win back her customers. The image stands for the pull of a proud trading empire trying to draw the nations back into its markets. That does not make every ordinary song suspect. It does show that a song can serve allurement as easily as worship.

4. Theological Significance

God values song, and He treats it as a gift that answers His saving acts. For that reason He judges it by the life of the singer. Where song is offered as a substitute for righteousness, He rejects it. Where a people persist in rebellion, He removes it. Amos warns, "the songs of the temple Shall be wailing in that day" (Amos 8:3), and God promises to turn "all your songs into lamentation" (Amos 8:10). He says to Tyre, "I will put an end to the sound of your songs" (Ezekiel 26:13). Isaiah describes a land where people "shall not drink wine with a song" (Isaiah 24:9). The prophets measure desolation by silence. When song disappears, joy has been withdrawn from the land.

Song is also a way God keeps His people. He uses it to fix His words in memory, to mark His deliverances, and to give His children something to hold in the dark. Psalm 42:8 says, "in the night His song shall be with me." The song belongs to God and is given by Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 15:1: the first song to the LORD. The word first appears in Genesis 31:27, where Laban complains that Jacob slipped away before Laban could send him off "with joy and songs." The first song addressed to God comes after the sea closes over Pharaoh's army: "I will sing to the LORD, For He has triumphed gloriously! The horse and its rider He has thrown into the sea!" Israel did not sing before the crossing, while trapped between the water and the chariots. They sang after God had acted. Their greatest songs were born on the far side of deliverance, and the song of Exodus 15 names the God who saved them and what He did.

Deuteronomy 31:19–22, 30; 32:44: the song as witness. God tells Moses that after Israel enters the land, they will turn to other gods (Deuteronomy 31:16–18). So He commands Moses to write a song and teach it to the people as a witness against them. When trouble comes, the song will testify, because it will not be forgotten in the mouths of their descendants (31:21). A law code can sit unread on a shelf, but a song is carried in memory from parents to children. God placed His warnings, His faithfulness, and His case against His unfaithful people inside a song so that it would reach generations Moses would never see. The point is sobering: what God's people sing will one day either confirm their faithfulness or bear witness against them.

2 Chronicles 29:27: the song begins with the offering. When Hezekiah cleansed the neglected temple, the priests first offered sin offerings to make atonement for all Israel (2 Chronicles 29:21–24). Then, the chronicler records, at the moment the burnt offering began, the song of the LORD began with it, joined by the trumpets and the instruments of David. The timing is intentional. Temple music did not start on its own schedule. It started when the sacrifice was laid on the altar, and it continued until the offering was finished (2 Chronicles 29:28). Joy in worship rested on the offering God had appointed. Hebrews later states the principle: "without shedding of blood there is no remission" (Hebrews 9:22).

Psalm 40:3: the new song. The psalmist was drawn up out of a horrible pit and his feet were set on a rock (40:1–2). Then he says, "He has put a new song in my mouth— Praise to our God; Many will see it and fear, And will trust in the LORD." In Psalm 40 a new act of mercy prompts the new praise, and God Himself supplies the song. That does not mean every "new song" is tied to a recent personal rescue, or that a new song could not also be new music. The same call returns in Psalm 33:3, Psalm 96:1, Psalm 98:1, Psalm 144:9 and Psalm 149:1, and in Isaiah 42:10, which summons "the ends of the earth" to sing as the Servant brings justice to the nations. Praise cannot live only on old testimonies, because God keeps acting.

Amos 5:23 and Psalm 137:3–4: songs God refuses, and songs that cannot be sung. Israel's festivals were crowded and loud, but God said, "Take away from Me the noise of your songs, For I will not hear the melody of your stringed instruments." He wanted something else: "let justice run down like water, And righteousness like a mighty stream" (Amos 5:24). In the next chapter Amos describes the wealthy who "sing idly to the sound of stringed instruments" while the nation decays (Amos 6:5). That verse uses a different word but is a fitting thematic companion to Amos 5:23. In such settings music becomes a sign of a diseased soul, indulgence standing in for righteousness. Psalm 137 shows the other side. In Babylon the captors demand, "Sing us one of the songs of Zion!" The exiles answer, "How shall we sing the LORD's song In a foreign land?" The LORD's song would not become entertainment for their conquerors. Taken together, Amos and Psalm 137 show that the Lord's song belongs to a living relationship with Him. It cannot cover rebellion, and it cannot be performed on command.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The pattern of song after sacrifice is fulfilled at the throne. In Revelation 5:8 the four living creatures and the twenty-four elders fall before the Lamb, and "they sang a new song, saying: 'You are worthy... For You were slain, And have redeemed us to God by Your blood'" (Revelation 5:9). Their song celebrates the Lamb's redemption. Here the new song of the Psalms and the temple song that began with the burnt offering meet in one person, the Lamb who was slain. The church sings because the sacrifice is finished. Revelation 15:3 then explicitly joins the first song and the last: the victors standing on the sea of glass "sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb." The Exodus offers a strong biblical parallel: Israel sang beside one sea after God's great deliverance, and the final victors sing beside another.

The Psalms point the same way. Psalm 45 is titled "A Song of Love" for the king's wedding, and Hebrews 1:8–9 applies its words to the Son. The finest of songs, Solomon's "song of songs," has long been read in light of this marriage of the King and His bride. The Greek New Testament uses its own words for song, but the story it tells is continuous with these Hebrew songs.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 42 is a psalm of tears, and yet it says, "in the night His song shall be with me." The psalmist does not sing his way out of darkness by effort. The LORD gives the song and keeps it with him. Moses could even say, using a synonymous Hebrew word for song, "The LORD is my strength and song" (Exodus 15:2). In a night season the believer may hold a song of God's lovingkindness before the circumstances change.

The Songs of Ascents carry the same comfort for the road. They were most likely sung by worshipers going up to Jerusalem for the feasts. They speak of lifting eyes to the hills for help (Psalm 121), of gladness at going to the house of the LORD (Psalm 122), and of hope while waiting for the morning (Psalm 130). God gave pilgrims songs to sing together on the way up. Believers still travel toward His presence, and singing together keeps hope alive on the way.

Numbers 21:17 adds a picture from the wilderness. At a dry place where God promised water, "Israel sang this song: 'Spring up, O well! All of you sing to it.'" The song did not create the water. It greeted God's promised provision in faith while the leaders dug. Isaiah's picture fits alongside it: "with joy you will draw water From the wells of salvation" (Isaiah 12:3). Singing in faith is one way God's people receive what He has promised.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 5:12 — Deborah is roused to sing a song after the LORD's victory over Sisera. Here, as at the sea, the song follows God's deliverance.

  • 2 Samuel 22:1 — David speaks the words of a song on the day the LORD delivered him from all his enemies and from Saul. The body of Psalm 18 closely parallels this song, and its title names the same occasion.

  • Psalm 28:7 — David trusts the LORD, is helped, and praises Him "with my song." Trust, help, and song follow one another.

  • Proverbs 25:20 — Singing songs to a heavy heart is like taking away a garment in cold weather. A right song still needs the right moment and a loving heart.

  • Isaiah 26:1 — A future song in Judah celebrates a strong city whose walls are salvation.

  • Ezekiel 33:32 — The people treat Ezekiel like a lovely song: pleasant to hear, but never obeyed.
