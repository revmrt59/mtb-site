BEREAVED, ROBBED, CHILDLESS - (shakkûwl, Strong's H7909)

Summary

The Hebrew word shakkûwl describes someone who has lost their young. It is not the ordinary word for a woman who could never conceive. It describes a mother whose offspring have been taken from her. The NKJV renders it "robbed," "deprived," "bereaved," and "barren." In four places it pictures the loss of young: three times a mother bear robbed of her cubs, and once wives left bereaved of their children in Jeremiah's prayer. The bear image pictured a fury few would want to meet in the field. The other two uses, in the Song of Solomon, are negative: they describe a flock in which no ewe has lost her lamb.

Scripture uses this small word for large purposes. Hushai uses it to describe David's enraged soldiers, and Proverbs uses it to warn about the fool. Jeremiah uses it in a prayer for judgment on the families of those who plotted his death. In Hosea, God takes the image for Himself: "I will meet them like a bear deprived of her cubs" (Hosea 13:8). Yet the Song of Solomon uses the same word in the negative to describe a flock in which "none is barren." Taken together, the six uses move from desperate loss, through God's fierce response when Israel forgot Him, to a picture of life where nothing is missing.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shakkûwl is a Hebrew adjective that appears six times in the Old Testament. It belongs to the family of the verb shakal (H7921), which means to be bereaved or to lose children. That verb appears in God's warning in Leviticus 26:22 that wild beasts "shall rob you of your children."

The word appears in a narrow range of settings: war counsel, wisdom sayings, prophetic judgment, and love poetry. In the first three settings it describes the condition of having lost offspring, whether the mother is a bear or a woman. The Song of Solomon specifically denies that condition: no ewe in its flock has lost her lamb.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning of shakkûwl is bereaved of offspring. The word assumes that young once existed and were then taken away. Two closely related uses appear:

1. Robbed of young, said of an animal, especially a mother bear whose cubs have been taken. Example: "a bear robbed of her cubs" (Proverbs 17:12).

2. Bereaved of children or lambs, said of a woman who has lost her children or of a ewe that has lost its lamb. Examples: Jeremiah 18:21, and in the negative, Song of Solomon 4:2.

The NKJV word "barren" in the Song should be read in light of this meaning. The point is that no ewe in the flock has lost a lamb.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The bear image dominates the word's use. In 2 Samuel 17:8 and Proverbs 17:12, the bereaved bear stands for raw, unreasoning danger. In Hosea 13:8, the same image is applied to God. This shifts the word from describing a threat in the natural world to describing divine judgment.

When the word describes people, as in Jeremiah 18:21, the loss is the destruction of a family line. The wives become "widows and bereaved of their children," so the household has lost both its present and its future.

One construction changes the picture entirely. In Song of Solomon 4:2 and 6:6, the word is negated: "none is barren among them." Because the word speaks of loss, saying that it is absent describes completeness. Every ewe bears twins, and every pair is intact.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God does not treat the rejection of His love with detachment. The bereaved bear was one of the most frightening images Israel knew for fury in the field. When God chooses that image for Himself, together with the lion and the leopard of the same passage (Hosea 13:7–8), the first thing it communicates is the severity of His judgment: sudden, violent and impossible to escape. Because the image is of a mother who has lost her young, readers may also hear in it a note of wounded love. Hosea does not spell that out, and God's grief should not be pictured as the same as an animal's, but the prophet's wider message shows a God who is personally bound to His people and responds personally when they abandon Him.

The word also shows that loss in Scripture is tied to the world's rebellion against God. Leviticus 26:22 names wild beasts robbing parents of children as a consequence of persistent disobedience. Jeremiah's prayer echoes that background. In Scripture, bereavement is grief, and it is also a visible sign of a world in which life is being taken away.

Against that background, the Song's "none is barren" offers a picture of life as God made it to be: fruitful, whole, and unbroken.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Hushai's warning and the proverb (2 Samuel 17:8; Proverbs 17:12). After Absalom seized the throne, Hushai, who was secretly loyal to David, urged him not to pursue his father quickly. He warned that David's men "are enraged in their minds, like a bear robbed of her cubs in the field." Hushai's stated point was that David and his men were "mighty men," seasoned fighters led by "a man of war," and that their rage made them as dangerous as a mother bear robbed of her cubs. The loss belongs to the bear in the picture; what Hushai stresses about David's men is their fury and their skill in battle. Proverbs 17:12 takes this well-known danger and ranks it: "Let a man meet a bear robbed of her cubs, Rather than a fool in his folly." The bear is terrible, yet the proverb says a fool in his folly is the greater danger. The warning is about how much harm folly can do to everyone who crosses its path.

God as the bereaved bear (Hosea 13:4–8). Hosea sets this image within a history of grace. The Lord reminds Israel, "I am the LORD your God Ever since the land of Egypt, And you shall know no God but Me; For there is no savior besides Me" (Hosea 13:4). He says He knew them "in the wilderness, In the land of great drought" (Hosea 13:5). Then He describes what went wrong: "When they had pasture, they were filled; They were filled and their heart was exalted; Therefore they forgot Me" (Hosea 13:6). His blessings became the occasion of their pride.

The judgment follows: "I will meet them like a bear deprived of her cubs; I will tear open their rib cage" (Hosea 13:8). Read alongside Proverbs 17:12, this is a sobering reversal. Proverbs says it is better to meet a robbed bear than a fool. Israel played the fool through pride and by forgetting the God who saved them, and the same chapter names their molded images and calf worship (Hosea 13:1–2). As a result, they met both the fool's ruin and the bear. The phrase "I will meet them" even echoes the proverb's "let a man meet."

What the image says directly is that God's judgment on this pride and forgetfulness will be terrible. Some readers also hear a further echo: a bear rages because what belonged to her has been taken, and Israel had turned from the God who had known them in the wilderness. That is a possible resonance of the picture, not something Hosea states, and it should not be pressed into a claim that idols stole Israel from God the way hunters steal cubs. The passage itself keeps the focus on Israel's own choice: "Therefore they forgot Me" (Hosea 13:6).

Jeremiah's prayer (Jeremiah 18:21). The people of Judah had plotted against Jeremiah and rejected the word he carried from God (Jeremiah 18:18). In response, Jeremiah asks God to bring the curses on them: famine, the sword, and wives "bereaved of their children." He does not take revenge himself. He hands his enemies over to the God whose warnings they had refused, warnings like those found in Leviticus 26. The prayer is severe, and it shows how seriously Scripture treats rejecting God's word: in this prayer, those who cut themselves off from God's word are pictured losing their future.

The Bride's smile (Song of Solomon 4:2; 6:6). The bridegroom compares his beloved's teeth to washed sheep, "Every one of which bears twins, And none is barren among them." The picture is of matched pairs with no gaps and nothing missing. A word that elsewhere describes rage and ruin is used here, by its absence, to describe flawless beauty. He sees her as whole.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The word follows a path that leads toward the gospel. Leviticus warns of bereavement, Jeremiah prays for it on those who reject God, and Hosea describes God Himself coming in that kind of fury. The same chapter of Hosea also contains a startling promise: "I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death. O Death, I will be your plagues! O Grave, I will be your destruction!" (Hosea 13:14). The setting is hard. The verse closes, "Pity is hidden from My eyes," and the chapter ends with Samaria's fall (Hosea 13:16). The hope of this verse is spoken in the middle of judgment, not instead of it. Paul takes up Hosea's death language in his proclamation of Christ's resurrection: "O Death, where is your sting? O Hades, where is your victory?" (1 Corinthians 15:55). His wording is not a word-for-word quotation of the NKJV Old Testament, but he hears in Hosea the final defeat of death that Christ's rising has accomplished.

Jesus also spoke over a people who were turning away from God. He used a parent's image too, though a gentler one, comparing Himself to a hen longing to gather her chicks (Matthew 23:37). At the cross, the Son bore the curse Himself (Galatians 3:13). The judgment Israel deserved fell on the One who came to bring them home. Seen in the light of the whole story, the Father, who had warned that His people would be robbed of their children, gave His own Son so that the lost might be restored.

The Song's picture also fits where the larger story is going. The Church is presented to Christ as His Bride, and Scripture's final vision is of a world without death or sorrow (Revelation 21:4). In that world nothing will be shakkûwl.

7. Spirit & Life

Hosea 13 reveals a danger that comes with blessing: "They were filled and their heart was exalted; Therefore they forgot Me." Israel did not forget God in the drought. They forgot Him at the full table. Believers today face the same risk, because comfort can slowly push gratitude aside.

The bereaved bear also corrects any idea that God's love is mild or indifferent. His anger over pride and idolatry and His tenderness toward His people come from the same commitment to them, and He will not give up His people quietly. That truth is a warning to anyone drifting from Him and a comfort to anyone who belongs to Him.

Jeremiah's prayer shows a righteous man under attack putting the matter in God's hands rather than taking revenge. In Christ, believers also learn to pray for their enemies (Matthew 5:44).

In the Song, the bridegroom praises his beloved as whole, comparing her teeth to a flock in which "none is barren." Christians may take a further step, from this human love to Christ's. Paul pictures Christ presenting the church to Himself "not having spot or wrinkle" (Ephesians 5:27), and read alongside that verse, the Song's image fits how Christ intends to present His people: complete.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All six occurrences of shakkûwl are discussed above: 2 Samuel 17:8, Proverbs 17:12, Jeremiah 18:21, Song of Solomon 4:2 and 6:6, and Hosea 13:8. Song of Solomon 6:6 repeats the wording of 4:2 almost exactly. The bridegroom's praise of his beloved has not changed between the two passages.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Lexical relative: H7921, shakal, the verb to be bereaved, to rob of children. Leviticus 26:22 uses it to name bereavement as a consequence of persistent disobedience, a background Jeremiah's prayer echoes.

  • Thematic connection: Lamentations 3:10 also compares God's judgment to a bear lying in wait, using different Hebrew wording. This shows that the bear was a known image of God's judgment.
