RISE EARLY, EARLY - (shâkam, Strong's H7925)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shâkam means to rise early or to make an early start. In the NKJV it nearly always appears as "early," usually in the familiar phrase "rose early in the morning." The word tells us when and how promptly something began. In the Bible's narratives it marks people who did not wait. Abraham set out at dawn with Isaac and the wood for the offering, and Joshua rose early to lead Israel to the Jordan and around Jericho. The same word also exposes eagerness in the wrong direction: Israel rose early to feast before the golden calf, and Isaiah condemned those who rose early to chase strong drink.

The most striking uses come in Jeremiah. There God applies the word to Himself and says again and again that He was "rising up early and sending" His prophets to a people who would not listen. A word drawn from the start of an ordinary workday becomes a picture of God's earnest, persistent pursuit of people He refuses to abandon. That pursuit reaches its fullest expression in Jesus Christ. It also searches the reader: whatever we rise early for shows what we treasure.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâkam is a Hebrew verb used mainly in narrative. It describes travelers setting out (Genesis 19:2), Abraham returning to the place where he had stood before the LORD (Genesis 19:27), David rising early on his father's errand (1 Samuel 17:20), and worshipers going up to God (1 Samuel 1:19). The verb is often joined to the word "morning," which gives the NKJV its repeated "rose early in the morning." Daylight mattered a great deal for work and travel. Fields were worked, journeys begun and battles fought while the sun was up, so the day's business commonly started at first light, and an early start was the natural way to show readiness.

Some lexicons, including Strong's, suggest that the verb is related to the Hebrew noun for shoulder, shekem, and that it may first have pictured loading the shoulder, or a pack animal, for the day's journey. That is a suggested derivation, not an established historical fact. If it is right, it would help explain the word's sense of readiness, but in actual biblical use the verb simply means rising or starting early.

The word also moves beyond narrative. Jeremiah uses it eleven times, nearly always of God. Hosea uses it in poetry for the "early" dew that disappears by midmorning.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To rise early in the morning, or to get up and set out at dawn. Abraham "rose early in the morning and saddled his donkey" (Genesis 22:3).

2. To act early in the sense of earnestly or persistently, especially when joined to another verb. God speaks of "rising up early and speaking" (Jeremiah 7:13), which means He spoke urgently and kept speaking.

3. Used as a description, "early" can describe something that appears in the morning, as with the early dew in Hosea 6:4. That the dew soon disappears comes from Hosea's surrounding words, "it goes away," not from this word itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In narrative the word usually reports the start of an action that the story considers important. What follows the early rising tells the reader why it matters. Jacob rose early and set up the stone at Bethel (Genesis 28:18). Moses rose early and carried the new tablets up Sinai (Exodus 34:4). Absalom rose early so he could catch people at the city gate and win their hearts away from his father (2 Samuel 15:2). The word itself carries no moral weight. Whether the early start is faithful or wicked depends on what the person rises to do.

One grammatical pattern changes the meaning noticeably. In Jeremiah and 2 Chronicles the word is paired with a second verb: "rising up early and sending," "rising early and speaking," "rising up early and teaching" (Jeremiah 32:33). In this pairing, "rising early" no longer marks the hour. It describes the way the second action was done: urgently, earnestly, again and again. Jeremiah 11:7 shows how close the meaning comes to "earnestly," because the NKJV renders the thought "I earnestly exhorted your fathers… rising early and exhorting."

4. Theological Significance

The word reveals something about God that might otherwise sound too bold to say: He takes the initiative with urgency. God neither sleeps nor needs a morning (Psalm 121:4). When He says He was "rising early," He is using human language to tell His people that He was never slow, careless or indifferent toward them. Second Chronicles 36:15 gives the reason directly: He sent His messengers "rising up early and sending them, because He had compassion on His people and on His dwelling place." The persistence comes from compassion.

The word also shows the human heart. The eagerness that should belong to God can be given just as readily to idols, pleasure and corruption. Zephaniah 3:7 describes a people who, after God's warnings, rose early and corrupted all their deeds. Sin is often a pursuit that people get up early for, not something they drift into. And in Hosea, devotion that appears "early" can be gone before the sun is high. Taken together, these uses set God's steady, early pursuit against human zeal that is misdirected or does not last.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Abraham at dawn (Genesis 21:14; 22:3). Twice Abraham received a command that tore at his heart, and twice the text says that he "rose early in the morning." Genesis 21 does not hide his distress. Sarah's demand to send Hagar and Ishmael away "was very displeasing in Abraham's sight because of his son" (21:11), and God had to reassure him (21:12–13). Once God had spoken, though, the next morning he gave Hagar bread and water and sent her and Ishmael away (21:14). In Genesis 22:3 he saddled the donkey, split the wood, and took Isaac toward Moriah. In both scenes, after God's word was given, the narrator records no delay, only the early start and the practical details. Abraham's faith shows in the order of his morning. The hardest obedience of his life was the first thing he did that day.

Joshua's mornings (Joshua 3:1; 6:12; 7:16; 8:10). Four times Joshua "rose early in the morning" at key turns in the conquest: to bring Israel to the Jordan, to send the priests with "the ark of the LORD" around Jericho, to bring the tribes forward when Achan's sin had to be exposed, and to lead the march against Ai. The pattern holds whether the task was a miracle, a painful act of judgment, or a second attempt after failure. Joshua met each assignment from God at the start of the day.

God rising early (Jeremiah 7, 25, 35). This is the word's richest use. God says He sent "all My servants the prophets, daily rising up early and sending them" from the Exodus "until this day" (Jeremiah 7:25). Jeremiah applies the same language to his own ministry: for twenty-three years, "I have spoken to you, rising early and speaking, but you have not listened" (Jeremiah 25:3). Chapter 35 adds a pointed contrast. The Rechabites still obeyed their ancestor Jonadab's command not to drink wine, "But although I have spoken to you, rising early and speaking, you did not obey Me" (35:14). A family kept a human father's word for generations, while Judah ignored the living God who kept calling. The message He sent so early was full of mercy: "Turn now everyone from his evil way, amend your doings, and do not go after other gods to serve them; then you will dwell in the land which I have given you and your fathers" (Jeremiah 35:15). Even at the edge of judgment His cry is personal: "Oh, do not do this abominable thing that I hate!" (Jeremiah 44:4). The repeated phrase shows that judgment, when it came, followed a long history of earnest pleading.

Dew that vanishes (Hosea 6:4; 13:3). Through Hosea, God says that Ephraim's and Judah's faithfulness is like a morning cloud and like the early dew that goes away. Dew looks like refreshment at dawn and is gone by midmorning. In Hosea 13:3 the same picture becomes a judgment: idolatrous Ephraim itself will pass away like the early dew. Repentance without roots does not last, and a people who will not last in their love for God will not last at all.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Jeremiah's "rising up early and sending" describes a sending that never stopped. Jesus told a parable about a vineyard owner who sent servant after servant to his tenants and finally sent his beloved son (Mark 12:1–8). That parable reads like Jeremiah's history carried to its conclusion. Hebrews 1:1–2 says the same thing plainly: God spoke through the prophets "at various times," and in these last days He has spoken by His Son. Jesus' lament over Jerusalem, that He often wanted to gather her children as a hen gathers her chicks and she was not willing (Matthew 23:37), shows the heart Jeremiah described now present in a human voice.

Jesus also fulfills the obedient side of the pattern. Mark records one particular early morning in Capernaum: "in the morning, having risen a long while before daylight, He went out and departed to a solitary place; and there He prayed" (Mark 1:35). Mark's account is in Greek, so this is a link of theme with Abraham and Joshua, not another use of the Hebrew word, and Mark does not say that every day of Jesus' ministry began this way. Still, the scene fits the pattern well. Jesus is both God's earnest sending and the Son who rose before dawn to seek His Father. Moriah's early morning, when Abraham took his son toward the place of sacrifice, finds its answer in the Father who did not spare His own Son.

7. Spirit & Life

Jeremiah's phrase should change how a struggling believer pictures God. A person who has ignored God's voice often imagines Him as tired of them, unwilling to speak again. Scripture describes Him as the One who kept rising early. Every renewed conviction, every sermon that strikes home, every verse that will not let go, is consistent with a God who has been calling "daily." His earnestness comes before our response.

The word also asks a searching question: what do we get up early for? Israel rose early for the calf, and the drunkards in Isaiah 5:11 rose early for strong drink. Our first energy and first thoughts show what we treasure. The psalmist's prayer fits Abraham's mornings: "I made haste, and did not delay to keep Your commandments" (Psalm 119:60).

Early rising is not a badge of holiness, though. Psalm 127:2 warns that rising up early and working late in anxious effort is vain, because the Lord gives His beloved sleep. The difference lies in why a person rises. Anxious striving gets up to secure what only God can give. Obedience gets up to answer what God has already said. Hosea adds that a bright start is not enough. God looks for love that is still there in the heat of the afternoon.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 32:6 — While Moses was on the mountain, the people rose early to offer sacrifices before the golden calf. They gave false worship the eagerness that belonged to God.

  • 1 Samuel 1:19 — Elkanah and Hannah rose early and worshiped before the LORD. Afterward the LORD remembered Hannah.

  • 1 Samuel 5:3–4 — Two mornings in a row the Philistines rose early and found Dagon fallen before the ark of the LORD.

  • 2 Chronicles 20:20 — The people rose early to go into battle, and Jehoshaphat urged them to believe the LORD and His prophets.

  • Job 1:5 — Job rose early to offer burnt offerings for his children in case they had sinned. His early start was a father's intercession.

  • 2 Kings 6:15 — Elisha's servant rose early, saw the enemy army surrounding the city, and that same day was shown the LORD's armies.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7926 (shekem, shoulder) — A suggested lexical relative. Some lexicons regard shâkam as formed from this noun, perhaps from loading up for the day's travel. This possible connection may explain the word's background, but it does not set its meaning in individual verses.
