DRUNK, MERRY, DRINK DEEPLY - (shâkar, Strong's H7937)

Summary

The Hebrew word shâkar means to be drunk or to drink until one is full. Sometimes it describes literal drunkenness, as when Noah drank wine from his vineyard and lay uncovered in his tent. Sometimes it describes glad, generous feasting, as when Joseph's brothers "drank and were merry with him." Most often, especially in the prophets, it becomes a picture of a person or nation overwhelmed, staggering and helpless under God's judgment.

The word's most important use is the prophets' image of the cup of God's wrath. Proud nations such as Babylon, Moab and Edom are made to drink until they stagger, vomit, fall, are exposed and sleep "a perpetual sleep." This background helps us hear what Jesus meant in Gethsemane when He prayed, "Take this cup away from Me," and then drank it in obedience to His Father. The same word also carries an invitation. In the Song of Solomon the beloved are urged to "drink deeply," and the question running through these passages is what will fill us: wine, pride, judgment, or the love of God.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâkar is a Hebrew verb. It belongs to a small family of words connected with intoxication, including the Hebrew terms for "strong drink" and "drunkard." It appears nineteen times. A few uses come from historical stories in Genesis and Samuel. Most come from poetry and prophecy, especially Isaiah and Jeremiah. In those books the word moves almost entirely from the banquet table to the battlefield and the courtroom of God.

In daily life, wine was ordinary at meals and celebrations. People in Israel also knew what heavy drinking did to a person. It loosened the tongue, weakened the legs, clouded judgment and left the drinker exposed and asleep. The prophets drew on these familiar effects to describe what happens to people under God's judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, shâkar describes drinking until the drink takes over. The word can be positive or negative, depending on what is being drunk and why.

1. To become drunk or intoxicated with wine, with the loss of control and judgment that follows (Genesis 9:21; 2 Samuel 11:13).

2. To drink one's fill, to be merry or deeply satisfied at a feast (Genesis 43:34; Song of Solomon 5:1; Haggai 1:6, where people drink but never reach that fullness).

3. Figuratively, to be overwhelmed or saturated until a person staggers or collapses. The cause may be God's judgment, spiritual confusion, or, in vivid poetry, blood (Jeremiah 25:27; Isaiah 29:9; Deuteronomy 32:42).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting decides the tone. At Joseph's table the word means warm, relieved celebration. In Noah's tent it means a shameful collapse. In Jeremiah it describes a sentence passed on whole nations.

One pattern deserves attention. Scripture often speaks of one person making another drunk. David makes Uriah drunk (2 Samuel 11:13). Habakkuk pronounces woe on the man who presses his bottle on his neighbor. God Himself says, "I will make them drunk" (Jeremiah 51:39) and "Made them drunk in My fury" (Isaiah 63:6). When the word takes this form, the reader should ask who holds the cup. In human hands, making someone drunk is usually a tool of exploitation. In God's hand, it is the administration of justice.

Isaiah adds a second pattern: drunk "but not with wine" (Isaiah 29:9; 51:21). Here the word describes the staggering itself without any alcohol involved. That shows how far the image had moved beyond the wine cup.

4. Theological Significance

These passages reveal a God whose judgment fits the sin. Habakkuk's woe falls on the man who intoxicates his neighbor to look on his nakedness, and the following verse hands the cup back to him. Edom rejoiced over Jerusalem's ruin, and Lamentations answers, "The cup shall also pass over to you." Moab is made drunk "because he exalted himself against the LORD" (Jeremiah 48:26). Pride is what lifts a nation up, and the cup is what brings it down. The arrogant drinker ends up wallowing on the floor.

The passages also show that God is the true host at every table. Psalm 75:8 places the cup "in the hand of the LORD," fully mixed, with dregs the wicked must drain. Nations that seemed to control history turn out to be drinking from His hand. Even the poetry of arrows and oppressors "drunk with blood" (Deuteronomy 32:42; Isaiah 49:26) conveys that His justice against unrepentant evil will be complete, with nothing left half-done.

The same God also decides who is satisfied. He can withhold fullness, as Haggai warns, and He can pour it out lavishly, as at Joseph's table. Both the emptiness and the fullness come from His hand.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 9:21 — drunkenness and uncovering. "Then he drank of the wine and was drunk, and became uncovered in his tent." Noah had just walked out of the ark as the man through whom God preserved the world. A vineyard and an evening of drinking left him unaware and exposed, and the episode ended in a curse on Canaan. Scripture repeats the link between drunkenness and nakedness. Habakkuk 2:15 condemns the one who makes his neighbor drunk "That you may look on his nakedness!" Lamentations 4:21 tells Edom, "you shall become drunk and make yourself naked." Drunkenness lowers a person's guard, and wicked people exploit that weakness. David tried the same thing with Uriah, getting him drunk so that he would go home and cover David's adultery. Even drunk, Uriah "did not go down to his house." A drunk man kept more loyalty than a sober king.

Jeremiah 25:27; 51:7, 39 — the cup of wrath. God sends Jeremiah to the nations with a cup and a terrible command: "Drink, be drunk, and vomit! Fall and rise no more." The verbs trace a humiliating sequence from drinking, to drunkenness, to vomiting, to falling, to never getting up. Then Jeremiah turns to Babylon: "Babylon was a golden cup in the LORD's hand, That made all the earth drunk. The nations drank her wine; Therefore the nations are deranged" (Jeremiah 51:7). The image reverses what a guest expects from a host. A golden cup promises honor and pleasure, yet this one hands the nations ruin. Babylon was God's instrument of judgment on others, and then it became the drinker itself. "I will prepare their feasts; I will make them drunk, That they may rejoice, And sleep a perpetual sleep And not awake." Babylon's last banquet becomes its grave. Jeremiah 51:57 repeats the sentence for Babylon's princes, wise men and warriors.

Isaiah 29:9; 51:21 — staggering without wine. "They are drunk, but not with wine; They stagger, but not with intoxicating drink." The next verse names the cause: "the LORD has poured out on you The spirit of deep sleep." People who repeatedly refused God's word received the dullness they had chosen, so that the prophets and seers could no longer see. Paul quotes this "spirit of stupor" in Romans 11:8 to explain Israel's unbelief in his own day. Isaiah does not leave the image there. In Isaiah 51:21 he speaks to Jerusalem, "you afflicted, And drunk but not with wine," and the following verse promises that the Lord will take the cup of trembling out of her hand and give it to her tormentors.

Genesis 43:34; Song of Solomon 5:1; Haggai 1:6 — fullness and its absence. Joseph's brothers came to Egypt in fear. Joseph seated them, gave Benjamin five portions, "So they drank and were merry with him." The feast came before the brothers knew Joseph's identity, but it already pointed toward reconciliation. The Song of Solomon goes further: "Eat, O friends! Drink, yes, drink deeply, O beloved ones!" The meaning of the invitation does not depend on settling exactly who speaks it or what occasion it marks. In this setting of love and celebration, the word means glad, unrestrained fullness, and it should not be read as moral failure. Haggai shows the opposite condition in a people who neglected God's house: "You drink, but you are not filled with drink." They drank and drank and never reached satisfaction.

1 Samuel 1:14 — misjudged prayer. Eli watched Hannah's lips move without sound and asked how long she would be drunk. Hannah answered that she had poured out her soul before the Lord. The priest at Shiloh could not tell the difference between drunkenness and desperate prayer.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets taught Israel to see God's judgment as a cup that leaves the drinker staggering. When Jesus knelt in Gethsemane and prayed, "Abba, Father... Take this cup away from Me; nevertheless, not what I will, but what You will" (Mark 14:36), He was not afraid of a simple drink. Mark does not explicitly identify that cup with Jeremiah's, and the New Testament does not quote Jeremiah at that moment. Read against the prophets, though, the cup He faced is well understood as the cup of Psalm 75:8 and Jeremiah 25, the cup reserved for the wicked of the earth. That canonical background gives His prayer its weight. He drank willingly what Babylon and Edom were forced to drink. Isaiah's promise that God would take the cup of trembling out of His people's hand finds its deepest answer at the cross.

The story continues to its end. Babylon's cup reappears in Revelation 17. Verse 2 supplies the drunkenness: "the inhabitants of the earth were made drunk with the wine of her fornication." Verse 4 supplies the golden cup: the woman has "in her hand a golden cup full of abominations" (Revelation 17:2, 4). The picture has turned around. In Jeremiah, Babylon was the golden cup in the LORD's hand. In Revelation, Babylon holds the cup herself and makes the earth drunk with her own corruption. Those who refuse God's mercy will "drink of the wine of the wrath of God, which is poured out full strength" (Revelation 14:10). The Bible's last word on the cup is therefore a choice. Christ has drunk the cup of wrath for all who trust Him, and those who reject Him will drink it themselves. In place of the "perpetual sleep" of Jeremiah 51, Christ went down into death and rose, so that His people will wake.

At Pentecost the onlookers mocked the Spirit-filled disciples as drunk, and Peter answered that it was only the third hour of the day (Acts 2:13–15). Luke states plainly that the disciples "were all filled with the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:4). The scene recalls Hannah at Shiloh, where heartfelt prayer was also mistaken for wine.

7. Spirit & Life

These passages show that people are made to be filled. The real question is what fills them. Haggai's people drank without satisfaction because they were filling their own houses while God's house lay in ruins. Many believers know that same thirst that never ends. The Song's invitation to "drink deeply" shows that God does not ration His love grudgingly. He spreads a feast and urges His beloved to take their fill.

Paul's command not to be drunk with wine but to be filled with the Spirit (Ephesians 5:18) draws on this whole background. Wine takes control and leaves a person uncovered, as it did Noah. The Spirit also takes hold of a person, but He produces worship, as the disciples' praise at Pentecost shows. Earnest prayer like Hannah's will sometimes look strange to observers, even religious ones. When the priest misjudged her, Hannah explained that she had "poured out my soul before the LORD" (1 Samuel 1:15).

Jeremiah 23:9 uses a related Hebrew word for "drunken man" to describe the prophet himself: "I am like a drunken man, And like a man whom wine has overcome, Because of the LORD, And because of His holy words" (Jeremiah 23:9). Jeremiah was overwhelmed by God's word, and he staggered under that weight with no wine and no judgment on him. The safest way to avoid the stupor of Isaiah 29 is to let God's word affect us that deeply while we can still hear it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 32:42 — In the Song of Moses, God says He will make His arrows drunk with blood, a picture of justice that leaves no enemy untouched.

  • Isaiah 49:26 — Israel's oppressors will be drunk with their own blood as with sweet wine. The violence they used against God's people turns back on them.

  • Isaiah 63:6 — "Made them drunk in My fury." The Lord treads the winepress of the nations alone.

  • Jeremiah 48:26 — Moab is made drunk "because he exalted himself against the LORD," and ends wallowing in derision.

  • Nahum 3:11 — "You also will be drunk; You will be hidden." Proud Nineveh, which once terrorized others, will stagger and look for a place to hide.
