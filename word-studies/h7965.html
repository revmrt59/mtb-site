PEACE, WELL-BEING - (shâlôwm, Strong's H7965)

Summary

The Hebrew word shâlôwm, usually translated "peace," describes well-being, safety and wholeness. Depending on the passage it can mean safety on a journey, health in the body, prosperity in a household, friendly relations between neighbors, an end to war, and the settled trust of a heart resting in God. When an Israelite asked, "Is he well?" (Genesis 29:6), he was asking about a person's shâlôwm. Not every use means that every part of life is whole; Job and Asaph even use the word for the outward security of the wicked (Job 21:9; Psalm 73:3). At its fullest, though, peace in Scripture is far more than the absence of trouble. It is life made whole as God intends it to be.

That fullness makes the word central to the Bible's hope. God gives peace in the priestly blessing, binds Himself to His people by a "covenant of peace" (Isaiah 54:10), and keeps in "perfect peace" the one whose mind is stayed on Him (Isaiah 26:3). The prophets warned against leaders who cried "Peace, peace!" while ignoring sin. They also looked ahead to a coming King called the "Prince of Peace" (Isaiah 9:6), of whom Micah could say, "this One shall be peace" (Micah 5:5). The New Testament announces the fulfillment of that hope in Jesus: "He Himself is our peace" (Ephesians 2:14).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâlôwm is a Hebrew noun that appears throughout the Old Testament. It occurs in family stories, battle reports, royal politics, temple worship, wisdom sayings and prophetic oracles. The NKJV usually renders it "peace," but the same word stands behind "well," "health," "safe," "safety," "prosperity," "welfare," and even "greet." These different English choices show how wide the word's meaning is.

In daily life, shâlôwm was the ordinary language of greeting. To "greet" someone was literally to ask about his peace (Judges 18:15; 1 Samuel 17:22). Israel's normal courtesy carried a question about the whole of a person's life: is everything right with you?

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its heart, shâlôwm means well-being, a condition of soundness and safety. At its fullest it describes a life in which nothing essential is missing or broken, but context determines which part of that well-being is in view, and a single use need not mean that everything is whole.

1. Personal welfare, health and safety. Joseph asks his brothers whether their father is well, and they answer about his health (Genesis 43:27–28). David confesses that there is no "health in my bones Because of my sin" (Psalm 38:3).

2. Prosperity and security. Job complains that the houses of the wicked "are safe from fear" (Job 21:9), and Asaph envied "the prosperity of the wicked" (Psalm 73:3). In both passages the word describes outward security that God's judgment will expose as temporary (Psalm 73:17–19).

3. Harmony between people or nations, including the end of war. Kings make peace with one another (1 Kings 5:12), and Israel offers terms of peace before battle (Deuteronomy 20:10).

4. Settled inner rest that comes from God. Examples include "perfect peace" (Isaiah 26:3) and the "great peace" of those who love God's law (Psalm 119:165).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the historical books, shâlôwm often appears in simple questions and greetings, such as "Is it well?" Those everyday words can carry heavy irony. Joseph's brothers "could not speak peaceably to him" (Genesis 37:4), yet Jacob sends Joseph to see whether it was well with them (Genesis 37:14). The errand ends with Joseph sold into slavery.

The Psalms and Proverbs connect peace with walking in God's ways. In the Prophets, the word becomes contested ground. True prophets such as Isaiah promise peace as God's gift, grounded in His faithfulness and joined to righteousness and repentance. Jeremiah and Ezekiel expose a different message: false assurances of peace spoken to people who were refusing to turn from sin. The difference lies not in the word but in whether God has spoken it and whether it deals honestly with sin.

Two Hebrew expressions shape the meaning in important ways. The first is a doubled noun. In Isaiah 26:3, "perfect peace" translates shâlôwm shâlôwm, the word repeated for emphasis. Hebrew often uses repetition to express fullness, so the phrase means peace in the fullest measure. False prophets use the same doubling in Jeremiah 6:14 when they cry "Peace, peace!" The shared wording makes a strong comparison, but the two utterances do not function alike: Isaiah describes the peace God gives to those who trust Him, while Jeremiah quotes an assurance the leaders gave on their own authority. The second is the idiom man of my peace, meaning a trusted friend or ally. The NKJV renders it "familiar friend" (Psalm 41:9), "acquaintances" (Jeremiah 20:10) and "close friends" (Jeremiah 38:22).

4. Theological Significance

Shâlôwm reveals that God is the source and giver of wholeness. Isaiah hears Him say that He forms light and creates darkness, that He makes peace and creates calamity (Isaiah 45:7). Peace is never simply a natural human achievement. Even when people pursue it, they receive it from God's hand.

Because peace comes from God, it cannot be separated from His character. Righteousness and peace belong together. The work of righteousness produces peace (Isaiah 32:17), and in Psalm 85:10 righteousness and peace kiss each other. The reverse is also true. "There is no peace," says the LORD, "for the wicked" (Isaiah 48:22; compare 57:21). Sin is a fracture in the order God created, and that fracture appears everywhere: in the body, as with David's sick bones, in the home, between nations, and before God.

Peace also shows the relational heart of God. He does more than issue a decree of peace. He turns His face toward His people, binds Himself to them, and dwells among them. Biblical peace ultimately means life restored in God's presence.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The priestly blessing (Numbers 6:24–26). Aaron's blessing rises through three lines and ends with the request that the LORD lift up His countenance on His people and give them peace (Numbers 6:26). Peace is the last word of the blessing, and it is closely joined to God's face. When God turns His full attention toward His people with favor, peace follows. Israel could not produce this peace on its own; it had to be given. The Psalms echo the same truth: the LORD blesses His people with peace (Psalm 29:11) and speaks peace to His people and to His saints (Psalm 85:8).

Peace, peace, when there is no peace (Jeremiah 6:14; Ezekiel 13:10). Jeremiah charged the prophets and priests of his day: "They have also healed the hurt of My people slightly, Saying, 'Peace, peace!' When there is no peace." These leaders were like physicians who covered a deep wound with a light bandage. Ezekiel saw the same deception in terms of a building: they said "Peace!" when there was no peace, "and one builds a wall, and they plaster it with untempered mortar" (Ezekiel 13:10). The fresh coating made a weak wall look solid until God's storm brought it down (Ezekiel 13:16). Micah adds that these prophets "chant 'Peace'" to anyone who fed them (Micah 3:5). False peace bypasses repentance and justice. It tells people that all is well with God while they continue in idolatry. Isaiah 26:3 uses the same doubled word for the peace God Himself keeps; the false prophets borrowed equally emphatic wording for a promise God had not made. Jeremiah 28:9 supplies the test: a prophet of peace is shown to be truly sent only when his word comes to pass.

Perfect peace through trust (Isaiah 26:3). "You will keep him in perfect peace, Whose mind is stayed on You, Because he trusts in You." The verse names who keeps the believer: God Himself. It names the condition: a mind fixed on Him. It names the reason: trust. This peace comes from leaning on the character of God more than from any change in a person's circumstances. Isaiah sings it in a song about a strong city whose walls are salvation (Isaiah 26:1), set in a world still facing judgment. Later he adds that righteousness produces quietness and assurance, and that God's people will live in a peaceful dwelling (Isaiah 32:17–18).

The covenant of peace (Numbers 25:12; Isaiah 54:10; Ezekiel 34:25). God first speaks of "My covenant of peace" when He rewards Phinehas for turning away His wrath at Peor (Numbers 25:12). Malachi later recalls that God's covenant with Levi was "one of life and peace" (Malachi 2:5). Isaiah widens the promise to the whole of God's restored people: "For the mountains shall depart And the hills be removed, But My kindness shall not depart from you, Nor shall My covenant of peace be removed" (Isaiah 54:10). The most solid things in creation are less permanent than God's commitment to His people's wholeness. Ezekiel gives that promise concrete form: "I will make a covenant of peace with them, and cause wild beasts to cease from the land; and they will dwell safely in the wilderness and sleep in the woods" (Ezekiel 34:25). God promises a land where it is safe to sleep outdoors, as if the curse on creation were being turned back. Zechariah describes the same restored world: "the seed shall be prosperous, The vine shall give its fruit" (Zechariah 8:12). Ezekiel 37:26 calls this an everlasting covenant of peace with God's sanctuary in the midst of His people. The covenant of peace ends where Eden began, with God dwelling with His people.

The man of my peace (Psalm 41:9). David laments that his familiar friend, a man he trusted and who ate his bread, has lifted up his heel against him. In Hebrew, the "familiar friend" is the man of my peace, the person with whom David's life was bound in loyalty and well-being. That idiom makes the betrayal worse. The very relationship that should have protected him became the source of the wound. Jeremiah knew the same grief when his "acquaintances" watched for him to stumble (Jeremiah 20:10), and Obadiah warns Edom of the men at peace with it who deceive it (Obadiah 7). Jesus quoted Psalm 41:9 about Judas (John 13:18). The Son of God knew the betrayal of a man of His peace.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets came to see that peace would be secured by a Person. Isaiah announces a Child whose name includes "Prince of Peace," and adds, "Of the increase of His government and peace There will be no end, Upon the throne of David" (Isaiah 9:6–7). God promised David's son Solomon peace and quiet in his days (1 Chronicles 22:9), but that peace was partial and did not last. The coming King's peace has no end. Zechariah's humble King "shall speak peace to the nations," cutting off chariot and battle bow (Zechariah 9:10). Micah goes further: "this One shall be peace" (Micah 5:5). The coming King does more than bring peace; He is peace.

Isaiah also reveals the cost. The same prophet who names the Prince of Peace describes the Servant who was wounded for transgressions, and says the punishment that brought us peace fell on Him (Isaiah 53:5). The King's reign of peace and the Servant's suffering belong together. God's favor cannot shine on rebels without dealing with their rebellion, and the Servant bears the breach Himself.

Paul announces the fulfillment: "For He Himself is our peace, who has made both one, and has broken down the middle wall of separation" (Ephesians 2:14), reconciling Jew and Gentile "to God in one body through the cross" (Ephesians 2:16). When Paul writes that Christ "came and preached peace to you who were afar off and to those who were near" (Ephesians 2:17), he echoes Isaiah 57:19, where God speaks peace to those far off and near. The false prophets put up whitewashed walls. Christ tears down the wall of hostility.

This message now travels. Isaiah pictured a runner on the mountains: "How beautiful upon the mountains Are the feet of him who brings good news, Who proclaims peace" (Isaiah 52:7; compare Nahum 1:15). Paul applies those words to gospel preachers (Romans 10:15) and calls believers to have their feet ready "with the preparation of the gospel of peace" (Ephesians 6:15). The story moves toward the city of peace as well. Psalm 122 prays, "Peace be within your walls," and God promises, "I will extend peace to her like a river" (Isaiah 66:12). In that promise, peace flows to Jerusalem's children like nourishment to a nursing child.

7. Spirit & Life

Shâlôwm teaches us to want more than relief. We often ask God only to make the trouble stop, but He intends to make us whole: body, relationships, conscience and future. When Joseph asked his brothers, "Is your father well, the old man of whom you spoke?" (Genesis 43:27), he used this word to ask about Jacob's health. That everyday question shows how broad the word is: it reaches from an old man's health to peace with God, and the God who gives shâlôwm cares about the full condition of our lives.

The prophets warn that the most dangerous comfort is the kind that ignores sin. Whenever we tell ourselves "all is well" while holding on to what God condemns, we are plastering a weak wall. Real peace may begin with an honest wound being cleaned before it can heal. When God refuses to cover a sin lightly, He is being kind.

Isaiah 26:3 shows where steady peace comes from. A mind "stayed on" God is like a house resting on its foundation. Its stability comes from what it rests on, not from the absence of wind. For the Christian, that foundation is a Person who secured our peace at the cost of His own wounds. Christ does not hand us a technique for staying calm. He gives us Himself.

Christ also understands the deepest relational wounds. Those betrayed by a trusted friend, a man of their peace, can bring that grief to Someone who heard Psalm 41 fulfilled at His own table. Because He is our peace, His people become carriers of peace. We are to seek the peace of the places where God has put us (Jeremiah 29:7) and to go with ready feet to people who are far off.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 6:23 – When Gideon fears he will die for seeing the Angel of the LORD, God says, "Peace be with you." Gideon then builds an altar named "The-LORD-Is-Peace" (Judges 6:24).

  • 2 Kings 4:26 – The Shunammite woman answers "It is well" while her son lies dead, then hurries on, takes hold of Elisha's feet and presses her appeal (2 Kings 4:27–28). The text does not explain her reply, but it records that she did not stop until she reached the man of God.

  • 2 Kings 9:22 – Jehu answers Joram's question about peace by asking what peace there can be while Jezebel's harlotries continue. Peace cannot coexist with entrenched evil.

  • Psalm 34:14 – The psalmist calls God's people to seek peace and pursue it. Peter later quotes this verse in 1 Peter 3:11.

  • Psalm 120:7 – The psalmist says, "I am for peace," but the people around him want war. Some people will refuse peace even when it is sincerely offered.

  • Jeremiah 29:7, 11 – God tells the exiles to seek the peace of Babylon, where He has sent them, and promises that His thoughts toward them are thoughts of peace.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H8002 (shelem), the peace offering. This word is related to shâlôwm and names the sacrifice that celebrated fellowship with God (Leviticus 3).

  • H2617 (chesed), steadfast kindness. This is a canonical pairing in Isaiah 54:10, where God's "kindness" and His "covenant of peace" stand side by side as things that will never be removed.

  • G1515 (eirēnē), peace. This Greek word carries the Old Testament hope of peace into the New Testament, as in Ephesians 2:14–17.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Many readers associate peace with the dove that returned to Noah with an olive leaf after the flood (Genesis 8:11). Shâlôwm does not appear in that passage, but the scene pictures what the word describes: judgment past, the earth renewed, and life beginning again under God's favor.
