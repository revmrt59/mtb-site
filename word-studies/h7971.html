SEND, LET GO, STRETCH OUT - (shâlach, Strong's H7971)

Summary

The Hebrew word shâlach is the Old Testament's ordinary verb for sending. It describes something moving out from a person toward a purpose. A king sends a messenger, a man stretches out his hand, a master lets a slave go free, and a husband sends a wife out of his house. The word itself does not say whether the sending brings blessing, grief, or judgment. The story around it decides that.

The most important uses center on God as the One who sends. He sent Moses to Pharaoh to demand that Israel be let go so they could serve Him. He sent His prophets, sent His word to heal, and sends forth His Spirit to renew the earth. On the Day of Atonement, the live goat was sent away into the wilderness carrying Israel's sins. All of these lines meet in Jesus, who called Himself the One the Father sent, and who then sent His disciples into the world with that same commission.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâlach is a Hebrew verb, and it is one of the most common in the Old Testament. It appears in every kind of writing: family stories, royal records, law, poetry, and prophecy. Much of its use reflects how authority worked in the ancient world. A ruler did not travel to every place where he needed to act. He sent someone. The servant who arrived carried the sender's words and the sender's authority.

That background explains why the word can carry so much moral weight. In 2 Samuel 11 the verb appears again and again as David sends for Bathsheba, sends for Uriah, and sends the letter that arranges Uriah's death. Each sending shows David using his royal power, and the repetition traces how his sin spread from one decision to the next.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, shâlach means to cause someone or something to go out from you. English translations use many words for it, including send, let go, put away, stretch out, and lay. The principal senses are these:

1. To send or commission. This is the sense used when a person is dispatched with a task or message. Abraham tells his servant that God will send His angel ahead of him to find a wife for Isaac (Genesis 24:7). The Lord tells Moses that He will send him to Pharaoh (Exodus 3:10).

2. To let go, release, or send away. Here the stress falls on what is left behind. Pharaoh is commanded to let Israel go (Exodus 4:23). A Hebrew servant was to be set free in the seventh year and not sent away empty-handed (Deuteronomy 15:12–13). A husband who divorced his wife sent her out of his house (Deuteronomy 24:1).

3. To stretch out or put forth. This sense is usually used with the hand. After the flood, Noah's dove came back to the ark: "So he put out his hand and took her, and drew her into the ark to himself" (Genesis 8:9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Hebrew verbs can appear in different forms, and those forms can change the emphasis of a word. The plain form of shâlach usually means simply "to send." A strengthened form often carries the idea of sending off, releasing, or dismissing. This is the form behind most of the "let go" passages in Exodus and the "put away" passages about divorce. The pattern is not absolute, so the surrounding context still decides the meaning. Even so, when English shifts from "send" to "let go," the Hebrew has often shifted with it.

The word is especially vivid when it is used with the hand. To stretch out the hand marks the move from intention toward action, though the action can still be stopped. Abraham "stretched out" his hand to take the knife (Genesis 22:10). Two verses later the Angel of the Lord uses the same verb in the command not to lay a hand on the boy (Genesis 22:12). In that one scene, Abraham's obedient hand is reached out and then held back by God.

David's story turns on the same idea. Twice David had Saul in his power, and twice he refused to stretch out his hand against the Lord's anointed (1 Samuel 24:6; 26:9). Uzzah, by contrast, put out his hand to steady the ark, and God struck him down (2 Samuel 6:6–7). The difference between the two men was not strength or opportunity. It was reverence for what belonged to God.

The same language is used of God's own hand. The Lord told Moses that He would stretch out His hand and strike Egypt, and that after this Pharaoh would let Israel go (Exodus 3:20). Both verbs in that promise are shâlach. God's outstretched hand would bring about Pharaoh's letting go. God's hand can also be held back. In Exodus 9:15 the Lord says He could have already stretched out His hand to wipe Egypt out. In 2 Samuel 24:16, when the angel stretched out his hand over Jerusalem, the Lord relented and stopped the destruction.

4. Theological Significance

Above all, this word shows a God who takes the initiative. He does not stay distant and wait for people to find their way to Him. He sends messengers, His word, His Spirit, deliverance, and discipline.

His sending can be severe. To a disobedient Israel He warns, "I will also send wild beasts among you" (Leviticus 26:22). Through Ezekiel He speaks of the time "When I send against them the terrible arrows of famine" (Ezekiel 5:16). The most sobering threat is found in Amos: "I will send a famine on the land, Not a famine of bread, Nor a thirst for water, But of hearing the words of the LORD" (Amos 8:11). The God who sends His word can also send its absence, and Amos treats that silence as worse than hunger.

The word also exposes a painful contrast. The same verb that described Israel's release from Egypt later described Israel's being sent away by God. Jeremiah records God saying of the northern kingdom, "I had put her away and given her a certificate of divorce" (Jeremiah 3:8). Isaiah turns the image into a question: "Where is the certificate of your mother's divorce, Whom I have put away?" (Isaiah 50:1). The answer in the verse is that Israel's own iniquities sold them into exile. Isaiah's question also implies that no such certificate exists for Judah. God has not written a final word of rejection. A few chapters later, God's people hear a promise of restoration. Isaiah uses a different Hebrew word for "forsaken" there, but the promise answers the same grief: "For a mere moment I have forsaken you, But with great mercies I will gather you" (Isaiah 54:7).

That background gives weight to Malachi 2:16, where the NKJV reads that "the LORD God of Israel says That He hates divorce." The Hebrew word behind "divorce" there is this verb of sending away. The wording of the Hebrew sentence is difficult, and translations render it in different ways, some making God the one who hates divorce and others describing the man who hates and sends away his wife. What the passage makes plain on any reading is its condemnation of treachery: "Therefore take heed to your spirit, That you do not deal treacherously." Scripture's wider story also begins with this kind of sending, because the Lord God sent Adam out of the garden (Genesis 3:23). Much of Scripture tells how God brings exiles home.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 3–12: Sent so that others may be let go. The Exodus account depends on this verb. God sends Moses (Exodus 3:10). Moses is to tell Israel that I AM has sent him (Exodus 3:14). Moses' message to Pharaoh is the demand to let God's people go. The Lord states the purpose plainly: "let My son go that he may serve Me" (Exodus 4:23). Pharaoh's answer is the opposite of that demand, since he does not know the Lord and will not let Israel go (Exodus 5:2). Through the plagues the demand to let Israel go is pressed again and again, and Pharaoh's heart remains hard, though the narrative does not record the same exchange after every plague. In the end the Egyptians urge Israel to leave quickly (Exodus 12:33). The release was never meant to make Israel free to live without any master. God sent Israel out of Pharaoh's service so they could enter His own.

Leviticus 16: The goat that was sent away. On the Day of Atonement, the second goat was "presented alive before the LORD, to make atonement upon it, and to let it go as the scapegoat into the wilderness" (Leviticus 16:10). Aaron laid his hands on its head and confessed Israel's sins over it. Then "The goat shall bear on itself all their iniquities to an uninhabited land; and he shall release the goat in the wilderness" (Leviticus 16:22). Here the act of sending away is the meaning of the ritual. Israel's guilt was carried out of the camp to a place from which it would not return. A psalmist later states the same truth without the ritual: "As far as the east is from the west, So far has He removed our transgressions from us" (Psalm 103:12).

Psalm 107:20; Isaiah 55:11; Psalm 104:30: God's word and Spirit sent out to work. God does not only send human messengers. Psalm 107 describes people who were close to death, and then says, "He sent His word and healed them, And delivered them from their destructions" (Psalm 107:20). In that psalm God's word acts like a messenger who reaches the sick and heals them. Isaiah adds the certainty that God's word will succeed: "It shall not return to Me void, But it shall accomplish what I please, And it shall prosper in the thing for which I sent it" (Isaiah 55:11). The same is true of God's Spirit: "You send forth Your Spirit, they are created; And You renew the face of the earth" (Psalm 104:30). In these verses God's sending accomplishes what He intends. Healing, renewal, and new life follow when He sends.

Isaiah 6; Jeremiah 1 and 23: Who sent you? In the temple Isaiah heard the Lord ask whom He should send, and Isaiah answered by asking to be sent (Isaiah 6:8). Both the question and the answer use this verb. Jeremiah was told he must go to everyone God would send him to (Jeremiah 1:7). Later the Lord said of the false prophets that He had not sent them, yet they ran (Jeremiah 23:21). That one difference decided whether a prophet's message was true. A prophet's authority did not come from his gift with words or his confidence. It came from the God who sent him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament ends with God promising to send someone. "Behold, I send My messenger," He says in Malachi 3:1, and then He promises to send Elijah before the great day of the Lord (Malachi 4:5). Jesus later identified John the Baptist as the messenger and the Elijah of these promises (Matthew 11:10, 14). Isaiah 61:1 describes the Spirit-anointed One whom the Lord has sent to bring good news and proclaim liberty to captives. In the synagogue at Nazareth, Jesus read that passage and declared it fulfilled that day (Luke 4:16–21). That passage is a direct statement of fulfillment.

John's Gospel returns again and again to Jesus as the One the Father sent. The New Testament uses the Greek verb apostellō for much of this sending, and the word "apostle" comes from it. Other patterns from the Old Testament also come together in Christ, even where the New Testament does not spell out every link. God once sent His word to heal, and that Word became flesh (John 1:14). The scapegoat carried sin away from the camp, and John the Baptist pointed to Jesus as the Lamb of God "who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). Moses was sent so that slaves could be released for worship, and Jesus came proclaiming liberty and making His people free to serve God. The God who sends forth His Spirit to renew the earth poured out the Spirit at Pentecost.

The story does not stop with Christ. Those He has set free are sent out in turn. After His resurrection Jesus told His disciples, "As the Father has sent Me, I also send you" (John 20:21). Paul asks, "And how shall they preach unless they are sent?" (Romans 10:15). The church continues God's sending, and its authority, like the prophets' authority, depends entirely on the One who sends it.

7. Spirit & Life

Exodus teaches that freedom has a purpose. God did not bring Israel out of Egypt to leave them on their own. He released them "that he may serve Me." A believer set free from sin has not been given a life without a master. He has been brought into the service of a better Master. When freedom in Christ starts to feel like permission to do as we please, Exodus 4:23 explains what the release was for.

The idiom of the outstretched hand asks a searching question: what are we reaching for? David stood over the sleeping Saul, whose spear was stuck in the ground by his head, while Abishai begged to strike him with it (1 Samuel 26:7–8). David refused to stretch out his hand against the Lord's anointed, and only afterward took the spear and the water jug as proof that he had spared Saul (1 Samuel 26:9–12). He would not take what God had not given. Uzzah's hand went out quickly toward something holy, and he treated it carelessly. Faith often shows itself in holding back the hand as much as in reaching it out.

Moses' first answer to his calling was a plea for God to send someone else (Exodus 4:13). The Lord did not reply by convincing Moses that he was capable. He kept sending him. Anyone who feels unqualified for what God has asked should notice that every authority in this word rests with the Sender. The same is true of the word we carry. When a believer speaks Scripture to a struggling friend or prays it over a child, Isaiah 55:11 gives quiet confidence that God's word is not sent out to fail.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 8:8–12. Noah "sent out from himself a dove." It came back with "a freshly plucked olive leaf" and later did not return at all. The bird Noah sent out became the first sign that a renewed earth was waiting after the judgment of the flood.

  • Genesis 32:26. Jacob, wrestling through the night, refused to let the Lord go unless He blessed him. In this verse the verb describes the one release a person should refuse to give.

  • Genesis 45:5–8. Joseph told his brothers three times that God had sent him ahead of them to Egypt. In this way Joseph saw the sins committed against him as part of God's sending.

  • 1 Samuel 20:20–22. Jonathan's arrows were "sent" as a hidden signal. Where they landed would tell David whether he was safe or whether the Lord was sending him away. A simple act of shooting arrows carried both the loyalty of a friend and the direction of David's life.

  • Jeremiah 27:3. Jeremiah made yokes and was told to "send them to the king of Edom, the king of Moab," and the other kings. The yokes that were sent became God's visible message that these nations must submit to Babylon.

  • Jeremiah 34:8–16. Judah set its Hebrew slaves free and then forced them back into slavery. God judged the people for taking back a release they had made before Him. The same concern appears in Isaiah 58:6, where the fast God chooses includes letting the oppressed go free.
