WEAPON, MISSILE, PLANT - (shelach, Strong's H7973)

Summary

The Hebrew word shelach has two distinct senses. Most often it names a hand weapon or missile: the weapon in the hand of a guard, the arms Hezekiah stockpiled, and the weapon Nehemiah's builders held while they laid stones. In Job the same word becomes a picture of sudden death, the strike that ends a life cut off from God. Then, once, in the Song of Solomon, it describes something entirely different: the growing "plants" of the bride's garden, putting out pomegranates and fragrant spices.

That range carries real spiritual weight. Scripture uses shelach to show God's people working and watching at the same time, and it shows that weapons, though wise to prepare, cannot save. Through Elihu's words in Job, it reveals God's discipline as a mercy that holds a person back from the fatal blow. In the Song, the word names shoots of life growing in a garden kept for the beloved, and setting that use beside the weapon passages makes a striking illustrative contrast. A believer can ask a searching question of this little word: what is my life sending out?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shelach is a Hebrew noun that lexicons commonly connect with the verb shalach, to send or to stretch out. That derivation is a proposed explanation of how one word came to name both a weapon and a plant. It appears only eight times in the Old Testament. These uses fall into three kinds of writing: historical narrative (2 Chronicles and Nehemiah), poetry and wisdom (Job and the Song of Solomon), and prophecy (Joel). The historical passages are set in moments of danger for Jerusalem: a palace coup, an Assyrian invasion, and hostile neighbors threatening the returned exiles. The poetic passages lift the word into images of death and of fruitfulness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has two distinct senses, a weapon and a plant, and context shows which is meant. The weapon sense is also used figuratively for death.

1. A weapon or missile used in battle or defense, as in Nehemiah 4:17, where each builder "with the other held a weapon."

2. A figure for violent or sudden death, as in Job 33:18, where God keeps a person's life "from perishing by the sword."

3. A shoot or plant growing out from its root, used once, in Song of Solomon 4:13: "Your plants are an orchard of pomegranates."

The two Job passages are poetic and somewhat difficult. Some translators read the phrase as crossing the river of death rather than "perishing by the sword." Either way, the verse describes a person passing into death, and the NKJV's rendering is well supported.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Chronicles and Nehemiah, shelach is a literal weapon held in the hand. The point is readiness. Armed men surround a child king, a city's armory is filled, and workers carry arms onto the wall. In Joel 2:8 the weapons belong to the defenders, and the invading army passes right through them: "Though they lunge between the weapons, they are not cut down."

In Job the word moves from the battlefield to the boundary of life. It stands in parallel with "the Pit," the grave, so "the sword" becomes a poetic way of naming death itself. The Song's single botanical use is a separate sense. If the proposed link with the verb for sending is right, the idea of something put out could explain how the word applied to a shoot as well as a spear, but that remains an explanation, not a demonstrated fact.

4. Theological Significance

Taken together, the passages using shelach teach that God is the true defender of His people. Preparation is right, and Scripture never mocks the guards, the armory, or the armed builders. Yet every passage that puts a weapon in human hands also points past it to the Lord.

These passages also reveal that God's judgment cannot be turned aside by human strength. When He sends judgment, no weapon stops it. When He holds back death, no weapon was needed.

The word shows, finally, that God is at work in the gap between a person and the fatal blow. In Job, God is neither indifferent nor merely destructive. He actively turns people back before the sword falls. Set side by side as an illustration, the warrior's weapon and the garden's shoot suggest two kinds of fruit: a fallen world puts out death, while the life God tends puts out fruit.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Nehemiah 4:16–18: building with one hand, guarding with the other. Jerusalem's enemies threatened to attack the half-finished wall. Nehemiah did not stop the work, and he did not send his people out unprotected. Half his servants stood guard with spears, shields, and bows (Nehemiah 4:16). Those who carried loads worked so that "with one hand they worked at construction, and with the other held a weapon" (Nehemiah 4:17). The builders kept their swords girded at their sides (Nehemiah 4:18). The weapon never replaced the trowel. It made the trowel possible. Nehemiah's rallying word holds both together: "Our God will fight for us" (Nehemiah 4:20). The people watched and worked, and they trusted God to fight.

2 Chronicles 32:5–8 and Joel 2:8: the limits of weapons. Facing Assyria, Hezekiah rebuilt the walls and "made weapons and shields in abundance" (2 Chronicles 32:5). He did everything a wise king should. Then he gathered the people and turned their eyes away from the stockpile: "With him is an arm of flesh; but with us is the LORD our God, to help us and to fight our battles" (2 Chronicles 32:8). Hezekiah's arsenal was stewardship. His confidence rested in God alone, as in Psalm 20:7: "Some trust in chariots, and some in horses; but we will remember the name of the LORD our God." Joel shows the other side. The army in Joel 2, which many read as the locusts of chapter 1 and others as an invading force, is in either case the Lord's own army sent in judgment (Joel 2:11), and it plunges between the defenders' weapons without being cut down (Joel 2:8). Weapons are useful against human enemies, but they cannot hold back what God has sent.

Job 33:18 and 36:12: discipline as rescue. Elihu describes God speaking to people through dreams and through pain in order to turn them from their deeds and humble their pride (Job 33:14–17). The purpose of this hard correction is mercy: "He keeps back his soul from the Pit, and his life from perishing by the sword" (Job 33:18). God works "twice, in fact, three times with a man, to bring back his soul from the Pit, that he may be enlightened with the light of life" (Job 33:29–30). The shelach here is the deadly strike awaiting the proud. God's discipline stands between a person and that strike. Elihu then gives the sober alternative: "But if they do not obey, they shall perish by the sword, and they shall die without knowledge" (Job 36:12). In Elihu's words, the pain God sends in such cases is not the sword. It is His hand pulling a person back from it.

Song of Solomon 4:12–13: the garden that sends out life. The bridegroom calls his bride "a garden enclosed... a fountain sealed" (Song of Solomon 4:12). Then he says, "Your plants are an orchard of pomegranates with pleasant fruits, fragrant henna with spikenard" (Song of Solomon 4:13). The word for "plants" is shelach, the same word used everywhere else for weapons. Only a few verses earlier, he compares her neck to "the tower of David, built for an armory, on which hang a thousand bucklers" (Song of Solomon 4:4). The text gives no evidence that the poet intended a wordplay between weapon and garden, so the link is best read as an illustrative comparison. As such it is a fruitful one: the word that elsewhere names what is thrust out to kill here names what grows to give fruit and fragrance. The bride, enclosed and kept for the one who loves her, sends out life.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The guards of 2 Chronicles 23:10 belong in this larger story. Athaliah had tried to destroy the entire royal family of Judah (2 Chronicles 22:10). If she had succeeded, the line of David would have ended, and with it God's promise that David's throne would endure (2 Samuel 7:16). Jehoiada stationed "every man with his weapon in his hand... all around the king" inside the temple courts. Then they crowned the boy Joash and cried, "Long live the king!" (2 Chronicles 23:11). The weapons guarded more than one child. They guarded the line through which the promised Son of David would come.

The New Testament carries the weapon imagery from physical battle into spiritual warfare. The enemy still sends out missiles, now "the fiery darts of the wicked one," and believers take up "the shield of faith" and "the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God" (Ephesians 6:16–17). Nehemiah's builder, working and armed, becomes a fitting picture of the church building on Christ's foundation while resisting the enemy.

Job's image of the deadly strike also finds its deepest answer in Christ. This is a theological connection, since Job does not prophesy it directly. God holds back the sinner's life from perishing because His Son, the One sent from the Father, went down into death and bore the judgment in our place. The garden image completes the movement. Isaiah foresaw a day when nations "shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks" (Isaiah 2:4). A single word that holds both "weapon" and "shoot" fits that hope. The story of God's people ends in a garden, not an armory. Jesus says, "I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit" (John 15:5).

7. Spirit & Life

This word asks what our lives are sending out. Words can be thrown like missiles, and so can resentment and judgment. The Song pictures another possibility: a life enclosed for the Lord, watered by a sealed fountain, putting out fruit and fragrance that bless others. That kind of fruit comes from belonging to Christ and abiding in Him. Effort alone does not produce it.

Nehemiah's builders teach a balance many Christians lose. Some only build and grow careless about the enemy. Others only guard and never lay a stone. God's people are meant to do the work of building up the church while staying alert, holding the Word of God ready. Hezekiah adds the necessary humility: prepare well, but do not mistake your preparations for your salvation.

Job turns this word toward our hardest seasons. The book as a whole warns against assuming that every painful loss is correction for sin; Job's friends made that mistake, and God rebuked them (Job 42:7). Elihu does show that God can use pain as corrective mercy, turning a person back from a path that leads to death (Job 33:17–18). Hebrews carries this teaching for Christians: "For whom the LORD loves He chastens," and His discipline "yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it" (Hebrews 12:6, 11). Where God is correcting us, we can stop resenting the hand that disciplines and begin trusting the Father who does it for our good.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Nehemiah 4:23 — The final clause is difficult in Hebrew; it reads roughly each his weapon, the water. The NKJV renders it "everyone took them off for washing," so the English hides the weapon, but the word stands in the verse. The sentence completes a chapter of round-the-clock readiness, in which Nehemiah and his men did not even take off their clothes.

The remaining occurrences of shelach have been discussed in the sections above.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H7971 (shalach), to send, stretch out — the verb with which shelach is commonly connected, describing sending or extending something outward, as when Noah "sent out" the dove (Genesis 8:8).

  • G0956 (belos), dart, missile — a thematic connection rather than a lexical relative. Paul uses it for "the fiery darts of the wicked one" (Ephesians 6:16), carrying the image of the enemy's projectiles into spiritual warfare.
