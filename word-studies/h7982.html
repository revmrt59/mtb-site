SHIELD - (sheleṭ, Strong's H7982)

Summary

The Hebrew word sheleṭ names a shield. It is a rare word, appearing only seven times in the Old Testament, and its exact shape, size and material are not certain. What stands out is where these shields appear: gold shields taken in battle, shields kept in the temple, and shields hung on towers and city walls where everyone could see the strength of the people who owned them. Almost every use links strength with display: these shields guarded a city, and they also announced its glory.

The word's most striking use runs through Israel's history. David captured gold shields from the servants of the Aramean king Hadadezer and brought them to Jerusalem. Generations later, when Queen Athaliah tried to wipe out David's royal family, the priest Jehoiada armed his guards with shields that had belonged to King David and were kept in the temple, to protect the hidden boy-king Joash. Scripture does not say whether these were Hadadezer's gold shields, but it does record that David's weapons protected David's heir. Elsewhere the word pictures the Bride's dignity and security in the Song of Solomon, the splendor of proud Tyre, whose walls were hung with the shields of men from other lands, and the shields of the Medes, which God summons against Babylon as "the vengeance for His temple." Together these passages show a God who uses His king's own weapons to protect His promises, and who controls even the weapons of empires.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Hebrew has several words that English translates "shield." The most common is magen, the round shield carried in hand-to-hand fighting. Another word, tsinnah, names the large shield that protected the whole body. Sheleṭ is a third word, and its exact difference from the others is not certain. It appears in settings of royalty and display: gold plunder, a temple armory, a tower hung with trophies, and the walls of a wealthy port.

A few older interpreters suggested that the word might mean a quiver. That view affects mainly Jeremiah 51:11. However, the word's pairing with spears, the image of shields hung on walls, and the consistent witness of major translations all support the meaning "shield."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Sheleṭ means a shield. The passages do not define its shape, size or material, but they place it in royal treasuries, the temple and public display. In every occurrence the English translation is plural. The word usually describes a group of shields that represented a city's or a king's strength.

Context reveals the shields' character, though the word itself does not specify it. In 2 Samuel 8:7 they are gold. In 2 Chronicles 23:9 they appear alongside another Hebrew word for shields (magen); the NKJV renders the pair as "the large and small shields." In Song of Solomon 4:4 and Ezekiel 27:11 they hang on a wall, where their purpose is to be seen.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The seven uses fall into three groups. In historical narrative, the word names the gold shields David took in battle and the shields of King David later kept in the temple (2 Samuel 8:7; 1 Chronicles 18:7; 2 Kings 11:10; 2 Chronicles 23:9). In poetry and prophetic description, hanging shields show beauty joined to strength, first for the Bride and then for Tyre (Song 4:4; Ezekiel 27:11). In prophecy, shields are gathered for battle at the Lord's command (Jeremiah 51:11).

The difference between Song 4:4 and Ezekiel 27:11 lies in ownership and trust. The tower of David displays the shields of "mighty men," while Tyre's walls display the shields of men from other lands who served in its army (Ezekiel 27:10–11).

4. Theological Significance

The passages that use sheleṭ keep returning to God's house. David's shields end up in the temple. The rightful king is hidden in the temple and guarded with weapons taken from it. When Babylon later burns that temple, God answers with the shields of the Medes. Taken together, these passages reveal a God who is jealous for His dwelling place and faithful to His promise to David (2 Samuel 7:12–16). He takes the wealth of His enemies into His service, and He decides whose shields will stand and whose will fall.

The word also warns about where people place their confidence. A shield on a wall seems to announce we are safe. Scripture lets that claim stand only when it rests on God.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

David's shields and the rescue of Joash (2 Samuel 8:7; 1 Chronicles 18:7; 2 Kings 11:10; 2 Chronicles 23:9). "David took the shields of gold that had belonged to the servants of Hadadezer, and brought them to Jerusalem" (2 Samuel 8:7). A few verses later, David dedicates the plundered gold to the Lord (2 Samuel 8:11). The shields were the glory of a defeated enemy, and David turned them into an offering to God.

More than a century later, the line of David nearly ended. Athaliah destroyed the royal heirs (2 Kings 11:1). Only the infant Joash survived, and he was hidden in the house of the Lord for six years. God had promised David a lasting throne, and that promise now depended on one hidden child. When Jehoiada the priest finally brought Joash out to be crowned, he gave the captains the spears and shields that had belonged to King David and were kept in the temple (2 Kings 11:10; 2 Chronicles 23:9).

The text does not say these were the same gold shields taken from Hadadezer. The rare word and the phrase "King David's" invite the connection, but the main point does not depend on it. What the text does record is enough: weapons that had belonged to David, stored in God's house, became the protection that preserved David's heir. God guarded His promise with what He had already won.

The tower of David and the walls of Tyre (Song 4:4; Ezekiel 27:11). The bridegroom tells his bride, "Your neck is like the tower of David, Built for an armory, On which hang a thousand bucklers, All shields of mighty men" (Song 4:4). The poem uses the shields to praise her dignity and strength: a tall, noble neck and a bearing that commands respect. Her beauty carries strength. Later the same poem calls her "awesome" like an army with banners (Song 6:4, 10).

Ezekiel uses a similar picture for Tyre. The men of Arvad and Gammad, who served in Tyre's army, "hung their shields on your walls all around; They made your beauty perfect" (Ezekiel 27:11). Tyre boasted that it was perfect in beauty (Ezekiel 27:3), and its confidence rested in trade and pride (Ezekiel 28:2–5). Yet Ezekiel foresees all its wealth and its "men of war who are in you" going down together: they "Will fall into the midst of the seas on the day of your ruin" (Ezekiel 27:27). The two passages show a similar display with opposite outcomes. The Song uses shields to praise the bride, while Tyre's impressive walls could not prevent its fall.

"Gather the shields!" (Jeremiah 51:11). "Make the arrows bright! Gather the shields! The LORD has raised up the spirit of the kings of the Medes. For His plan is against Babylon to destroy it, Because it is the vengeance of the LORD, The vengeance for His temple." Babylon burned the house where David's shields had been kept (2 Kings 25:9). God answers by calling foreign shields into His service. The commands to make arrows bright and gather shields sound like orders from a general, and the general is the Lord. The Medes' strength served His plan, and His purpose was to vindicate His own house.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The story of David's shields points toward the greater Son of David. Jesus described His work in these terms: "when a stronger than he comes upon him and overcomes him, he takes from him all his armor in which he trusted, and divides his spoils" (Luke 11:22). Hadadezer's gold shields were exactly that kind of armor, the strength an enemy trusted until a stronger king took it away. Paul says that at the cross Christ disarmed "principalities and powers, He made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them in it" (Colossians 2:15).

The pattern continues beyond the battlefield. Joash was hidden in God's house until the right time, and then he was revealed and crowned (2 Kings 11:12). The New Testament does not call Joash a type of Christ, but the story echoes the gospel: an enemy tries to destroy the royal seed, God preserves the promised King, and a priest presents Him to the people. The psalmist prays, "O God, behold our shield, And look upon the face of Your anointed" (Psalm 84:9). That verse uses a different Hebrew word for shield, but it expresses the same hope. The king was meant to protect his people, and the King God preserved now protects those who belong to Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Christians are not defended by untested weapons. When Joash's guards took their places, they held weapons that had belonged to King David. Scripture does not say those particular shields had seen David's battles, but they came from the king whose victories God had given. The believer's security works in a similar way, by application. It rests on a victory Christ has already won, and that victory is kept safe in God's presence. When accusation, fear, or spiritual opposition comes against God's purposes in a believer's life, the defense is what Christ has already accomplished.

Joash was safest while he was hidden in the temple. He did not survive by his own strength. He survived because he was in God's house, under the care of God's priest.

By application, Song 4:4 and Ezekiel 27:11 also call for honest self-examination. People can hang other kinds of borrowed shields on their walls: reputation, money, connections, or an appearance of spiritual strength. Tyre looked perfect and sank. The Bride's strength came from belonging to the King. Real security is being His.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Chronicles 18:7 — The Chronicler repeats that David took "the shields of gold that were on the servants of Hadadezer." Recording the event twice keeps the plunder of David's victories in view for Israel's history.

  • 2 Chronicles 23:9 — The NKJV includes sheleṭ in the phrase "the large and small shields." The account emphasizes that these weapons were David's and that they were kept in the temple of God.

The remaining occurrences are discussed in Section 5.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H4043 (magen) — the common Hebrew word for shield, often translated "buckler." It is a different word, but it appears with sheleṭ in Song 4:4 ("a thousand bucklers") and in 2 Chronicles 23:9, where the two kinds of shields are named side by side.
