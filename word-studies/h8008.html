GARMENT, CLOTHES - (salmâh, Strong's H8008)

Summary

The Hebrew word salmâh names an ordinary garment, especially the outer cloak an Israelite wore by day and slept under at night. For a poor man, this cloak might be the only blanket he owned. Scripture uses this everyday item to show how closely God pays attention to ordinary life. God commands that a poor neighbor's cloak, taken as a pledge for a loan, be returned before sunset. He promises to hear the cry of the man left shivering without it. In the wilderness He kept Israel's clothes from wearing out for forty years. The prophet Ahijah also tore a new garment into twelve pieces to announce that God would divide Solomon's kingdom.

The same word also carries some of the Bible's most striking images. Job says that his own clothes would "abhor" him before a holy God. The psalmist pictures the Lord covering Himself "with light as with a garment." The bridegroom in the Song of Solomon delights in the fragrance of his bride's garments. Together these passages set a frail, needy humanity, whose coverings wear out, get torn and become soiled, beside a God who guards the poor man's cloak and is Himself clothed in light. For Christian readers that contrast points toward Christ, who comes from that light to clothe and sustain His people.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Salmâh is one of the ordinary Hebrew words for clothing. In the ancient world, cloth was costly and took a great deal of labor to make, so a family owned few garments and depended on each one. The outer cloak served as a coat by day and as bedding at night, and the law describes it as a poor man's "only covering" (Exodus 22:27). That background explains why the word appears in laws about lending and theft, in reports of tribute and spoil, and in poetry that uses clothing to picture a person's condition before God. The word occurs sixteen times, in the Law, the historical books, the poetic books and the prophet Micah.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Salmâh means a garment or clothing. Context shows two closely related uses:

1. A single outer garment or cloak worn by one person, such as the pledged cloak a poor man sleeps in (Exodus 22:26) or the "new garment" Ahijah wore (1 Kings 11:29).

2. Clothing in general, often in the plural, such as the clothes that did not wear out in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 29:5) or the garments brought to Solomon as gifts (1 Kings 10:25).

The word itself means cloth that a person wears. The deeper meanings in Job, the Psalms and the Song of Solomon come from how those passages use the image.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Law, salmâh is valuable property that needs legal protection. Clothing stands beside an ox, a donkey and a sheep as goods that could be stolen or disputed before the judges (Exodus 22:9). In the historical books, garments are wealth. Returning soldiers carry them home as spoil (Joshua 22:8), and kings bring them to Solomon as yearly gifts (1 Kings 10:25; 2 Chronicles 9:24).

The condition of a garment can also tell a story about the person wearing it. The Gibeonites put on "old garments" to fake a long journey (Joshua 9:5). Ahijah's garment is "new" before he tears it (1 Kings 11:29–30). In poetry the word becomes a picture of something larger: defilement in Job 9:31, divine glory in Psalm 104:2 and the beloved's fragrance in Song of Solomon 4:11.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God who notices the smallest needs of His people. He writes a law about one cloak and one cold night, and He ties that law to His own character. The garment shows His justice and mercy toward the vulnerable. It also shows His power to sustain His people, since clothes ordinarily wear out but did not in the wilderness. He is also the Judge, and He can tear apart a kingdom that looks newly made and secure.

Above all, the word marks the distance between Creator and creature. People need clothing because they are exposed, frail and ashamed. God needs nothing to protect Him, yet Scripture borrows this humblest piece of human life to describe His majesty. The everyday cloak becomes a way of seeing both our need and His glory.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The pledged cloak (Exodus 22:26–27; Deuteronomy 24:13; Micah 2:8). A lender could take a poor neighbor's garment as security for a loan, but he had to return it "before the sun goes down." God gives the reason in plain, tender terms: "For that is his only covering, it is his garment for his skin. What will he sleep in?" He then adds, "when he cries to Me, I will hear, for I am gracious." God places Himself beside the cold man in the dark and listens for his voice. Deuteronomy carries this further. When the cloak is returned, the borrower may "sleep in his own garment and bless you; and it shall be righteousness to you before the LORD your God." God counts a returned cloak as righteousness.

Micah describes the opposite. Israel had become a nation where people "pull off the robe with the garment From those who trust you, as they pass by" (Micah 2:8). The law forbade keeping a poor man's cloak overnight. Micah's generation stripped it from travelers who trusted them as they passed by. How a people treated this single garment showed how far they had moved from God's gracious heart.

Clothes that did not wear out (Deuteronomy 29:5; Nehemiah 9:21; Joshua 9). At the end of the wilderness years, Moses speaks for the Lord: "I have led you forty years in the wilderness. Your clothes have not worn out on you." Deuteronomy 8:3–4 puts this miracle beside the manna, so that Israel would learn that "man lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of the LORD." Every morning for forty years, Israel's clothing was quiet evidence that God was holding back ordinary decay. Centuries later the returned exiles still praised Him for it: "They lacked nothing; Their clothes did not wear out" (Nehemiah 9:21).

Joshua 9 follows soon afterward and makes a sharp contrast. The Gibeonites, who lived nearby, put on "old garments" and claimed their clothes had grown old on a very long journey (Joshua 9:13). Israel's clothing showed what God had done. The Gibeonites' worn clothing was a costume meant to deceive. Israel's leaders judged by appearances and "did not ask counsel of the LORD" (Joshua 9:14). The contrast lies in the clothes themselves. Israel's unworn garments testified to God's care, while the Gibeonites' worn garments were a staged imitation of hardship, and Israel's leaders accepted that evidence without seeking the Lord.

The torn new garment (1 Kings 11:29–31). Ahijah meets Jeroboam alone in a field, wearing "a new garment." He tears it "into twelve pieces" and tells Jeroboam to take ten, explaining the sign himself: "Behold, I will tear the kingdom out of the hand of Solomon and will give ten tribes to you." The garment stands for the twelve tribes, and its newness matters. The kingdom under David and Solomon was young, rich and apparently secure, yet Solomon's idolatry tore it apart. Earlier, Samuel's robe tore in Saul's grip and Samuel declared, "The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today" (1 Samuel 15:27–28). Israel's history had already shown that a kingdom which turns from God can be torn away like cloth.

Clothes that abhor and a God clothed in light (Job 9:30–31; Psalm 104:1–2). Job imagines making himself as clean as he possibly could: "If I wash myself with snow water, And cleanse my hands with soap." Yet before God he would still lose: "Yet You will plunge me into the pit, And my own clothes will abhor me." Job is not confessing hidden crimes. He is describing what it is like to argue his case before an infinitely holy Judge. No washing he could do would win his case, and even his own cloak would draw back from him. In the same speech Job laments that God "is not a man, as I am," that they could "go to court together," and that there is no mediator between them "Who may lay his hand on us both" (Job 9:32–33). His longing is for someone who could bring him and God together so that he could plead his case without dread (9:34–35).

Psalm 104 sets God beside that picture. The psalm opens, "You are clothed with honor and majesty," and continues, "Who cover Yourself with light as with a garment." The psalmist takes the most ordinary word for a cloak and applies it to the Lord. Human garments hide our weakness, keep out the cold and cover our shame. God's "garment" hides nothing. His covering is light, which reveals Him and comes from His own nature. Paul describes Him as "dwelling in unapproachable light" (1 Timothy 6:16), and John writes that "God is light and in Him is no darkness at all" (1 John 1:5).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The God who wears light as a garment came near in Jesus Christ. On the mountain of transfiguration, Jesus' clothes became as white as the light (Matthew 17:2). Matthew does not cite Psalm 104, but the scene is a vivid parallel: for a moment the disciples saw the One whose clothing shone with the light the psalmist described. Job was not predicting Christ when he lamented that he had no mediator; he was voicing his own complaint about how to plead before God. The New Testament, however, teaches directly that "there is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5), and Hebrews calls Him "the Mediator of the new covenant" (Hebrews 9:15). A Christian reading Job's cry can see in Jesus the answer Job could not name, the One who stands with both God and man.

The story of the garment also reaches the cross. Micah condemned those who stripped travelers of their cloaks. Jesus allowed Himself to be stripped, and soldiers divided His garments among themselves (John 19:23–24). He was left exposed so that those whose own clothes "abhor" them could be clothed in His righteousness. The New Testament does not directly link these passages, but the pattern fits the whole story of God covering the naked, which begins with the garments He made for Adam and Eve.

The torn kingdom also looks forward to Christ. Ahijah's twelve pieces were not God's final word. The prophets promised that God's divided people would again become one nation under one Shepherd from David's line (Ezekiel 37:22–24). Jesus, the Son of David, gathers a people that cannot be torn apart. Even the heavens, which Psalm 104:2 pictures God stretching out "like a curtain," will one day "grow old like a garment" (Psalm 102:26). Yet Hebrews 1:10–12 applies Psalm 102 to the Son and declares that He remains unchanged. Those words use a different Hebrew word for garment, but they confirm the pattern: everything else wears out, and Christ does not.

Finally, the bridegroom in the Song tells his bride, "the fragrance of your garments Is like the fragrance of Lebanon" (Song of Solomon 4:11). Lebanon was known for its cedar forests and their fresh scent. A cloak takes on the smell of the places where its wearer has been. Read in light of Scripture's larger picture of Christ and His bride, the church (Ephesians 5:25–27), this verse suggests that the people who spend time with the King begin to carry His fragrance.

7. Spirit & Life

The law of the pledged cloak shows what God hears at night. He listens for the man who cannot sleep because he is cold. He does not regard that man's need as too small to deserve a law, and He does not treat its return as a minor courtesy. A believer can take heart from this. The God who notices one cloak in one cold tent also notices our ordinary needs. The same law also tells His people how to treat others, because God counts even the return of a poor man's pledged cloak as righteousness.

The wilderness clothes show one way God sustained His people. The same wilderness account recalls the pillar of cloud and fire, the manna and the water from the rock (Nehemiah 9:12–21). Yet God's faithfulness was also shown in the clothes that lasted, a cloak that should have been threadbare and was not, one ordinary day after another. That gave His care an ordinary, daily expression. Many believers can look back and see a similar faithfulness in seasons they once thought would wear them out. The Gibeonites add a warning. Appearances can be staged, and God's people need to ask counsel of the Lord instead of trusting what looks convincing.

Ahijah's torn garment cautions anyone who trusts in fresh strength, new structures or impressive beginnings. His garment was new, yet it tore, and Solomon's young kingdom proved no safer when God judged its idolatry.

Job and Psalm 104 together give the deepest lesson. Job spoke of snow water and soap while arguing over how he could plead his case before God, but his words voice something true of every person: our best efforts to cleanse ourselves cannot make us acceptable before Him. The God who wears light did not stay distant from that problem. In Christ He comes to clothe us. A believer can stop trying to wash himself clean and can rest in the righteousness God provides. And if the Song's fragrant garments are read as a picture of Christ and His people, a life spent near the King can begin to carry the fragrance of His presence.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 22:9 — Clothing appears beside an ox, a donkey and a sheep as property worth taking to the judges, which shows how valuable a garment was.

  • Joshua 22:8 — The eastern tribes return home with "very much clothing" among their spoil, a sign of God's blessing after the conquest.

  • 1 Kings 10:25 — Kings who came to hear Solomon's wisdom brought "articles of silver and gold, garments, armor, spices" each year. The scene gives a small preview of the nations bringing their honor to God's King (compare Isaiah 60; Revelation 21:26).

  • 2 Chronicles 9:24 — The Chronicler repeats the same report of tribute, confirming the honor God gave Solomon's reign at its height.
