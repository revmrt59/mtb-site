WOMAN, WIFE - (ʼishshâh, Strong's H0802)

Summary

Ishshâh is the ordinary Hebrew word for a woman. Hebrew has no separate word for "wife," so the same word names both a woman and a married woman. "His woman" means "his wife," and context decides which English word fits. The word runs from the creation of Eve to Malachi's teaching about marriage. It names the matriarchs and the women protected in Israel's laws. It names mothers, widows, prophetesses, queens, and the wise and foolish women of Proverbs. In a handful of places it simply means "each" or "one another."

Scripture uses this everyday word for some of its weightiest moments. A woman is God's answer to the first thing He called "not good." After the Fall, God names the woman in His first promise of a Deliverer, and that promise reaches its goal when God's Son is born of a woman. The prophets use the same word to picture Israel as the LORD's unfaithful wife, and Hosea is told to keep loving an adulterous woman just as the LORD keeps loving Israel. Malachi declares that the LORD Himself stands as witness between a man and the wife of his youth. Across the Old Testament, the way a people treats its women shows how faithfully that people is walking with God.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Ishshâh is one of the most common nouns in the Hebrew Old Testament. It appears in every kind of writing: family stories and genealogies, laws, battle reports ("the men, women, and little ones"), wisdom sayings, and prophetic oracles. Genesis uses it most, because Genesis is full of marriages, households, and the women through whom God's promises were carried.

The word sounds like ʼîsh, "man," and Genesis 2:23 plays on that likeness. English keeps the effect: "She shall be called Woman, Because she was taken out of Man." Scholars debate how the two words are related historically. The point Genesis makes rests on the sound, which shows that she belongs alongside him.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Woman, female. A human female as distinct from a male, as when the LORD makes the rib "into a woman" (Genesis 2:22). In Genesis 7:2 the word even describes animals entering the ark as "male and his female."

2. Wife. A woman in her married relationship, usually marked by a possessive: "his wife," "your wife," "the wife of Uriah." Genesis 2:24 moves directly from "woman" to "be joined to his wife."

3. Each, one another. In an idiom used with feminine nouns, "a woman to her sister" means "one to another," as with the curtains of the tabernacle in Exodus 26:3.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most of the time the possessive decides the sense. Sarai is "a woman of beautiful countenance" and also Abram's "wife" in the same verse (Genesis 12:11).

Hebrew often places ishshâh beside a second noun that describes the woman's situation: "a woman, a widow" (1 Kings 17:9), "a woman, a prophetess" (Judges 4:4), "a woman, a harlot" (Judges 11:1). This is why the NKJV sometimes renders the word as "widow," "prophetess," or "harlot." The first noun names the person, and the second tells her circumstance.

The distributive idiom deserves a closer look. The tabernacle's curtains are coupled "one to another," and its boards have tenons fitted the same way (Exodus 26:3, 5, 17). Ezekiel uses the same expression for the wings of the living creatures that touch "one another" beneath the throne of God (Ezekiel 1:9, 23; 3:13). This is grammar, not a hidden definition. Still, it is striking that Hebrew describes how God's dwelling and the creatures around His throne hold together by saying "a woman to her sister."

4. Theological Significance

The passages using ishshâh show a God who notices women and holds men accountable for how they treat them. Israel's law spoke into a hard world and set limits on it. A captive woman taken as a wife had to be given a month to mourn her parents. If her husband later rejected her, he had to let her go free, and he could not sell her or treat her as property, because he had humbled her (Deuteronomy 21:11–14). A husband's jealous suspicion was brought before the LORD in Numbers 5 instead of being settled by his own anger. A newly married man was kept home from war for a year to bring happiness to his wife (Deuteronomy 24:5).

Judges shows what happens when those boundaries collapse. In Judges 19 a Levite's concubine is handed over to a mob, abused through the night, and found at dawn fallen at the door with her hands on the threshold (Judges 19:26). The book closes by explaining that everyone did what was right in his own eyes (Judges 21:25). The violence done to one woman lays bare a nation that has forsaken its God.

Proverbs carries the word into wisdom. A prudent wife comes from the LORD (Proverbs 19:14), and a man who finds a wife finds a good thing (Proverbs 18:22). The "seductress" and the adulterous woman lead to death (Proverbs 2:16; 6:24–32; 7:5–10). In chapter 9, a foolish woman calls from the heights of the city in the same way Wisdom does, inviting the simple to stolen waters that end in the depths of the grave. The book closes with the woman who fears the LORD (Proverbs 31:10, 30). These are practical warnings about marriage and sexual purity. They also set before every reader two voices and two houses, and a choice between them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 2:18–25: God's answer to "not good." Everything in creation was good until the LORD said, "It is not good that man should be alone" (2:18). His answer was a person, not an idea. He built the rib "into a woman, and He brought her to the man" (2:22). God made her, and God brought her. Adam's first recorded words are a cry of recognition: "This is now bone of my bones And flesh of my flesh" (2:23). Then the same word slides from "woman" into "wife": a man "shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife," and the two were "naked... and were not ashamed" (2:24–25). Marriage begins in creation, arranged by God's own hand.

Genesis 3:1–16: from accusation to promise. The serpent approaches "the woman" and questions God's word (3:1). Once she and her husband have eaten, the delight of 2:23 turns to blame: "The woman whom You gave to be with me, she gave me of the tree" (3:12). Adam now treats God's gift as an accusation against God. The judgment, however, holds a surprise. Speaking to the serpent, the LORD puts enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between his offspring and hers. He declares that her offspring will crush the serpent's head while the serpent bruises his heel (3:15, paraphrased). The woman the serpent deceived becomes the one through whom his defeat will come.

Jeremiah 3, Ezekiel 16, Hosea 1–3: the LORD's unfaithful wife. The prophets take the ordinary word for "wife" and apply it to Israel. Jeremiah 3:1 recalls the law that a divorced wife who married another could not return to her first husband (Deuteronomy 24:1–4). Israel has gone further than that, playing the harlot with many lovers, and yet the LORD calls her to return to Him. He invites back what the law said could never come back. Later He grieves that as a wife treacherously leaves her husband, so Israel has dealt treacherously with Him (Jeremiah 3:20). Ezekiel calls Jerusalem an adulterous wife who takes strangers instead of her husband (Ezekiel 16:32). Hosea lives out the message: he is told to take a wife of harlotry (1:2), hears the LORD say "she is not My wife" (2:2), and is then told to go again and love a woman who is committing adultery, just as the LORD loves Israel while they turn to other gods (3:1). Idolatry is the wounding of a Husband, and His love goes after the one who wounded Him.

Malachi 2:14–15: the Witness at the wedding. The men of Judah weep at the altar and ask why the LORD no longer accepts their offerings. His answer is that He has been witness between them and the wife of their youth, whom they have betrayed, though she is their companion and their wife by covenant (paraphrased). A marriage has three parties. Dealing treacherously with one's wife is a sin against the God who heard the vows, and it closes the door of worship.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The promise of Genesis 3:15 waits through the whole Old Testament for the woman's offspring. Paul announces its arrival when he writes that in the fullness of time God sent forth His Son, born of a woman (Galatians 4:4). The Deliverer came by the route God named in Eden. Paul also extends the victory to Christ's people, assuring the Romans that the God of peace will soon crush Satan under their feet (Romans 16:20).

The prophetic marriage story also comes to rest in Christ. Ephesians 5:25–32 calls husbands to love their wives as Christ loved the church and gave Himself for her. Paul then quotes Genesis 2:24 and calls it a mystery that speaks of Christ and the church. The Husband whom Hosea and Jeremiah portrayed as betrayed now gives Himself to cleanse His bride. Isaiah had already pictured the LORD calling His people back like a wife forsaken and grieved, a young wife who had been cast off (Isaiah 54:6). Malachi's men abandoned the wives of their youth. The LORD does the opposite and goes back for His.

7. Spirit & Life

Genesis 2 teaches us to receive people as gifts placed in our lives by God. Genesis 3:12 shows how quickly sin rewrites a gift as a grievance: "the woman whom You gave." Bitterness in a marriage often sounds like that sentence. Repentance begins when we see the person again as God's provision and stop treating them as evidence against Him.

The prophets also change how we think about our own sin. God calls it unfaithfulness, and He speaks about it with the grief of a husband. That should make us more serious about sin and more hopeful at the same time. The God who said "Return to Me" through Jeremiah, and who sent Hosea back to an adulterous wife, goes beyond what anyone could rightly demand of Him. No one is too far gone to come home.

Malachi binds worship and the home together, and the New Testament keeps that bond. Peter tells husbands to honor their wives so that their prayers will not be hindered (1 Peter 3:7). A man cannot treat his wife carelessly on Saturday and expect easy fellowship with God on Sunday. The LORD was in the room when the vows were made, and He is still listening.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ruth 4:10 — Boaz declares, "Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife." A foreign widow is brought into the line that leads to David.

  • 1 Samuel 1:15 — Hannah calls herself a woman of sorrowful spirit who has poured out her soul before the LORD. Her prayer is answered with Samuel.

  • Isaiah 49:15 — The LORD asks whether a woman can forget her nursing child, then promises that even if she could, He will not forget His people.

  • 2 Kings 22:14 — Huldah the prophetess, the wife of Shallum, speaks the word of the LORD to King Josiah's officials.

  • Exodus 35:25–26 — Women whose hearts stirred them with wisdom spun yarn and goats' hair for the tabernacle.

  • Job 14:1 — Job laments that man born of woman lives few days, full of trouble.
