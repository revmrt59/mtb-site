NAME, RENOWN, FAME - (shêm, Strong's H8034)

Summary

The Hebrew word shêm means "name." It is the ordinary word for what a person, place, or thing is called, from the rivers of Eden to the sons of Jacob. In Hebrew usage, though, a name goes further than a label. It stands for how someone is known: their reputation and their remembered identity. Men of great reputation are "men of renown," a ruined reputation is a "bad name," and a man's name surviving in Israel means his family line and memory continue.

When Scripture speaks of the name of the LORD, it means God as He has made Himself known. At the burning bush and on Sinai, God revealed His name, and what He revealed was His character. He chose a place where His name would dwell. He saves His people "for His name's sake." He puts His name on them so that they belong to Him. The prophets announced the names of the coming Messiah, and those names describe exactly who Jesus would be. To trust the name of the LORD is to lean on God as He has shown Himself to be.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shêm is one of the most common nouns in the Old Testament. It appears in nearly every book and in every kind of writing: genealogies, census lists, place-name explanations, laws, prayers, psalms, and prophecy. The densest clusters are in Genesis, with its many namings and genealogies, and in the Psalms, where worship is directed again and again to God's name.

The narratives themselves show that a name often carried meaning in Israel. Parents frequently explain the names they give. Leah, for example, names each of her sons after what God had done for her (Gen 29:32–35). Places were named after what happened there, as when Abraham named the mountain of provision after the LORD's provision (Gen 22:14). This feature of the biblical text gives naming its weight. It does not mean every name hides a secret meaning, and the word shêm itself should not be read through speculative root meanings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shêm means "name," with a few distinct senses:

1. A personal or proper name, the designation of a person, animal, or place. Of the animals Adam called, Scripture says "that was its name" (Gen 2:19).

2. Reputation, renown, or fame: how someone is known and spoken of. The "men of renown" (Gen 6:4; Num 16:2), Solomon's fame among the nations (1 Kings 4:31), and the "good name" preferred over riches (Prov 22:1) all use this sense. It can also be negative, as with the bad name falsely given a bride (Deut 22:14) or Jerusalem made infamous (Ezek 22:5).

3. Continuing identity or memorial: a name that lives on after a person dies, especially through descendants. The law of the kinsman aimed to keep a dead man's name from being blotted out of Israel (Deut 25:6; Ruth 4:10).

4. The person himself as made known, especially in "the name of the LORD." Here the name stands for God as He has revealed Himself, as in "The name of the LORD is a strong tower" (Prov 18:10).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The most frequent pattern is the naming formula: someone "called his name" this or that. Usually it is a parent or the one with authority. When God does the naming or renaming, the act carries His purpose (see Section 4).

Several recurring phrases change the sense noticeably. To "call on the name of the LORD" (Gen 4:26; 12:8) means to worship and pray to Him. To act "in the name of the LORD" means to act by His authority and in reliance on Him, as David did when he faced Goliath (1 Sam 17:45). The phrase "for His name's sake" gives God's own reputation and character as the reason for His action. "Called by My name" marks people or places as belonging to God. The opposite is to have one's name "blotted out," which means erasure from memory and from the community.

The word also carries moral weight. God's name can be taken in vain (Ex 20:7), profaned (Lev 22:32), or honored. A name is never treated as a neutral label in these passages.

4. Theological Significance

The God of Scripture makes Himself known by name. He is not an unnamed force. He tells Moses who He is (Ex 3:13–15), and later He proclaims His name by describing His character (Ex 34:5–7, developed below). The phrase "the name of the LORD" therefore points to God's revealed self, His character and His presence among His people.

God also exercises authority over names. He let Adam name the creatures, which reflected Adam's stewardship under God (Gen 2:19–20). When God binds Himself to His people by promise, He often renames them. Abram becomes Abraham, father of many nations (Gen 17:5). Sarai becomes Sarah (Gen 17:15). Jacob, after wrestling through the night, becomes Israel (Gen 32:28; 35:10). Each new name declares what God intends to make of that person and shows that God has claimed them.

God then places His name on His people. The priestly blessing ends with God saying the priests will put His name on the children of Israel (Num 6:27). Israel is the people called by His name (2 Chron 7:14), and Isaiah says those called by His name were created for His glory (Isa 43:7). This is an identity of ownership and protection, so Jeremiah could plead in drought that God not abandon those called by His name (Jer 14:9). It is also a responsibility, because the people carry God's reputation before the nations. When they live wickedly, His name is dragged down with them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 3 and 34 — The Name Revealed as Character. Moses asks what he should tell Israel when they ask God's name (Ex 3:13). God answers with the self-description I AM and then identifies Himself as the LORD, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, adding that this is His name forever (Ex 3:14–15). Later Moses asks to see God's glory, and God promises to proclaim His name before him (Ex 33:19). When the LORD descends in the cloud and proclaims His name (Ex 34:5), what follows is a description of who He is: merciful and gracious, longsuffering, abounding in goodness and truth, forgiving iniquity, yet by no means clearing the guilty (Ex 34:6–7). A few verses later He says His name is Jealous (Ex 34:14). On Sinai, God's name is His character spoken aloud.

Genesis 11:4 and 12:2 — Making a Name or Receiving One. At Babel the people say, "let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth" (Gen 11:4). Their aim was security and lasting significance built by their own hands and set against God's command to fill the earth. God scattered them. In the very next chapter He calls Abram and promises that He Himself will make Abram's name great (Gen 12:2). Abram builds no tower. He builds altars and calls on the name of the LORD (Gen 12:8). Lasting significance is something God gives. The people who grasped for a name are remembered for confusion, and the man who received one is remembered as the father of the faithful.

Deuteronomy 12 and 1 Kings 8 — The Place of the Name. Moses told Israel to seek the place the LORD would choose to put His name (Deut 12:5, 11). Solomon's dedication prayer returns to the phrase over and over. God had chosen no city "in which to build a house, that My name might be there" (1 Kings 8:16), and Solomon has now "built a temple for the name of the LORD God of Israel" (8:20). Solomon is not naive about what a building can hold: "heaven and the heaven of heavens cannot contain You. How much less this temple which I have built!" (8:27). Yet he asks that God's eyes be open "toward the place of which You said, 'My name shall be there,' that You may hear the prayer" (8:29). The temple did not confine God. It was where He had committed Himself to be approached. The rest of the prayer shows what that meant: sinners, the defeated, the drought-stricken, and even foreigners could pray toward the place of the Name and be heard (8:33–43). The Name was God's presence made accessible to people who needed mercy. Later, Jeremiah warned that a temple called by God's name offered no safety to those who trusted it while living in sin, and he pointed to Shiloh as proof (Jer 7:10–14).

Psalm 23:3, Psalm 106:8, and Ezekiel 36 — For His Name's Sake. David says the Shepherd "leads me in the paths of righteousness for His name's sake" (Ps 23:3). Psalm 106 recalls Israel's rebellion at the Red Sea and then says, "Nevertheless He saved them for His name's sake, That He might make His mighty power known" (106:8). The word "nevertheless" carries the point: the reason for the rescue lay in God, not in Israel. Ezekiel presses this further. In exile, Israel profaned God's holy name among the nations, because the nations looked at the scattered people and mocked their God (Ezek 36:20). God declares that He will act for His holy name, not for Israel's sake, and will show His great name to be holy by restoring them (36:21–23; compare 20:9, 14, 22). This may sound cold at first, but it is the firmest ground of hope Israel had. Their sins could not cancel a rescue that rested on God's own faithfulness to Himself.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets used shêm to announce the Messiah. The virgin's son would be called Immanuel, God with us (Isa 7:14). The child born for us would carry names such as Wonderful, Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, and Prince of Peace (Isa 9:6). The Righteous Branch from David would be named the LORD our righteousness (Jer 23:6), and Zechariah speaks of the Man whose name is the Branch, who will build the temple of the LORD (Zech 6:12). These names describe the person and His work.

The New Testament brings these threads together in Jesus. His name, given by the angel, is explained by His mission: He will save His people from their sins (Matt 1:21). The God who once put His name in a temple now dwells among His people in the Son, and Jesus describes His ministry as having made the Father's name known (John 17:6). Joel's promise that whoever calls on the name of the LORD will be delivered (Joel 2:32) is applied to calling on Jesus (Rom 10:13). God has given Him the name above every name (Phil 2:9), and salvation is found in no other name (Acts 4:12). The New Testament speaks of this name with the Greek word onoma, but the weight that word carries comes from the Old Testament shêm. The story ends where God's purpose has always pointed. His servants will see His face, and His name will be on their foreheads (Rev 22:4). Ezekiel's restored city bears the name THE LORD IS THERE (Ezek 48:35).

7. Spirit & Life

"The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run to it and are safe" (Prov 18:10). The proverb gives a picture of running for cover. What the righteous run to is God as He has revealed Himself: merciful, faithful, just, the One who saved at the sea. Psalm 20 draws the same line in wartime: "Some trust in chariots, and some in horses; But we will remember the name of the LORD our God" (20:7). To remember His name means calling to mind who He has shown Himself to be and what He has done, and setting that against the visible strength of the enemy.

"For His name's sake" frees a troubled conscience. A believer who knows his failures can still pray with confidence, because God's commitment to save rests on His own character. David prays for pardon on that ground (Ps 25:11), and so can we. The one who leads us in right paths has staked His reputation on finishing the work.

Bearing His name also carries joy and seriousness together. Jeremiah, in the middle of persecution, rejoiced that he was called by God's name (Jer 15:16). The same name that shelters us is the name our conduct either honors or profanes, and Ezekiel shows how quickly the watching world judges God by His people. Even the overlooked are not forgotten. To the eunuch who could leave no descendants, God promises a name better than sons and daughters, an everlasting name (Isa 56:5). When those who fear the LORD meditate on His name, He writes a book of remembrance for them (Mal 3:16).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 4:26 — After Seth's son is born, people begin to call on the name of the LORD. This is the first mention of public worship directed to God's name.

  • Exodus 28:9–12 — The high priest wears onyx stones engraved with the names of Israel's sons and carries them before the LORD as a memorial. The people are represented in God's presence by name.

  • 1 Samuel 17:45 — David meets Goliath's sword and spear in the name of the LORD of hosts. Acting in God's name means acting on God's authority.

  • Malachi 1:11 — Even while priests in Jerusalem despised His name, God declares that His name will be great among the nations, from the rising to the setting of the sun.

  • Zechariah 14:9 — On the coming day the LORD will be King over all the earth, and His name will be the one name.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • G3686 (onoma), "name" — The New Testament Greek word for "name." It is not a lexical relative of shêm, but it carries the Old Testament theology of the name into New Testament usage, as in calling on the name of the Lord (Rom 10:13, citing Joel 2:32).
