SHEM - (Shêm, Strong's H8035)

Summary

Shem was the son of Noah, the brother of Ham and Japheth, and one of the eight people who came through the Flood alive. His name is spelled exactly like the ordinary Hebrew word for "name," and it carries the sense of renown or reputation. That simple meaning becomes surprisingly important in Genesis. The builders of Babel tried to "make a name" for themselves and were scattered. The very next section of Genesis traces the family line of the man named Shem down to Abram, to whom God promised to bless him "And make your name great" (Genesis 12:2). Genesis does not let people build a lasting name through pride. God gives it, and He gives it through Shem's line.

Shem appears mostly in Genesis 5–11 and in the genealogies of 1 Chronicles 1, but his role is pivotal. When Noah was shamed in his tent, Shem and Japheth covered their father without looking. Afterward Noah spoke the words "Blessed be the LORD, The God of Shem" (Genesis 9:26). This is the first time Scripture calls the LORD the God of a particular person, and it points ahead to "the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob." Shem forms the bridge between the world before the Flood and the story of Abraham. Luke's genealogy of Jesus passes through "the son of Shem, the son of Noah" (Luke 3:36). The God of Shem kept His word across many generations until He brought the promised Seed into the world.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H8035 refers to one person, Shem the son of Noah. All seventeen occurrences, across sixteen verses (Genesis 5:32 through 1 Chronicles 1:24), point to him; Genesis 11:10 names him twice. His brothers were Ham and Japheth. The New Testament gives his name in Greek as Sēm (G4590) in Luke 3:36, and that form is indexed separately.

The name Shem is identical in Hebrew to the common noun shem (H8034), which means "name." The word also carries the ideas of fame, renown, and reputation. Strong's lists the two as separate entries, one for the personal name and one for the common word, but they have the same form. Genesis itself does not explain why Noah gave his son this name. Readers should not assume that the meaning of the name sums up Shem's life. Genesis 11–12 does make deliberate literary use of the word, though. It sets the Babel builders' grasping for a shem beside the genealogy of the man Shem and God's promise of a great shem to Abram. That is a wordplay within the narrative, and it is different from a claim that his name predicted his destiny.

Genesis usually lists the brothers in the order "Shem, Ham, and Japheth" (Genesis 5:32; 6:10; 7:13). This order may reflect Shem's importance rather than his birth order. Genesis 10:21 can be read in Hebrew in two ways. The NKJV renders it "Shem, the father of all the children of Eber, the brother of Japheth the elder," which makes Japheth the older brother. Other translations make Shem the elder. If the NKJV reading is right, Shem's place at the head of the list rests on God's choice rather than on birthright.

His descendants also gave rise to the modern word "Semitic." That modern language category does not line up exactly with the family groupings of Genesis 10, so the two should not be treated as equivalent.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Before and Through the Flood

Shem first appears in the summary statement that "Noah was five hundred years old, and Noah begot Shem, Ham, and Japheth" (Genesis 5:32). That summary does not mean all three were born in that year; Genesis 7:6 and 11:10 imply that Shem was born when Noah was about 502 and was about 98 at the Flood. He is named again among Noah's sons in the years when violence filled the earth (Genesis 6:10). On the day the Flood began, Shem entered the ark with his father, his mother, his brothers, and their wives (Genesis 7:13). He was part of the small family God preserved when He judged the world, and he came out of the ark to begin the world again (Genesis 9:18).

The Covering of Noah

After the Flood, Noah planted a vineyard, became drunk, and lay uncovered in his tent. Ham "saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brothers outside" (Genesis 9:22, paraphrased closely). Shem and Japheth responded very differently: "But Shem and Japheth took a garment, laid it on both their shoulders, and went backward and covered the nakedness of their father. Their faces were turned away, and they did not see their father's nakedness" (Genesis 9:23). In Hebrew the verb "took" is singular, and some readers take that to mean Shem led the way. Either way, the two brothers deliberately protected their father's dignity rather than adding to his disgrace.

Noah's Words over His Sons

When Noah awoke, he spoke about the future of his family. He pronounced a curse, but he placed it on Canaan, Ham's son, and not on Ham himself (Genesis 9:25). Then he said, "Blessed be the LORD, The God of Shem, And may Canaan be his servant" (Genesis 9:26). For Japheth he said, "May God enlarge Japheth, And may he dwell in the tents of Shem" (Genesis 9:27).

Noah's blessing on Shem is unusual because it speaks first about God. Noah does not praise Shem's strength or promise him wealth. He blesses the LORD and names Him as Shem's God.

Verse 27 leaves a question open. The "he" who dwells in Shem's tents could be Japheth, meaning that Japheth's descendants would one day share in Shem's blessing. It could also be God, meaning that He would make His dwelling among Shem's descendants. The Hebrew allows both readings.

The Table of Nations

Genesis 10 traces how the families of Noah's sons spread across the earth. Shem is introduced with a title his brothers do not receive: "the father of all the children of Eber" (Genesis 10:21). Eber's line leads on toward Abram, who is later called "the Hebrew" (Genesis 14:13). Many connect that title with Eber's name, though Genesis does not spell out the link.

Shem's sons were Elam, Asshur, Arphaxad, Lud, and Aram (Genesis 10:22). Several of these names are tied to well-known peoples. Asshur is linked with Assyria, Aram with the Arameans of Syria, and Elam with the land east of Mesopotamia. The identity of Lud is less certain. The chapter closes by describing Shem's descendants "according to their families, according to their languages, in their lands, according to their nations" (Genesis 10:31, paraphrased).

From Babel to Abram

Genesis 11 opens with people united in one plan: "let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:4). God came down, confused their language, and scattered them. The narrative then turns to "the genealogy of Shem" (Genesis 11:10). It records that Shem was one hundred years old when he fathered Arphaxad two years after the Flood, and that he lived five hundred more years (Genesis 11:10–11). The list moves generation by generation to Terah and then to Abram (Genesis 11:26).

According to the Hebrew (Masoretic) figures, Shem's long life overlapped with Abraham's. The Greek Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch give different ages for these patriarchs, so the chronology should not be pressed too hard.

Later Record

After the exile, the Chronicler opens Israel's story with Adam and moves through "Noah, Shem, Ham, and Japheth" (1 Chronicles 1:4). He lists Shem's sons (1 Chronicles 1:17) and retraces the line "Shem, Arphaxad, Shelah" toward Abram (1 Chronicles 1:24). A people returning from exile were reminded that their story reached back through Shem to the beginning.

Luke's genealogy then traces Jesus back through Abraham to "the son of Shem, the son of Noah" (Luke 3:36). Luke places a Cainan between Arphaxad and Shelah, as the Septuagint of Genesis 11 does. The Hebrew lists go directly from Arphaxad to Shelah. Matthew begins his genealogy with Abraham, so Shem appears only in Luke.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God preserved Shem through the Flood, that Noah blessed the LORD as "the God of Shem," and that the line from Shem leads to Abram and finally to Jesus. These facts give Shem his real importance. After the judgment of the Flood and the scattering at Babel, God did not abandon the nations. He narrowed His attention to one family line, and through it He would bring blessing to all of them (Genesis 12:3).

The phrase "the God of Shem" deserves particular attention. Until Genesis 9:26, God has not been described as belonging to anyone in this way. Noah's words announce a relationship. The LORD binds His name to a person and to that person's descendants. The same pattern runs through the rest of Scripture, as God becomes known as the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob (Exodus 3:6). Shem's greatest inheritance was the LORD Himself.

The placement of Shem's genealogy right after Babel is clearly intentional. Genesis 11:4 and 11:10 sit side by side, and Genesis 12:2 follows soon after. People who try to win a name in defiance of God end in confusion. The line of Shem receives a name as a gift. This is a careful reading of how Genesis is arranged, and the shared word shem makes the contrast hard to miss.

If Japheth was the elder brother, as the NKJV renders Genesis 10:21, Shem fits a pattern seen across Genesis and beyond. God passes over the expected heir and chooses Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, and later David over his older brothers. Shem's place is a matter of God's choosing.

Noah's curse fell on Canaan, not on Ham's descendants in general. That detail matters, because Genesis 9 has sometimes been misused in ways the text does not support. Later Scripture also shows mercy reaching Canaanites who turned to the LORD, such as Rahab (Joshua 2; 6:25).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest connection is explicit. Luke 3:36 names Shem among the ancestors of Jesus. God promised that the Seed of the woman would bruise the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15). That promise passed through Seth and Noah to Shem, then through Shem to Abraham, and through Abraham to Christ. Shem is a real link in the human ancestry of the Son of God, and the Bible treats him that way.

The wordplay on "name" also develops across the canon. The world tried to make a name for itself at Babel. God made Abram's name great. In the end the Father gave His Son "the name which is above every name" (Philippians 2:9, paraphrased) because He humbled Himself rather than grasping. This follows from the way Genesis frames the theme. The New Testament does not cite Shem in this connection.

Noah's words that Japheth would "dwell in the tents of Shem" find a fitting realization in the gospel. Gentiles from the nations now share in blessings that came through Israel's Messiah (Galatians 3:8; Ephesians 2:12–13). If God is the one dwelling in Shem's tents, the verse also resonates with the Word who "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14, paraphrased) as a descendant of Shem. These are thematic connections. Scripture does not name them as direct fulfillments of Genesis 9:27, but they follow the direction the promise was already moving.

The covering of Noah also resonates with the gospel. God clothed Adam and Eve after their sin (Genesis 3:21), and the gospel speaks of shame covered by Another's provision. Shem's act is not presented as a prophecy, but it fits a pattern Scripture values highly: "love covers all sins" (Proverbs 10:12), and "love will cover a multitude of sins" (1 Peter 4:8).

Ancient Jewish tradition identified Shem with Melchizedek, partly because the Hebrew chronology lets Shem live into Abraham's day. Scripture never makes that identification. Hebrews also presents Melchizedek as one whose genealogy is not recorded (Hebrews 7:3), while Shem's genealogy is recorded in detail. The tradition is a piece of reception history, not biblical teaching.

5. Spirit & Life

Noah's blessing tells us what God counts as true wealth. He did not say, "Blessed be Shem." He said, "Blessed be the LORD, The God of Shem." The best thing anyone can inherit is not a reputation, a fortune, or even a strong family name. It is to belong to the LORD and to have Him call Himself your God. Every believer who has come to God through Christ shares in that same blessing.

Genesis 11 and 12 confront a very human desire. The Babel builders wanted to be known, secure, and remembered, and they wanted it on their own terms. God scattered that effort. Then, through a list of names most readers skim past, He quietly carried His promise forward to a man He would call and bless. We do not have to build our own significance. God gives a name to those who receive His call. Often He does it through long, unremarkable stretches of faithfulness that look like nothing more than a genealogy.

The covering of Noah shows what love looks like when someone we respect has failed. Ham looked and told others. Shem and Japheth walked backward with a garment on their shoulders. They did not deny what had happened, but they refused to make their father's shame a spectacle. God dealt with our sin in a similar way. He did not ignore it. He covered it at great cost in Christ. People who have been covered in that way learn to cover others rather than expose them.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 5:32; 6:10; 7:13 – Shem's birth, his place among Noah's sons, and his entry into the ark.

  • Genesis 9:18–23 – Shem leaves the ark and, with Japheth, covers his father's shame.

  • Genesis 9:26–27 – "Blessed be the LORD, The God of Shem," the first time the LORD is called the God of a person, and the promise about the tents of Shem.

  • Genesis 10:21–22, 31 – Shem as "the father of all the children of Eber," his sons, and the nations descended from him.

  • Genesis 11:4, 10–11 – The Babel builders seek a name, followed immediately by the genealogy of Shem and his lifespan.

  • Genesis 12:2 – God promises to make Abram's name great, completing the contrast with Babel.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:4, 17, 24 – Israel's post-exilic record retracing its roots through Shem.

  • Luke 3:36 – Shem named in the genealogy of Jesus Christ.

  • Genesis 3:15 – The promise of the Seed that passes through Shem's line.

  • Proverbs 10:12; 1 Peter 4:8 – Love that covers sin, echoing Shem's response to his father.
