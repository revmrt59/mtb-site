ASSHUR - (ʼAshshûwr, Strong's H0804)

Summary

Asshur is the Hebrew name for Assyria. Scripture uses the name for a man, a land, and an empire: Asshur the son of Shem (Genesis 10:22), the land along the upper Tigris that bore his name, and the empire that rose from that land to dominate the ancient Near East. The meaning of the name is uncertain. Older lexical tradition proposes "a step" or "successful," but neither meaning is firmly established. What is clear is that Asshur was also the name of Assyria's chief god and its original capital city, so the name carried the nation's religious and political identity together. Assyria is a major biblical subject. It appears from Genesis through Zechariah, and in the eighth and seventh centuries BC it became the power that destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel, nearly swallowed Judah, and filled the preaching of Isaiah, Hosea, Micah, and Nahum.

Scripture treats Assyria as more than an enemy army. God calls it "the rod of My anger" (Isaiah 10:5). He used it to discipline His unfaithful people, and He then judged it for the arrogance of a king who thought he had done it all himself. Assyria exposes the folly of trusting worldly power. Ahaz bought its help with temple silver, and Hosea mourned that Ephraim ran to Assyria for a cure it could not give. Assyria also shows the faithfulness of God, who struck down Sennacherib's army when Hezekiah spread the enemy's threats before Him. The story does not end in ruin. Micah sets "the Assyrian" before the coming Ruler from Bethlehem, who "shall be peace" (Micah 5:5), and Isaiah foresees a day when God will say, "Blessed is Egypt My people, and Assyria the work of My hands, and Israel My inheritance" (Isaiah 19:25). The empire that once carried God's people away is finally pictured as a people gathered in to worship Him.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The lemma ʼAshshûwr names a person, a land, and a nation. Readers need to keep the three distinct, even though Scripture moves easily between them.

Asshur the person. Asshur is listed as a son of Shem, alongside Elam, Arphaxad, Lud, and Aram (Genesis 10:22; 1 Chronicles 1:17). The Table of Nations places the Assyrian people within Shem's family line. That means they are distant relatives of Abraham's line through Arphaxad.

Asshur the land. The name already appears in the Eden account. The Hiddekel, the Tigris, "goes toward the east of Assyria" (Genesis 2:14). Later, the Ishmaelites are said to dwell from Havilah to Shur "as you go toward Assyria" (Genesis 25:18). In these verses Asshur works as a geographical marker for the region along the upper Tigris in northern Mesopotamia.

Asshur the nation and empire. In most occurrences, the name means the Assyrian state, its king, or its people. English versions render it "Assyria," "the Assyrian," or "Assyrians." Its capital, Nineveh, is indexed separately (H5210). Individual Assyrian kings such as Tiglath-Pileser, Shalmaneser, Sargon, Sennacherib, and Esarhaddon also have their own entries.

One verse needs careful handling. Genesis 10:11 can be read in two ways. It may say that Nimrod went out from Shinar into Assyria and built Nineveh, which is the reading the NKJV follows. Or it may say that Asshur went out from that land and built it. Either way, the verse ties Assyria's great cities to the early spread of humanity after the flood, but the text does not settle who built them.

The name's meaning. The meaning of "Asshur" is uncertain. Traditional lexical suggestions include "a step" or "successful," but these are proposals rather than settled conclusions, and the Bible itself makes no wordplay on the name. More secure is the fact that Asshur was also the name of the Assyrians' national god and of their ancient capital city. The name of the land, the people, the old capital, and the chief deity were bound together. Assyria's power and its religion were one identity, and that fact explains why alliance with Assyria so quickly became a spiritual matter for Israel and Judah. The name's possible meaning does not define the nation's role in Scripture. Its biblical history does.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Early Glimpses and Balaam's Oracle

Before Assyria becomes a looming empire, it appears at the edges of the biblical story. It is a landmark beside the Tigris, which "goes toward the east of Assyria" (Genesis 2:14), a descendant of Shem, and a direction of travel. In Balaam's final oracles the name takes on a darker tone. He warns that Asshur would carry the Kenites away captive, and then that ships from Cyprus would come and afflict Asshur and Eber (Numbers 24:22, 24). Centuries before the empire's rise, God's word was already announcing that Assyria would take captives and would itself be brought low.

Assyria and the Northern Kingdom

Assyria enters Israel's history in force during the last decades of the northern kingdom. Menahem paid Pul king of Assyria a thousand talents of silver, raised by taxing Israel's wealthy men, so that Pul would confirm his hold on the throne (2 Kings 15:19-20). First Chronicles 5:26 sets Pul and Tiglath-Pileser side by side, and many interpreters understand them to be the same king. The text adds a striking theological note: "the God of Israel stirred up the spirit" of the Assyrian king, who carried off the Reubenites, Gadites, and half-tribe of Manasseh (1 Chronicles 5:6, 26). Under Pekah, Tiglath-Pileser took Galilee and the land of Naphtali and deported its people (2 Kings 15:29).

Hosea's ministry belongs to this period, and he exposes the spiritual sickness beneath the politics. Ephraim swung back and forth between Egypt and Assyria (Hosea 7:11; 12:1). Hosea compares Ephraim to a silly dove and to a wild donkey going off alone to hire lovers (Hosea 8:9). He writes, "When Ephraim saw his sickness, and Judah saw his wound, then Ephraim went to Assyria and sent to King Jareb; yet he cannot cure you, nor heal you of your wound" (Hosea 5:13). The nation looked for healing from the very power that would destroy it. Hosea warns that Israel would eat unclean food in Assyria, that the golden calf would be carried there as tribute, and that the Assyrian would be their king because they refused to return to the Lord (Hosea 9:3; 10:6; 11:5).

The end came under Hoshea. He became Shalmaneser's vassal, then secretly sought help from Egypt and withheld tribute. The king of Assyria imprisoned him, besieged Samaria for three years, and carried Israel away to Assyria, settling the captives in Halah, by the Habor, and in the cities of the Medes (2 Kings 17:3-6; 18:9-11). This took place around 722 BC. Scripture is explicit about the cause. The fall did not happen because Assyria was strong. It happened because Israel had sinned against the Lord, feared other gods, and rejected His statutes and warnings (2 Kings 17:7-23).

The Resettlement of Samaria

Assyria then brought people from Babylon, Cuthah, Ava, Hamath, and Sepharvaim to live in Samaria's cities (2 Kings 17:24). When lions killed some of them, the newcomers concluded they did not know "the rituals of the God of the land." The king of Assyria sent back one of the deported Israelite priests to teach them (2 Kings 17:26-28). The result was a mixed religion in which the settlers feared the Lord while still serving their own gods (2 Kings 17:33). Generations later, the descendants of these settlers told Zerubbabel that they had worshiped Israel's God since Esarhaddon king of Assyria brought them there, and they asked to help rebuild the temple. Judah's leaders refused them, and these people became adversaries of the work (Ezra 4:1-3).

Ahaz's Fatal Bargain

In Judah, King Ahaz faced an alliance of Syria and Israel. Isaiah urged him to stand firm in faith and gave him the sign of Immanuel (Isaiah 7:1-14). Ahaz chose another path. "So Ahaz sent messengers to Tiglath-Pileser king of Assyria, saying, 'I am your servant and your son. Come up and save me from the hand of the king of Syria and from the hand of the king of Israel, who rise up against me.' And Ahaz took the silver and gold that was found in the house of the LORD, and in the treasuries of the king's house, and sent it as a present to the king of Assyria" (2 Kings 16:7-8).

The words are revealing. Ahaz called himself the servant and son of a pagan king, using language that belonged to Israel's relationship with God, and he paid for it with what had been given to the Lord's house. Assyria took Damascus as he asked (2 Kings 16:9). Ahaz then went to Damascus to meet Tiglath-Pileser, saw an altar there, and sent its pattern back to Urijah the priest. The new altar took the central place in the temple. The bronze altar was moved aside, and Ahaz rearranged parts of the temple "on account of the king of Assyria" (2 Kings 16:10-18). The Chronicler's verdict is blunt. The king of Assyria distressed Ahaz rather than strengthening him, and the treasures did not help (2 Chronicles 28:16, 20-21).

Isaiah had warned that this would happen. The Lord would whistle for the fly of Egypt and the bee of Assyria, and would shave Judah with a razor "hired" from beyond the River (Isaiah 7:17-20). Because the people refused the gently flowing waters of Shiloah, the Lord would bring on them the mighty waters of the River, the king of Assyria. That flood would sweep into Judah and reach up to the neck, filling the breadth of "your land, O Immanuel" (Isaiah 8:6-8). The help Ahaz hired became the flood that nearly drowned his kingdom.

Hezekiah, Sennacherib, and the Great Deliverance

Hezekiah took a different course. He invited the remnant of Israel who had escaped the kings of Assyria to return to the Lord and keep the Passover in Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 30:6). He also rebelled against the king of Assyria (2 Kings 18:7). Sennacherib came and captured Judah's fortified cities. For a moment Hezekiah faltered. He confessed, "I have done wrong," paid a heavy tribute, and even stripped the gold from the temple doors (2 Kings 18:13-16). The threat remained.

Sennacherib sent the Rabshakeh to Jerusalem's walls, and his speech is one of the most calculated assaults on faith in Scripture (2 Kings 18:17-35; Isaiah 36). He mocked Judah's trust in Egypt as leaning on a broken reed. He claimed that Hezekiah had offended the Lord by removing the high places, twisting Hezekiah's reforms into a supposed insult to God. He even claimed that the Lord Himself had sent Assyria to destroy the land. He then spoke over the heads of the officials directly to the people in Hebrew. He told them not to let Hezekiah deceive them or make them trust in the Lord. He promised that if they surrendered, each would eat from his own vine and fig tree until Assyria took them to a land like their own. Finally, he listed the gods of Hamath, Arpad, and Sepharvaim and asked which of them had saved its land.

The people were silent, as Hezekiah had commanded. The king tore his clothes and went into the house of the Lord. He sent to Isaiah, who answered that the Lord had heard the blasphemy and would turn the king back (2 Kings 19:1-7). When a threatening letter arrived, Hezekiah spread it before the Lord. He prayed that all the kingdoms of the earth might know that the Lord alone is God (2 Kings 19:14-19). God answered through Isaiah. The king of Assyria would not enter the city or shoot an arrow there, because God would defend it for His own sake and for David's (2 Kings 19:32-34). That night the angel of the Lord struck 185,000 in the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib returned to Nineveh, where his own sons killed him while he worshiped in the house of his god (2 Kings 19:35-37; Isaiah 37:36-38; 2 Chronicles 32:21-22).

Hezekiah's counsel to his officers summarizes the faith the story calls for. Paraphrased, he told them there were more with Judah than with the Assyrian, because with him was only an arm of flesh while with Judah was the Lord their God (2 Chronicles 32:7-8). The promise to Hezekiah in his illness also included deliverance from the hand of the king of Assyria (2 Kings 20:6; Isaiah 38:6).

Manasseh and the Last Years

Assyria's reach did not end with Sennacherib's retreat. The captains of the king of Assyria seized Manasseh, bound him, and took him to Babylon. In his affliction he humbled himself before God and was restored (2 Chronicles 33:11-13). God used Assyrian chains to bring Judah's most wicked king to repentance. Near the end of the empire, Pharaoh Necho went up to the king of Assyria at the Euphrates, and Josiah died at Megiddo trying to stop him (2 Kings 23:29).

The Prophets and Assyria's Fall

The prophets saw Assyria's end long before it came. Isaiah declared that God would break the Assyrian in His land and trample him on His mountains (Isaiah 14:25). He said the Assyrian would fall by a sword "not of man" and would be beaten down by the voice of the Lord (Isaiah 30:31; 31:8). He also acted out a sign. For three years he walked naked and barefoot to show that the king of Assyria would lead away Egyptians and Ethiopians as captives, so that those who trusted in Egypt would be ashamed (Isaiah 20:1-6). Zephaniah foretold that God would stretch out His hand against the north, destroy Assyria, and make Nineveh a desolation (Zephaniah 2:13). Nahum's prophecy against Nineveh closes by addressing the empire directly: its shepherds slumber, its people are scattered on the mountains, and no one gathers them (Nahum 3:18). The capital fell in 612 BC. Jonah's earlier ministry to Nineveh, where the city repented at God's warning, belongs to the same history, though the name Asshur does not occur in that book.

Assyria Remembered

After its fall, Assyria became a lesson that later Scripture returned to. Jeremiah rebuked Judah for drinking the waters of the River and warned that the nation would be ashamed of Egypt as it had been ashamed of Assyria (Jeremiah 2:18, 36). He described Israel as a scattered sheep. The king of Assyria devoured it first, and then Nebuchadnezzar broke its bones, so God would punish Babylon as He had punished Assyria (Jeremiah 50:17-18). Lamentations confesses that Judah had given its hand to Egypt and Assyria to get bread (Lamentations 5:6).

Ezekiel turned Assyria into two great images. The first is the image of unfaithfulness. In Ezekiel 16:28 and Ezekiel 23:5-12, Samaria (Oholah) and Jerusalem (Oholibah) lust after the Assyrians, described as handsome warriors and horsemen clothed in purple. God gives Samaria into the hands of the very lovers she desired. The second is the image of a fallen tree. In Ezekiel 31, Assyria is a cedar of Lebanon, towering above every tree of God's garden, which God cut down because its heart was lifted up. Ezekiel holds it before Pharaoh as a warning, and he later places Assyria among the slain nations in the pit (Ezekiel 31:3-18; 32:22). In Psalm 83:8, Assyria joins the confederacy of nations conspiring against God's people, and the psalmist asks God to act so that they will know His name.

The name survived even the empire. Ezra 6:22 says the Lord turned the heart of "the king of Assyria" toward the returned exiles to help with the temple. Here the title refers to the Persian ruler who now governed the old Assyrian lands. The empire that once deported God's people now, under a new ruler, funded the rebuilding of His house. In their great prayer of confession, the Levites remembered all the hardship that had come on Israel "From the days of the kings of Assyria until this day" (Nehemiah 9:32). Isaiah 52:4 sums up the history in one line: God's people went down to Egypt at first, and the Assyrian oppressed them without cause.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

God rules the empires. Scripture states plainly that God directed Assyria. He stirred up the spirit of Pul (1 Chronicles 5:26). He whistled for the Assyrian bee (Isaiah 7:18). He named Assyria "the rod of My anger and the staff in whose hand is My indignation" (Isaiah 10:5) and sent it against "an ungodly nation" (Isaiah 10:6). The fall of Samaria is explained as the Lord's judgment on a people who rejected His word (2 Kings 17:7-23). The most powerful empire of its day was a tool in God's hand. It did not compete with His purposes.

The instrument is still accountable. Isaiah 10 holds both truths together. Assyria served God's purpose, "Yet he does not mean so, nor does his heart think so; but it is in his heart to destroy, and cut off not a few nations" (Isaiah 10:7). The king's boasts reveal his heart: "By the strength of my hand I have done it, and by my wisdom, for I am prudent" (Isaiah 10:13). He even pictured himself gathering the earth like eggs from a nest while no one "moved his wing, nor opened his mouth with even a peep" (Isaiah 10:14). God's answer exposes the absurdity: "Shall the ax boast itself against him who chops with it?... As if a rod could wield itself against those who lift it up, or as if a staff could lift up, as if it were not wood!" (Isaiah 10:15). God will punish "the fruit of the arrogant heart of the king of Assyria, and the glory of his haughty looks" (Isaiah 10:12). The text states this directly. God's sovereignty over the nations never excuses their cruelty or pride.

The Lord is not one god among many. The Rabshakeh's argument rested on the idea that the Lord was a local god like those of Hamath and Arpad. Isaiah records the same logic in the king's own boast. He asked why Jerusalem and her idols should fare differently from Samaria and hers (Isaiah 10:10-11). The deliverance of Jerusalem answered that question in history. Hezekiah's prayer asked that the nations would know the Lord alone is God (2 Kings 19:19), and the destruction of Sennacherib's army showed it. Even the lion episode in 2 Kings 17 carries the same point. The foreign settlers thought of the Lord as "the God of the land," but He was the God who judged both Israel and Assyria.

Trusting empire is a spiritual act. The historical books and the prophets agree that turning to Assyria for safety meant turning from God. Ahaz's payment and his Damascus altar are recorded facts (2 Kings 16:7-18). Hosea's charge that Assyria could not heal Ephraim's wound is a direct prophetic verdict (Hosea 5:13). Ezekiel names the same pattern as unfaithfulness to the Lord (Ezekiel 23:5-9). From these passages it is a fair inference that political dependence on Assyria drew Israel and Judah into compromise with the powers they leaned on. Ahaz shows how far that compromise could reach: under Assyrian pressure he remade the temple's altar after a pattern seen in Damascus and rearranged the temple "on account of the king of Assyria" (2 Kings 16:10-18), and he sacrificed to "the gods of Damascus" (2 Chronicles 28:23). Scripture does not present the altar as the adoption of Assyria's own national god, but it shows dependence on a foreign power turning into compromise in worship.

Judgment leads toward mercy. The prophets who announced Assyria's fall also announced the return of its captives and even the turning of Assyria itself. The details belong to Section 4, but the point matters here. In God's purposes, Assyria's story does not end with a destroyed empire. It ends with a gathered people.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

Immanuel in the Assyrian crisis. The Immanuel sign was given to Ahaz at the moment he was turning to Assyria (Isaiah 7:14-17). Isaiah then speaks of the Assyrian flood filling "your land, O Immanuel" (Isaiah 8:8). Matthew explicitly applies the Immanuel prophecy to the birth of Jesus (Matthew 1:22-23). The setting adds meaning. God offered His people the sign of His presence at the very moment their king chose to trust Assyria. The land the Assyrian overran belonged to Immanuel, and the world's empires never owned it.

The Ruler who is peace. Micah 5:2 promises a Ruler from Bethlehem whose goings forth are from of old, and Matthew 2:6 applies this to Christ. Micah continues, "And this One shall be peace. When the Assyrian comes into our land, and when he treads in our palaces, then we will raise against him seven shepherds and eight princely men... Thus He shall deliver us from the Assyrian" (Micah 5:5-6). By Micah's time Assyria was the great enemy. In this passage "the Assyrian" is the concrete invader of Micah's day, and the Messiah is the one who delivers from him. Many readers rightly see a wider pattern here, applying Micah's Assyrian to the hostile powers that threaten God's people in every age. That is a canonical interpretation rather than Micah's stated meaning. The New Testament does not call Micah's Assyrian a type of a final enemy. Still, the pattern is well supported in Scripture: the coming King brings peace by overcoming the enemy of His people. Christ's victory over sin, death, and the devil fulfills that pattern at its deepest level.

A highway for the remnant. Isaiah 11 describes the shoot from the stem of Jesse. In that day the Lord will recover the remnant of His people "from Assyria" and from many other lands, and there will be a highway for them from Assyria, as there was for Israel when it came up from Egypt (Isaiah 11:11, 16). Isaiah 27:13 adds that a great trumpet will sound and those perishing in Assyria will come and worship the Lord on the holy mountain in Jerusalem. Hosea pictures them coming back trembling like a dove from Assyria (Hosea 11:11). Micah 7:12 and Zechariah 10:10-11 also speak of a gathering from Assyria and the bringing down of Assyria's pride. These promises of a new exodus led by the Messiah's reign run throughout the prophets. They find their center in Christ, who gathers God's scattered people to Himself (John 11:51-52).

Assyria, the work of My hands. The most astonishing Assyria passage is Isaiah 19:23-25: "In that day there will be a highway from Egypt to Assyria... In that day Israel will be one of three with Egypt and Assyria—a blessing in the midst of the land, whom the LORD of hosts shall bless, saying, 'Blessed is Egypt My people, and Assyria the work of My hands, and Israel My inheritance.'" Egypt enslaved Israel and Assyria carried it away, yet God gives both nations names that belonged to Israel. The New Testament does not quote this verse. Even so, the vision fits closely with the gospel's gathering of the nations. In Christ, God breaks down the dividing wall and makes former enemies one new people (Ephesians 2:14-16). The promise to Abraham that all the families of the earth will be blessed reaches its goal in the same way (Genesis 12:3; Galatians 3:8). Isaiah shows that God's purpose for the nations was redemption, not destruction alone.

Related history in the Gospels. Two threads connect Assyria to the New Testament through related names and events rather than through the word itself. First, Jesus held up the men of Nineveh, Assyria's capital, who repented at Jonah's preaching, as a rebuke to His own generation (Matthew 12:41). Second, the Samaritans whom Jesus met and loved (John 4; Luke 10:33; Acts 8:5-25) were connected in Jewish memory to the mixed population Assyria settled in Samaria (2 Kings 17:24-41; Ezra 4:2). The Gospels do not explicitly trace this background, so the link is a historical inference. Even so, it is worth noticing. Jesus offered living water and true worship "in spirit and truth" (John 4:23) to people from that very background. That was the opposite of the mixed worship Assyria's policy had produced.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

The Assyria passages reveal a God who is never threatened by the powers that frighten His people. The king of Assyria thought of himself as a great hand gathering the earth's riches, but God saw him as an ax in someone else's grip (Isaiah 10:13-15). Believers who feel overwhelmed by forces larger than themselves can take real comfort here. The powers of this world move only as far as God allows, and He holds them accountable for every proud word.

Ahaz shows how quietly compromise begins. He did not set out to rebuild the temple. He went looking for help, paid for it with what belonged to God, and called himself the servant and son of a man instead of the Lord (2 Kings 16:7-8). The altar from Damascus followed. Hosea's word still applies: the power we run to "cannot cure you, nor heal you of your wound" (Hosea 5:13). What we trust for security becomes the master we serve. That is why Hosea's call to repentance includes the confession, "Assyria shall not save us" (Hosea 14:3). Turning back to God includes naming the false saviors we have leaned on.

The Rabshakeh's speech shows how the enemy of faith works. He did more than threaten. He twisted God's truth. He called Hezekiah's faithful reforms an offense against God. He claimed God had sent him. He offered a comfortable surrender and told the people not to let anyone make them trust in the Lord (2 Kings 18:22-32). Satan still attacks in similar ways, by suggesting that God is against us, that obedience was a mistake, or that giving in would be easier. Hezekiah's response deserves study. He did not answer the taunts. He tore his clothes, went into the house of the Lord, sought the prophetic word, and spread the letter out before God. Then he prayed that God's name would be known among the nations (2 Kings 19:1-19). He took the enemy's words straight to the Lord and made the matter about God's glory.

Assyria's story also shows mercy in surprising places. God used Assyrian hooks to humble Manasseh, and the humbled king found grace (2 Chronicles 33:11-13). God spared repentant Nineveh in Jonah's day. Isaiah heard God call Assyria "the work of My hands" (Isaiah 19:25). No nation, and no person, is beyond the reach of a God who can turn an oppressor into a worshiper. That hope rests on the cross. There Christ made peace between God and sinners and between enemies who once hated each other.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 2:14; 10:11, 22; 25:18 — Asshur as a land beside the Tigris, which runs "toward the east of Assyria," as a son of Shem, and as the setting of early cities such as Nineveh.

  • Numbers 24:22, 24 — Balaam foresees Assyria taking captives and later being afflicted.

  • 2 Kings 15:19-20, 29; 1 Chronicles 5:26 — Pul and Tiglath-Pileser in Israel's affairs. God stirs up the Assyrian king against the eastern tribes.

  • 2 Kings 16:7-18; 2 Chronicles 28:16-21 — Ahaz buys Assyria's help with temple silver and installs the Damascus altar. Assyria distresses rather than strengthens him.

  • 2 Kings 17:3-41 — The fall of Samaria, the reasons God gives for it, and the mixed religion of the resettled land.

  • 2 Kings 18–19; Isaiah 36–37; 2 Chronicles 32 — Sennacherib's invasion, the Rabshakeh's taunts, Hezekiah's prayer, and God's deliverance of Jerusalem.

  • 2 Chronicles 33:11-13 — Manasseh taken captive by Assyrian commanders and restored after repentance.

  • Isaiah 7:17-20; 8:4-8 — Assyria as the hired razor and the overflowing River that floods Immanuel's land.

  • Isaiah 10:5-15 — Assyria as "the rod of My anger," judged for the arrogant heart that boasted against the hand that used it.

  • Isaiah 11:11, 16; 27:13 — A remnant gathered from Assyria on a highway like the exodus.

  • Isaiah 19:23-25 — Egypt, Assyria, and Israel blessed together in worship of the Lord.

  • Hosea 5:13; 7:11; 11:11; 14:3 — Ephraim's futile reliance on Assyria, the promise of return, and the confession that Assyria cannot save.

  • Micah 5:5-6 — The Ruler from Bethlehem who "shall be peace" and delivers His people from the Assyrian.

  • Nahum 3:18; Zephaniah 2:13 — The prophesied end of Assyria and Nineveh.

  • Ezekiel 23:5-12; 31:3-18 — Assyria as the object of Israel's unfaithful longing and as the fallen cedar that warns proud empires.

  • Jeremiah 50:17-18; Ezra 6:22; Nehemiah 9:32 — Assyria remembered after its fall, from the scattering of Israel to the title borne by the king who aided the temple's rebuilding.
