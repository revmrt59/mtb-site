LEFT, LEFT HAND, NORTH - (sᵉmôʼwl, Strong's H8040)

Summary

The Hebrew word sᵉmôʼwl means the left: the left hand, the left side, or a leftward direction. Because Israelites oriented themselves facing east, toward the sunrise, the left could also mean north. The word is plain and physical. It names which hand Jacob laid on a grandson's head, which side of the temple a pillar stood on, and which way a traveler must not turn. Scripture often pairs it with the right to describe something complete. At the Red Sea the waters stood as a wall on Israel's right and left. The olive trees in Zechariah's vision grow on both sides of the lampstand. The man in linen in Daniel lifts both hands to swear his oath.

From these ordinary uses Scripture draws out much of God's character. Moses and Joshua command Israel not to turn aside to the right or to the left, because God's way is a defined road. Jacob's left hand on the firstborn shows that God gives His blessing in ways that overturn human ranking. God pities Nineveh's multitudes who "cannot discern between their right hand and their left." The bride rests with the bridegroom's left hand under her head. At the end of the biblical story, Jesus places the goats on His left in the final judgment. The left side is not evil in itself. It is a direction, and in Scripture what matters most is whether a person stays on the path God has set.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Sᵉmôʼwl is a common Hebrew noun found throughout the Old Testament. It appears in Israel's laws, its battle stories, the plans for its temple, its wisdom books, and its prophetic visions. Hebrew speakers set their directions by facing east, so the left hand pointed north. That is why Hobah lies "north" of Damascus in Genesis 14:15, and why Samaria lies to Jerusalem's "north" in Ezekiel 16:46. In both verses the Hebrew says on the left.

In the ancient world the right hand generally stood for strength, skill, and honor, and the left hand ranked second. The Old Testament reflects that ranking, but it never treats the left as wicked. Ordinarily the left hand is the right hand's partner, and the two together express wholeness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word's meaning is simple. Context decides which of three closely related uses is in view:

1. The left hand. The physical hand, as when Ehud "reached with his left hand" for his dagger (Judges 3:21).

2. The left side or direction. A position or a way of turning, as when the temple pillar Boaz stood "on the left" (2 Chronicles 3:17), or when Israel is told not to turn aside to the left (Deuteronomy 5:32).

3. The north. A compass direction based on facing east (Genesis 14:15; Ezekiel 16:46).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses of sᵉmôʼwl fall into a few patterns.

The first is the right-or-left pairing, which works in two directions. Sometimes it describes completeness: water, pillars, lampstands, olive trees, or attendants standing on both sides. At other times it describes any departure from a straight course. Balaam's donkey reaches a place too narrow to turn right or left (Numbers 22:26). Asahel refuses to turn right or left from chasing Abner (2 Samuel 2:19–21). The cows pulling the ark go straight toward Beth Shemesh without turning aside (1 Samuel 6:12). These literal journeys give flesh to the moral command that runs through Deuteronomy and Joshua.

The second pattern is the left hand as an unexpected instrument of deliverance. Ehud the Benjamite, "a left-handed man" (Judges 3:15), fastens his dagger under his clothes on his right thigh (Judges 3:16) and reaches across with his left hand to draw it (Judges 3:21). Gideon's three hundred hold torches in their left hands and trumpets in their right (Judges 7:20). The blind Samson braces his hands against two pillars, one on his right and one on his left, and brings down the temple of Dagon (Judges 16:29). Scripture elsewhere honors Benjamite warriors who were left-handed (Judges 20:16) or who could fight with either hand (1 Chronicles 12:2). Those two verses use different Hebrew expressions, but they belong to the same picture. In each story God works through the hand the enemy discounted.

The third pattern is architectural symmetry. Solomon places carts, lampstands, and lavers in balanced sets on the right and left of the temple (1 Kings 7:39, 49; 2 Chronicles 4:6–8). He sets the two bronze pillars at its entrance, naming the one on the right Jachin and the one on the left Boaz (2 Chronicles 3:17). God's house was ordered and complete on every side.

4. Theological Significance

The command not to turn aside to the right or to the left (Deuteronomy 5:32; 28:14; Joshua 23:6) shows that God's will is a road, not an open field. He has marked its course by His Word, and the right and left are its edges. Either turn is a departure. Swerving to one side might mean idolatry; swerving to the other might just as easily mean a sensible-looking shortcut that replaces what God said with what seemed wise. Neither the right nor the left is the wrong direction in itself; leaving the road is. That is why the law for Israel's king (Deuteronomy 17:18–20) required him to copy and read the law so that he would not lift himself above his brothers or turn aside to the right or left. Joshua 1:7 connects courage directly to this steadiness: "Be strong and very courageous," and do not turn from the law to the right hand or to the left. In Joshua 1:7, strength means keeping to the course God set rather than charging ahead on one's own plans. Psalm 119:105 adds the supply for the journey: "Your word is a lamp to my feet And a light to my path."

Ecclesiastes carries this outward road inward: "A wise man's heart is at his right hand, But a fool's heart at his left" (Ecclesiastes 10:2). Because the right hand was the skilled and ready hand, a heart "at the left" is a heart that is clumsy and poorly aimed. Solomon describes foolishness as something wrong at the center of a person, where desires and choices start. A fool may know many facts and still keep fumbling, because his heart points the wrong way. Proverbs 4:27 gives the remedy: do not turn to the right or left, and keep your foot from evil.

Alongside this warning, the word repeatedly shows God securing His people on every side and confounding human expectations. Scripture holds these truths together. The same God who marks out the road also guards both edges of it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 48:13–14 — Jacob's crossed hands. Joseph arranges his sons carefully, "Ephraim with his right hand toward Israel's left hand, and Manasseh with his left hand toward Israel's right hand." The older son, Manasseh, would then receive Jacob's right hand, the hand of the greater blessing. Instead, Jacob stretches out his right hand to Ephraim and lays his left on Manasseh, "guiding his hands knowingly, for Manasseh was the firstborn." Joseph is displeased and protests, "Not so, my father" (Genesis 48:18). Jacob refuses to change his hands and says he knows exactly what he is doing: the younger will be greater (Genesis 48:19). The old man's left hand resting on the firstborn's head is a deliberate act. It shows that the blessing comes from God's choosing, not from birth order. Jacob, the younger twin who received the birthright, now passes on that same surprising pattern.

Exodus 14:22, 29 — Walls on the right and the left. Twice the text says that the waters stood as a wall to Israel on their right hand and on their left, once as they enter the sea and again as the account closes. The sea had been the trap that pinned Israel against Pharaoh's chariots. God turned it into protection on both sides. The repetition matters because between those two verses the same waters return and cover the Egyptians (Exodus 14:28). What guarded Israel's flanks became the judgment of their enemies. God did more than open a path. He controlled everything around the path.

Jonah 4:11 — Those who cannot tell right from left. Jonah sits outside Nineveh hoping to watch it burn and grieves when the plant that shaded him withers. God answers him: "And should I not pity Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred and twenty thousand persons who cannot discern between their right hand and their left—and much livestock?" The phrase may point to young children too small to know one hand from the other, to adults without moral understanding because they had no knowledge of God's ways, or to both; the verse does not settle it. Either way, God sees people who cannot find their way and pities them. The contrast is sharp. Jonah has the revealed Word and cares most about his own comfort. God looks at a violent pagan city and sees people who have lost their way, whom He longs to spare. The book ends on God's question, leaving every reader to answer whether he shares God's heart for the lost.

Zechariah 4:3, 11 — Olive trees on both sides. Zechariah sees a golden lampstand with two olive trees, one at the right of its bowl and one at its left. The trees feed the lamp with golden oil through pipes, with no human hand tending it (Zechariah 4:12). The angel explains the vision: God's work will be done by His Spirit, not by might or power (Zechariah 4:6). The two trees are the "anointed ones" who stand beside the Lord of the whole earth (Zechariah 4:14). In the setting of chapters 3 and 4, these are most likely Joshua the high priest and Zerubbabel the governor. With a tree on each side, the lamp never runs dry. The left-hand tree is as necessary as the right.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The road Israel was told not to leave is the road Jesus walked without wavering. He said His food was to do His Father's will (John 4:34) and that He always did what pleased Him (John 8:29). Israel's kings turned aside, and even Josiah's faithfulness (2 Kings 22:2) could not hold back the exile. Christ is the King who fully kept the course described in Deuteronomy 17, and He is now Himself the Way for His people.

Paul speaks directly of Rebecca's sons, Jacob and Esau: before the twins were born it was said that "The older shall serve the younger," so that God's purpose might stand, "not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:10–12). Jacob's crossed hands over Ephraim and Manasseh are a separate scene at the end of Jacob's life, but they show a comparable reversal. God "has chosen the weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty" (1 Corinthians 1:27). The gospel is built on this kind of reversal. When James and John asked to sit at Jesus' right and left in His glory (Mark 10:37), the places on either side of Him at His "coronation" went instead to two criminals on crosses (Luke 23:33). The Gospels do not draw out this connection, but it fits how God has always worked.

Zechariah's two anointed figures, priest and governor, point toward one Person. Zechariah himself later speaks of the Branch who would be a priest on his throne (Zechariah 6:12–13), and Jesus fulfills both offices. Revelation picks up the olive-tree image for God's witnesses (Revelation 11:4). Daniel's man in linen lifts "his right hand and his left hand to heaven, and swore by Him who lives forever" (Daniel 12:7). Raising one hand was the usual gesture for an oath, but both hands lifted together put the whole person behind the promise that suffering would end and God's people would be delivered. Revelation 10:5–6 echoes the scene as an angel swears that there will be no more delay.

The story ends with the left hand's most solemn use. Christ, the Shepherd-King, divides the nations: "And He will set the sheep on His right hand, but the goats on the left" (Matthew 25:33). Those on His left hear the sentence to depart into everlasting fire (Matthew 25:41). Throughout the Old Testament the left was mainly the right's partner. In this parable the left becomes the place of those who would not come to Him. This image of placement at the judgment is different from the old command not to turn aside to the right or to the left, where either turn was a departure. The left is not inherently the wrong direction; what Matthew 25 shows is the final separation between those who belong to the Shepherd and those who do not.

7. Spirit & Life

The Song of Songs gives the warmest image connected with this word: "His left hand is under my head, And his right hand embraces me" (Song of Songs 2:6; repeated in 8:3). The left hand does not grip or strike. It cradles the head of someone at rest. Moses pictured God the same way, with everlasting arms beneath His people (Deuteronomy 33:27). Believers who know Christ's love find this kind of support. He does not shout directions from far away. He holds up the weary mind while His other arm draws them close.

The Red Sea and Zechariah's olive trees show the same God surrounding and supplying His people. These pictures do not promise that those who obey will never face danger. Israel walked between the walls of water with Pharaoh's army still behind them, and the builders in Zechariah's day faced real opposition (Ezra 5:1–5). What the images show is that God provides on every side for the way and the work He gives. The water that once threatened becomes a wall, and the oil that keeps the lamp burning comes from both sides at once. That is why turning aside is so foolish: it means leaving the road on which God has promised to guide and supply His people.

Wisdom's hands give their own encouragement: "Length of days is in her right hand, In her left hand riches and honor" (Proverbs 3:16). Even the "lesser" hand is full when a person walks with God.

Finally, Jonah 4:11 asks whether we share the Father's heart. Whatever the exact sense of God's words there, the people around us who cannot find their way in spiritual matters should stir our pity, not our scorn. Jesus looked at crowds like that and saw sheep without a shepherd (Matthew 9:36).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 13:9 — Abram offers Lot first choice: if Lot goes left, Abram will go right. Abram's generosity trusts God to provide whatever land is left to him.

  • 1 Kings 22:19 — Micaiah sees the host of heaven standing on the Lord's right and left. The whole heavenly court surrounds God's throne, unseen by Israel's kings.

  • Job 23:9 — Job searches for God on the left, where He works, but cannot see Him. He knows God is active even when he cannot perceive Him.

  • Nehemiah 8:4 — Leaders stand on Ezra's right and left as he reads the law. God's Word stands at the center of the restored community.

  • Isaiah 54:3 — Zion is promised that she will spread out to the right and to the left. The barren city grows to include descendants among the nations.
