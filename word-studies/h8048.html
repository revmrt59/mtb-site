SHAMMAH - (Shammâh, Strong's H8048)

Summary

Shammah is a name shared by several different men in the Old Testament, and the first task is keeping them apart. One is an Edomite chief, a grandson of Esau (Genesis 36:13, 17; 1 Chronicles 1:37). Another is the third son of Jesse of Bethlehem, an older brother of David, who passed before Samuel and heard the prophet say, "Neither has the LORD chosen this one" (1 Samuel 16:9). A third, Shammah the son of Agee the Hararite, was one of David's top three warriors, the man who stood alone in a field of lentils after Israel's troops ran (2 Samuel 23:11–12). Two more names in David's roster of mighty men, Shammah the Harodite and Shammah the Hararite, may refer to further individuals (2 Samuel 23:25, 33). The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is often linked to a Hebrew root meaning "desolation" or "astonishment," but some interpreters propose a shortened name connected to "hear" (shama), and more tentatively to "name" (shem). No passage in Scripture explains the name or builds on it.

Two of these men carry real spiritual weight. Jesse's son Shammah stands in the scene where God declares that "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). His family story continues under the alternate names Shimea and Shimeah, through two sons who could hardly have been more different. Jonadab's crafty counsel opened the way for disaster in David's house, and Jonathan killed a Philistine giant. The warrior Shammah gives one of the most vivid pictures in the Old Testament of a single person holding ground everyone else has abandoned. Even so, the writer gives the credit to God: "So the LORD brought about a great victory" (2 Samuel 23:12). That pattern of one man standing firm while the LORD wins the battle echoes forward to the Son of David, who stood alone when His followers fled, and to the church's call to "stand" in the strength God supplies (Ephesians 6:13).

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Strong's entry H8048 gathers eight occurrences of the name Shammah. These occurrences do not belong to one man. They cover at least three clearly distinct people and possibly five:

1. Shammah the Edomite chief. He was a son of Reuel, Esau's son by Basemath, which makes him a grandson of Esau. He is listed among the chiefs of Edom (Genesis 36:13, 17), and the list is repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:37.

2. Shammah the brother of David. He was the third son of Jesse of Bethlehem, after Eliab and Abinadab (1 Samuel 16:6–9; 17:13). Elsewhere he is called Shimea (1 Chronicles 2:13; 20:7) and Shimeah (2 Samuel 13:3, 32). Those spellings are indexed under separate Strong's numbers, so their occurrences are not counted in H8048. Still, the texts plainly identify the same man as David's brother and the father of Jonadab and Jonathan.

3. Shammah the son of Agee the Hararite. He was one of the elite "three" among David's mighty men and defended the lentil field (2 Samuel 23:11–12).

4. Shammah the Harodite. He was a member of the larger group of the Thirty (2 Samuel 23:25). The parallel list calls him Shammoth the Harorite (1 Chronicles 11:27), a related but separately indexed form. "Harodite" probably points to his hometown. One possibility is the area around the spring of Harod known from Gideon's story (Judges 7:1), but the text does not confirm the link.

5. Shammah the Hararite. He also appears among the Thirty (2 Samuel 23:33). His name sits right after a man named Jonathan, and the parallel in 1 Chronicles 11:34 reads "Jonathan the son of Shageh the Hararite." For that reason many readers understand 2 Samuel to mean Jonathan son of Shammah. This Shammah may be the same as Shammah son of Agee (#3), since both are called Hararite. The lists do not settle the question, and it is best left open.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. The Hebrew form Shammâh looks like a word from a root meaning "desolation," "horror," or "astonishment." For that reason the name is often explained that way. Because that sense would be an unusual thing to call a child, some interpreters suggest the name is a shortened form of a longer name built on shama ("to hear," as in "God has heard"), and, more tentatively, on shem ("name, renown"). It is worth noticing that David's brother is also called Shimea and Shimeah, and these forms sound close to the verb for hearing. That fact fits the "hearing" proposal but does not prove it. Scripture never comments on the name or plays on its meaning in any of these passages. Readers should therefore not treat "desolation" as a hidden message about any of these men's lives.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Shammah the Brother of David

Shammah's most familiar appearance comes on the day Samuel arrived in Bethlehem to anoint a king from Jesse's sons. Samuel looked first at the eldest, Eliab, and was sure he had found the man: "Surely the LORD's anointed is before Him!" (1 Samuel 16:6). God corrected him at once: "Do not look at his appearance or at his physical stature, because I have refused him. For the LORD does not see as man sees; for man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (16:7). Abinadab came next, and then "Jesse made Shammah pass by. And he said, 'Neither has the LORD chosen this one'" (16:8–9). Seven sons passed before Samuel. The one God had chosen was still out with the sheep (16:10–13).

Shammah appears again in the Valley of Elah. He was one of Jesse's three oldest sons who had followed Saul to war against the Philistines (1 Samuel 17:13). Day after day Goliath defied Israel, and the whole army was dismayed and afraid (17:11, 24). Shammah was in those ranks when his youngest brother arrived with food from home and then went out to face the giant in the name of the LORD. Scripture records nothing Shammah said. It was Eliab who rebuked David (17:28). He simply stands among the frightened soldiers whose fear David's faith exposed.

Shammah's Sons in David's Court

Under the name Shimeah, Shammah's story continues through his sons, and these chapters belong to his legacy even though they fall outside the H8048 occurrence list.

His son Jonadab was Amnon's friend, and Scripture describes him as a very crafty man (2 Samuel 13:3). When Amnon was sick with desire for his half-sister Tamar, Jonadab supplied the plan. Amnon was to pretend to be ill and ask the king to send Tamar to care for him (13:4–5). The result was Tamar's violation, Absalom's two years of silent hatred, and the murder of Amnon. When a false report reached David that all his sons were dead, Jonadab was the one who calmly corrected it. Only Amnon had died, he said, because Absalom had determined this since the day Tamar was violated (13:32–33). His knowledge is chilling. By the time the report came, he knew what Absalom had determined; Scripture does not say when he learned it or whether he could have stopped it.

Another son, Jonathan, appears in a very different light. In a later war with the Philistines, a huge man with six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot defied Israel, as Goliath once had. "Jonathan the son of Shimea, David's brother" killed him (2 Samuel 21:20–21; 1 Chronicles 20:6–7). A generation earlier Jonathan's father had stood frozen while a giant mocked Israel. Now his son did what David had done.

Shammah the Son of Agee, Defender of the Field

The fullest account of courage under this name belongs to a different Shammah. In the honor roll of David's mighty men, he is ranked among the three greatest:

"And after him was Shammah the son of Agee the Hararite. The Philistines had gathered together into a troop where there was a piece of ground full of lentils. So the people fled from the Philistines. But he stationed himself in the middle of the field, defended it, and killed the Philistines. So the LORD brought about a great victory." (2 Samuel 23:11–12)

The scene is ordinary in the best sense. It was not a fortress or a famous battlefield. It was a farm plot of lentils, the kind of food that kept Israelite families alive. Philistine raiding parties often struck at harvests. The army ran, and Shammah stayed. He planted himself in the middle of the field, fought, and won. The writer's last sentence moves the spotlight from the man to God.

First Chronicles 11:12–14 tells a very similar story. There the field is full of barley, the place is Pasdammim, the hero named is Eleazar son of Dodo, and the text says "they stationed themselves in the middle of that field." Shammah's name does not appear in the Chronicles list at this point. Many students of the text think a line was lost in copying, so that Eleazar's notice and Shammah's exploit now run together. Others read the plural "they" as including more than one hero. Either way, Chronicles closes with the same words of praise: "So the LORD brought about a great victory" (1 Chronicles 11:14).

The Other Mighty Men Named Shammah

Shammah the Harodite (2 Samuel 23:25) and Shammah the Hararite (2 Samuel 23:33) are known only by name and hometown in the list of the Thirty. Their inclusion matters. These were the men whose loyalty and skill stood behind David's kingdom, and Scripture remembered them by name. Beyond that, the text tells us nothing about them.

Shammah the Edomite Chief

The earliest Shammah lived in a different world, among the descendants of Esau in the region of Seir. He was a son of Reuel and a grandson of Esau and Basemath, and he became one of the chiefs, or clan leaders, of Edom (Genesis 36:3–4, 13, 17). His name appears only in these family and leadership lists, repeated in 1 Chronicles 1:37.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God chose David and refused Jesse's older sons, and it gives the reason: the LORD looks at the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). Shammah's role in that scene is to be one of the men who looked like a possible king and was not chosen. The text does not say he was wicked or unworthy. It says only that God had not chosen him. Seen this way, the story teaches about God's freedom in choosing rather than about Shammah's failures. God's choice set aside birth order, which mattered greatly in that culture, and the impressions even a prophet formed.

Through his sons, Shammah's household shows how differently people can respond to living near God's chosen king. Both Jonadab and Jonathan were David's nephews, related to the king's household. One used his sharp mind to feed a cousin's lust and helped set off the troubles Nathan had foretold for David's house (2 Samuel 12:10–11). The other carried on David's own fight against the enemies of Israel. Scripture records both without comment. The contrast is drawn from the narrative itself, and it is a sober one.

The warrior Shammah's story belongs to the larger account of how God secured David's kingdom against the Philistines. Scripture states two things clearly: Shammah stood, and the LORD gave the victory. Neither cancels the other. Human courage was real and is honored by name, but the outcome is God's work. The writer does not tell us Shammah's motive. Still, the rest of the Old Testament treats Israel's land as the LORD's gift, held in trust by families as their inheritance (Leviticus 25:23; compare Naboth's refusal in 1 Kings 21:3). It is a fair inference that a field of lentils in Israel was worth defending because it was part of what God had given His people to live on. That inference fits the biblical outlook, but the passage does not say so directly.

The Edomite Shammah belongs to God's dealings with the wider family of Abraham. God had said two nations would come from Rebekah's twins (Genesis 25:23), and Genesis 36 shows that word fulfilled in the rise of Edom's chiefs, even before Israel had a king (36:31). Later, God told Israel that He had given Mount Seir to Esau and that Israel must not seize it (Deuteronomy 2:5). The Edomite chief's name is a small mark of a larger truth. God kept His word to Esau's line as well as to Jacob's.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never mentions any Shammah, but two of these stories sit squarely within paths that lead to Christ.

The first is a matter of strong canonical development. The scene of Shammah being passed over is part of the story of God choosing David, and God's promise to David's house leads directly to Jesus, the Son of David. Both Matthew and Luke trace Jesus' line through David. Matthew follows it through Solomon, and Luke through David's son Nathan (Matthew 1:6; Luke 3:31–32). The pattern of God's surprising choice runs all the way to Christ. The Messiah came from Bethlehem, from an unimpressive family line, and was rejected by many who expected something more outwardly impressive (Isaiah 53:2–3; John 1:11). Even His own brothers did not believe in Him for a time (John 7:5). Shammah does not stand for Christ. He stands among those God did not choose, so that the one God did choose would be seen clearly.

The second is a thematic resonance. Shammah son of Agee stood his ground when "the people fled," and "the LORD brought about a great victory." The Gospels describe a far greater moment when the disciples all forsook Jesus and fled (Mark 14:50). He alone stood in the place of battle and won the decisive victory over sin, death, and the devil (Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14). The New Testament does not name Shammah as a picture of this, and he should not be called a type of Christ. Still, the shape of the two stories is strikingly alike: one man holds the ground, everyone else runs, and the victory is God's.

The same pattern carries over to believers. Paul calls the church to "take up the whole armor of God, that you may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand" (Ephesians 6:13). Paul is clear that "we do not wrestle against flesh and blood" (6:12), so the connection to Shammah is an illustration, not a direct parallel. Shammah's lentil field is still a memorable picture of what that kind of standing looks like.

5. Spirit & Life

The story of Jesse's sons is a quiet correction to the way we measure people, including ourselves. Even Samuel, a seasoned prophet, was drawn to height and bearing. God's words to him, "the LORD does not see as man sees," still challenge every church that is tempted to look for leaders by their presence, polish, or position in the family. God's choices may surprise us, but they are never careless. He sees what we cannot see.

Shammah's household also warns us that being near the things of God is not the same as being changed by them. Jonadab was the king's nephew and a very crafty man. He used his cleverness to arrange sin, and later he spoke knowingly about its consequences. His brother Jonathan, from the same family, killed a giant who defied Israel. Family background, access, and intelligence do not decide what a person becomes. The direction of the heart does.

The lentil field speaks to anyone tempted to think that faithfulness matters only in large and visible places. Shammah fought for a farm plot, not a palace. Much of the Christian life is spent in places that look just as ordinary: a home, a job, a small church, a single friendship. When others are giving ground, God still honors the one who stays and holds what He has entrusted. The text also keeps the credit where it belongs. Shammah stood, and the LORD won the battle. Standing firm is our part, but the victory is His. That frees us from both cowardice and pride.

Finally, the Edomite chief reminds us that God's faithfulness extends beyond the people we might expect. He gave Esau's descendants their land and later told Israel, You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother (Deuteronomy 23:7, paraphrased). The God who chose Jacob did not forget Esau's grandsons.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:13, 17; 1 Chronicles 1:37. These verses identify Shammah the Edomite as a son of Reuel, a grandson of Esau, and a chief of Edom.

  • 1 Samuel 16:6–13. Jesse's sons pass before Samuel, Shammah is not chosen, and God declares that He looks on the heart.

  • 1 Samuel 17:13. Shammah serves in Saul's army at the Valley of Elah when David faces Goliath.

  • 1 Chronicles 2:13. This verse confirms Shammah, here called Shimea, as Jesse's third son.

  • 2 Samuel 13:3–5, 32–33. Jonadab son of Shimeah gives the crafty counsel behind Amnon's sin and later reveals that he knew Absalom's intent.

  • 2 Samuel 21:20–21; 1 Chronicles 20:6–7. Jonathan son of Shimea, David's nephew, kills a Philistine giant.

  • 2 Samuel 23:11–12. Shammah son of Agee defends the lentil field, and the LORD gives a great victory.

  • 1 Chronicles 11:12–14. The parallel account of the field battle, set in a barley field and connected with Eleazar.

  • 2 Samuel 23:25, 33; 1 Chronicles 11:27, 34. These lists name Shammah the Harodite (Shammoth) and Shammah the Hararite among the Thirty, along with the Jonathan/Shageh parallel.

  • Ephesians 6:11–14. The call to stand firm in God's armor, which the lentil-field story illustrates.
