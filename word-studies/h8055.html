REJOICE, BE GLAD - (sâmach, Strong's H8055)

Summary

The Hebrew verb sâmach means to rejoice or be glad, and in some uses to make someone else glad. It can describe quiet gladness of heart, as when Aaron would be "glad in his heart" to see Moses (Exodus 4:14), but it very often describes joy that shows. People eat, sing, shout and celebrate with it. The Old Testament uses the word for families at the table, crowds at a coronation and worshipers at a festival. It also uses it for the earth and sky, and even for the LORD Himself. Deuteronomy commands Israel to rejoice before God and to bring the widow, the orphan and the stranger into the celebration. The Psalms place God at the center of that joy: "I will be glad and rejoice in You" (Psalm 9:2).

The word has a dark side too. Enemies rejoice over Jerusalem's ruin, and Scripture judges that gladness severely. The value of joy depends on what a person rejoices in. The prophets look ahead to a gladness greater than any harvest or victory. That joy belongs to the day God says, "I am coming and I will dwell in your midst" (Zechariah 2:10). The New Testament develops that same hope of God dwelling among His people in Jesus Christ, the Word who "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Sâmach is one of the main Hebrew verbs for joy. It appears more than 150 times. About a third of those uses are in the Psalms, with clusters in Deuteronomy, Proverbs and Ecclesiastes. The word belongs to public life as much as private feeling. It appears at harvest feasts (Deuteronomy 12:7; 14:26), at the crowning of kings (1 Samuel 11:15; 2 Kings 11:20), at the dedication of gifts for the temple (1 Chronicles 29:9) and at the completion of Jerusalem's walls (Nehemiah 12:43). Israel's festivals were meals eaten "before the LORD," so rejoicing often meant food, music and gathered people. A related Hebrew noun means "joy," and the two words often stand side by side. In Nehemiah 12:43, for example, God made the people "rejoice with great joy."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Sâmach describes gladness that is felt inwardly and is often expressed openly, especially in shared, audible celebration. Context supplies the reason for the joy and shows whether that joy is right or wrong. Two senses stand out:

1. To be glad or rejoice. This is the delight a person feels and shows over something good, as when the psalmist says, "I will be glad and rejoice in You" (Psalm 9:2), or when Aaron is glad to see Moses (Exodus 4:14).

2. To make glad, cheer or gladden. In this sense the word describes something or someone that causes joy in another. A wise son makes his father glad (Proverbs 10:1). A river makes glad the city of God (Psalm 46:4). Wine cheers the heart (Judges 9:13; Psalm 104:15). God made His people rejoice (Nehemiah 12:43).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The second sense matters because it shows that joy can be given. Sâmach can describe one person's gladness, or it can describe the person who caused it. That allows Scripture to say that a good word makes an anxious heart glad (Proverbs 12:25). It also lets Scripture say that a newly married man should stay home for a year to bring happiness to his wife (Deuteronomy 24:5).

The object of the joy changes everything. Some people are glad "in the LORD" or "in Your salvation." Others rejoice over a fallen rival. The verb is the same in both cases, yet the moral meaning is opposite. Some passages command joy: "Rejoice" (Psalm 97:12). Others forbid it: "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls" (Proverbs 24:17). The setting also matters. Many uses of sâmach take place "before the LORD," at His altar and in His presence, so the word is often an act of worship.

4. Theological Significance

The Psalms make God Himself the reason for joy. Harvests, victories and good news all produce gladness, yet the psalmists keep going past the gift to the Giver. "I will be glad and rejoice in You; I will sing praise to Your name, O Most High" (Psalm 9:2). Psalm 5:11 joins rejoicing to taking refuge: those who hide in God are the ones who shout for joy. Psalm 32:11 calls the righteous to be glad in the LORD right after David describes the relief of confessed and forgiven sin. This kind of joy rests on who God is, His mercy, His righteous judgments and His saving help. For that reason it can outlast circumstances that would otherwise crush it.

Scripture also presents gladness as something God gives. Ecclesiastes looks hard at the emptiness of life "under the sun." Even there the Preacher concludes that the ability to eat, work and rejoice in one's labor is "the gift of God" (Ecclesiastes 3:12–13; 5:19). That conclusion keeps the book from despair. Ordinary pleasures are real goods, and enjoying them gratefully honors their Maker. Ecclesiastes also adds a sober check: rejoice, young man, but know that God will bring you into judgment (Ecclesiastes 11:9). God's gifts are meant for enjoyment, yet the enjoyment still answers to Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 16:11, 14 — Joy commanded and shared. Deuteronomy treats rejoicing as part of obedience. At the Feast of Weeks and the Feast of Tabernacles, Israel is told to rejoice before the LORD with sons and daughters, male and female servants, the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless and the widow. The list is deliberate. These were people who could be especially vulnerable. The Levite had no tribal land of his own, and the stranger, the fatherless and the widow often lacked the family support that others relied on. Deuteronomy 26:11 repeats the pattern at the offering of firstfruits. Israel's joy was to be eaten at a crowded table. A worshiper who celebrated God's provision while the widow went hungry had misunderstood the feast. The joy God commanded was generous by design.

Nehemiah 12:43 and 1 Chronicles 29:9 — Joy that God gives. At the dedication of the rebuilt wall, "they offered great sacrifices, and rejoiced, for God had made them rejoice with great joy; the women and the children also rejoiced, so that the joy of Jerusalem was heard afar off." Sâmach appears three times in this verse. Its center is the second, causative sense: God made them rejoice. The returned exiles had worked under threat and ridicule, and the completed wall was real labor. Yet the text names God as the source of their gladness. Earlier, when the people gave freely for the temple, they rejoiced because they had offered willingly, and David rejoiced greatly with them (1 Chronicles 29:9). Generosity, restoration and worship come together there, and the joy that results is loud enough to be heard far off.

Psalm 104 and Psalm 96 — The joy of God and of creation. Psalm 104 uses sâmach three times, and together those uses trace a circle of gladness. God gives wine that makes glad the heart of man (Psalm 104:15). The psalmist then prays, "May the LORD rejoice in His works" (Psalm 104:31). Finally he answers for himself: "I will be glad in the LORD" (Psalm 104:34). God delights in what He has made, which echoes His verdict over creation in Genesis 1:31 that it was very good. People in turn delight in Him. Psalm 96:11 widens the circle and calls the heavens to rejoice and the earth to be glad, because the LORD is coming to judge the world in righteousness (Psalm 96:13). Creation's joy is tied to God's rule being set right. Joy is the world's proper response to its rightful King.

Obadiah 12 and Proverbs 24:17 — Joy that God judges. Edom watched Babylon destroy Jerusalem and rejoiced over the people of Judah in the day of their ruin (Obadiah 12). God condemned Edom for that gladness, as He did Ammon, which clapped and rejoiced over Israel's desolation (Ezekiel 25:6; compare Ezekiel 35:14–15). Lamentations 2:17 adds a sobering detail: the LORD Himself allowed the enemy to rejoice over Jerusalem as part of His discipline. Even so, the enemy's gloating was still sin. Proverbs gives the same warning in personal terms: "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls, And do not let your heart be glad when he stumbles." The next verse warns that the LORD may see this, be displeased and turn His wrath away from the enemy (Proverbs 24:18). God takes no pleasure in a heart that feasts on another person's ruin, even when that ruin is deserved.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The prophets place the highest joy at the arrival of God Himself. "Sing and rejoice, O daughter of Zion! For behold, I am coming and I will dwell in your midst," says the LORD (Zechariah 2:10). The next verse adds that many nations will join themselves to the LORD in that day. Zephaniah 3:14 calls Jerusalem to be glad and rejoice with all her heart, because the King of Israel, the LORD, is in her midst (Zephaniah 3:15). Then Zephaniah turns the joy around: the LORD will rejoice over His people with gladness (Zephaniah 3:17).

The New Testament takes up the same biblical promise of God dwelling among His people. John writes that "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). At the end of the story John hears, "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them," and God wipes away every tear (Revelation 21:3–4). Neither passage quotes Zechariah 2:10 or lays out a sequence of fulfillments, but the connection is strong: the hope Zechariah announced, God coming to dwell in the midst of His people, is the hope these passages see realized in Christ and brought to completion in the new creation. The joy the prophets announced is the joy of God's nearness.

Other uses of sâmach point the same way. Psalm 16:9 says, "Therefore my heart is glad," because God will not leave His Holy One to see corruption. Peter applies this psalm to the resurrection of Jesus (Acts 2:25–31). Isaiah describes people rejoicing as at harvest because a Child is born who will rule forever (Isaiah 9:3, 6). Isaiah 56:7 promises that foreigners will be made joyful in God's house of prayer, and Jesus quoted that promise when He cleansed the temple (Mark 11:17). The Deuteronomy table that welcomed the stranger widens in the gospel to include the nations.

7. Spirit & Life

Paul's command "Rejoice in the Lord always" (Philippians 4:4) is written in Greek. It stands in the same line as the Psalms' call to be glad in the LORD. Joy can be commanded because it rests on God, who does not change. Moods do change. That is why Micah can tell his enemy not to rejoice over him, because when he falls he will rise, and when he sits in darkness the LORD will be his light (Micah 7:8). Gladness that rests on God is not at the mercy of circumstances.

Sâmach also teaches that joy is meant to be passed on. Proverbs returns again and again to a wise child who makes a father and mother glad (Proverbs 10:1; 23:15, 24–25; 27:11). Our choices do not stay private. Obedience, wisdom and kindness add to other people's joy, and folly takes joy away from them. Deuteronomy asks the same question at every feast: who is at your table? A believer who has tasted God's goodness has something to share with the lonely and the poor.

The word also gives us a test for our own hearts. What makes us glad? A rival's failure, an opponent's embarrassment, a critic's downfall? Edom's gladness is a warning, and Psalm 35:27 offers the better path. There the psalmist asks God to let those who favor His righteous cause shout for joy and be glad. Zechariah 4:10 adds a gentle word: God's eyes rejoice to see the plumb line in Zerubbabel's hand, even on a day of small things. God takes delight in faithful work long before it looks impressive.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 4:14 — The LORD tells Moses that Aaron will be glad in his heart to see him. This is a simple, warm use of the word for a brother's welcome.

  • Psalm 19:8 — The statutes of the LORD rejoice the heart. God's Word is itself a source of gladness.

  • Psalm 90:14–15 — Moses asks God to make His people glad according to the days of their affliction, so that joy answers their sorrow.

  • Psalm 118:24 — The day the LORD has made calls for rejoicing and gladness.

  • Psalm 122:1 — The pilgrim is glad when others say, "Let us go into the house of the LORD."

  • Isaiah 39:2 — Hezekiah is pleased with the Babylonian envoys and shows them his treasures. His misplaced gladness brings a word of judgment.

  • Jonah 4:6 — Jonah is very grateful for the shade plant. He rejoices over his own comfort while caring nothing for Nineveh.
