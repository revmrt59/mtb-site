JOY, GLADNESS - (simchâh, Strong's H8057)

Summary

The Hebrew noun simchâh is one of the Old Testament's main words for joy. NKJV usually translates it "joy" or "gladness," and sometimes "mirth," "pleasure," or "rejoicing." It can describe the gladness a person feels in the heart, and it can also describe the joy people express together through singing, feasting, music, and shouting. Scripture uses the word for ordinary human happiness at harvests and weddings, and also for the celebration that filled Jerusalem when God's people worshiped Him. The same word warns that joy pursued for its own sake turns empty.

The word's richest uses place the source of joy in God Himself. David says, "In Your presence is fullness of joy" (Psalm 16:11). Another psalmist calls the Lord "God my exceeding joy" (Psalm 43:4). Zephaniah goes further and says God rejoices over His people "with gladness" (Zephaniah 3:17). In the prophets, joy falls silent when judgment comes and returns when God redeems His people. Isaiah pictures the ransomed returning to Zion with "everlasting joy on their heads." For a believer, this joy is a gift that God gives, restores after sin, and promises will never end.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Simchâh belongs to the common Hebrew word family for rejoicing. It is related to the Hebrew verb sâmach (Strong's H8055), usually translated rejoice or be glad. Although it is a noun, it often names something people do as much as something they feel. That is why it fits so naturally with songs, instruments, feasts, and festival days.

The word appears throughout the Old Testament. Many uses cluster in the historical books, where they describe temple worship, the return of the ark, and the Purim celebration in Esther. Other uses appear in the Psalms, in the wisdom books of Proverbs and Ecclesiastes, and in the prophets Isaiah and Jeremiah. Simchâh is often paired with the companion Hebrew noun sâsôn (Strong's H8342), and the pair is usually translated "joy and gladness." Joining the two words expresses joy that is complete and overflowing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Simchâh means joy or gladness. Context shows whether a passage stresses the feeling, the celebration, or the pleasure being pursued.

1. Inner gladness of heart. This is joy as a person experiences it within. "You have put gladness in my heart, More than in the season that their grain and wine increased" (Psalm 4:7).

2. Festive rejoicing. This is joy expressed aloud and together through music, feasting, and celebration. When David organized the Levite singers, they were to raise their voices "with resounding joy" (1 Chronicles 15:16).

3. Pleasure or mirth. This is enjoyment pursued as an experience. The word itself is neutral, but this use is sometimes empty. The Preacher in Ecclesiastes says, "I will test you with mirth" (Ecclesiastes 2:1), and Proverbs 21:17 warns that whoever loves pleasure will become poor.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The person rejoicing and the reason for the joy determine its character. God's people rejoice at His feasts, at the dedication of His house, and at deliverance from their enemies. God's enemies can also rejoice. The Philistines gathered to rejoice before their god Dagon over the captured Samson (Judges 16:23), and Edom rejoiced at Israel's ruin (Ezekiel 35:15; 36:5). The word does not make joy holy. Its object does.

Scripture often presents God as the giver of joy. Ezra 6:22 says that "the LORD made them joyful." Nehemiah 12:43 says the same thing even more strongly: "God had made them rejoice with great joy."

Poets also give joy a concrete form. It can be worn like clothing (Psalm 30:11) or set like a crown on the head (Isaiah 35:10). It can be sown like seed (Psalm 97:11). Jeremiah hears it as a "voice" that fills the streets (Jeremiah 7:34).

One grammatical pattern helps the reader. Hebrew sometimes joins the verb rejoice with this noun, so that people literally rejoice a great joy (Nehemiah 12:43; Jonah 4:6). The repetition intensifies the meaning. English translations bring out the same force with words like "greatly," "very," or "exceeding."

4. Theological Significance

Joy in Scripture flows from God and returns to Him in praise. Joy is a fitting response to who He is and what He has done. Moses treated this response as a serious matter. One reason Deuteronomy 28:47 gives for Israel's coming judgment is that the people did not serve the LORD their God with joy and gladness of heart when He had given them abundance. A people blessed by God but joyless before Him had lost sight of Him.

For the same reason, joy in Israel's worship was public and organized. David appointed singers to raise the sound of joy (1 Chronicles 15:16). The people gave toward the temple with joy (1 Chronicles 29:17). At the dedication of the rebuilt wall, "the joy of Jerusalem was heard afar off" (Nehemiah 12:43), and the women and children joined in. Joy over God's goodness was meant to be heard by others.

Ecclesiastes adds an important balance. The Preacher tested mirth as a goal in itself and concluded, "I said of laughter—'Madness!'; and of mirth, 'What does it accomplish?'" (Ecclesiastes 2:2). Proverbs warns that "the end of mirth may be grief" (Proverbs 14:13). Yet Ecclesiastes later describes a person whom God keeps busy with the joy of his heart (Ecclesiastes 5:20). It also says God gives joy to the one who is good in His sight (Ecclesiastes 2:26) and urges a person to eat bread with joy because God has accepted his works (Ecclesiastes 9:7). The pleasures of life are the same in both places. What differs is whether a person grabs at joy or receives it from God's hand.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Psalm 16:11 and Psalm 43:4: joy found in God's presence. David closes Psalm 16 with this confession: "In Your presence is fullness of joy; At Your right hand are pleasures forevermore." Earlier in the psalm, he set this against a warning: "Their sorrows shall be multiplied who hasten after another god" (Psalm 16:4). One road multiplies sorrow, and the other leads to fullness of joy. The psalm does not locate joy in what God gives apart from Himself. Joy reaches its fullness where He is. Psalm 43:4 says the same thing in personal terms. The psalmist goes to the altar "To God my exceeding joy." In Hebrew, simchâh is joined there to the Hebrew noun gîl (Strong's H1524), meaning rejoicing, so God is literally the gladness of my joy. God is the joy, and He is also its source.

Zephaniah 3:17: God's own gladness. The prophet first calls the daughter of Zion to sing and rejoice (Zephaniah 3:14). Then he turns the picture around: "He will rejoice over you with gladness, He will quiet you with His love, He will rejoice over you with singing." The word used for Israel's festival joy is now used for God's joy. The Mighty One who saves is not a reluctant rescuer. He rejoices over the people He restores. Their security rests on that delight, which comes from His own heart and not from how well they perform.

Nehemiah 8 and Jeremiah 15:16: joy that comes through God's word. When Ezra read the Law, the teachers "gave the sense, and helped them to understand the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8). The people wept as the words exposed their sin (Nehemiah 8:9). The leaders then told them to stop mourning and celebrate, and the people went home "to eat and drink, to send portions and rejoice greatly, because they understood the words that were declared to them" (Nehemiah 8:12). Their conviction gave way to joy as they understood. Days later the people kept the Feast of Booths with very great gladness (Nehemiah 8:17). The famous line "the joy of the LORD is your strength" (Nehemiah 8:10) uses a different Hebrew word, chedvâh (Strong's H2304), but it belongs to the same scene. Jeremiah describes the same pattern in one person. Though he was lonely and persecuted, he could say, "Your words were found, and I ate them, And Your word was to me the joy and rejoicing of my heart" (Jeremiah 15:16).

Psalm 51:8 and Psalm 30:11: joy restored. After his sin with Bathsheba, David does not try to produce joy himself. He asks for it: "Make me hear joy and gladness, That the bones You have broken may rejoice." The broken bones describe God's discipline. Gladness has to be heard from God. In the psalm, this request comes right after David's plea to be washed "whiter than snow" (Psalm 51:7) and before he asks God to create a clean heart in him and not to take His Holy Spirit from him (Psalm 51:10–11). Psalm 30:11 tells what happens when God answers such a prayer: "You have put off my sackcloth and clothed me with gladness." God removes the clothing of grief and dresses the psalmist in joy.

Jeremiah and Isaiah: joy silenced and joy crowned. Jeremiah announces judgment with a repeated phrase: God will silence "the voice of mirth and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" (Jeremiah 7:34; compare Jeremiah 16:9; 25:10). The phrase means more than the loss of good feelings. A city without weddings and celebrations is dying. Yet Jeremiah also promises that the same voices will be heard again in the desolate streets (Jeremiah 33:11). Isaiah carries the hope to its end: "The ransomed of the LORD shall return, And come to Zion with singing, With everlasting joy on their heads. They shall obtain joy and gladness, And sorrow and sighing shall flee away" (Isaiah 35:10; repeated in Isaiah 51:11). In place of the dust and ashes of mourning, the redeemed wear a crown of joy. The joy that judgment took away does not simply return for a while. It becomes everlasting, and sorrow runs from it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament applies Psalm 16 directly to Jesus. In his Pentecost sermon, Peter quotes the psalm's closing lines about being made full of joy in God's presence. He applies them to the risen Christ, whose flesh did not see corruption (Acts 2:25–31). Peter explains that David, "being a prophet," foresaw and "spoke concerning the resurrection of the Christ" (Acts 2:30–31). In Peter's reading, the joy in God's presence that David confessed finds its fullest meaning in Jesus, whom God raised up and exalted to His right hand (Acts 2:32–33). Hebrews says Jesus endured the cross "for the joy that was set before Him" (Hebrews 12:2). On the night before His death, He told His disciples that He had spoken to them so that His joy would remain in them and their joy would be full (John 15:11).

Isaiah 61 makes a related connection. Jesus read the opening of that chapter in Nazareth and declared it fulfilled (Luke 4:18–21). The same chapter promises the mourners of Zion that "everlasting joy shall be theirs" (Isaiah 61:7). In this way, the anointed Servant who proclaims good news brings the everlasting joy promised in Isaiah 35 and 51.

The bridegroom imagery also develops across Scripture. Jeremiah's silenced "voice of the bridegroom" is heard again when John the Baptist rejoices at the Bridegroom's voice (John 3:29). Song of Solomon 3:11 calls King Solomon's wedding day "the day of the gladness of his heart." That verse describes Solomon, and Scripture does not interpret it as a prophecy. Still, it resonates with the larger biblical picture of Christ rejoicing over His bride. The story ends where Isaiah pointed. In the New Jerusalem, God wipes away every tear and sorrow is no more (Revelation 21:4). Zephaniah's God who rejoices over His people dwells among them forever.

7. Spirit & Life

God's delight in His people comes before their joy in Him. Before anyone could call God "my exceeding joy," He had already determined to rejoice over His people with singing. A believer's gladness answers His, and that is why it can survive seasons when feelings run low. Even then, His delight in His redeemed has not changed.

Joy usually comes through understanding. The weeping crowd in Nehemiah's day did not cheer itself up. The people rejoiced "because they understood the words." Jeremiah ate God's words, and they became the joy of his heart while he was suffering. A believer who wants lasting joy should go back to Scripture with a desire to understand it, not merely to skim it.

When sin has silenced joy, Psalm 51 shows the way back. David asked God to wash him and to let him hear gladness again. Confession does not earn joy, but it clears the way for the gladness God is willing to give.

Psalm 97:11 offers encouragement for dark seasons: "Light is sown for the righteous, And gladness for the upright in heart." Sown seed is hidden before it sprouts. God may already have planted gladness in a believer's life before any sign of harvest appears.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ezra 3:12–13: When the foundation of the second temple was laid, the older people wept while others shouted for joy. The sounds mixed so completely that no one could tell joy from weeping.

  • Esther 9:22: Purim celebrated the month that was turned for the Jews from sorrow to joy. They marked it by feasting and by sending gifts to the poor.

  • Psalm 137:3, 6: In Babylon, the captors "requested mirth" from the exiles. The exiles refused to forget Jerusalem and set it above their chief joy.

  • Isaiah 22:13: Jerusalem responded to God's call to mourn with joy and feasting, saying in effect, "Eat and drink, for tomorrow we die." Paul later quotes these words (1 Corinthians 15:32).

  • Jonah 4:6: Jonah rejoiced greatly over a shade plant while he resented God's mercy to Nineveh. His joy revealed what he truly valued.

  • Proverbs 21:15: Doing justice is a joy to the righteous but brings destruction to evildoers. The same justice affects the two groups in opposite ways.
