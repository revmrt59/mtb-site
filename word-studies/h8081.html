OIL, OINTMENT - (shemen, Strong's H8081)

Summary

The Hebrew word shemen means oil, above all the oil pressed from olives. In Israel it was part of ordinary life. Women baked with it, families burned it in their lamps, people rubbed it on their skin and faces, and healers applied it to wounds. It was also the oil God chose for His holiest purposes. Poured on Aaron's head, it set him apart as priest. Poured from Samuel's horn, it marked David as king, and Scripture adds that "the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward" (1 Samuel 16:13). Burned in the golden lampstand, it kept light shining continually in God's house.

Oil becomes one of Scripture's important pictures of the Holy Spirit's consecrating presence and power. When Samuel anointed David, the Spirit of the LORD came upon him; in Zechariah's vision, an oil-fed lampstand accompanies God's promise that His work will be accomplished by His Spirit. These passages prepare us to understand Jesus, the Anointed One, whom God anointed with the Holy Spirit and power. Through Him, believers receive the Spirit who dwells in them and equips them to serve God. Oil also carries the Bible's pictures of healing, light, fellowship, and joy. Its meaning in each passage grows from what God does there, while the anointing theme draws these gifts together around His presence with His people.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Olive oil was one of the three great staples of the land, usually listed with grain and new wine. Moses described Canaan as a land of olive oil and honey (Deuteronomy 8:8). God required pure oil from pressed olives for the sanctuary lamp (Exodus 27:20).

Most uses of shemen fall into ordinary settings: cooking, lamps, trade, cosmetics, and medicine. Others belong to worship, where oil was mixed into offerings, burned before the LORD, and compounded with spices into the holy anointing oil (Exodus 30:25). Hebrew also uses the word to describe richness, and in the phrase "wood of oil" it names the olive or oil-producing tree (1 Kings 6:23). The act of anointing is expressed by a different Hebrew verb, so shemen is the substance poured, and the anointing is what that pouring accomplished.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shemen names oil, especially olive oil, along with things marked by oil's richness.

1. Olive oil as a household and commercial substance, used in food, fuel, and trade. Solomon paid Hiram in wheat and pressed oil (1 Kings 5:11).

2. Perfumed oil or ointment for the body, translated "ointment" or "perfumes" in the NKJV, as in the fragrant ointments of Song of Solomon 1:3 and Esther's months of beauty treatment with oil of myrrh (Esther 2:12).

3. Sacred oil used in worship, whether mixed into offerings, burned in the lamp, or blended into the holy anointing oil (Exodus 29:7; Leviticus 2:1).

4. Richness or fertility in a descriptive sense, as in the "very fruitful hill" of Isaiah 5:1, the "verdant valleys" of Isaiah 28:1, and the "fat things" of the Lord's feast (Isaiah 25:6).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

More than half the occurrences sit in Exodus through Numbers, where oil appears again and again in instructions for the tabernacle, grain offerings, priestly ordination, and cleansing rites. In Leviticus 2, the fine flour is either mixed with oil or has oil poured over it. In Numbers 7, each tribal leader brings flour mixed with oil, and the repetition itself shows a whole nation bringing the same gift to God.

The historical books move oil from the sanctuary into public life. It is poured on the heads of Saul (1 Samuel 10:1), David, Solomon (1 Kings 1:39), and Jehu (2 Kings 9:6). It also becomes the substance of prophetic miracles. Wisdom literature uses oil for hospitality, gladness, and reputation. In the prophets it marks God's abundance and also Israel's betrayal of Him.

A few Hebrew phrases deserve attention. When oil is joined to another noun, as in "the oil of gladness" (Psalm 45:7) or "the oil of joy" (Isaiah 61:3), the phrase names what the oil brings or signifies. When God calls it "My oil" (Ezekiel 16:18), the possessive makes plain who supplied it and who was robbed when it went to idols.

4. Theological Significance

Oil teaches first that every good thing in Israel's hands came from God. Psalm 104:15 places "Oil to make his face shine" among the Creator's gifts alongside wine and bread. That truth sharpens the prophets' indictment. In Ezekiel 16 the LORD recalls how He had washed and anointed Jerusalem with oil (16:9). Then she "set My oil and My incense before them," meaning the idols, and offered "My food which I gave you—the pastry of fine flour, oil, and honey" as sweet incense to false gods (16:18–19). Hosea adds that Israel credited her lovers with supplying her oil (Hosea 2:5) and that Ephraim sent its oil abroad to buy foreign protection: "they make a covenant with the Assyrians, And oil is carried to Egypt" (Hosea 12:1). The grain offering of Leviticus 2 had taught Israel to give God's gifts back to Him in worship. A wayward heart takes the same gifts and spends them on idols or on political insurance, and in doing so it withholds from God what was already His.

Oil expresses holiness and the power needed for a holy calling. God commanded the tabernacle, its furnishings, and Aaron and his sons to be anointed with the sacred oil; they were set apart for His service (Exodus 30:26–30). He also forbade copying that oil for ordinary use (30:31–33). When kings were anointed, the pouring marked God's choice of a servant. Saul's and David's stories take the meaning further: God gives His Spirit to equip those He calls. The visible anointing and the Spirit's coming belong together in these accounts. Oil thus gives us a concrete picture of consecration joined to divine empowerment.

Joy belongs here as well. Oil on the head marked celebration, so mourners went without it (2 Samuel 14:2). When God promises "the oil of joy for mourning," He promises to reverse the grief of His people.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Anointed with oil, empowered by the Spirit (Exodus 29:7; Leviticus 8:12; 1 Samuel 10:1–10; 16:13). Moses poured oil on Aaron's head to consecrate him for priestly service. Samuel later poured oil on Saul and told him that the Spirit of the LORD would come upon him. The promise was fulfilled when the Spirit came upon Saul and he prophesied (1 Samuel 10:6, 10). At David's anointing the connection is immediate: Samuel poured from the horn of oil, and “the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward” (16:13). The pouring makes God's setting apart visible; the Spirit supplies the power for the calling. Anointing is therefore more than appointment to an office. These stories direct our attention to the presence of God enabling a person to serve Him.

The lamp that never went out (Exodus 27:20; Leviticus 24:2). God told Moses to command the people "that they bring you pure oil of pressed olives for the light, to cause the lamp to burn continually." The light in the Holy Place depended on oil that was pure and that had been crushed out of the olive. The people supplied it. God gave the light of His presence, and He also called His people to keep bringing what sustained it.

The oil-fed lampstand and the Spirit (Zechariah 4). Zechariah sees a golden lampstand with a bowl, lamps, and two olive trees beside it. Later he asks about the branches supplying golden oil (4:2–3, 12). God's word to Zerubbabel interprets the vision's encouragement: “Not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit” (4:6). Zerubbabel faces the task of completing God's house, and the vision turns his confidence toward God's supply. The oil sustaining the light gives a fitting picture of the Spirit enabling God's work to continue. The chapter also identifies the two anointed ones who stand beside the Lord (4:14), keeping the image connected to His servants and their calling. God's people are called to labor with confidence in the Spirit who supplies what their own strength cannot.

Oil on the blood (Leviticus 14:10–18). The cleansing of a healed leper contains one of the most moving uses of the word. The priest put blood from the guilt offering on the man's right ear, right thumb, and right big toe (14:14). He then sprinkled oil seven times before the LORD and put oil on the same three places, directly on top of the blood (14:16–17). The rest of the oil went on the man's head (14:18). Those same three marks of blood had been placed on Aaron and his sons at their ordination (Leviticus 8:23–24), and oil had been poured on Aaron's head. The returning outcast therefore receives marks that recall the consecration of priests. The order matters. Oil follows blood and rests on it, so the man is first atoned for and then set apart in his hearing, his work, and his walk. God did more than tolerate the cleansed leper at the edge of the camp. He brought him back as a man marked for Himself.

Oil running down (Psalm 23:5; Psalm 133:2). In Psalm 23 the LORD becomes the Host: "You anoint my head with oil; My cup runs over." Enemies are watching, and God honors His guest at a full table anyway. Psalm 133 compares brothers dwelling in unity to "the precious oil upon the head, Running down on the beard, The beard of Aaron, Running down on the edge of his garments." The oil begins on the high priest's head and does not stay there. It flows down to the edge of his garments. The unity of God's people is pictured as priestly anointing spreading from the head to the whole body, and the psalm closes by saying that there the LORD commanded the blessing (133:3).

The oil that did not run out (1 Kings 17:12–16; 2 Kings 4:2–7). The widow of Zarephath had only a handful of flour and a little oil, and she expected to die. Through Elijah, God promised the jar would not run dry, and it did not. Elisha told another widow to borrow empty vessels, "not a few," and pour her last jar of oil into them. When no vessel was left, "the oil ceased" (4:6). The flow stopped only when there was nowhere left for it to go. In both homes God took the last thing in the house and made it enough.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The anointed priests and kings of Israel point forward to one Anointed One, the Messiah. In Psalm 45:7 God anoints the King "with the oil of gladness more than Your companions." Hebrews 1:9 quotes those words of the Son, which makes this a stated fulfillment. Jesus is the King whose joy exceeds that of every anointed king before Him.

Isaiah 61 brings the anointing theme directly into the Messiah's mission: the Spirit of the Lord is upon Him because the LORD has anointed Him to bring good news and liberty (61:1). His work gives mourners “The oil of joy for mourning” (61:3). Jesus read the opening of this prophecy in Nazareth and announced its fulfillment in Himself (Luke 4:18–21). Peter states the connection plainly: God anointed Jesus “with the Holy Spirit and with power,” and Jesus went about doing good and healing the oppressed, “for God was with Him” (Acts 10:38). Israel's oil-anointed servants prepare us for the Messiah whose ministry displays the Spirit's power. Consecration, compassionate service, deliverance, and joy meet in Him.

Leviticus 14 fits this story as a pattern rather than a stated prophecy. Blood comes first, and oil rests on it. In the gospel, Christ's sacrifice cleanses, and the Spirit is poured out on those who have been cleansed. The order of that ancient rite matches the order of redemption.

The pressed oil of Exodus 27:20 finds a striking echo in the garden where Jesus prayed on the Mount of Olives (Matthew 26:36). The name Gethsemane is commonly understood to mean "oil press." Scripture does not draw this connection explicitly, but it is hard to miss. Oil is drawn from pressed olives, and on the night He was betrayed the Anointed One was pressed under the weight of the sins He came to bear.

7. Spirit & Life

The anointing reaches the believer through Christ. Paul says that God establishes us in Christ, has anointed us, and has given us the Spirit in our hearts as a guarantee (2 Corinthians 1:21–22). John likewise tells believers that they have an anointing from the Holy One and that this anointing abides in them (1 John 2:20, 27). His concern is their protection from deceivers: they are to remain in the truth they heard from the beginning (2:24–27). The Spirit keeps their attention on Christ and His truth. Anointing belongs to life in Him, rather than being a spiritual distinction reserved for a few impressive people.

The oil placed over the sacrificial blood in Leviticus 14 gives this life of service a moving picture. The cleansed person's ear, hand, and foot are marked for God. Read in the light of Christ's sacrifice and His gift of the Spirit, the order helps us see that service grows from grace already received. We do not earn God's presence by making ourselves useful. Those whom Christ has cleansed can offer their hearing, work, and walk to the Spirit's direction and enabling.

Oil-anointed servants and the oil-fed light also challenge self-reliance. A title, skill, or opportunity cannot supply the Spirit's power. The Lord who calls us to serve invites us to depend upon Him in prayer and to obey what He has made known. His presence enables faithful service, and the gladness associated with oil points us toward the joy of sharing in the Anointed One's life.

The widows' stories carry their own lesson. God's supply was never the limit. Elisha told the widow to gather "not a few" vessels, and the oil stopped only when she had no more. Bringing God our emptiness is a fitting response to Him, because empty vessels were exactly what He filled.

Wisdom literature describes what a life near God looks like day to day. "There is desirable treasure, And oil in the dwelling of the wise, But a foolish man squanders it" (Proverbs 21:20). The wise keep and steward what God gives, while the fool spends it all. Ecclesiastes 9:8 says, "Let your garments always be white, And let your head lack no oil," calling the reader to enjoy God's gifts with gladness in a short life. Song of Solomon 1:3 adds fragrance: "Your name is ointment poured forth." A life near God gives off something others notice.

Oil also heals. Isaiah grieved that Israel's wounds had not been "soothed with ointment" (Isaiah 1:6). The Good Samaritan did what Israel's leaders had not, "pouring on oil and wine" (Luke 10:34), and the church is still told to pray for the sick, "anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (James 5:14). God has never been indifferent to wounds.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 11:8 — The manna tasted like pastry prepared with oil. Even Israel's wilderness bread carried the taste of God's goodness.

  • Deuteronomy 32:13 — God fed Israel with oil from the flinty rock, abundance drawn from the hardest ground.

  • Psalm 55:21 — The betrayer's words were softer than oil, yet were drawn swords. Smoothness can hide violence.

  • Proverbs 5:3 — The immoral woman's mouth is smoother than oil, a warning that pleasant speech can lead to death.

  • Micah 6:7 — Even ten thousand rivers of oil could not buy God's favor. He asks for justice, mercy, and a humble walk with Him (6:8).

  • Ecclesiastes 10:1 — Dead flies make the perfumer's ointment stink, as a little folly spoils a respected life.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Oil and a good name. Scripture itself makes this comparison. "Your name is ointment poured forth" (Song of Solomon 1:3), and Ecclesiastes 7:1 compares a good name to precious ointment. In these poetic settings, fragrant oil stands for character and reputation spreading outward. This symbolism belongs to these passages and should not be read back into lamp oil or offerings.

Oil marking where God meets people. The first appearance of shemen comes long before the tabernacle. After his dream of the ladder, Jacob set up his stone pillow as a pillar, poured oil on it, and named the place Bethel, "house of God" (Genesis 28:18–19). He did so again on his return (35:14). From its first mention, oil marks a place where heaven touched earth.

"wood of oil" in Solomon's temple. The cherubim in the inner sanctuary were carved from "wood of oil," translated "olive wood" (1 Kings 6:23). So were the doors into the inner sanctuary and the doorposts of the temple entrance (6:31–33). The phrase probably names the kind of tree. Still, the guardians and gateways of God's dwelling were made from the tree that supplied the anointing oil.

Oil and battle. Soldiers oiled leather shields to keep them supple and serviceable. In his lament for Saul, David mourns the shield of the mighty "cast away," not anointed with oil (2 Samuel 1:21). The Hebrew is difficult and may refer to Saul himself. Either way, oil belongs to readiness for conflict as well as to feasting and healing, and a believer may fairly think of the shield of faith (Ephesians 6:16) as needing to be kept ready.

Mixed and poured. In Leviticus 2:4–5 the flour of the grain offering is either mixed with oil or has oil poured on it. Some Christian teachers have seen here the Spirit's work in Christ's conception and His anointing at baptism. This is a meditation drawn by analogy, not a meaning stated in the text, and it is best held loosely.
