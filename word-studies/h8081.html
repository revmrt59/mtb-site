OIL, OINTMENT - (shemen, Strong's H8081)

Summary

Shemen is the Hebrew word for oil, above all the olive oil pressed from the fruit of Israel's hillsides. In Scripture, the anointing oil becomes one of the clearest pictures of the Holy Spirit consecrating and empowering people for God's service. Moses poured it on Aaron's head to set him apart as priest. Samuel poured it on Saul and told him that the Spirit of the LORD would come upon him. When Samuel anointed David, “the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward” (1 Samuel 16:13). Zechariah saw a golden lampstand supplied by two olive trees and heard God's word for the rebuilding of His house: “Not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit” (Zechariah 4:6). The oil showed whom God had chosen and set apart. The Spirit's presence and power equipped that person to do the work.

These passages prepare us to understand Jesus, the Messiah, which means the Anointed One. God anointed Him “with the Holy Spirit and with power” (Acts 10:38), and through Him believers are themselves anointed and given the Spirit in their hearts (2 Corinthians 1:21–22). Oil also carries a gospel-shaped picture of restoration. When a healed leper was cleansed, the priest placed oil on top of sacrificial blood on his ear, thumb, and toe, marking him in the same places where Aaron's sons had been marked for priesthood (Leviticus 14:14–18). Psalm 133 pictures the unity of God's people as anointing oil flowing down from Aaron's head to the edge of his garments.

The ordinary uses of oil remain real and meaningful. Oil was in Israel's bread and lamps, on wounds and faces, and in storerooms as wealth. It speaks of joy, hospitality, healing, and God's care for two widows whose last jar of oil kept flowing. The prophets also warned against giving God's gift of oil to idols. Not every mention of oil points to the Spirit, but the anointing oil stands at the center of the word's biblical significance: God sets people apart and gives His Spirit to enable them to serve Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Olive trees grew well in Israel's hill country, and olive oil was one of the great staples of the land along with grain and new wine. Moses described the Promised Land as “a land of olive oil and honey” (Deuteronomy 8:8). Oil was pressed from olives, stored in jars and horns, traded abroad (1 Kings 5:11; Ezekiel 27:17), and counted among a king's treasures (2 Kings 20:13). God required “pure oil of pressed olives” for the sanctuary lamp (Exodus 27:20).

The word occurs 193 times, and exactly 100 of those occurrences fall in Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers. There oil appears again and again in instructions for the tabernacle lamp, the grain offerings, priestly ordination, cleansing rites, and the holy anointing oil compounded with spices (Exodus 30:25). In the historical books, the word gathers around the anointing of kings and the miracles of Elijah and Elisha. In the Psalms, Proverbs, and Prophets, oil becomes imagery for joy, honor, healing, smooth talk, and prosperity.

Hebrew expresses the act of anointing with a different verb. Shemen is the substance poured; the anointing is what God accomplished through that pouring.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shemen names oil, especially olive oil, along with things marked by oil's richness.

1. Olive oil as a household and commercial substance. Oil for food, lamp fuel, medicine, and trade, as in the “little oil in a jar” of the widow of Zarephath (1 Kings 17:12) or the oil Solomon sent to Hiram (1 Kings 5:11).

2. Fragrant oil or ointment. Oil blended with spices for grooming, perfume, or honor, translated “ointment” or “perfumes” in the NKJV, as in the fragrant ointments of Song of Solomon 1:3, Esther's oil of myrrh (Esther 2:12), and the “precious ointment” of Ecclesiastes 7:1.

3. Sacred oil in worship. Oil mixed into offerings, burned in the lamp, or compounded into the holy anointing oil that God reserved for His sanctuary and servants (Exodus 29:7; 30:25; Leviticus 2:1).

4. Richness or fertility. A descriptive use for what is rich and choice: the “very fruitful hill” of Isaiah 5:1, the “verdant” valley of Isaiah 28:1, and the “fat things” of the Lord's feast (Isaiah 25:6). In Psalm 109:24 it is the “fatness” a starving man has lost. Joined with the word for wood, it names olive wood, as in the cherubim and doors of Solomon's temple (1 Kings 6:23, 31).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines what oil means in a passage. In the sanctuary, oil is holy, and God forbids copying the anointing oil for private use (Exodus 30:31–33). In the home, it is food and comfort. At a feast, it is honor for a guest. On a wound, it is relief, so Isaiah's picture of a nation whose sores were not “soothed with ointment” (Isaiah 1:6) describes a people left without healing.

In the worship laws, the details of oil carry weight. In Leviticus 2, the fine flour of the grain offering is either mixed with oil or has oil poured on it. In Numbers 7, each tribal leader brings flour mixed with oil, and the repetition shows a whole nation bringing the same gift to God.

A few Hebrew phrases are worth noticing. When oil is joined to another noun, as in “the oil of gladness” (Psalm 45:7) or “the oil of joy” (Isaiah 61:3), the phrase names what the oil brings. When God calls it “My oil” (Ezekiel 16:18), the possessive makes plain who supplied it and who was robbed when it went to idols.

The poets also compare things to oil's physical qualities. Its smoothness describes flattery: the betrayer's “words were softer than oil, Yet they were drawn swords” (Psalm 55:21), and the immoral woman's “mouth is smoother than oil” (Proverbs 5:3). The way oil soaks deep into a material describes a curse sinking “like oil into his bones” (Psalm 109:18). These comparisons draw on texture and behavior. Oil does not stand for the Holy Spirit every time it appears; frying a cake in oil is cooking. The Spirit significance comes from the passages where Scripture itself sets the anointing oil beside the Spirit's coming and God's work, and those passages are among the most important in the Bible.

4. Theological Significance

God sets apart and equips those He calls. The anointing oil belonged to God alone. He commanded the tabernacle, its furnishings, and Aaron and his sons to be anointed with it, and whatever the oil touched now belonged to His service (Exodus 30:26–30; 40:9; Leviticus 8:10–12). Moses even sprinkled the oil on the altar seven times (Leviticus 8:11). When kings were anointed, the pouring declared God's choice. Saul's and David's stories carry the meaning further: the God who chooses also gives His Spirit to enable the one He has chosen. Consecration and empowerment belong together. God does not appoint servants and then leave them to their own resources.

Every good gift comes from God. Psalm 104:15 places “Oil to make his face shine” among the Creator's gifts with wine and bread. That is why the prophets grieve when the gift is turned against the Giver. In Ezekiel 16 the LORD recalls how He washed Jerusalem and anointed her with oil (16:9). Then she “set My oil and My incense before them,” offering the fine flour, oil, and honey He had fed her as sweet incense to idols (16:18–19). Hosea adds that Israel credited her lovers with supplying her oil (Hosea 2:5) and that Ephraim bought foreign protection: “they make a covenant with the Assyrians, And oil is carried to Egypt” (Hosea 12:1). The grain offering had taught Israel to give God's gifts back to Him in worship. A wayward heart takes the same gifts and spends them on idols or on political insurance.

Oil marks gladness and favor, and its absence speaks. Oil on the head marked celebration, so mourners went without it (2 Samuel 14:2). The ordinary grain offering came with oil and frankincense (Leviticus 2:1). Two offerings were required to have none. The poor person's flour sin offering was to have no oil “for it is a sin offering” (Leviticus 5:11), and the offering in a case of suspected adultery brought iniquity to remembrance (Numbers 5:15). The law states those reasons and goes no further. Even so, the contrast is telling: where sin or broken trust was in view, the offering came without the oil that accompanied gladness.

God cares for the wounded. Isaiah 1:6 describes a people covered with bruises and sores that no one had bound or soothed. Later Isaiah hears the Anointed One say He is sent “to heal the brokenhearted” (Isaiah 61:1). The healing missing in chapter 1 is supplied by the One God anoints.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Anointed with oil, empowered by the Spirit (Exodus 29:7; Leviticus 8:12; 1 Samuel 10:1–10; 16:13). The instruction for Aaron was simple: “take the anointing oil, pour it on his head, and anoint him” (Exodus 29:7). Moses did so “to consecrate him” (Leviticus 8:12), setting him apart to stand before God for the people.

With Saul the story moves from consecration to empowerment. Samuel “took a flask of oil and poured it on his head,” then asked, “Is it not because the LORD has anointed you commander over His inheritance?” (1 Samuel 10:1). The prophet held the flask, but the LORD did the anointing. Samuel then told Saul what would follow: “the Spirit of the LORD will come upon you, and you will prophesy with them and be turned into another man” (10:6), and “do as the occasion demands; for God is with you” (10:7). As Saul turned to leave, “God gave him another heart,” and at the hill “the Spirit of God came upon him, and he prophesied among them” (10:9–10). The oil marked him for a task. The Spirit made him able for it, and the promise “God is with you” set him free to act.

David's anointing makes the connection plainest. Samuel had passed over every one of Jesse's older sons until the youngest was brought in from the sheep, and the LORD said, “Arise, anoint him; for this is the one!” Then “Samuel took the horn of oil and anointed him in the midst of his brothers; and the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward” (16:12–13). The oil made God's choice visible in front of the very brothers who had been passed over. The Spirit gave David what the calling required, long before he wore a crown. The next verse adds a sober contrast: “But the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul” (16:14). The oil was never a charm. The power for service lay in the Spirit's presence, and Saul's disobedience shows that an anointed position cannot replace a life yielded to God. Later Solomon was anointed with “a horn of oil from the tabernacle” (1 Kings 1:39), so his kingship came from God's house, not from palace maneuvering.

The lamp that never went out, and the lampstand fed by olive trees (Exodus 27:20; Leviticus 24:2; Zechariah 4). Israel was told to bring “pure oil of pressed olives for the light, to cause the lamp to burn continually” (Exodus 27:20). This oil was for light. The olives had to be pressed and the oil had to be pure. The people supplied it, and the priests tended the lamp so that it never went out. If the oil failed, the holy place went dark.

Zechariah's vision takes up that picture. He sees “a lampstand of solid gold with a bowl on top of it,” seven lamps with seven pipes, and “Two olive trees” beside it (Zechariah 4:2–3). Israel's lamp needed people to keep bringing oil; this lampstand is fed straight from living trees, with “golden oil” draining through gold pipes (4:12). The interpretation comes as a word to Zerubbabel, the governor charged with rebuilding the temple after the exile: “Not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit,” says the LORD of hosts (4:6). The work looked impossible, a “great mountain” before a small and discouraged community. God promised that the mountain would become a plain, that Zerubbabel's hands which “laid the foundation of this temple” would “also finish it,” and that the capstone would be brought out with shouts of “Grace, grace to it!” (4:7, 9). He also asked, “who has despised the day of small things?” (4:10). Finally Zechariah learns that the two olive branches are “the two anointed ones, who stand beside the Lord of the whole earth” (4:14).

Zechariah describes the oil with different Hebrew vocabulary than shemen, so this chapter belongs to the canonical picture of anointing oil rather than to the word's own occurrences. The picture is unmistakably the same: light in God's house depends on oil God supplies, and the vision ties that supply to His Spirit and to His anointed servants. The vision is about work, about a temple to be finished by ordinary hands strengthened by a power not their own. Oil in this chapter means God's Spirit supplying what His servants need to complete what He has called them to do.

Oil on the blood (Leviticus 14:10–20). The cleansing of a healed leper contains one of the most moving uses of the word. A man who had lived outside the camp, cut off from worship and community, came on the eighth day with lambs, flour mixed with oil, and “one log of oil” (14:10). The priest waved the oil with the trespass offering before the LORD (14:12). Then he took blood from the trespass offering and put it “on the tip of the right ear of him who is to be cleansed, on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot” (14:14). He poured oil into his own left palm, dipped his right finger in it, and sprinkled it “seven times before the LORD” (14:15–16). Next he put oil on the same ear, thumb, and toe, “on the blood of the trespass offering” (14:17). “The rest of the oil” went on the man's head. “So the priest shall make atonement for him before the LORD” (14:18).

Those three marks are not random. At the ordination of the priests, Moses put the blood of the ram of consecration “on the tip of Aaron's right ear, on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot,” and did the same for Aaron's sons (Leviticus 8:23–24). He had also poured anointing oil on Aaron's head (8:12), sprinkled the altar seven times with oil (8:11), and sprinkled oil and blood together on Aaron and his garments (8:30). The returning outcast receives marks that recall the consecration of priests. He is not made a priest, but he is brought back to God marked in his hearing, his work, and his walk.

The order matters. Blood comes first, and oil rests on top of it. The man is atoned for, and then he is set apart. God did more than tolerate the cleansed leper at the edge of the camp. He brought him back into fellowship as someone marked for Himself.

Oil running down (Psalm 133). “Behold, how good and how pleasant it is For brethren to dwell together in unity! It is like the precious oil upon the head, Running down on the beard, The beard of Aaron, Running down on the edge of his garments” (Psalm 133:1–2). The oil is the priestly anointing oil, and it begins on the high priest's head. It does not stay there. It runs down his beard to the edge of his garments, covering the man who carried the names of Israel's tribes before God. The psalm then compares unity to “the dew of Hermon, Descending upon the mountains of Zion; For there the LORD commanded the blessing— Life forevermore” (133:3). Both pictures move downward. The unity of God's people is pictured as a gift that descends from above, flowing from the anointed head to the whole body, and the LORD commands His blessing where it is found.

The oil that did not run out (1 Kings 17:12–16; 2 Kings 4:1–7). Both stories begin with almost nothing. The widow of Zarephath had “a handful of flour in a bin, and a little oil in a jar” and expected to eat it with her son and die (1 Kings 17:12). Through Elijah, God promised the jar would not run dry until the rain returned, and it did not. Elisha met a widow whose creditor was coming to take her two sons. All she had was one jar of oil. Elisha told her to borrow empty vessels, “not a few,” shut the door, and pour (2 Kings 4:3–4). The oil kept flowing until there were no vessels left: “So the oil ceased” (4:6). The oil did not run out; the empty jars did. She sold the oil, paid the debt, and lived on the rest. In both homes God took the last thing in the house and made it enough.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The anointed priests and kings of Israel point forward to one Anointed One. The title Messiah comes from the Hebrew word for an anointed person, and Scripture moves steadily toward Him. Isaiah 61 brings oil and Spirit together in His own voice: “The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon Me, Because the LORD has anointed Me To preach good tidings to the poor; He has sent Me to heal the brokenhearted, To proclaim liberty to the captives, And the opening of the prison to those who are bound” (Isaiah 61:1). Here the anointing is defined by mission. The Spirit rests on Him so that He can preach, heal, free, and comfort. In the synagogue at Nazareth, Jesus read this passage and said, “Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing” (Luke 4:18–21).

Peter later summed up the whole ministry of Jesus in the same terms: “God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with Him” (Acts 10:38). Every thread of the Old Testament pattern meets here. As with David, the anointing and the Spirit belong together. As with Saul's promise, “God is with you,” God was with Him. As in Zechariah's vision, the work is accomplished by the Spirit's power. And unlike Saul, the Spirit never departed from Him. Jesus is the Servant whose whole life was consecrated to the Father and carried out in the Spirit's power.

Isaiah's prophecy continues: “To give them beauty for ashes, The oil of joy for mourning, The garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness” (Isaiah 61:3). Psalm 45 pictures the King Himself: “You love righteousness and hate wickedness; Therefore God, Your God, has anointed You With the oil of gladness more than Your companions” (Psalm 45:7). Hebrews 1:9 applies these words to the Son, making this a stated fulfillment. Jesus first receives the oil of gladness, anointed by the Father above every king before Him. He also gives it, turning His people's mourning into joy. The gladness believers receive is His gladness shared with them.

Leviticus 14 fits this story as a pattern rather than a stated prophecy. The New Testament never identifies the leper's cleansing with the gospel, but the order of the rite matches the order of redemption. Blood comes first, and oil rests upon it. Christ's sacrifice cleanses, and the Spirit is poured out on those who have been cleansed, setting them apart for God in what they hear, what they do, and where they go. Psalm 133 offers a similar resonance. Oil flowing from the high priest's head to the edge of his garments is a fitting picture of the Spirit's life flowing from Christ, the Head, to His whole body, binding His people together.

The pressed oil of Exodus 27:20 finds a striking echo on the night Jesus was betrayed. He prayed in His deepest agony at “a place called Gethsemane” on the Mount of Olives (Matthew 26:36), a name commonly understood to mean “oil press.” The Gospels do not draw out the connection, but it fits the larger story. The lamp in God's house needed oil from pressed olives, and the Anointed One went into the press, bearing the weight of the sins He came to carry, before the gladness and the Spirit could be poured out on His people.

Finally, the anointing reaches those who belong to Christ. “Now He who establishes us with you in Christ and has anointed us is God, who also has sealed us and given us the Spirit in our hearts as a guarantee” (2 Corinthians 1:21–22). John writes, “But you have an anointing from the Holy One, and you know all things,” and “the anointing which you have received from Him abides in you” (1 John 2:20, 27). The anointing that once rested on a few priests, kings, and prophets now belongs to every believer in Christ, because the Spirit who rested on Him has been given to His people.

7. Spirit & Life

The heart of this word for believers is dependence on the Spirit for service. God has never asked His servants to do His work in their own strength. Saul was told to act “as the occasion demands; for God is with you.” David went from the sheepfold to years of trial with the Spirit upon him “from that day forward.” Zerubbabel faced a mountain of discouragement and heard, “Not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit.” Jesus Himself went about doing good because God anointed Him with the Holy Spirit and with power. If the Son walked in the Spirit's power, His followers cannot expect to serve faithfully without it.

That challenges self-reliance in very practical ways. A title, a skill, an opportunity, or a long record of ministry cannot supply what the Spirit supplies. Saul had been anointed, yet the Spirit departed from him when he set aside God's word. The safeguard is not more effort but a life that keeps depending on God in prayer and keeps obeying what He has made known. Zechariah's lampstand is a lasting picture of that life: the light burns because the oil keeps flowing from a source beyond the lamp. Zerubbabel's small beginnings were not to be despised, because the outcome rested on God's Spirit rather than on the size of the work or the strength of the workers.

Leviticus 14 shows where such service begins. The oil was placed on the blood, not beside it. Read in the light of Christ's sacrifice and His gift of the Spirit, the order helps us see that service grows from grace already received. We do not earn God's presence by making ourselves useful. Those whom Christ has cleansed can offer their ears to hear His word, their hands to do His work, and their feet to walk in His ways, and they can trust the Spirit to direct and enable them.

The anointing also keeps believers in the truth. John's words came to Christians troubled by teachers who denied that Jesus is the Christ (1 John 2:22, 26). His answer was not to send them to a spiritual elite but to remind them that every one of them had an anointing from the Holy One, and that by it they would abide in the Son and in the Father (2:24, 27). Anointing belongs to ordinary life in Christ, not to a few impressive people. The same Spirit who equips us for service keeps our attention fixed on Jesus.

Psalm 133 brings this into the life of the church. Unity among God's people flows down like the anointing oil and the dew. It is received from above before it is practiced among us, and the LORD commands His blessing there. Christians who depend on the same Spirit are drawn together around the same Lord.

The widows' stories add their own encouragement. Elisha told the woman to gather “not a few” vessels, and the oil stopped only when she had nothing left to fill. God's supply was never the limit. A believer who comes to God empty, conscious of having nothing to offer, brings exactly what He has always filled.

Oil also reveals how God treats His people. “You anoint my head with oil; My cup runs over” (Psalm 23:5). The LORD honors His guest at a full table while enemies look on. Jesus noticed when that honor was withheld: “You did not anoint My head with oil, but this woman has anointed My feet with fragrant oil” (Luke 7:46). The woman who had been forgiven much gave Him the honor His host held back. Ecclesiastes adds, “A good name is better than precious ointment” (Ecclesiastes 7:1), a play on the similar-sounding Hebrew words for name (shem) and oil (shemen). Fragrance fades, but a name that honors God endures. Wisdom literature also calls God's people to receive His gifts with gladness, “let your head lack no oil” (Ecclesiastes 9:8), and to steward them well: “There is desirable treasure, And oil in the dwelling of the wise, But a foolish man squanders it” (Proverbs 21:20).

God has never been indifferent to wounds. Isaiah grieved that Israel's sores were not “soothed with ointment.” The Good Samaritan did what Israel's leaders had not, “pouring on oil and wine” (Luke 10:34), and the church is still told to pray for the sick, “anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord” (James 5:14). Yet the same smooth oil can describe words that hide swords. Psalm 141:5 shows the better way, comparing a righteous person's rebuke to excellent oil on the head, painful perhaps, but a true kindness.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 28:18 — After his dream of the ladder, Jacob set up his stone pillow as a pillar and poured oil on it, naming the place Bethel, “house of God.” In the word's first appearance, oil marks a place where God met a man.

Numbers 11:8 — The manna tasted like pastry prepared with oil. Even Israel's wilderness bread carried the taste of God's goodness.

Deuteronomy 32:13 — God fed Israel with oil from the flinty rock, abundance drawn from the hardest ground.

Psalm 89:20 — “I have found My servant David; With My holy oil I have anointed him.” God Himself claims the oil and the anointing, confirming that David's calling came from Him.

Psalm 92:10 — “I have been anointed with fresh oil.” The psalmist celebrates God's renewing strength.

Ecclesiastes 10:1 — Dead flies make the perfumer's ointment stink, as a little folly spoils a respected life.

Micah 6:7 — Even “ten thousand rivers of oil” cannot buy God's favor. He asks for justice, mercy, and a humble walk with Him (6:8).

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

Lexical relative: H8080 (shaman), “to be or grow fat,” the verb with which this noun is generally associated. The connection fits sense 4, richness.

Canonical pairings: H4886 (mashach), “to anoint,” which appears with oil in 1 Samuel 16:13. H4899 (mashiach), “anointed one,” the background of the title Messiah (Psalm 2:2).

Thematic connection: H3323 (yitshar), “fresh oil,” a different Hebrew word belonging to the oil imagery of Zechariah 4, including the “two anointed ones” of 4:14.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Olive wood in Solomon's temple. The cherubim in the inner sanctuary were carved from olive wood (1 Kings 6:23), and so were the doors of the inner sanctuary and the doorposts at the temple entrance (6:31–33). The phrase names the kind of tree, yet the guardians and gateways of God's dwelling were made from the tree that supplied the anointing oil.

Oil and battle. In his lament for Saul, David mourns the shield of the mighty “cast away,” not anointed with oil (2 Samuel 1:21). Soldiers oiled leather shields to keep them ready for use. The Hebrew is difficult and may refer to Saul himself. Either way, oil belongs to readiness for conflict as well as to feasting and healing, and a believer may fairly think of keeping the shield of faith (Ephesians 6:16) ready.

Mixed and poured. In Leviticus 2:4–5 the flour of the grain offering is either mixed with oil or has oil poured on it. Some Christian teachers have seen here a picture of the Spirit's work in Christ's conception and in His anointing at His baptism. This is a meditation drawn by analogy rather than a meaning stated in the text, and it is best held loosely.
