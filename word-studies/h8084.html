EIGHTY, EIGHTIETH - (shᵉmônîym, Strong's H8084)

Summary

The Hebrew word shᵉmônîym simply means eighty. Scripture uses it to count years, people, soldiers, workers, and days. Sometimes it stands alone, as when Moses is eighty years old, and sometimes it forms part of a larger number, such as the 185,000 Assyrians struck down in one night or the "four hundred and eightieth year" after the Exodus. The number carries no hidden meaning of its own. Its importance comes from the events God chose to record with it.

Those events are often striking. Psalm 90 speaks of eighty years as a long life granted "by reason of strength." Eighty is the age at which Barzillai tells David his senses have faded, and also the age at which God sends Moses to face Pharaoh. Eighty courageous priests stand together to resist proud King Uzziah in the temple. Under Ehud the land rests eighty years after its deliverance, twice the usual span in Judges. Eighty thousand stonecutters labor out of sight in the mountains so that God's house can rise in silence. In each case the precise count anchors the account in concrete detail, and the events show how God acts through weakness, faithfulness, and hidden labor.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shᵉmônîym is the plural form of the Hebrew word for eight, and it functions as the number eighty, just as the plural of ten gives twenty in Hebrew. The NKJV nearly always translates it as "eighty." In 1 Kings 6:1 it is rendered "eightieth" within a date.

The word appears mostly in historical books: the family records of Genesis, the census lists of Numbers and Chronicles, military reports in Kings and Chronicles, and the returning exiles in Ezra and Nehemiah. It also appears once in a psalm, once in the Song of Solomon, and in the prophets Isaiah and Jeremiah. These are the places where Scripture counts things carefully and records people and events in specific detail.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shᵉmônîym has one meaning: the quantity eighty. It is used in two ways:

1. As a number on its own, counting eighty years, people, or objects, as with Moses' age in Exodus 7:7 or the eighty priests in 2 Chronicles 26:17.

2. As part of a compound number or date, as in 180 years (Genesis 35:28), 80,000 workers (1 Kings 5:15), or "the four hundred and eightieth year" (1 Kings 6:1).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting determines what is being measured. In Genesis and Exodus, eighty measures human ages and lifespans: Methuselah and Lamech in Genesis 5, Abram at eighty-six when Ishmael is born (Genesis 16:16), Isaac dying at 180 (Genesis 35:28), and Moses and Aaron at eighty and eighty-three (Exodus 7:7). In Judges, it measures a period of peace. In Kings and Chronicles, it counts work crews and armies, such as Rehoboam's 180,000 chosen men (1 Kings 12:21) and the large divisions under Jehoshaphat (2 Chronicles 17:15, 18). After the exile, it numbers the faithful who return, including Zebadiah and eighty men with him (Ezra 8:8).

Psalm 90:10 is the only poetic passage that treats eighty as a measure of life itself, and that verse gives the number its most memorable setting.

4. Theological Significance

Because shᵉmônîym is a counting word, its theological weight lies in what its precision shows about God. He acts in real history, at definite times, among a definite number of people. First Kings 6:1 dates the start of the temple "in the four hundred and eightieth year after the children of Israel had come out of the land of Egypt." That single sentence ties Israel's rescue from slavery to the building of the house where God would dwell among them. The Lord who brought His people out also brought them to a place of worship, and Scripture marks the span between those events by the calendar.

The same precision runs through God's judgments and rescues. When Assyria surrounded Jerusalem, Hezekiah had no army that could win. Second Kings records that "it came to pass on a certain night that the angel of the LORD went out, and killed in the camp of the Assyrians one hundred and eighty-five thousand" (2 Kings 19:35). Isaiah 37:36 reports the same event with different opening words. The exact figure states the size of the deliverance plainly. God did not merely encourage His people. He ended the threat completely, before morning.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Eighty years: the end of human strength and the beginning of God's work (Psalm 90:10; 2 Samuel 19:32–35; Exodus 7:7). Moses wrote Psalm 90, and in it he describes a typical lifespan in poetic terms: "The days of our lives are seventy years; And if by reason of strength they are eighty years, Yet their boast is only labor and sorrow." Seventy years is a full life, and eighty is a longer one granted "by reason of strength," yet still heavy.

Barzillai shows what such an age can feel like. At eighty he had generously supported David during Absalom's rebellion, and David invited him to live at court. Barzillai declined. He told the king that he could no longer tell good from bad in taste and could no longer hear the voices of singing men and women, so why should he become a burden? He asked only to return home and die near his parents' grave (2 Samuel 19:32–37).

Yet eighty is exactly the age Exodus records for Moses when he and Aaron stood before Pharaoh (Exodus 7:7). God began the great deliverance of His people through a man who had reached the age Moses himself would later call "labor and sorrow." No one could credit the plagues, the Red Sea, or the long walk out of Egypt to the vigor of an eighty-year-old shepherd. Caleb adds another witness. At eighty-five he claimed that the Lord had kept him alive and as strong as he had been forty-five years earlier, and he asked for the hill country where the giants lived (Joshua 14:10–12). Barzillai's honesty about his weakness is not wrong. He simply shows the natural decline that God is free to overrule when He chooses to work.

Eighty valiant priests before a proud king (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). Uzziah was one of Judah's most successful kings, with an army of more than three hundred thousand. When his heart was lifted up, he entered the temple to burn incense, a duty God had given only to the consecrated priests. Azariah the priest went in after him with eighty priests of the Lord, whom the Chronicler calls valiant men, and they told the king plainly that this was not his place. Uzziah raged, and leprosy broke out on his forehead while he stood holding the censer. Azariah did not go in alone; eighty priests shared the risk of confronting a powerful king, and together they defended the holiness of God's house.

Eighty years of rest under Ehud (Judges 3:30). Again and again in Judges, the land rests forty years after a deliverance: after Othniel (Judges 3:11), after Deborah and Barak (Judges 5:31), and after Gideon (Judges 8:28). After the Lord raised up Ehud and Moab was subdued, the land rested eighty years. The record does not explain the doubled length, but it stands out within the book's repeated cycle of sin and rescue. God gave a peace that outlasted a generation and reached into the next.

Eighty thousand stonecutters in the mountains (1 Kings 5:15–17; 6:7; 2 Chronicles 2:2, 18). Solomon "had seventy thousand who carried burdens, and eighty thousand who quarried stone in the mountains." The figure appears three times across Kings and Chronicles. These men cut "large stones, costly stones, and hewn stones, to lay the foundation of the temple" (1 Kings 5:17). Their work was so complete that the temple "was built with stone finished at the quarry, so that no hammer or chisel or any iron tool was heard in the temple while it was being built" (1 Kings 6:7). The quiet on Mount Moriah depended on the noise in the hills. Their labor was done away from the temple site, yet the house rested on what they had shaped.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Each of these passages points beyond itself. Moses at eighty fits a pattern Scripture develops fully in Christ: God saves through weakness so that the power is clearly His. Paul heard the Lord say that His strength is made perfect in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9). The greatest rescue of all came through a crucified Messiah, whom the world judged powerless.

Uzziah grasped at a priesthood that was not his. Hebrews draws the opposite picture: no one takes the honor of priesthood for himself, and even Christ did not glorify Himself but was appointed by the Father (Hebrews 5:4–5). The king who forced his way to the altar left as a leper. The Son who waited for His Father's appointment became the High Priest who brings His people into God's presence.

The temple's journey from the Exodus to Solomon finds its fulfillment in Jesus, who spoke of His body as the temple (John 2:19–21). His people are now being built together as a dwelling for God (Ephesians 2:20–22), and Peter calls them "living stones" (1 Peter 2:5). Like the stones from Solomon's quarries, they are shaped before they are placed. The stone-quarry comparison is an analogy, but it fits closely with what the New Testament says about God's people.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 90 and Barzillai describe aging honestly, and Scripture never pretends that the body does not wear out. Moses and Caleb show that God's calling is not limited by that decline. A believer at seventy or eighty has not passed beyond God's use. The work may look different, but the God who sent an eighty-year-old to Pharaoh still chooses the weak.

The eighty priests teach that faithfulness often requires company. Azariah did not go into the sanctuary alone. When God's honor is at stake before powerful people, believers need one another's courage, and standing together is part of obeying God.

The stonecutters speak to everyone whose service goes unseen. Many people work far from public worship: in prayer, in preparation, and in faithful labor no one notices. God's house rose in silence because others had done the heavy work beforehand. In His record, that hidden labor is counted down to the last eighty thousand.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 35:28 — Isaac lived 180 years, long enough to see Jacob return home before he died.

  • 2 Kings 6:25 — During the siege of Samaria, a donkey's head sold for eighty shekels of silver. The price shows how desperate the famine had become under Israel's unfaithful king.

  • 2 Kings 10:24 — Jehu "had appointed for himself eighty men on the outside" to make sure that none of Baal's worshipers escaped his judgment.

  • Esther 1:4 — King Ahasuerus displayed his wealth for 180 days, a long show of human glory that opens a book in which God quietly protects His people.

  • Jeremiah 41:5 — Eighty men came from Shechem, Shiloh, and Samaria with shaved beards and torn clothes, bringing offerings for the house of the Lord. Ishmael son of Nethaniah murdered most of them, a grim scene of grief in the ruins after Jerusalem fell.
