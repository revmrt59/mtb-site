HEAR, LISTEN, OBEY - (shâmaʻ, Strong's H8085)

Summary

Shâmaʻ is the Hebrew verb behind "hear," "listen," "heed," and "obey" in the Old Testament. It begins with sound reaching the ear, but it rarely stays there. In Scripture, hearing God means taking His word in, understanding it, and acting on it. Very often, when the NKJV says "obey," this is the word behind it, so the vocabulary itself ties hearing to obeying. When Samuel tells Saul that "to obey is better than sacrifice," the verb is shâmaʻ. When Moses gathers Israel and says, "Hear, O Israel," he is calling for the full response of a life.

The word runs in both directions. God calls His people to hear Him, and God Himself hears. He heard Hagar's affliction in the wilderness and Israel's groaning in Egypt. Again and again, when He hears, He acts: He remembers His promises, rescues, forgives, and heals. Across Scripture the word tells a story. Adam hid in fear when he heard God's voice after sin, and Israel had ears but would not hear. Then the Father pointed to His Son and told the disciples to hear Him, and Jesus promised that His sheep would hear His voice and follow.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâmaʻ is one of the most frequent verbs in the Hebrew Bible, and it appears in nearly every book. It is especially common in Deuteronomy, where Moses keeps calling Israel to listen, and in Jeremiah, where the prophet keeps saying that Israel would not. In the Psalms the direction often reverses, and the psalmist pleads with God to hear him.

The verb also has a form that means to make someone hear. English translations render this form as "proclaim," "declare," or "announce." The trumpet warning of Jeremiah 4:5 is one example: "Declare in Judah and proclaim in Jerusalem." A proclamation is simply news that someone has caused others to hear.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is to hear, with the attention and response that real hearing involves. Context determines how far that response goes.

1. To hear a sound or receive news. Joshua 2:10 is an example: the Canaanites heard what the LORD had done at the Red Sea.

2. To listen with attention or understanding, including understanding a language. At Babel, God confused human speech "that they may not understand one another's speech" (Genesis 11:7).

3. To heed or obey, which means responding to a voice by doing what it says. Jeremiah 7:23 is an example: "Obey My voice, and I will be your God."

4. To make others hear, which means to proclaim or announce (Jeremiah 4:5; Isaiah 52:7).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning shifts with the speaker and the listener. When people hear a report, the word is ordinary. When the object is "the voice of the LORD," English versions usually choose "obey," because the context demands action. When God hears the afflicted, He often acts to help them. Yet the same verb is used when the LORD heard Israel complaining and His anger was aroused against them (Numbers 11:1).

Hebrew often stresses a verb by stating it twice. In Exodus 19:3–5 the LORD calls to Moses from the mountain and gives him words for Israel that use this doubled form. In Deuteronomy 28:1 Moses uses it himself as he addresses Israel. Literally the phrase is hearing, you will hear. The NKJV renders this as "if you will indeed obey" (Exodus 19:5) and "if you diligently obey" (Deuteronomy 28:1). The doubled verb asks for wholehearted listening, the kind that does not drift.

The word can also describe the heart. Solomon asks for what the NKJV translates as "an understanding heart" (1 Kings 3:9). The Hebrew phrase is literally a hearing heart.

4. Theological Significance

God speaks, and He also listens. Idols "have ears, but they do not hear" (Psalm 115:6), but the living God hears, and His hearing moves Him to act. When Hagar fled into the wilderness after Sarai dealt harshly with her (Genesis 16:6), the Angel of the LORD told her to name her son Ishmael, which means God hears, "because the LORD has heard your affliction" (Genesis 16:11). Years later Abraham sent her and the boy away (Genesis 21:14). When the water ran out, she sat apart so she would not watch her boy die. "God heard the voice of the lad" (Genesis 21:17). First God heard her affliction, and later He heard her son. In both wilderness scenes the forgotten woman learned that God's ear was turned toward her and her child.

The same pattern sets the Exodus in motion: "So God heard their groaning, and God remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob" (Exodus 2:24). God's hearing leads to His remembering, and His remembering leads to rescue. After the temple's dedication, when the LORD appeared to Solomon by night, He promised that if His people would humble themselves and turn, "then I will hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin and heal their land" (2 Chronicles 7:14). The psalmist's testimony is simple: "This poor man cried out, and the LORD heard him" (Psalm 34:6).

Human hearing has gone wrong from the beginning. The word first appears in Genesis 3:8, just after the Fall, when Adam and his wife heard the sound of the LORD God walking in the garden and hid. Adam explained that he heard God's voice and was afraid because he was naked (Genesis 3:10). The capacity to hear remained, but sin turned the sound of God's approach into something to run from. Much of the rest of Scripture shows God restoring a people who can hear Him without hiding.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

1 Samuel 15:22 — obeying is the real offering. Saul was told to destroy Amalek completely. He spared the king and the best livestock, and then he claimed he had obeyed and that the animals were meant for sacrifice. Samuel answered: "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to heed than the fat of rams." In this verse shâmaʻ stands behind "obeying" and "obey." The parallel word "heed" translates a different Hebrew verb for paying close attention, so Samuel sets listening and obedience side by side. Saul acted as if religious activity could make up for ignoring what God had said. Samuel shows that God wants the listening heart that comes before the offering.

Jeremiah traces the same failure across the whole nation. God had asked one thing at the start: "Obey My voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be My people." "Yet they did not obey or incline their ear, but followed the counsels and the dictates of their evil hearts, and went backward and not forward" (Jeremiah 7:23–24). Refusing to hear did not leave Israel standing still. They went backward. Deuteronomy 28 had already set the two roads side by side. The blessings would come upon Israel and "overtake" them "because you obey the voice of the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 28:2). In this way of speaking, a person who will not act on God's word has not truly heard it.

Deuteronomy 6:4–5 — the Shema. "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!" Israel's confession of faith, later recited daily, takes its name, the Shema, from its first word. Moses is not asking for polite attention. The next sentence shows what hearing this truth requires: "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength." If the LORD alone is God, then He alone receives the heart's devotion. Hearing becomes the doorway into undivided love, and the other calls to hear (Deuteronomy 4:1; 5:1) belong with this one.

1 Kings 3:9–11 — a hearing heart. God offered young King Solomon whatever he wished. Solomon asked for a heart that could hear, so that he could judge God's people and tell good from evil. God was pleased that he had not asked for long life, riches, or victory over his enemies, but for the ability to discern justice (1 Kings 3:11). The request shows what a wise ruler needs most. Wisdom begins with a heart that keeps listening, first to God and then honestly to the people who stand before it in judgment.

Isaiah 6:9–10 and Jeremiah 5:21 — ears that do not hear. Isaiah's commission was sobering. He was told to say to the people, in effect, "Keep on hearing, but do not understand," and his message would make their ears heavy (Isaiah 6:10). Jeremiah called Judah a foolish people who had ears and did not hear. Ezekiel described Israel the same way, as a rebellious house with ears that would not hear (Ezekiel 12:2). In Isaiah 6 this dullness is God's judgment on a people who had turned from Him, and it was to last "until the cities are laid waste" (Isaiah 6:11). Even then, God promised that "the holy seed" would remain (Isaiah 6:13). The passage is a sober warning about refusing God's word, but it does not lay down a rule that anyone who refuses Him loses the ability to hear forever. The judgment also runs back toward them. To a people whose hands were full of blood, God said that even when they made many prayers, He would not hear (Isaiah 1:15).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Moses promised that God would raise up a Prophet like him, and he told Israel to hear that Prophet (Deuteronomy 18:15). Peter applies that promise to Jesus (Acts 3:22). At the Transfiguration the Father's voice came from the cloud, called Jesus His beloved Son, and told the disciples to hear Him (Mark 9:7). The command that once called Israel to hear the LORD now gathers around the Son.

Jesus Himself kept the Shema at the center. Asked for the greatest commandment, He answered: "The first of all the commandments is: 'Hear, O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one. And you shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength'" (Mark 12:29–30).

Jesus is also the one Israelite who heard perfectly. Isaiah's Servant says that the Lord wakens his ear morning by morning to hear as one who is taught (Isaiah 50:4). Saul preferred sacrifice to obedience, but Christ united the two. His listening obedience led Him to the cross, where He offered Himself. The deafness Isaiah announced is the very passage Jesus quoted to explain why many heard His parables without understanding (Matthew 13:14–15). He also opened deaf ears as a sign that God's promised restoration had arrived (compare Isaiah 29:18). Adam heard God and hid. Jesus says that His sheep hear His voice, that He knows them, and that they follow Him (John 10:27).

7. Spirit & Life

Because shâmaʻ joins hearing and doing, a believer cannot treat sermons, Bible reading, or quiet times as a substitute for obedience. Saul had the sacrifices ready. What he lacked was a willingness to do what God had already said. The question for us is often the same. Am I answering God's voice in the matter He has already spoken to, or am I offering Him something else instead?

The word also carries great comfort. The God who calls us to listen is Himself the God who listens. Hagar was a foreign servant woman who fled harsh treatment and was later sent away, yet God heard her affliction and then heard her son. Israel's groaning under Egypt's whips was heard. The cry of "this poor man" was heard. Prayer is not words thrown into silence. It is speech to a God whose hearing moves Him to remember and to rescue.

Job gives the word one more turn. After God answered him out of the whirlwind, Job confessed that he had heard of God with his ear but now his eye saw Him (Job 42:5). Job sets hearing about God beside seeing Him for himself. In a separate word, God then rebuked Job's friends because they had not spoken of Him "what is right" (Job 42:7). After Job's long suffering, God's own speech brought him from hearing about God into God's presence. Faith comes by hearing, and that hearing is meant to lead a believer into knowing the Lord Himself.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 22:18 — After Abraham showed himself willing to offer Isaac, and God stopped him before he harmed the boy (Genesis 22:12), God promised that all nations would be blessed through his seed because Abraham had obeyed His voice.

  • Exodus 3:7 — At the burning bush God told Moses that He had seen His people's oppression and heard their cry, and that He had come down to deliver them.

  • Nehemiah 8:9 — When the returned exiles heard the words of the Law read aloud, they wept, and soon afterward they gathered and "confessed their sins" (Nehemiah 9:2).

  • Psalm 95:7 — The psalmist urges God's people that today, if they will hear His voice, they must not harden their hearts. Hebrews 3–4 takes up this warning.

  • Proverbs 1:5 — A wise man will hear and increase learning, so in Proverbs listening is a mark of wisdom.

  • Isaiah 52:7 — The messenger on the mountains "proclaims" peace. Here the word means making the good news of God's reign heard.
