RAISIN CAKE, CAKE OF RAISINS - (ʼăshîyshâh, Strong's H0809)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼăshîyshâh names a cake of pressed raisins: a sweet, compact food made from dried grapes. It was a treat for special occasions, valued for its sweetness and the quick strength it gave. The word occurs 5 times in 5 verses of the Hebrew Bible. Four of them are clearly rendered as food in the NKJV, and each carries weight. When David brought the ark of God into Jerusalem, he gave every Israelite, man and woman alike, "a loaf of bread, a piece of meat, and a cake of raisins" (2 Samuel 6:19). In the Song of Solomon, the bride, faint with love, asks to be sustained with cakes of raisins (Song of Solomon 2:5).
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

The last use is the saddest. Through Hosea, the LORD describes Israel as a people "who look to other gods and love the raisin cakes of the pagans" (Hosea 3:1). The same sweet food that once marked joy in God's presence had become a token of devotion to Baal. Across these four verses a small food tells a large story. God gives His people real sweetness, and the human heart can love that sweetness more than the One who gives it. Hosea's answer is that God strips away the counterfeit so His people will return to "the LORD their God and David their king" (Hosea 3:5).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼăshîyshâh is a Hebrew noun for a food made by drying grapes and pressing them into a firm cake. Dried and pressed this way, the fruit of the vineyard kept long after the harvest. A cake of raisins was dense and sweet, and it was easy to carry and share. Some older explanations tied the word to a root meaning fire and treated it as a general word for baked goods. Most modern scholars no longer accept that explanation and link the word to pressed raisins.

The setting matters for understanding the word. Bread kept people alive from day to day. A raisin cake was a treat for festival days, for a royal celebration, or for someone who needed reviving. Because raisins came from the grape harvest, these cakes were also tied to harvest festivals. In Hosea's day that tie made them part of the worship of the Canaanite fertility gods.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one meaning: a cake or cluster of pressed raisins. The NKJV renders it as "a cake of raisins" (2 Samuel 6:19; 1 Chronicles 16:3), "cakes of raisins" (Song of Solomon 2:5), and "raisin cakes" (Hosea 3:1). In these four passages the word names this food. The Hebrew form in Isaiah 16:7 is also read as "raisin cakes," while the NKJV, following a different reading, renders that verse "the foundations of Kir Hareseth" (some numbering systems treat it as a separate word, H0808). Whether it suggests generosity, love, or idolatry depends on who gives the cake and why. The exact recipe and size are unknown, but the basic identity of the food is secure.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The four food passages fall into three settings. In the two historical accounts, the raisin cake is a royal gift handed to each person at a national celebration. In the Song of Solomon, it is something that revives a person who is weak, set beside "apples" and asked for by someone overwhelmed with love. In Hosea, it belongs to pagan worship, and Israel's love for it stands for Israel's love for other gods.

The food is the same in each setting. What changes is the relationship around it. David hands it out after worshiping the LORD. The bride wants it because love has worn her out. Israel reaches for it at the shrines of gods who never gave the harvest. The raisin cake itself is never the problem, and David's use of it shows this. The problem in Hosea is where Israel looks for its sweetness.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show a God who intends joy for His people as well as survival. Bread would have been enough to keep the crowd in Jerusalem alive. When the ark came to rest among His people, they went home carrying something sweet. God's presence among His people was meant to be celebrated and tasted by everyone, women and men alike.

The same passages show how easily the heart can separate a gift from its Giver. In Hosea 2, Israel credits her grain, wine, and oil to her "lovers" and fails to recognize that the LORD gave them (Hosea 2:5, 8, paraphrased). The raisin cakes of Hosea 3:1 come from the same harvest the LORD provided, but Israel now loves them in the worship of other gods. Idolatry here is a form of misplaced love. Israel loves the sweetness while giving thanks to the wrong god. God's response is also striking. He commands Hosea to love again an adulterous woman, "just like the love of the LORD for the children of Israel." His love outlasts their betrayal.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

2 Samuel 6:19 and 1 Chronicles 16:3: The king's gift in God's presence. David's first attempt to move the ark ended in death and fear (2 Samuel 6:6–9). The second attempt ends in dancing, sacrifices, and blessing "in the name of the LORD of hosts" (2 Samuel 6:18). Only then does David distribute to "all the people, among the whole multitude of Israel, both the women and the men," a loaf, a portion of meat, and a cake of raisins. The text names both women and men. No one was left out. The rightful king, acting in the LORD's name, made sure that everyone in the assembly took home a share of the feast. The raisin cake was the sweet part of that gift. It told each family that life with God dwelling among them was a reason for joy.

Song of Solomon 2:5: Love that needs sustaining. "Sustain me with cakes of raisins, Refresh me with apples, For I am lovesick." The bride is not ill from sorrow. She has been overwhelmed by love in the beloved's "banqueting house," where "his banner over me was love" (Song of Solomon 2:4). Love has left her faint, so she asks for food that will revive her. The Song mixes country images with royal luxury, and the raisin cake fits both. It comes from the vineyard but tastes like the king's table. Read as a picture of God's love for His people, the verse shows that a soul overwhelmed by that love needs to be sustained by Him.

Hosea 3:1–5: Loving the wrong sweetness. This is the turning point of the word's story. The LORD tells Hosea, "Go again, love a woman who is loved by a lover and is committing adultery." The comparison follows: the children of Israel "look to other gods and love the raisin cakes of the pagans." The verse places two loves side by side. The LORD loves Israel, and Israel loves raisin cakes. The cakes probably belonged to the harvest festivals of the fertility gods, whom Israel credited with the land's abundance (Hosea 2:5). Israel had once received sweetness from its king in the LORD's presence. Now Israel was seeking it at Baal's festivals.

The remedy comes in Hosea 3:4–5. Israel "shall abide many days without king or prince, without sacrifice or sacred pillar, without ephod or teraphim." God removes both the false worship and the comforts the nation leaned on. The emptiness has a purpose: "Afterward the children of Israel shall return and seek the LORD their God and David their king. They shall fear the LORD and His goodness in the latter days." The prophecy names David, the same king who once handed out raisin cakes before the LORD. The way back from the false sweetness leads to the true King and to "His goodness."

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Hosea 3:5 looks beyond the David who had died long before Hosea's time. It points to a coming king from David's line, a hope the prophets developed (Jeremiah 30:9; Ezekiel 34:23–24) and the New Testament fulfills in Jesus, the Son of David. The direct line from the raisin cake to Christ runs through this promise. Israel's return to "David their king" is fulfilled in its return to Jesus.

Other connections are resonance rather than stated fulfillment, but they are fruitful. David fed the whole assembly after bringing God's presence near. Jesus fed multitudes on hillsides and called Himself the bread of life (John 6:35). At His final Passover He spoke of drinking the fruit of the vine new in His Father's kingdom (Matthew 26:29). The Bible's story moves toward a royal banquet where the King Himself is host, and His gifts are never separated from His presence. The bride of the Song who is faint with love, and the adulterous woman of Hosea 3 who is loved anyway, both find their fullest answer in Christ, who loves His bride and gave Himself for her (Ephesians 5:25).

7. Spirit & Life

The raisin cake asks what we actually love. Israel did not give up sweetness. Israel gave up the Giver and kept the sweetness. Believers can do the same with good things: the comfort of a ministry, the warmth of worship, answered prayers, prosperity, even spiritual feelings. When those gifts are loved for their own sake and pursued apart from the Lord, they become "raisin cakes of the pagans," no matter how holy their setting appears.

The trouble in Hosea was never the food. Raisins are good fruit from God's own harvest, and the Song rightly pictures them reviving the faint. The trouble was a good gift loved apart from its Giver. Israel reached for the sweetness of the pagan feasts as a counterfeit consolation, seeking that sweetness while refusing faithfulness to the LORD, the true Husband (Hosea 2:16). The LORD offers Himself, and His goodness lasts.

Hosea 3:4 also helps the believer understand seasons of loss. "Many days without" can be God's mercy, removing what the heart has clung to so that the heart will seek Him again. That emptiness is not abandonment. Its goal is a people who "fear the LORD and His goodness."

The Song gives the positive side of the same truth. It pictures love strong enough to leave a person faint, and the Lord who awakens love for Himself also sustains it. One illustration fits here, offered as an analogy rather than a teaching of the text: raisin cakes are grapes that have been dried and pressed, and they keep long after the harvest ends. The joy God gives can likewise outlast a single season, held together even through times of pressing.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The four food passages have been discussed above. The fifth occurrence in STEPBible's count, Isaiah 16:7, is the one the NKJV renders "foundations" (see Section 2). One detail in 1 Chronicles 16:3 adds to the picture. Chronicles places David's gift of bread, meat, and "a cake of raisins" just before he appoints Levites to minister before the ark and to give thanks and praise to the LORD (1 Chronicles 16:4). In this account, the sweetness of the feast and the ongoing thanksgiving to God belong together. That is the very connection Israel later broke in Hosea's day.
