KEEP, GUARD, WATCH, OBSERVE - (shâmar, Strong's H8104)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shâmar means to keep something with careful attention. It describes the alert care of a guard over a person, a flock, a gate, or a holy place, and the careful obedience of someone who treasures God's words and does them. English Bibles render it "keep," "guard," "preserve," "watch," "observe," "take heed," and "beware." The verb's "-ing" form also names people by their work: keeper, watchman, doorkeeper. Biblical keeping is active work, done by someone awake and paying attention to something precious.

The word first appears in Eden, where God placed Adam in the garden to tend and keep it, and it runs through the whole Old Testament. Israel is told to keep God's commandments, the Sabbath, and His feasts, and to guard their own hearts lest they forget what He has done. The Levites keep watch over the sanctuary. Prophets stand as watchmen through the night. The word's deepest comfort, though, is that the Lord Himself is the Keeper of His people: "He who keeps Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). Human keeping often fails, as Adam's, Cain's, and Israel's did. In Jesus Christ, who said of His disciples, "I kept them in Your name" (John 17:12), and in the Spirit who causes God's people to keep His judgments (Ezekiel 36:27), God supplies the faithful keeping His people need.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâmar is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Old Testament. It is concentrated in Deuteronomy, Psalms, and Proverbs, the books most concerned with how God's people remember and walk in His ways. It belongs to everyday life before it belongs to worship. Shepherds kept flocks (Hosea 12:12), servants kept their master's property (Exodus 22:7), soldiers guarded a king (1 Samuel 26:15–16), and watchmen walked the city walls at night (Song of Solomon 3:3).

Scripture takes these ordinary pictures of guarding and applies them to the life of faith. The same alert care a sentry gives to a gate, Israel must give to God's word, to the holy place where He dwelt, and to their own hearts. And the Lord gives that same care to them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, shâmar means to give careful, protective attention to something or someone. Context determines the object and the nuance:

1. To guard, protect, or preserve a person, place, or thing. The cherubim guard the way to the tree of life (Genesis 3:24), and the Lord preserves His saints (Psalm 37:28).

2. To keep or observe what God has commanded, giving careful, continuing obedience to His commandments, statutes, Sabbath, feasts, and covenant (Exodus 12:17; Deuteronomy 5:12; Exodus 19:5).

3. To watch closely, pay attention to, or wait for. Eli watched Hannah's mouth as she prayed (1 Samuel 1:12), Job complained that God watched his paths closely (Job 13:27), and the psalmist's soul waits for the Lord like those who watch for the morning (Psalm 130:6).

4. To take heed or be careful, as a warning to guard oneself against danger (Genesis 31:24; Deuteronomy 4:9).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The direction of the keeping changes the tone of the word. When God keeps a person, shâmar speaks of protection and safety (Genesis 28:15; Psalm 121). When people keep God's word, it speaks of attentive obedience. Psalm 119 uses the verb again and again for a heart that both treasures and practices God's testimonies. When the object is a place, it speaks of guarding a boundary. Levites kept watch over the tabernacle (Numbers 3:7–8), doorkeepers stood at the temple entrances (2 Kings 22:4; 1 Chronicles 9:19), and Nehemiah stationed Levites to guard the gates so the Sabbath would be kept holy (Nehemiah 13:22).

Two grammatical patterns help the reader. First, many warnings turn the verb back on the hearers: "take heed to yourself," "beware" (Deuteronomy 6:12; 8:11). The guard's alertness is directed inward, toward one's own heart. Second, the participle, the "-ing" form of the verb, often works as a title. "He who keeps Israel" (Psalm 121:4) names the Lord by what He continually does. The keeper, the watchman, and the doorkeeper are each defined by the work of keeping.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture describes the God of Israel as the One "who keeps His covenant and mercy" with those who love Him (Deuteronomy 7:9; compare Nehemiah 1:5; Daniel 9:4). His faithful love always comes first. He brought Israel out of Egypt before He called them to obey His voice and keep His covenant, and He promised that they would be His special treasure (Exodus 19:5). In that verse shâmar is the word for keeping the covenant; "obey My voice" uses the Hebrew verb for hearing. Israel's keeping answers God's keeping, and their obedience depends on His help. That is why the psalmist prays, "Revive me according to Your lovingkindness, so that I may keep the testimony of Your mouth" (Psalm 119:88). He asks God to give him life so that he will be able to obey.

God's watchfulness also has two sides. For those who trust Him, His attention means protection (Psalm 145:20). For Job in his anguish, the same verb described an inescapable gaze: "You put my feet in the stocks, and watch closely all my paths" (Job 13:27). Job's words voice the anguish of a man suffering without explanation, and the book insists that he was "blameless and upright" (Job 1:1, 8). His cry shows that even a righteous person can feel God's attention as a weight in a season of suffering. It is not evidence that Job stood wrongly before God. Yet the word also carries hope. Through Jeremiah, God gives voice to Israel's own question, "Will He remain angry forever? Will He keep it to the end?" (Jeremiah 3:5). The prophet goes on to answer it with a call to return to the Lord, who is merciful. God does not hold on to anger the way He holds on to His promises.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 2–4: the first keepers. God placed Adam in Eden to tend it and keep it (Genesis 2:15). Because shâmar also means to guard, Adam's work in the place where God walked with man included guarding the garden as well as farming it. After Adam failed and was driven out, the same verb describes the cherubim, stationed "to guard the way to the tree of life" (Genesis 3:24). Genesis does not say that Adam's charge was handed to them, but the repeated word links the two scenes: the garden Adam was to keep is now kept against him. The vocabulary returns at the tabernacle. The Levites "attend to" the needs of the sanctuary (Numbers 3:7–8), keeping watch over the place where God dwelt among His people, and Ezekiel's vision of a renewed temple assigns the same charge to faithful priests (Ezekiel 44:14–16). Genesis does not give Adam the Levites' role, but a biblical pattern is clear: guarding the place where God meets His people began in Eden and continued in Israel's worship.

One chapter after the cherubim are stationed, the word appears in the mouth of a murderer. When God asked Cain where Abel was, Cain answered with a question of his own: was he supposed to be his brother's keeper (Genesis 4:9)? He took the word for humanity's first calling and threw it back at God as a denial. Keeping was never only about guarding gardens and sanctuaries. It also includes responsibility for the people God places beside us.

Psalm 121: the Keeper who never sleeps. This short song for pilgrims going up to Jerusalem uses shâmar six times. The traveler looks toward the hills, where danger and false shrines could both be found, and confesses that help comes from the Lord who made heaven and earth. Then the promises build. "He will not allow your foot to be moved; He who keeps you will not slumber" (Psalm 121:3). The next verse widens the circle from one pilgrim to the whole nation: "Behold, He who keeps Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). The Lord is "your keeper" and your shade at your right hand, so that neither sun by day nor moon by night will strike you (Psalm 121:5–6). He will preserve you from all evil, preserve your soul, and preserve your going out and your coming in "from this time forth, and even forevermore" (Psalm 121:7–8).

Every human watchman eventually nods off at his post. Psalm 127:1 admits as much: unless the Lord guards the city, the watchman stays awake in vain. Psalm 121 is the Bible's answer to that weakness. The safety of God's people rests on His watchfulness, which never tires, and their own watchfulness, which often fails, is held within His. Like the priestly blessing, "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Numbers 6:24), this psalm places God's keeping over every step of the believer's life.

Deuteronomy 4:9: keeping yourself. Moses uses the verb twice in one breath: "Only take heed to yourself, and diligently keep yourself, lest you forget the things your eyes have seen, and lest they depart from your heart all the days of your life." The commandments were to be kept, but the first thing Israel had to guard was their own memory. Moses understood that obedience fails from the inside before it fails on the outside. His warnings repeat the pattern. When Israel is living in houses they did not build and eating from vineyards they did not plant, they must beware lest they forget the Lord who brought them out of Egypt (Deuteronomy 6:10–12; 8:11–14). The greatest danger is comfort, because a people who lack nothing can slowly stop remembering the God who gave it all. That is also why the verse ends with teaching children and grandchildren. A heart that remembers God's works passes them on to the next generation.

Psalm 130 and Isaiah 21: watching for the morning. Psalm 130 uses shâmar in two opposite directions. In Psalm 130:3 the psalmist trembles at the thought that the Lord might "mark," or keep watch over, iniquities, because then no one could stand. But forgiveness is with God (Psalm 130:4), and that changes who is watching. By Psalm 130:6 the psalmist himself is the watchman: "My soul waits for the Lord more than those who watch for the morning—yes, more than those who watch for the morning." The repeated line captures the long, aching hours before dawn. A forgiven sinner does not stand guard against God. He waits for Him.

Isaiah turns the night watch into a prophetic picture. A voice calls out of Seir, "Watchman, what of the night?" and the watchman answers that the morning comes, and also the night (Isaiah 21:11–12). The prophet sees further than others into God's dealings with the nations and must report honestly what is coming. Isaiah also speaks of watchmen set on Jerusalem's walls who never keep silent day or night, calling on the Lord until He establishes the city (Isaiah 62:6). Ezekiel received the same calling, "I have made you a watchman for the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 3:17), though there a different Hebrew word is used for watchman. Biblical keeping looks back to what God has said and also forward, with alert hope, to what He will do.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Old Testament story of shâmar is largely a story of failed keepers. Adam did not guard the garden. Cain refused to keep his brother. Israel did not keep the covenant (Psalm 78:10), and Malachi rebuked priests who had not kept God's ways (Malachi 2:9). The need was for a Keeper who would not fail.

Jesus stands in that place. In His prayer the night before the cross, He told the Father, "While I was with them in the world, I kept them in Your name. Those whom You gave Me I have kept; and none of them is lost except the son of perdition, that the Scripture might be fulfilled" (John 17:12). John wrote in Greek, so shâmar itself does not appear there, but the reality is the same one the Old Testament described. Where Adam lost the garden, Christ kept those the Father gave Him. Cain disowned his brother, while Jesus is not ashamed to call His people brothers (Hebrews 2:11). In Gethsemane, the disciples He asked to watch fell asleep, but the Keeper of Israel stayed awake and went to the cross for them.

Two psalms that use shâmar reach Christ directly. Psalm 34:20 says the Lord guards all the bones of the righteous so that not one is broken. John records that Jesus' legs were not broken "that the Scripture should be fulfilled" (John 19:36). John does not name the passage he means. His words match the Passover command not to break the lamb's bones (Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12), and they also echo Psalm 34:20, so his quotation is best read alongside both. Psalm 91:11 promises angels to keep the righteous in all their ways. Satan quoted that promise to tempt Jesus to throw Himself from the temple (Luke 4:9–12). Jesus refused to test the Father's keeping, and in that refusal He showed what trusting it really looks like.

Ezekiel looked ahead to a day when God would do in His people what they could not do on their own: "I will put My Spirit within you and cause you to walk in My statutes, and you will keep My judgments and do them" (Ezekiel 36:27). The Keeper who guards His people also gives them His Spirit, so that they become keepers of His word.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 121 teaches a believer to rest without growing careless. Moses' warning that we must keep our own hearts still stands, and Proverbs carries the same concern when it says, "Keep my commands and live, and my law as the apple of your eye" (Proverbs 7:2). The eye protects itself by reflex, and God's word is meant to become that close and that instinctive to us. (Proverbs 4:23, "Keep your heart with all diligence," expresses the same thought with a different Hebrew verb.) We guard our hearts because God guards us. We do not guard them in order to make Him start.

The word also shows how sin can twist keeping into something dark. Amos condemns Edom because "he pursued his brother with the sword, and cast off all pity; his anger tore perpetually, and he kept his wrath forever" (Amos 1:11). Edom guarded its grudge the way a watchman guards a fire to keep it from going out, and once again the victim was a brother. A resentment that is fed and protected will not die on its own. Believers are called to keep God's word and to keep watch over one another, and they must refuse to keep the record of another person's wrongs.

Finally, Psalm 130 shows what waiting on God looks like. The night watchman does not create the dawn. He watches for it because he knows it is coming. Christians keep that same kind of watch for Christ's return, with the alert expectancy of people who have been forgiven and know the morning is on its way.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 28:15 – At Bethel, God promised the fleeing Jacob that He would be with him and keep him wherever he went. Psalm 121 later extends this promise to the whole nation.

Genesis 37:11 – After Joseph described his dreams, his brothers envied him, "but his father kept the matter in mind." Keeping can mean holding something in the heart and pondering it.

1 Samuel 1:12 – Eli "watched" Hannah's mouth as she prayed silently. Close attention without understanding led him to misjudge her as drunk.

Psalm 25:20 – "Keep my soul, and deliver me; let me not be ashamed, for I put my trust in You." Here the verb becomes a prayer that rests on trust.

Malachi 2:7 – The priest's lips should "keep" knowledge, because people seek the law from his mouth. A spiritual leader is entrusted with guarding the truth.

Ecclesiastes 12:13 – The Preacher ends his search with the conclusion of the whole matter: fear God and keep His commandments.
