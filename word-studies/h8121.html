SUN - (shemesh, Strong's H8121)

Summary

The Hebrew word shemesh means "sun." It names the great light God set in the sky to rule the day. Because the sun dominated every ancient sky, Scripture also uses it to mark direction and time. Its rising meant east, its going down meant west, and sunset closed the working day. The biblical writers return to the sun again and again to say who God is. He made it, He commands it, and He alone deserves the worship that Israel's neighbors gave to it. When Joshua prayed, the sun stood still. When Israel's leaders turned their backs on the temple to bow toward the sunrise, God called it an abomination. When the Day of the Lord comes, the prophets say the sun will go dark.

The Old Testament also does something surprising with the sun. God forbids anyone to worship it, yet He uses its glory to describe Himself: "the LORD God is a sun and shield" (Psalm 84:11). Malachi promises that "the Sun of Righteousness shall arise / With healing in His wings" (Malachi 4:2). Isaiah looks toward a day when the sun is no longer needed because "the LORD will be to you an everlasting light" (Isaiah 60:19). Ecclesiastes measures life "under the sun" and finds it empty without God. The prophets and psalmists look past that limit to the One whose light outlasts the sun, and the New Testament finds that light in Jesus Christ.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shemesh is the ordinary Hebrew word for the sun. It appears throughout the Old Testament, from Genesis to Malachi. About a quarter of its uses come from Ecclesiastes, where the Preacher keeps returning to the phrase "under the sun."

In the ancient Near East the sun was widely worshiped as a god. That setting explains why Scripture is so careful about the sun. Genesis 1:16 does not even use its name. It calls the sun "the greater light to rule the day." It is a lamp God hung in the sky, and it has no power of its own. Every later use of shemesh stands on that foundation: the sun is a created servant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shemesh means the sun, the heavenly body, together with its light and heat. Context brings out a few distinct uses:

1. The sun itself, as a light, a source of heat, or a heavenly body. Psalm 121:6 promises, "The sun shall not strike you by day."

2. A marker of direction or time. "The rising of the sun" means the east and "the going down of the sun" means the west (Joshua 1:4; Psalm 50:1). Sunset marks the end of the day (Deuteronomy 24:13). The NKJV sometimes translates these phrases simply as "east," "west," "sunrise," or "sunset."

3. Something that gleams like the sun. In Isaiah 54:12 the word is translated "pinnacles": "I will make your pinnacles of rubies." The exact architectural feature is uncertain. Translators suggest battlements, shields, or windows that flash in the light.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the historical books the sun mostly tells readers where and when. Boundaries run "toward the sunrise" (Joshua 1:15). Bodies of executed kings are taken down at sunset (Joshua 8:29; 10:27). Moses' hands stayed raised "until the going down of the sun" (Exodus 17:12).

In the Law, sunset becomes a deadline for mercy. A poor man's cloak taken as a pledge must be returned "when the sun goes down, that he may sleep in his own garment" (Deuteronomy 24:13). His wages must be paid before sunset as well (Deuteronomy 24:15).

The poets and prophets give the sun weightier work. It praises its Maker (Psalm 148:3). Its heat can either test people or be held back from them (Jonah 4:8; Psalm 121:6). Its permanence measures God's promises (Psalm 89:36). Its darkening announces judgment (Joel 2:31).

Ecclesiastes uses the word in its own way. The repeated phrase "under the sun" marks out the area the Preacher is examining: human life on earth, viewed from within its own limits (Ecclesiastes 1:3, 14; 2:11, 17). The Preacher can also speak of the sun simply as a sun. It rises and sets on its endless round (Ecclesiastes 1:5), and it is "pleasant for the eyes" to see it (11:7).

4. Theological Significance

The first truth the sun teaches is that God rules it. "The day is Yours, the night also is Yours; / You have prepared the light and the sun" (Psalm 74:16). Jeremiah 31:35 describes the Lord as the One who gives the sun for light by day. Psalm 104:19–23 shows Him using the sun to order daily life. When the sun rises, the lions "lie down in their dens," and "Man goes out to his work / And to his labor until the evening."

Because God made the sun, worshiping it is rebellion. Moses warned Israel not to look up, see the sun, moon, and stars, and be drawn into bowing to them (Deuteronomy 4:19). The Law treated sun worship as a capital offense (Deuteronomy 17:3). Josiah's reform removed priests who "burned incense to Baal, to the sun, to the moon" (2 Kings 23:5), and he burned the chariots that earlier kings had dedicated to the sun (23:11). Scripture keeps a firm line between the Creator and the brightest thing He made.

The sun also measures God's faithfulness. God swore that David's "throne as the sun before Me" would endure (Psalm 89:36). Psalm 72:5 says God's people will fear Him "As long as the sun and moon endure." Every sunrise testifies that God keeps His word.

That same sun becomes a sign of judgment when God withdraws its light. "I will make the sun go down at noon, / And I will darken the earth in broad daylight" (Amos 8:9). In Joel the sun is "turned into darkness... Before the coming of the great and awesome day of the LORD" (Joel 2:31). The sun sustains life and keeps time, so when it fails, the order of creation is coming apart. Micah applies the image to false prophets: "The sun shall go down on the prophets, / And the day shall be dark for them" (Micah 3:6).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Joshua 10:12–14 — The sun obeys the Lord. While the Lord was handing the Amorites over to Israel, Joshua spoke "in the sight of Israel: 'Sun, stand still over Gibeon.'" The sun stopped and did not hurry to set for about a whole day (10:13). The narrator gives the reason: "the LORD heeded the voice of a man; for the LORD fought for Israel" (10:14). The sun halted at a prayer offered to Israel's God. The scene shows the sun as a servant, and it shows the Lord taking a man's request seriously enough to hold the day open for His people. Habakkuk 3:11 later says, "The sun and moon stood still in their habitation," as God marches out to save His people. Habakkuk does not name Joshua, but his words may well echo this day.

Ezekiel 8:16 — Backs to God, faces to the sun. In a vision Ezekiel is brought into the inner court of the temple. "There, at the door of the temple of the LORD, between the porch and the altar, were about twenty-five men with their backs toward the temple of the LORD and their faces toward the east, and they were worshiping the sun." The location makes the sin worse. These men stood in the holiest public space, where priests served. To face the sunrise there, they had to turn their backs on the Lord's dwelling. Earlier in the chapter God says such abominations are driving Him "far away from My sanctuary" (8:6). In chapters 10–11 Ezekiel watches the glory depart. Turning toward the created light meant turning away from the living God.

2 Samuel 12:11–12 — Secret sin brought into the daylight. David took Bathsheba and arranged Uriah's death in secret. Through Nathan, the Lord answered that He would bring judgment on David's house openly, before all Israel and in the sight of the sun. David had worked in the dark, and God's discipline would happen in the light. The word came true in 2 Samuel 16:22. Absalom pitched a tent on the palace roof, the very place where David had first looked at Bathsheba, and took his father's concubines there in full public view. God did not leave David's hidden sin hidden.

Psalm 19:4–6 — Nothing hidden from its heat. God "has set a tabernacle for the sun," which comes out "like a bridegroom" and "rejoices like a strong man to run its race." The psalm ends the image this way: "there is nothing hidden from its heat." Then the psalm turns at once to "The law of the LORD is perfect" (19:7). The placement is deliberate. The sun reaches every corner of the earth, and God's word reaches every corner of the heart. That is why the psalmist ends by asking to be cleansed from "secret faults" (19:12).

Ecclesiastes — Life under the sun. The Preacher asks, "What profit has a man from all his labor / In which he toils under the sun?" (1:3). He studies work, pleasure, wisdom, and wealth, and he keeps reaching the same verdict: "vanity and grasping for the wind" (2:11). The sun itself becomes a picture of the problem. It rises, sets, and hurries back to where it began (1:5), and "there is nothing new under the sun" (1:9). The phrase defines the limits of the search. Whatever can be measured within the circuit of the sun cannot carry the weight of a human life. The book closes by pointing beyond those limits: "Fear God and keep His commandments" (12:13).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Scripture's story of the sun moves in a clear direction. God appoints it to rule the day (Genesis 1:16). His people are repeatedly tempted to worship it, and the prophets warn that it will be darkened in judgment. Then God begins to borrow its glory for Himself. "The LORD God is a sun and shield; / The LORD will give grace and glory" (Psalm 84:11). David's last words describe the righteous ruler as "like the light of the morning when the sun rises, / A morning without clouds" (2 Samuel 23:4). Malachi takes the image further: "to you who fear My name / The Sun of Righteousness shall arise / With healing in His wings" (Malachi 4:2). The verse is a picture of dawn after a dark night. The "wings" fit the image of the sun's rays spreading at sunrise and carrying healing to all they touch. Malachi does not explain who or what the Sun of Righteousness is. The NKJV's capital letters reflect the translators' Christian reading, and the New Testament gives that reading its footing.

The New Testament takes up this light and connects it with Jesus. Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, praises God because "the Dayspring from on high" has visited His people "To give light to those who sit in darkness" (Luke 1:78–79). "Dayspring" means the dawn, the rising light, and Zechariah speaks it over the coming of the Lord whose way John would prepare. That is the closest the New Testament comes to Malachi's rising sun. On the mountain of transfiguration, Jesus' face shone like the sun (Matthew 17:2). John sees the risen Christ with a face like the sun shining in its strength (Revelation 1:16). Together these passages show the New Testament presenting Jesus as the light Malachi's dawn image anticipated. At the cross, darkness covered the land from noon until three in the afternoon (Matthew 27:45). Those hours strongly recall Amos's sun going down at noon, as judgment fell on the Son in the middle of the day.

The promise to David belongs here too. His throne would endure "as the sun before Me" (Psalm 89:36), and Gabriel announced that Jesus would receive the throne of His father David and reign forever (Luke 1:32–33). Malachi also foresaw that from the rising of the sun to its going down, God's name would be great among the nations (Malachi 1:11). The gospel going out to every people fulfills that hope.

The story ends where Isaiah said it would: "The sun shall no longer be your light by day... But the LORD will be to you an everlasting light" (Isaiah 60:19). Isaiah adds, "Your sun shall no longer go down... And the days of your mourning shall be ended" (Isaiah 60:20). John sees the same city: it "had no need of the sun or of the moon to shine in it, for the glory of God illuminated it. The Lamb is its light" (Revelation 21:23). The sun was always a lamp that pointed beyond itself.

7. Spirit & Life

The sun teaches believers to look higher than the sun. Ecclesiastes is honest about how life feels when it is measured only by what happens beneath the sky: work is forgotten and death comes for everyone. That honesty is a mercy. It sends us looking for the One Psalm 84 calls "a sun and shield," who gives "grace and glory." The psalm adds, "No good thing will He withhold From those who walk uprightly" (Psalm 84:11).

The sun's heat appears in Scripture as both testing and protection. God prepared a vehement east wind, and the sun beat on Jonah's head until he grew faint (Jonah 4:8), because He was confronting a prophet who resented His mercy to Nineveh. To His trusting people God promises, "The sun shall not strike you by day" (Psalm 121:6), and that promise reaches into eternity: "the sun shall not strike them, nor any heat" (Revelation 7:16). Jesus used the same image in His parable of the sower. "When the sun was up they were scorched, and because they had no root they withered away" (Matthew 13:6). Bildad had described the godless man in similar words: "He grows green in the sun" (Job 8:16), yet he does not last. Trouble does not create shallow faith. Trouble reveals it. The heat that withers a rootless plant cannot destroy a life sheltered by the Lord.

Every sunset also sets a limit, though Scripture applies it to particular cases. A hired worker who was poor was to be paid his wages each day, without letting the sun go down on them (Deuteronomy 24:14–15). A poor man's cloak taken as a pledge had to be returned by sunset, so that he would not go to bed cold (Deuteronomy 24:13; Exodus 22:26–27). Paul applies a similar daily limit to the heart: "do not let the sun go down on your wrath" (Ephesians 4:26). These passages do not set a sunset deadline for every kind of debt. They do show God's concern that the poor not be left waiting and that anger not be carried into the night. Wages owed to someone who depends on them, a neighbor's basic needs, and nursed anger all deserve prompt attention before the day is done.

The sun also warns believers about secrets. David found that what he did in the dark became visible in broad daylight. Psalm 19 offers a better path: let the word that reaches everywhere like the sun's heat search you now, and confess your secret faults before God, who already sees them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 15:12, 17. When the sun went down on Abram, a smoking oven and burning torch passed between the pieces of his sacrifice. God bound Himself to His promise in the darkness.

Genesis 32:31. The sun rose on Jacob as he crossed over Penuel, limping after wrestling with God. The new day dawned on a man who had been humbled and blessed.

Genesis 37:9. In Joseph's dream, "the sun, the moon, and the eleven stars bowed down to me." The sun stood for his father, Jacob. Revelation 12:1 later pictures a woman "clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet," an image widely understood to represent the people of Israel, from whom the Messiah came.

Isaiah 38:8. God made the shadow on the sundial of Ahaz go back ten degrees as a sign to Hezekiah. As in Joshua's day, the sun's movement obeyed the Lord who had promised to heal the king.

Psalm 113:3. From the rising of the sun to its going down, the Lord's name is to be praised. The phrase covers the whole earth and the whole day.

Song of Solomon 1:6. The bride asks the others not to look on her because the sun has darkened her skin while she worked in the vineyards. It is a reminder of the ordinary, hard labor that happens under the sun.
