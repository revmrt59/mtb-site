TOOTH, TEETH, IVORY - (shên, Strong's H8127)

Summary

The Hebrew word shên means "tooth." Scripture uses it for the teeth of people and animals. It also uses it for ivory, since an elephant's tusk is a tooth, and for things shaped like teeth, such as the prongs of a fork or a jagged point of rock. Because teeth are what bite, chew, and tear, the word shows up wherever the Bible talks about appetite and power. The law protects a servant's tooth. The psalmists ask God to break the teeth of the wicked. Amos condemns rich people lounging on ivory, and God answers with "cleanness of teeth," which means famine.

The word's most searching use comes in a bitter proverb from Judah's last days: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge." Through Jeremiah and Ezekiel, God refuses to let His people hide behind their ancestors' sins. He holds each person responsible and opens to each person the way to turn and live. In the same chapter, Jeremiah promises a new covenant. Across Scripture, this ordinary word for a tooth shows a God who restrains violence, disarms those who prey on others, disciplines His people so they will come back to Him, and delights in a people He has washed clean.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shên is the everyday Hebrew word for a tooth. It appears in law, history, poetry, wisdom, and prophecy. Hebrew often names objects after body parts, so the word easily stretched to describe anything tooth-like. A rocky point became a "tooth" of rock. The prongs of a meat fork became its "teeth." The tusk of an elephant was simply a large tooth, so the same word names the ivory that decorated royal thrones and wealthy homes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is concrete: a tooth. Context shows which of three closely related senses is meant.

1. A literal tooth, human or animal. Jacob blesses Judah with "teeth whiter than milk" (Genesis 49:12), and the law speaks of a servant's lost tooth (Exodus 21:27).

2. Ivory, the tooth material of the elephant. Solomon "made a great throne of ivory, and overlaid it with pure gold" (1 Kings 10:18).

3. Something shaped like a tooth. The priest's servant carried "a three-pronged fleshhook" (1 Samuel 2:13), a fork with three "teeth." Jonathan's path ran between a "sharp rock" on each side (1 Samuel 14:4), and the eagle nests on "the crag of the rock" (Job 39:28).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Most uses build on what teeth do. Several vivid expressions recur.

Gnashing the teeth expresses seething hatred and scorn. David's enemies, "ungodly mockers at feasts," "gnashed at me with their teeth" (Psalm 35:16). Jerusalem's enemies "hiss and gnash their teeth" over the fallen city (Lamentations 2:16). In most of the cited cases the gnashing comes from the wicked or from enemies (Psalm 35:16; 37:12). In Job 16:9 the image is part of Job's own anguished description of an adversary he experiences as attacking him: "He gnashes at me with His teeth." Where the psalms set the gnashing of the wicked against God's care for the righteous, it proves helpless in the end. When the wicked man sees God honor the righteous, "he will gnash his teeth and melt away" (Psalm 112:10).

Teeth as weapons turn human greed into the image of a predator. Proverbs describes "a generation whose teeth are like swords, and whose fangs are like knives, to devour the poor from off the earth" (Proverbs 30:14). The devouring here is social: whole classes of people feed on those too weak to resist.

Food between the teeth pictures judgment that falls in the middle of indulgence. Israel demanded meat in the wilderness, and "while the meat was still between their teeth, before it was chewed," the LORD struck them with a plague (Numbers 11:33). Zechariah reverses the image for the Philistines. God will take "the abominations from between his teeth," and a cleansed remnant "shall be for our God" (Zechariah 9:7).

4. Theological Significance

Because teeth are the point where appetite takes hold of something, shên repeatedly brings God's judgment to the mouth itself. Again and again Scripture asks what people are consuming and at whose expense. The wicked consume the poor. Eli's sons used a three-toothed fork to take the meat of offerings that belonged to the Lord (1 Samuel 2:13–17). The guilt in that passage lies in the priests' greed, and the fork's "teeth" make an apt picture of it. Israel's rich consumed luxury while their neighbors suffered. In each case God meets the appetite where it acts. He breaks the teeth, takes food from between them, or leaves them clean and empty.

This pattern shows God's holiness and His care at once. He takes human bodies seriously, even a servant's single tooth. He limits the violence people do to one another. He refuses to let predators keep their power. His discipline also has a goal beyond punishment. He wants His people to return to Him, and He holds each person answerable before Him while keeping the door of repentance open.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

"Tooth for tooth": justice with limits (Exodus 21:24, 27; Leviticus 24:20; Deuteronomy 19:21). The law sets the penalty at "eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (Exodus 21:24). Modern readers often hear this as harsh. In its setting, it capped punishment at the size of the injury and gave courts no room for escalating revenge. Deuteronomy applies it to a false witness, and judges carry it out. Three verses later Exodus adds a striking provision: "And if he knocks out the tooth of his male or female servant, he shall let him go free for the sake of his tooth" (Exodus 21:27). A master's power over a servant ended at that servant's mouth. In God's eyes, the least person in the household had a body worth protecting, and abuse of it cost the abuser his claim on the servant.

Sour grapes: God refuses fatalism (Jeremiah 31:29–30; Ezekiel 18:2). In Judah's collapse a proverb spread: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge." Anyone who has bitten into unripe fruit knows that puckered, raw feeling. The proverb claimed that the children were feeling the sharp sting of what their parents had eaten. They were suffering for someone else's sin, and nothing they did now could change it. The saying offered both an excuse and a counsel of despair.

God takes the proverb on directly. Through Ezekiel He demands, "What do you mean when you use this proverb?" (Ezekiel 18:2). He then declares that every soul belongs to Him and that the one who sins is the one who dies. The rest of Ezekiel 18 opens the hopeful side: a wicked person who turns from his sins will live, and God takes no pleasure in anyone's death, so He calls His people to turn and live. Jeremiah promises that a day is coming when "they shall say no more" this proverb, because "every man who eats the sour grapes, his teeth shall be set on edge" (Jeremiah 31:29–30).

The very next verses announce the new covenant. God will write His law on their hearts, all of them will know Him from the least to the greatest, and He will remember their sin no more (Jeremiah 31:31–34). Personal accountability and personal grace arrive together. The sour taste of a family's past does not decide anyone's standing with God. Each person may come to Him directly.

Ivory beds and clean teeth (Amos 3:15; 4:6; 6:4). Ivory first appears as a rare glory of Solomon's throne (1 Kings 10:18). By Ahab's reign it decorated a whole "ivory house" (1 Kings 22:39). In Amos, Samaria's wealthy stretch out on beds of ivory and feast while ignoring the ruin of their nation (Amos 6:4), and God announces that their houses of ivory will perish (Amos 3:15). The Hebrew sharpens the point, because the same word serves for the ivory they rest on and the famine God sends: "I gave you cleanness of teeth in all your cities, and lack of bread in all your places; yet you have not returned to Me" (Amos 4:6). People who rested on teeth were given empty teeth. The refrain "yet you have not returned to Me" repeats through the chapter (Amos 4:6–11). It shows that God sent these hardships to bring His people back, and it records the grief of their refusal.

God breaks the predator's teeth (Psalms 3:7; 58:6; 124:6). David prays and then gives thanks: "You have broken the teeth of the ungodly" (Psalm 3:7). He asks God, "Break out the fangs of the young lions" (Psalm 58:6). Eliphaz describes the same picture from nature: when "the teeth of the young lions are broken," the old lion "perishes for lack of prey" (Job 4:10–11). A lion without teeth cannot feed, so God's judgment strikes at the enemy's ability to destroy. The redeemed respond with worship: "Blessed be the LORD, who has not given us as prey to their teeth" (Psalm 124:6).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Each of these threads finds its fullness in Jesus. He quotes "An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth" (Matthew 5:38). Matthew's Greek uses its own word for "tooth," but Jesus is citing this same Hebrew law. He leaves the law's justice standing and calls His followers away from personal revenge: "whoever slaps you on your right cheek, turn the other to him also" (Matthew 5:39). Jesus lived this teaching Himself when He was struck and mocked and did not strike back.

The new covenant that Jeremiah announced beside the sour-grapes proverb is the covenant Jesus established in His blood (Luke 22:20). Hebrews 8 quotes Jeremiah's promise as fulfilled in Him. In Christ the inherited sourness of the past gives way to forgiveness and a heart that knows God.

The gnashing of the wicked also reaches its end in Jesus' words. What began as mockery against the righteous in the Psalms becomes, for those who reject the kingdom, final grief: "There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth" (Matthew 8:12).

The ivory appears in a very different setting as well. Psalm 45 celebrates a royal bridegroom whose garments are fragrant "Out of the ivory palaces" on his wedding day (Psalm 45:8). Set beside Amos, the contrast is striking: in Samaria ivory furnished self-indulgence that ignored the poor, while in Psalm 45 it adorns a king who loves righteousness. Hebrews 1:8–9 applies words from this psalm, about the King's everlasting throne and His love of righteousness, to the Son. Hebrews does not turn the ivory palaces into a description of heaven's architecture, but the psalm's royal splendor, given its true Owner, belongs to the King who comes for His bride.

7. Spirit & Life

The sour-grapes proverb still circulates in modern forms: "This is just how my family is," or "What happened to me decides who I am." The consequences of other people's sin are real, and Scripture never denies the pain of them. Before God, though, no one is trapped by another person's guilt. Ezekiel's call to "turn and live" is addressed to you personally. The new covenant is not inherited secondhand. God promises that each of His people will know Him.

Amos asks an uncomfortable question about what we rest on. Comfort that makes us deaf to God and blind to the poor is ivory waiting to be taken away. Not every loss a believer suffers is a sign of God's discipline, and Scripture warns against reading every hardship that way (Job's friends did, and God rebuked them, Job 42:7). But in Amos the meaning is explicit. Five times God names a hardship He sent and adds, "Yet you have not returned to Me" (Amos 4:6–11). That refrain shows what He was seeking from Israel, and it is the ground for the call to examine our own hearts and return to Him.

The Song of Solomon adds a tender counterpoint. The bridegroom praises his bride's teeth as being like freshly shorn sheep coming up from the washing, matched in pairs with none missing (Song of Solomon 4:2; 6:6). In a world where good teeth were rare, this praised health and wholeness. The image fits Paul's picture of Christ, who cleanses His church by washing it with the word so He can present her without spot or blemish (Ephesians 5:26–27). Paul does not cite the Song, so the link is an analogy. Still, it is a fitting one. The Lord who breaks the teeth of predators also delights in a people He has washed and made whole.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 49:12. Judah's eyes are "darker than wine" and his teeth "whiter than milk." The image pictures overflowing abundance in the tribe from which the King would come.

  • Job 19:20. "I have escaped by the skin of my teeth." Job uses this phrase for survival with nothing left. Even at that extremity he goes on to confess that his Redeemer lives (Job 19:25).

  • Job 41:14. Leviathan has "terrible teeth all around." The creature's ferocity humbles Job before the God who made it.

  • Proverbs 25:19. Trusting an unfaithful person in trouble is compared to a bad tooth, which fails exactly when you bite down.

  • Micah 3:5. Micah condemns false prophets who promise peace as long as they have something to chew and declare war on anyone who will not feed them. Like Eli's sons, they turn holy service into a meal.
