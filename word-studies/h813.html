ASHKENAZ - (ʼAshkᵉnaz, Strong's H0813)

Summary

Ashkenaz appears only three times in the Hebrew Bible, but those few verses carry the name from the first generations after the flood to the fall of Babylon. In Genesis 10:3 and 1 Chronicles 1:6, Ashkenaz is a son of Gomer, a grandson of Japheth, and a great-grandson of Noah. In Jeremiah 51:27 the name has become a kingdom, one of the northern powers God summons against Babylon alongside Ararat and Minni. The meaning of the name is unknown. Scripture offers no explanation of it and no wordplay on it. Many scholars connect Ashkenaz with a people that Assyrian records call the Ashguza and that Greek writers called the Scythians, fierce horse-riding warriors from the northern highlands.

These brief references matter because they show God's reach. Genesis lists Ashkenaz among Noah's descendants, which means even the most distant northern peoples are part of one human family that God knows by name. Jeremiah shows the same people answering God's trumpet to bring down the proudest empire of the age. If the Scythian identification is correct, the line continues into the New Testament. In Colossians 3:11, Paul names the Scythian, the ancient world's picture of the untamed outsider, among those for whom "Christ is all and in all." The people once summoned as instruments of judgment are welcome in the new humanity Christ creates.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew name Ashkenaz is used in two related ways. In Genesis 10:3 and 1 Chronicles 1:6 it names an individual, the first-listed of Gomer's three sons. His brothers are Riphath and Togarmah. In Jeremiah 51:27 it names a kingdom, which is most naturally understood as the people descended from or associated with that ancestor. The two uses are closely connected, but they are not identical. One is a man in a genealogy, and the other is a nation many centuries later.

The meaning of the name is unknown. The Bible gives no explanation of it, and its derivation is uncertain. It would be wrong to build any spiritual lesson on a supposed meaning. What scholars do discuss is identification. Seventh-century BC Assyrian records mention a warlike people called the Ashguza or Ishkuza, and many scholars take this to be the same name as Hebrew Ashkenaz. These are commonly identified with the Scythians of Greek literature. This identification is widely proposed but debated, and it does not come from the biblical text itself, but it fits Jeremiah's grouping well. Ararat corresponds to the Urartu region of the Armenian highlands, and Minni to the Mannaeans near Lake Urmia. Ashkenaz is placed beside both as a northern neighbor.

Modern readers usually know the name through the term "Ashkenazi Jews." In the Middle Ages, Jewish writers began applying biblical place names to the lands where they lived. Ashkenaz came to mean Germany, and Sepharad came to mean Spain. This was a way of mapping new surroundings with biblical vocabulary. The label itself named a region; it does not establish that Jews of Central and Eastern Europe descend physically from Gomer's son. The biblical Ashkenaz and the later Jewish usage should be kept distinct.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Genesis 10 opens with "the genealogy of the sons of Noah" and notes that "sons were born to them after the flood" (Gen. 10:1). Japheth's line comes first: "Gomer, Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, Meshech, and Tiras" (10:2). Then: "The sons of Gomer were Ashkenaz, Riphath, and Togarmah" (10:3). Japheth's descendants are generally associated with peoples to the north and west of Israel. Ashkenaz belongs to that northern branch.

First Chronicles 1:6 repeats the same record for Israel after the exile, as part of a genealogy that begins with Adam and moves toward the people of God. Here the second brother is spelled Diphath rather than Riphath. This difference is commonly explained as a copying variation between two similar Hebrew letters, and it does not affect Ashkenaz himself.

The name then disappears from Scripture for a long stretch of history. It returns in Jeremiah's great oracle against Babylon (Jer. 50–51). The Lord commands: "Set up a banner in the land, Blow the trumpet among the nations! Prepare the nations against her, Call the kingdoms together against her: Ararat, Minni, and Ashkenaz. Appoint a general against her; Cause the horses to come up like the bristling locusts" (Jer. 51:27). The image of horses "like the bristling locusts" suits a people known for mounted warfare. In the surrounding verses Jeremiah also names the Medes as the leading power against Babylon (51:11, 28), so Ashkenaz appears as one part of a northern coalition rather than as Babylon's sole conqueror.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states two things plainly. First, Ashkenaz belongs to Noah's family. The Table of Nations does not treat distant peoples as nameless outsiders. It records them as kin, descended from the same family God preserved through the flood. Second, God commands Ashkenaz. Jeremiah does not present the northern kingdoms as acting on their own ambition. The Lord sets up the banner, sounds the trumpet, and calls them together. No promise binds God to Ashkenaz, and no worship of the Lord among them is recorded, yet His word sets them in motion.

It is a reasonable inference that this demonstrates the scope of God's rule. Babylon had destroyed Jerusalem and carried Judah into exile. When God brought that empire down, He drew on peoples from the far edges of Israel's known world. Jeremiah's wider message supports this reading. Earlier in the book, disaster comes "out of the north" against Judah (Jer. 1:14; 4:6; 6:22). In chapters 50–51 the direction is reversed, and an assembly of nations from the north comes against Babylon (50:3, 9, 41). Ashkenaz is part of that reversal, as the threat that once fell on God's people now falls on their oppressor.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The name Ashkenaz never appears in the New Testament, and the Greek word "Scythian" is a separate term, not an occurrence of this Hebrew name. Still, if Ashkenaz is rightly identified with the Scythians, Colossians 3:11 brings their story to a striking point. Paul writes that in the new man "there is neither Greek nor Jew, circumcised nor uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave nor free, but Christ is all and in all."

In the Greek and Roman world, the Scythian stood for the farthest and roughest kind of outsider. Paul places him in the same sentence as the Greek and the Jew, with no ranking among them. This connection is a canonical resonance built on a historical identification, not an explicit New Testament interpretation of Jeremiah. It is a fruitful one, though. The people Jeremiah saw riding against Babylon in judgment are named among those Christ gathers into one body. Genesis 10 shows the nations scattered, Jeremiah shows them under God's command, and Colossians shows them made one in Christ.

5. Spirit & Life

Ashkenaz shows that no people lies beyond God's notice or outside His authority. Scripture records no worship of the LORD among this northern people, yet they still moved when He blew the trumpet. That should steady believers who watch powerful and arrogant systems in the world. Babylon looked permanent, yet God had already named the kingdoms that would bring it down.

The larger arc speaks of mercy as well as judgment. Rome's word for the most distant and least civilized people appears in Paul's list of those who belong to Christ. The gospel does not stop at the edge of what we consider respectable or familiar. No people, however feared or looked down on, is too far away to be gathered into Christ, because He "is all and in all."

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:1–3 — Places Ashkenaz in Noah's family through Japheth and Gomer, as part of the post-flood Table of Nations.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:6 — Preserves the same genealogy in Israel's post-exilic record (with the spelling Diphath for Riphath).

  • Jeremiah 51:27 — The only appearance of Ashkenaz as a kingdom, summoned by the Lord with Ararat and Minni against Babylon.

  • Jeremiah 50:3, 9, 41; 51:11, 28 — Context of the northern coalition, led by the Medes, that God raises against Babylon.

  • Jeremiah 1:14; 4:6; 6:22 — Earlier "from the north" warnings against Judah, which help explain the reversal in chapters 50–51.

  • Colossians 3:11 — Names the Scythian among those united in Christ; related to Ashkenaz only through the probable historical identification.
