SCARLET, CRIMSON - (shânîy, Strong's H8144)

Summary

The Hebrew word shânîy means "scarlet": the brilliant red dye, the thread or yarn colored with it, and cloth made from that yarn. In the ancient world, scarlet was costly and hard to produce, so it marked honor, wealth, and beauty. Most of its uses appear in Exodus, where God commands scarlet yarn to be woven with blue, purple, and fine linen into the curtains of His tabernacle and the garments of the high priest. The same color appears in the cleansing rites for a healed leper and in the ashes of the red heifer, where it lies beside sacrificial blood.

A few uses outside the law carry great weight. A scarlet thread on a newborn's wrist claims a firstborn right that God overturns (Genesis 38). A scarlet cord in Rahab's window marks the household in Jericho that God spares (Joshua 2; 6:22–25). Isaiah uses scarlet for sin that no one can wash out, then promises: "Though your sins are like scarlet, They shall be as white as snow" (Isaiah 1:18). Scarlet can clothe a bride, a careful household, or a city playing the harlot. It all depends on who wears it and why. The color appears once more when soldiers mock Jesus with a scarlet robe before He goes to the cross, where the stain Isaiah described is washed away.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Scarlet dye came from a small insect gathered from oak trees. The insects were dried and crushed to release a deep red color. Gathering enough of them to dye cloth took a great deal of labor, so scarlet fabric was expensive and belonged to people of means. Dyers valued it because the color held fast in the fibers. Hebrew often pairs shânîy with tola'ath, the word for the "worm" or insect that produced the dye, and English versions render the pair as "scarlet" or "crimson."

Most uses of shânîy appear in the tabernacle and priesthood chapters of Exodus. Others occur in purification law, historical narrative, poetry, and prophecy. NKJV almost always translates the word "scarlet." It uses "crimson" in Jeremiah 4:30 and "scarlet thread" in Exodus 39:2.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shânîy names a red color and the material that carries it. It does not mean "blood" or "sin." Those associations come from how Scripture uses the color.

1. Scarlet yarn or thread as a material, woven into curtains and garments or tied as a marker, as in Exodus 26:1 and Genesis 38:28.

2. Scarlet cloth or clothing as a sign of wealth, honor, or beauty, as in 2 Samuel 1:24 and Proverbs 31:21.

3. The color scarlet used as a picture or comparison, as with the bride's lips in Song of Solomon 4:3 or sins "like scarlet" in Isaiah 1:18.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the tabernacle chapters, scarlet is almost never mentioned alone. It is nearly always listed with blue, purple, and fine linen. The repetition shows that the color belongs to a single set of costly materials God chose for His dwelling. Scarlet appears in the inner curtains with cherubim woven into them (Exodus 26:1), in the veil before the Most Holy Place (Exodus 26:31), in the screens at the door and the court gate (Exodus 26:36; 27:16), and in the high priest's ephod, breastplate, and the pomegranates on his robe's hem (Exodus 28:6, 15, 33). Israel gave the material willingly, and skilled women spun it with their own hands (Exodus 35:23, 25).

In Leviticus 14 and Numbers 19, scarlet becomes part of the cleansing rites, alongside cedar wood and hyssop. In the narratives, a scarlet thread or cord serves as a visible marker. In poetry and prophecy, the color becomes a comparison, describing lips, luxury, seduction, or sin. The moral meaning of scarlet changes with the setting. The same color adorns God's sanctuary in Exodus and dresses the harlot city in Jeremiah.

4. Theological Significance

Scarlet first shows that God's dwelling deserves the best a people can give. A dye that ordinary households could rarely afford ran through every curtain and veil. When God told Israel to build His tent from scarlet, blue, and purple, He was teaching them that approaching Him means approaching royalty. The cherubim woven into the scarlet curtains (Exodus 26:1) and the scarlet veil (Exodus 26:31) marked the boundary of His holy presence with beauty and with warning. Exodus 26:14 adds a covering of rams' skins dyed red. That covering uses a different Hebrew word, yet it surrounded the meeting place with a similar color.

Scarlet also stands beside blood. In the cleansing of the healed leper, the scarlet yarn, cedar, and hyssop were dipped together with a living bird into the blood of the bird that had been killed (Leviticus 14:4–6). In Numbers 19:6, the priest threw scarlet into the fire that burned the red heifer, whose ashes would later cleanse those defiled by death. The color does not cleanse anyone. Instead, it stands beside the blood that does, a visible reminder that the unclean are restored through a life given in their place. Hebrews names the scarlet wool among the things Moses used when he sprinkled the book and the people with blood (Hebrews 9:19). The point the writer develops, though, is the blood: "without shedding of blood there is no remission" (Hebrews 9:22).

A third truth appears in the tension between these uses. God requires scarlet in His holy house, and He also calls human sin "scarlet." The color of His glory is also the color of our guilt. Scripture leaves that tension open until the cross.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 38:28–30: the thread that marked the wrong son. As Tamar gave birth to twins, one baby put out his hand. The midwife tied a scarlet thread on it and said that this one came out first. Then the hand drew back, and his brother broke through ahead of him. The midwife named him Perez, meaning "breach," and the child with the scarlet thread was called Zerah. The thread recorded what everyone in the room assumed, a firstborn right claimed and marked for all to see. God passed over that marked claim, and the line of promise ran through Perez to David (Ruth 4:18–22). Paul describes the same pattern with Jacob and Esau: "The older shall serve the younger," so "that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11–12).

Joshua 2:18–21: the cord in the window. The spies told Rahab to tie a scarlet cord in the window through which she had let them down and to gather her family inside. Anyone who stepped out of the house would bear his own guilt, and anyone who stayed inside would be protected. Rahab tied the cord at once (Joshua 2:21), before any Israelite army had crossed the Jordan. The scene follows the Passover closely. In Egypt, a mark of red on the door set apart the households God would pass over (Exodus 12:13), and no one was to go out until morning. In Jericho, a red cord set apart the household that would be spared when the city fell. Joshua records that the spies brought out Rahab and all her relatives, and that Joshua spared her and her father's household (Joshua 6:22–25). The person saved was a Canaanite and a harlot, from the very city under judgment. Hebrews 11:31 names the cause: "By faith the harlot Rahab did not perish with those who did not believe." Her faith took a visible, concrete form, and God honored it.

Isaiah 1:18: sins like scarlet. God summons a corrupt nation: "Come now, and let us reason together." Then He describes their sin with the very dye prized because it would not wash out: "Though your sins are like scarlet, They shall be as white as snow." The comparison makes sin a deep stain soaked through the fibers rather than a smudge on the surface, and no scrubbing on Israel's part could remove it. Earlier in the chapter, the people's hands are "full of blood" (Isaiah 1:15), which deepens the picture. Against all that, God promises to make the stain white as snow. David prayed for the same impossible result: "Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow" (Psalm 51:7). Only God can do what the dye seems to forbid.

Jeremiah 4:30 with 2 Samuel 1:24 and Proverbs 31:21: scarlet worn well and worn badly. David praised Saul for clothing Israel's daughters in scarlet, and the wise wife does not fear snow because her household is clothed in scarlet. In both passages, scarlet expresses honor and loving provision. Jeremiah then turns the same luxury against Jerusalem: "Though you clothe yourself with crimson... In vain you will make yourself fair; Your lovers will despise you." The city dressed in fine clothing to win the favor of foreign allies while it abandoned God, and those allies would turn on it. The fabric was the same in each case, but its use had changed. A gift meant for honor became a disguise for unfaithfulness.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Both scarlet threads in the narratives lead into the family of Jesus. Matthew's genealogy names Tamar, mother of Perez and Zerah, and Rahab, mother of Boaz (Matthew 1:3, 5). The thread that marked a human claim and the cord that marked a foreigner's rescue both belong to the story of the Messiah's ancestry. God built that line through unexpected choices and undeserved mercy.

The New Testament brings the color into the passion narrative: "they stripped Him and put a scarlet robe on Him" (Matthew 27:28). Mark and John call the robe purple, but Matthew names its scarlet color. The soldiers meant the robe as mockery, a cheap imitation of royal dress. Matthew does not interpret the color, and he does not link it to the sanctuary or to Isaiah's stain. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, though, the scene invites a canonical comparison: the true King, mocked in scarlet, was about to shed the blood that makes scarlet sins white as snow. Hebrews makes the fulfillment explicit, not through the color but through the blood. The sprinkling Moses performed (Hebrews 9:19–22) pointed to Christ, who entered heaven "with His own blood" to obtain eternal redemption (Hebrews 9:12).

The dye's source gives a further picture, and it should be held as an illustration, not as the psalm's meaning. Ancient scarlet came from the bodies of small scale insects that were gathered, dried and crushed to release their color, and Strong's dictionary describes the related Hebrew word tola'ath as the crimson grub that yielded it. The same Hebrew word is the ordinary word for "worm," and it is the word the sufferer uses in Psalm 22:6: "But I am a worm, and no man." The psalm itself speaks of being despised and scorned, not of dye. The connection is an analogy drawn from how the color was made: scarlet came from a creature crushed to yield its color, and the One who prayed this psalm was crushed for our sins (Isaiah 53:5).

Scripture's last use of the color presents a counterfeit. The great harlot of Revelation is "arrayed in purple and scarlet," rides "a scarlet beast," and is "drunk with the blood of the saints" (Revelation 17:3–6). She wears the colors of the sanctuary, but her scarlet means sin and spilled blood. Her end follows Jeremiah's warning to Jerusalem: Babylon's mourners lament the city "clothed in fine linen, purple, and scarlet" (Revelation 18:16) as it falls.

7. Spirit & Life

Isaiah 1:18 tells the truth about sin first. The stain is real and deep, and no one removes it by effort. That honesty is a mercy, because people who believe a stain will scrub out keep scrubbing. God's invitation begins with "Come now, and let us reason together." Around that invitation Isaiah also calls Judah to "Cease to do evil, Learn to do good; Seek justice" (Isaiah 1:16–17) and to be "willing and obedient" (1:19). God's offer of cleansing came with a call to turn. For Christians, John grounds the same hope in Christ: "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9), for "the blood of Jesus Christ His Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7), including the sin that has soaked into the fibers.

Rahab teaches what faith looks like in practice. She tied the cord while Jericho still stood, and when the city fell she and her household were brought out and spared. A believer's refuge is just as concrete: sheltered under the blood of Christ, with nothing hidden and nowhere else to go.

Genesis 38 humbles every claim to priority. Spiritual standing comes from the God who calls, not from being first in line or carrying the right marker. That keeps the proud from boasting and keeps the overlooked from despair.

Scarlet's two uses in Proverbs 31 and Jeremiah 4 test the heart. God gives beauty and provision gladly. The bride's lips are "like a strand of scarlet" (Song of Solomon 4:3), and a faithful home is warmly clothed. The danger comes when those same gifts become a costume for winning the world's approval. Our scarlet glorifies God when it adorns His house and serves His people, and it becomes a disguise when it hides a heart that has left Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 28:33 — Pomegranates of blue, purple, and scarlet hung on the hem of the high priest's robe, so the color of honor moved with him as he served before the Lord.

  • Exodus 35:25 — Skilled women spun scarlet with their own hands for the tabernacle. Costly material became a costly offering through willing labor.

  • Numbers 4:8 — When Israel traveled, a scarlet cloth covered the table of showbread. God's presence remained honored even on the road.

  • Leviticus 14:49–52 — The cleansing rite with scarlet, cedar, and hyssop also applied to a house struck by mildew. God's concern for purity reached the homes where His people lived.

  • Exodus 39:29 — The sash of fine linen and blue, purple, and scarlet completed the priestly garments "as the LORD had commanded Moses," which marks obedience in the smallest details.

10. Other Relevant Insights

In the cleansing rites, scarlet is joined to cedar wood and hyssop. Elsewhere Scripture uses that same pair to span creation's full range, "from the cedar tree of Lebanon even to the hyssop that springs out of the wall" (1 Kings 4:33). Read together, the rite gathers the loftiest and the lowliest beside the color of sacrifice. The law does not explain the pairing, so this is a fitting meditation rather than a stated meaning. It still points rightly to the blood of Christ, which reaches from heaven's height to the lowest sinner.
