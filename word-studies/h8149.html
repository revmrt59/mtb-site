SENIR - (Shᵉnîyr, Strong's H8149)

Summary

Senir is a name for the great mountain on Israel's far northern horizon, the snow-topped height better known as Mount Hermon, northeast of the Sea of Galilee. Moses explains that the Amorites called Hermon "Senir," while the Sidonians called it "Sirion" (Deuteronomy 3:9). The meaning of the name is uncertain. Some scholars suggest "coat of mail" or "snow mountain," both of which suit a high, armored-looking ridge that holds its snow. Neither meaning can be confirmed.

Senir appears only four times, but each appearance shows the mountain from a different angle. In Deuteronomy, Senir is a landmark of Amorite territory that God gave into Israel's hand. In 1 Chronicles it marks the northern limit of the half-tribe of Manasseh's growth. In the Song of Solomon it is a wild summit of lions and leopards, and from that summit the beloved is called to come away with the one who loves her. In Ezekiel its fir trees become planks for Tyre, a proud trading city that God would bring down. Taken together, these passages show a mountain that served as a boundary, as a picture of a lonely and dangerous height, and as raw material for human pride. Over all of it stands the God who gave the land, who calls His people into close fellowship, and who chose to dwell on Zion rather than on the loftiest peaks.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Senir is a mountain or mountain ridge in the Anti-Lebanon range, the line of heights running northeast from the Sea of Galilee. It is closely tied to Mount Hermon, the tallest peak in the region. Deuteronomy 3:9 gives the earliest explanation: "(the Sidonians call Hermon Sirion, and the Amorites call it Senir)." In that passage, Senir is the Amorite name for the same mountain Israel called Hermon. Hermon (Strong's H2768) and Sirion (Strong's H8303) are separately indexed names for this mountain, and their occurrences are not counted here.

The meaning of "Senir" is uncertain. Proposals include "coat of mail" and "snow mountain." Either would fit a high, rugged mountain, and Hermon's snowcap is visible from far away. These are scholarly suggestions, however, not established derivations, and Scripture itself offers no wordplay on the name. The name is best treated as an inherited local name whose original sense is no longer certain.

Two later passages raise a question of identity. Song of Solomon 4:8 speaks of "the top of Senir and Hermon," and the Hebrew of 1 Chronicles 5:23 is often understood to list Senir and Mount Hermon side by side. Because of this, many readers think "Senir" may later have come to mean a particular peak or section of the Hermon range rather than the whole mountain. The NKJV takes the other view in 1 Chronicles 5:23 and reads the names as equivalents: "to Baal Hermon, that is, to Senir, or Mount Hermon." The exact boundary between Senir and Hermon cannot be fixed with certainty. What is clear is that Senir belongs to the Hermon massif at the northern edge of Israel's world.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

An Amorite landmark (Deuteronomy 3:9). Senir first appears as Moses looks back on the conquest east of the Jordan. God had given Israel victory over Sihon and over Og, king of Bashan, and Israel took the land of the two Amorite kings as far north as Mount Hermon (Deuteronomy 3:1–10). Moses pauses to record what other peoples called the mountain. This short aside matters. Senir was the name the Amorites themselves used for their great northern landmark. By preserving it, the account shows how far God's gift reached: the territory of the Amorites, right up to the mountain they had named, had passed to Israel.

A boundary of growth (1 Chronicles 5:23). Centuries later, the Chronicler describes the half-tribe of Manasseh settled east of the Jordan: "So the children of the half-tribe of Manasseh dwelt in the land. Their numbers increased from Bashan to Baal Hermon, that is, to Senir, or Mount Hermon." Here Senir marks the northern edge of a tribe that God had multiplied. The next verses complete the story in a sobering way. These same tribes were unfaithful to the God of their fathers and turned to the gods of the peoples of the land. God then allowed the king of Assyria to carry them into exile (1 Chronicles 5:25–26). Senir therefore stands at the edge of a land that was freely given, richly filled, and lost through unfaithfulness.

A wild summit (Song of Solomon 4:8). In the Song, the mountain becomes poetry: "Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse, With me from Lebanon. Look from the top of Amana, From the top of Senir and Hermon, From the lions' dens, From the mountains of the leopards." The beloved is pictured among the highest and most remote peaks of the north. Those heights are splendid, but they are also the haunt of predators. The bridegroom's appeal, "Come with me," invites her out of that dangerous grandeur and into his company.

Timber for a proud city (Ezekiel 27:5). In Ezekiel's lament over Tyre, the Phoenician port is pictured as a magnificent ship: "They made all your planks of fir trees from Senir; They took a cedar from Lebanon to make you a mast." The best materials of the northern mountains went into Tyre's glory. The Hebrew word translated "fir" names a large evergreen. The exact species is uncertain and may have been fir, cypress, pine, or juniper. The fine planks did not save the ship. Later in the same lament, the east wind breaks it in the heart of the seas (Ezekiel 27:26), and Tyre's pride ends in ruin.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God gave Israel the Amorite lands up to Hermon and that Manasseh grew there as far as Senir. These facts testify to God's faithfulness to His promise of land. A mountain the Amorites had named became a marker for the inheritance of God's people. From this we may draw a careful implication: no landmark of the nations stands outside the reach of the LORD's purposes.

The Chronicler adds a second lesson. The tribe that spread to Senir did not hold the land, because its people forsook the God who gave it. The gift was real, but it did not exempt them from faithfulness. Ezekiel carries a related truth onto the world stage. Senir's trees were good gifts of creation, yet Tyre used them to build a monument to its own wealth and self-sufficiency, and God judged that pride. Whether Senir is the edge of Israel's land or a source of Tyre's timber, the mountain shows that what God provides is meant to be received with faithfulness, not turned into a ground for boasting.

Psalm 68 offers a fitting backdrop, though it names Bashan rather than Senir. The towering mountains of that northern region are pictured as envious: "Why do you fume with envy, you mountains of many peaks? This is the mountain which God desires to dwell in; Yes, the LORD will dwell in it forever" (Psalm 68:16). Hermon and Senir were far grander than Zion. Yet God chose the lower hill as His dwelling place. Height and majesty do not determine where He makes Himself known.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Senir is not named in the New Testament, so any connection to Christ comes through the wider canon. The connections below are thematic resonances, not explicit fulfillments.

The first connection comes through the Song of Solomon. The New Testament presents Christ as the Bridegroom of His people (John 3:29; Ephesians 5:25–32; Revelation 19:7). For that reason, Christians have long heard in the Song's "Come with me" an echo of Christ's call to His church. Read in that light, Song 4:8 pictures the Lord calling His beloved away from remote and perilous heights to share life with Him.

The second connection comes through the Gospels. Jesus asked His disciples "Who do you say that I am?" near Caesarea Philippi, a town at the foot of Mount Hermon (Matthew 16:13–16). Six days later He led Peter, James, and John up "a high mountain" and was transfigured before them (Matthew 17:1–2). The Gospel does not name that mountain. Many have proposed Hermon, while an old tradition favors Mount Tabor. If the Transfiguration took place on the Hermon range, the same northern heights once known by their Amorite name became the place where the Father declared, in Matthew's words, that Jesus is His beloved Son.

5. Spirit & Life

The Song of Solomon gives Senir its most searching use. The top of Senir offers a breathtaking view, but it is also the country of "lions' dens" and "the mountains of the leopards." Some places look lofty and impressive and are still unsafe to live in. A person can settle into a kind of spiritual isolation in pride, in self-reliance, or in a distance from God's people that feels safe and superior. The bridegroom does not scold his beloved for being on the mountain. He calls her to come with him. That invitation reflects the heart of God, who seeks fellowship with His people and draws them out of danger into His company.

Ezekiel adds a needed warning. The firs of Senir were sound timber, and nothing was wrong with the trees. The trouble lay in what Tyre built with them and in the trust Tyre placed in what it had built. The gifts God gives, including skill, resources, and opportunity, can be turned into a ship of our own glory. No vessel built that way survives the east wind.

1 Chronicles 5 holds both truths together. Manasseh grew all the way to Senir because God blessed the tribe, and the tribe lost everything because it forgot Him. God's generosity calls for an answering faithfulness, and Psalm 68 shows where that faithfulness looks. It does not look to the grandest mountain on the horizon. It looks to the place God has chosen for His dwelling. In the fullness of Scripture, that place is found in Christ Himself.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Deuteronomy 3:9 – Identifies Senir as the Amorite name for Mount Hermon and places it at the northern limit of the lands God gave Israel east of the Jordan.

  • 1 Chronicles 5:23 – Marks Senir as the northern reach of the half-tribe of Manasseh's growth. Verses 25–26 record that tribe's later unfaithfulness and exile.

  • Song of Solomon 4:8 – Pictures Senir as a majestic, dangerous summit from which the beloved is invited, "Come with me."

  • Ezekiel 27:5 – Names Senir's fir trees as planks for proud Tyre, whose shipwreck follows in Ezekiel 27:26.

  • Psalm 68:15–16 – Provides a related contrast between the lofty northern mountains and the hill God chose for His dwelling.

  • Matthew 16:13; 17:1–2 – Places Jesus near Hermon at Caesarea Philippi and then on "a high mountain" whose identity is unnamed.
