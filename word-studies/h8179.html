GATE, GATES, GATEWAY - (shaʻar, Strong's H8179)

Summary

The Hebrew word shaʻar means "gate," the opening in the wall of a city, a temple court, a palace, or even the boundary of a camp. In ancient Israel the city gate was where public life happened. Elders sat there to judge cases, business was settled there, and news arrived there first. Kings took their seats there. Because so much passed through the gate, the word could stand for the whole town. When Deuteronomy speaks of the people "within your gates," it means everyone living in Israel's towns.

Scripture builds large truths on this ordinary word. At the gate of Bethlehem, Boaz publicly redeems Ruth's family inheritance and takes her as his wife. Through the east gate Ezekiel watches the glory of the LORD leave the temple and later return. In Nehemiah the rebuilt gates mark a people restored after exile. Psalms and prophets speak of the "gates of death," the gates of righteousness, and gates the King of glory enters. The storyline ends with a city whose gates never close, and Jesus Christ is at the center of that story. He redeems in the place of justice. He breaks the power of death's gates. He opens the way into God's presence.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shaʻar is a common Hebrew noun found from Genesis to Zechariah. Its settings are concrete: Sodom's gate, where Lot sat (Genesis 19:1). The "gate of the court" of the tabernacle (Exodus 27:16). The "entrance of the camp" in the wilderness (Exodus 32:26). The king's gate at the Persian palace, where Mordecai sat (Esther 2:19). The many gates of Jerusalem and of Ezekiel's visionary temple.

A city gate was more than a doorway. Scripture shows elders sitting in it (Ruth 4:2), food being sold there (2 Kings 7:1), and kings holding court there (2 Samuel 19:8). Ezekiel's measured gateways have side chambers built into them (Ezekiel 40:7). The gate worked as a courthouse, a market, and a public square at once, and that is why the word carries so much weight.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A gate or gateway: the actual entrance of a city, court, temple, palace, or camp. Examples include the gate of Samaria (2 Kings 7:17) and the temple gateways in Ezekiel 40.

2. A town or its community: the gate standing for the whole town and its people. The phrase "within your gates" means "in your towns" (Deuteronomy 14:29). The NKJV translates the word "town" in Ruth 3:11 and "cities" in 1 Kings 8:37.

3. A figurative entrance to a realm or state: for example, the "gates of death" (Job 38:17) or the "gates of righteousness" (Psalm 118:19).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The gate as a place of justice. God commanded Israel to appoint judges and officers "in all your gates" (Deuteronomy 16:18). Parents brought a rebellious son to the elders at the gate (Deuteronomy 21:19), and a wronged widow went there to plead her case (Deuteronomy 25:7). Because justice was done at the gate, a society could be judged by what happened there. Amos condemns Israel because "They hate the one who rebukes in the gate" (Amos 5:10), and he calls the people to restore justice there (5:15).

Sitting in the gate. To sit in the gate meant having standing in the community. When Lot "was sitting in the gate of Sodom" (Genesis 19:1), the detail tells us how far he had settled into a city God was about to destroy. When David "arose and sat in the gate" after Absalom's death, the people heard "There is the king, sitting in the gate," and they returned to him (2 Samuel 19:8). The king's presence at the gate ended the chaos.

The gate as strength and weak point. A gate was the most heavily defended part of a wall, yet it was also the wall's only opening. Isaiah promises "strength to those who turn back the battle at the gate" (Isaiah 28:6). Against Jerusalem, God says, "I have set the point of the sword against all their gates" (Ezekiel 21:15). This is why God's promise that Abraham's descendants would possess the gate of their enemies (Genesis 22:17, paraphrased) meant full victory. Whoever held the gate held the city.

Gates that mourn. When judgment emptied a city, the prophets pictured its gates grieving: "All her gates are desolate" (Lamentations 1:4), "her gates languish" (Jeremiah 14:2), "Wail, O gate! Cry, O city!" (Isaiah 14:31). A silent gate meant there were no elders, no trade, no weddings, and no feasts left.

4. Theological Significance

The gate shows that God cares about public life. His law was administered in the most visible place in town, and His care reached everyone inside the walls. Deuteronomy extends Sabbath rest to the stranger "within your gates" (Deuteronomy 5:14). It also requires that the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow within your gates be fed and satisfied (Deuteronomy 14:29). The gate defined a community responsible for its weakest members.

The gate also marks the boundary of holiness. The tabernacle court had one gate. Ezekiel's temple has gateway after gateway, each one measured. God is approachable, but only on His terms. Beyond every earthly wall stands the final boundary, the "gates of death" (Job 38:17). God's question to Job exposes human helplessness there. The psalmist calls on God as "You who lift me up from the gates of death" (Psalm 9:13). God alone has authority over the one gate no human being can open.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Ruth 4:1–11: Redemption at the gate. "Now Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there" (Ruth 4:1). He took the matter to the public court, called ten elders as witnesses, and laid out the closer relative's right to redeem. The relative wanted the land but backed away once Ruth was part of the bargain: "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance" (4:6). Boaz then paid the cost the other man would not pay. He acquired Ruth "that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from his position at the gate" (4:10). A dead man's place in the community was restored at the very spot where community standing was recognized. The people answered, "We are witnesses" (4:11). Redemption here was lawful, public, costly, and beyond dispute. The book of Ruth does not present Boaz as a prophecy, but as a canonical comparison his public, costly redemption helps readers understand the greater Redeemer who would pay for His people in full view.

Ezekiel 10–11 and 43–44: The glory leaves and returns. Ezekiel saw the glory of the LORD stand at the east gate of the temple (Ezekiel 10:19; 11:1) before it left the city under judgment. Years later in a vision, he was brought back to "the gate that faces toward the east" (Ezekiel 43:1). There the glory came from the east, and "the glory of the LORD came into the temple by way of the gate which faces toward the east" (Ezekiel 43:4). God returned by the way He had left. Then He spoke the promise: "this is the place of My throne … where I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel forever" (Ezekiel 43:7). Later Ezekiel found the east gate shut, and the LORD gave the reason: "no man shall enter by it, because the LORD God of Israel has entered by it; therefore it shall be shut" (Ezekiel 44:2). The shutting honors the LORD's own entry; the promise that He will stay comes from 43:7. The prince alone could sit in the gateway to eat bread before the LORD, and even he was to come in and go out "by way of the vestibule of the gateway" (Ezekiel 44:3), not through the shut outer gate. Ezekiel's long, careful measuring of the gates belongs to this same message: God's holy presence is guarded, and it is restored only by His own initiative.

Nehemiah 1–13: Rebuilding the gates. Nehemiah's grief began with a report that Jerusalem's gates were "burned with fire" (Nehemiah 1:3). A city without gates had no defense and no settled identity, and its neighbors mocked it. The rebuilding began at the Sheep Gate, which the high priest and his fellow priests built and consecrated (3:1). Nehemiah ordered the gates kept shut until the sun was hot (7:3), and he later shut them to protect the Sabbath (13:19). The restored gates then became a meeting place for God's Word. The people gathered in the open square before the Water Gate to hear Ezra read the Law (8:1). God restored more than masonry. He gave back to the returned exiles a protected life as His people, centered on His Word.

Psalms 24 and 118, with Exodus 32: Who may enter? Psalm 24 commands the gates themselves: "Lift up your heads, O you gates! … And the King of glory shall come in" (Psalm 24:7). The entrance is opened first for God. Psalm 118 then asks who may follow: "Open to me the gates of righteousness," and the answer comes, "This is the gate of the LORD, Through which the righteous shall enter" (Psalm 118:19–20). The question of entrance had already been posed sharply at the camp's gate after the golden calf. There Moses cried, "Whoever is on the LORD's side—come to me!" (Exodus 32:26). The gate is where God's people are sorted. Those welcomed in come with thanks: "Enter into His gates with thanksgiving" (Psalm 100:4).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The gate's story runs toward Jesus. Boaz's public redemption at Bethlehem's gate offers a canonical comparison with Christ, who redeemed His people at full cost and in full view. Hebrews states the purpose of His death in connection with a gate: "Jesus also, that He might sanctify the people with His own blood, suffered outside the gate" (Hebrews 13:12). He was put outside the city's boundary so that His people could be made holy and brought near to God.

The Old Testament pictured death as a fortified city with gates no one could break. Jesus promised that the gates of Hades would not overpower His church (Matthew 16:18, paraphrased). The risen Christ holds the keys of Hades and of Death (Revelation 1:18). God's promise that Abraham's seed would possess the gate of his enemies reaches its fullest meaning in Christ's victory over the last enemy.

Jesus also speaks of Himself as the entrance. John uses a different, Greek word, but the image completes the pattern: "I am the door. If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved" (John 10:9). The King of glory who entered the gates is now the way through them. The story ends where Isaiah looked: "your gates shall be open continually; They shall not be shut day or night" (Isaiah 60:11). John sees the same city: "Its gates shall not be shut at all by day (there shall be no night there)" (Revelation 21:25). Gates were built to keep danger out. In the new city nothing threatens anymore, and only "those who are written in the Lamb's Book of Life" enter (21:27).

7. Spirit & Life

God speaks at the gate. Wisdom does not hide in a library: "At the openings of the gates in the city She speaks her words" (Proverbs 1:21). God sent Jeremiah to stand in the gate of the LORD's house and speak to everyone entering (Jeremiah 7:2). The Lord confronts people in the middle of their work, trade, and public decisions. A believer should expect to hear Him there, and should practice justice there, because Amos shows that God judges a nation by its gates.

Lot is a warning. He gained a seat in Sodom's gate (Genesis 19:1), the place of standing in a city God was about to destroy. When he later warned his sons-in-law, "to his sons-in-law he seemed to be joking" (Genesis 19:14). Scripture does not say that his seat caused that, but the two details sit side by side, and they invite reflection on where we sit and whose approval we seek. Moses' call at the camp gate still asks the question every heart must answer: whose side are we on?

Nehemiah shows that restored life needs guarded gates. A believer's life, like that city, needs boundaries kept with care, and its open spaces should be gathering places for God's Word.

Finally, every gate in this world must be guarded, because every wall is vulnerable. Christ has already broken through the strongest gates, those of death. The believer lives toward a city whose gates stand open, and can enter God's courts now with thanksgiving.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 28:17: At Bethel, Jacob called the place of his dream the house of God and the gate of heaven, the first time Scripture links a gate with access to God.

  • Deuteronomy 6:9: Israel was to write God's words on their doorposts and gates, so His Word met them in both private and public life.

  • 2 Kings 7:1, 17–20: Elisha's promise of cheap food was fulfilled at Samaria's gate, and the officer who mocked the promise was trampled to death in that same gate.

  • Esther 2:19–21: Mordecai, sitting at the king's gate, overheard the plot against the king. God's providence worked through an ordinary post at the gate.

  • Proverbs 31:23, 31: The husband of the capable wife is respected in the gates, and her own works praise her there.

  • Isaiah 60:18: In restored Zion, the walls will be called Salvation and the gates Praise.
