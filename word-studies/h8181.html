HAIR, HAIRY - (sêʻâr, Strong's H8181)

Summary

Sêʻâr is the ordinary Hebrew word for hair, whether on a person's head and body or on an animal's hide woven into a garment. It describes everyday things: a baby's appearance at birth, a bride's beauty, a priest's examination of a skin disease. Because hair is so visible and so personal, Scripture also uses it to show what is happening between people and God. In Leviticus, the priest reads hair to tell whether a disease has gone deep, and a cleansed person shaves every hair before entering the camp and again on the seventh day. A Nazirite's uncut hair marked a life set apart to the Lord. Ezra tore out his hair in grief over the people's sin, and Isaiah warned that the Lord would shave Judah with a "hired razor."

Taken together, these passages treat hair as a person's natural glory and vigor. That glory can be offered to God, as in the Nazirite vow. It can be prized for its own sake, as Absalom did. It can also be stripped away, either for cleansing or in judgment. The most hopeful line in this word's history is a small one in Samson's story. In a Philistine prison, blind and shamed, "the hair of his head began to grow again" (Judges 16:22). God had not finished with him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Sêʻâr is a common Hebrew noun for hair. More than half of its uses cluster in Leviticus 13–14, the laws for diagnosing and cleansing skin disease. The rest are spread across narrative (Esau, Samson, Absalom, Elijah), vows (Numbers 6), prophecy (Isaiah, Ezekiel, Zechariah), prayer of repentance (Ezra), and poetry (Psalms, Song of Solomon). In Israel's world, hair belonged to a person's dignity. Shaving or tearing it out was a public sign of grief or disgrace, and letting it grow uncut could be an act of devotion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means hair. Context settles which kind:

1. Hair on a human head or body, such as Absalom's thick hair (2 Samuel 14:26) or the hair a cleansed person shaves off (Leviticus 14:9).

2. Animal hair, especially as material for a rough garment. Elijah is called "a hairy man" (2 Kings 1:8), most likely because of his garment, and Zechariah speaks of "a robe of coarse hair" (Zechariah 13:4).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of hair in a passage depends on what happens to it. When hair is examined, as in Leviticus 13, it becomes evidence. When it is left uncut, as in Numbers 6:5, it is a vow made visible. When it is torn out, as in Ezra 9:3, it expresses grief. When it is shaved by an enemy, as in Isaiah 7:20, it becomes humiliation, and when it is admired, as in Song of Solomon 4:1, it becomes beauty. The hair stays the same physical thing throughout. What changes is the hand acting on it and the reason that hand acts.

4. Theological Significance

Across these passages, hair stands for the natural glory God gives a person: strength, vigor and attractiveness. Ezekiel's parable of Jerusalem shows the pattern. God rescues an abandoned infant and cares for her until she matures and "your hair grew" (Ezekiel 16:7). Her beauty was His gift. Yet the indictment that follows is that she trusted in her own beauty and turned it toward other lovers (Ezekiel 16:15). Gifts from God can become rivals to God.

Scripture traces three directions this glory can take. Offered to the Lord, it becomes consecration, as with the Nazirite. Admired for its own sake, it cannot prevent a fall, as with Absalom. Under God's dealings, it can be removed, whether in cleansing, as with the healed leper, or in judgment, as with Judah under Assyria. God is the One who gives this glory, weighs it, and can take it away.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 13–14: hair that tells the truth. The priest did not judge by the surface alone. A spot was serious when "the hair on the sore has turned white" and the sore went deeper than the skin (Leviticus 13:3). A scaly patch with thin yellow hair meant uncleanness (Leviticus 13:30). Healing was confirmed by two signs together: the scale stood still and did not spread, and black hair had grown up in it (Leviticus 13:37). The hair showed outwardly what was happening beneath the skin. When healing was confirmed, the person washed their clothes, shaved off all their hair and bathed, and after that came into the camp, though they stayed outside their own tent for seven days. On the seventh day they shaved again: head, beard, eyebrows, everything, and washed once more (Leviticus 14:8–9). The total shaving suggests a fresh start, like a newborn's bare beginning, though the text does not say so directly. What it does show plainly is that God's cleansing went all the way through before a person was fully restored to the community and to their own home.

Numbers 6 and Judges 16: the crown of consecration and the grace of regrowth. The Nazirite let his hair grow because "no razor shall come upon his head" during his separation to the Lord (Numbers 6:5). At the end of the vow, he shaved his consecrated hair at the tabernacle door and put it in the fire under the peace offering (Numbers 6:18). The hair itself was offered to God. Samson's hair was the visible mark of a lifelong vow, and it held no magic. When Delilah had it cut, the narrator explains what was really lost: the Lord had departed from him, and Samson did not know it (Judges 16:20). Then comes a single quiet sentence in the prison at Gaza: "However, the hair of his head began to grow again after it had been shaven" (Judges 16:22). Samson was blind and grinding grain for his enemies, a ruin of his own making. Even so, the sign of his belonging to God was returning. His strength came back when he called on the Lord (Judges 16:28), and Hebrews 11:32 later names him among the faithful. Failure had disciplined Samson, but it had not cancelled God's claim on him.

2 Samuel 14:26: Absalom's glory. The narrator stops to describe Absalom's hair. He cut it every year "because it was heavy on him," and it weighed "two hundred shekels according to the king's standard." The detail belongs to a portrait of Absalom's appeal: in all Israel no one was praised as much for his good looks (2 Samuel 14:25). Later Absalom rebelled against David, and the revolt collapsed. As his mule ran under a great terebinth, Absalom's "head caught in the terebinth," and he was left hanging until he was killed (2 Samuel 18:9). The text does not say his hair caught him, and it does not say his looks caused his revolt. What it does let the reader see is the contrast: the man whose appearance all Israel admired came to a humiliating end.

Ezra 9:3 and Isaiah 7:20: hair torn and shaved. When Ezra learned that the returned exiles had again joined themselves to the idolatrous peoples around them, he "tore my garment and my robe, and plucked out some of the hair of my head and beard, and sat down astonished." His own body showed the shame of the people's sin. Isaiah shows the reverse: God Himself does the stripping. "The Lord will shave with a hired razor... the king of Assyria, The head and the hair of the legs, And will also remove the beard" (Isaiah 7:20). The phrase "hired razor" carries a bitter irony. King Ahaz was trusting Assyria instead of the Lord, and he paid Assyria with silver and gold (2 Kings 16:7–8). The power Judah hired would become the razor that shamed Judah. Forced shaving was a deep disgrace, as David's envoys learned when Hanun shaved off half their beards (2 Samuel 10:4–5). Ezra's response shows the better way. He grieved over sin before judgment had to strip the people bare.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Elijah, "a hairy man wearing a leather belt around his waist" (2 Kings 1:8), wore the rough clothing of the wilderness while confronting a corrupt king's court. The garment became so closely identified with prophets that Zechariah foresees a day when false prophets "will not wear a robe of coarse hair to deceive" (Zechariah 13:4). The robe itself proved nothing about the man inside it. Then John the Baptist appears in camel's hair with a leather belt (Matthew 3:4), and Jesus identifies him as the Elijah who was to come (Matthew 11:14). Jesus also says John was no man in "soft garments" from kings' houses (Matthew 11:8). The hair garment marked the forerunner of the Messiah, who called Israel to repent before the King arrived. Some readers also hear a faint echo of Genesis 3:21, where God clothed fallen humanity in skins. The rough garment would then fit a call back to humility before God.

The larger story moves toward Christ in another way as well. Ezra plucked out his own beard in grief over the people's sin. Isaiah's Servant gave His cheeks to those who plucked out His beard (Isaiah 50:6, paraphrased). The Servant did not suffer shame for His own failure. He bore it for others. Isaiah 7 warned of a shaving that would strip a rebellious people. In Christ, the One who had no rebellion willingly accepted being stripped, mocked and shamed, so that a defiled people could be cleansed and brought into God's presence. The leper's shaved return to the camp gives a picture of that restoration. The cross is where that restoration was accomplished.

7. Spirit & Life

These passages ask what we are doing with the glory God has given us. Absalom was admired for his, and it could not keep him from ruin. The Nazirite grew his for the Lord and finally laid it on the altar fire. The choice between those two paths is real, and Isaiah 7 shows a third: glory kept back from God may eventually be taken away. Ezra's example teaches believers to grieve over sin honestly before the Lord rather than wait for discipline to strip away what they will not surrender.

Leviticus 13 adds a searching truth. The priest looked at the hair because the hair revealed what lay beneath. Our outward lives tend to show, sooner or later, what is true underneath. That is sobering, and it is also merciful. God examines deeply because He intends to heal deeply.

Judges 16:22 belongs to anyone who has wrecked their own spiritual life. Samson sat in a prison he had walked into himself. No prophet came and no rescue arrived, yet his hair began to grow again. God's restoring work often starts quietly, before anyone sees it, in the very place of consequences. The way back for Samson was the same as for us: he turned to the Lord and prayed, "remember me" (Judges 16:28).

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Genesis 25:25. Esau was born red and "like a hairy garment all over," in contrast to Jacob, who later calls himself "a smooth-skinned man" (Genesis 27:11). Many readers hear a wordplay between this hair and Seir, the region that became Edom's home. The verse describes only his appearance at birth. His later choice to trade away his birthright is a separate matter, and Genesis records it on its own terms: "Thus Esau despised his birthright" (Genesis 25:34).

Psalm 68:21. "But God will wound the head of His enemies, The hairy scalp of the one who still goes on in his trespasses." The full head of hair pictures the defiant vigor of those who persist in sin. God's judgment strikes the very place where their pride is displayed.

Song of Solomon 4:1; 6:5. "Your hair is like a flock of goats, Going down from Mount Gilead." Dark goats streaming down a hillside picture abundance and living movement. Hair here is simply a gift to be delighted in, which reminds readers that God made beauty and called it good.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H8165 (Seir), wordplay connection: the name of Esau's territory sounds like sêʻâr. Genesis 25:25 and Genesis 36:8 bring the hairy Esau and Mount Seir together. This is a recognized pun, not a demonstrated shared derivation.
