LIP, LANGUAGE, EDGE, BANK - (sâphâh, Strong's H8193)

Summary

The Hebrew word sâphâh means "lip," and in Scripture it reaches outward from there. It names the lips of the body, the speech and language that come from the lips, and the edge of almost anything: the bank of a river, the shore of a sea, the brim of a basin, or the hem of a curtain or garment. English translations use many different words for it, so readers rarely notice that "language" at Babel, "lips" in Proverbs, and "bank" along Ezekiel's river all translate the same Hebrew word.

That single picture of an edge runs through some of Scripture's most important scenes. Humanity's "one language" at Babel becomes a tool of pride, and God confuses the language and scatters the people. Through Zephaniah He later promises to "restore to the peoples a pure language" so that they may call on His name together (Zephaniah 3:9). Moses calls himself a man of "uncircumcised lips," doubting his own speech, and in a separate scene Isaiah cries out over "unclean lips" until God touches them with fire from the altar. Proverbs treats the lips as the place where a person's heart shows itself. The lips are the edge of the inner life, where what fills the heart crosses into the world. God claims that edge, cleanses it, and turns it into a place of praise.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Sâphâh is a common Old Testament word, found from Genesis to Malachi. More than a quarter of its uses fall in Proverbs, with many more in Psalms and Job, where it almost always means "lips." In the books of Moses, Joshua, and Kings it more often marks a physical boundary: the bank of the Nile or the Jordan, the shore of the Red Sea, the edge of a tabernacle curtain, the brim of Solomon's great bronze basin. The Prophets use both kinds of meaning. The image behind every use is the outer rim of something, the place where one thing ends and another begins.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Lip or lips. This is the part of the body used for speaking, kissing, or showing scorn, as in the mocking crowd of Psalm 22:7.

2. Speech or language. By a natural extension, the lip stands for what it produces: a language, a manner of speaking, or plain talk. Genesis 11:1 says the whole earth had "one language," which reads literally as one lip.

3. Edge, shore, bank, brim, or binding. The word also names the border of a body of water or of a made object. Examples include the river's bank (Exodus 2:3), the seashore (Exodus 14:30), and the woven binding around the neck of the high priest's robe (Exodus 28:32).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When the lips belong to people, Scripture rarely treats them as neutral. Lips flatter (Psalm 12:2), lie (Proverbs 12:22), keep knowledge (Malachi 2:7), and praise God (Psalm 63:3). Sometimes they almost act on their own. Eliphaz tells Job, "your own lips testify against you" (Job 15:6), and the psalmist remembers promises "which my lips have uttered... when I was in trouble" (Psalm 66:14).

The word often appears beside "mouth" or "tongue." When it does, the lips usually stand as the outer gate of speech. Psalm 141:3 prays, "Keep watch over the door of my lips."

In its geographic sense, sâphâh forms a familiar biblical phrase: "the sand which is on the seashore." God first uses it in His promise to Abraham (Genesis 22:17). Later the same words describe the armies that come against Israel (Joshua 11:4; Judges 7:12; 1 Samuel 13:5). God let Israel's enemies match the size of His own promise, and He still delivered His people, as Gideon learned with only three hundred men. The image turns inward in 1 Kings 4:29, where God gives Solomon "largeness of heart like the sand on the seashore." The shore marks the place where human counting gives up.

4. Theological Significance

Through this word Scripture teaches that speech always reveals who rules a person. The wicked in Psalm 12:4 say it plainly: "Our lips are our own; Who is lord over us?" Claiming ownership of the lips amounts to rejecting God's authority. The opposite prayer comes from a broken sinner in Psalm 51:15: "O Lord, open my lips, And my mouth shall show forth Your praise." The same organ that declares independence from God can be handed back to Him.

Proverbs fills in this teaching day by day. "In the multitude of words sin is not lacking, But he who restrains his lips is wise" (Proverbs 10:19). "The lips of the righteous feed many" (Proverbs 10:21), which turns the lips' normal job upside down: they produce food for others instead of only taking it in. Lying lips are hateful to the Lord (Proverbs 12:22), while truthful lips last forever (Proverbs 12:19). Ordinary conversation is part of the fear of the Lord.

God also works at physical edges. He preserves baby Moses "in the reeds by the river's bank" (Exodus 2:3). He shows Israel His finished victory when they see "the Egyptians dead on the seashore" (Exodus 14:30). The edge of the water, where people are most exposed, is often where God makes His saving power visible.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Babel and the pure language (Genesis 11:1–9; Zephaniah 3:9). At Babel, the human race had "one language and one speech" (Genesis 11:1). The people used that unity to build a tower and make a name for themselves, and God confused their language and scattered them across the earth (Genesis 11:7–9). Zephaniah uses the same word for his promise, and setting the two passages side by side makes a fruitful biblical comparison: "For then I will restore to the peoples a pure language, That they all may call on the name of the LORD, To serve Him with one accord." At Babel the people were united around their own name. In Zephaniah they are united around calling on the Lord's name. God did not oppose human unity as such. He broke a unity built on pride so that He could later create a unity built on worship. The new language is called "pure," which means its purity comes from the One it serves, not from human achievement.

Uncircumcised and unclean lips (Exodus 6:12, 30; Isaiah 6:5–7). Moses protests, "How then shall Pharaoh heed me, for I am of uncircumcised lips?" (Exodus 6:12). This is Moses' own description of his speech, and it echoes his earlier objection that he was "slow of speech and slow of tongue" (Exodus 4:10). Circumcision was the sign that marked a man as belonging to God's people, and an "uncircumcised" part of the body was one not yet fit for its purpose. Moses is saying that his speech is inadequate for the task, not confessing ritual uncleanness. God's answer came earlier, in His promise to "be with your mouth and teach you what you shall say" (Exodus 4:12), and He sent Moses anyway, with Aaron to speak alongside him. Isaiah's experience is a different episode. When he sees the King, he does not first confess sins of the hand. He cries, "I am a man of unclean lips, And I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5). Then a seraph takes a burning coal from the altar, and the prophet hears: "Behold, this has touched your lips; Your iniquity is taken away, And your sin purged" (Isaiah 6:7). God places the cleansing exactly where Isaiah felt the problem, and the cleansing comes from the altar, the place of sacrifice. Only afterward does Isaiah hear, "Whom shall I send?" In both scenes God takes the initiative. He does not wait for His servants to make their own lips adequate.

Sacrifices of the lips (Hosea 14:2; Psalm 63:3). Hosea calls Israel back with these words: "Take words with you, And return to the LORD... For we will offer the sacrifices of our lips." The Hebrew can be rendered literally as the calves of our lips. Israel had offered many animals while its heart wandered, so the prophet tells the people to bring honest confession and praise as their offering. Psalm 63:3 shows what such an offering sounds like: "Because Your lovingkindness is better than life, My lips shall praise You." When this happens, the lips become a kind of altar.

The riverbank of life (Ezekiel 47:6–12). Ezekiel's guide leads him back "to the bank of the river" flowing from the temple. There he sees trees growing "along the bank of the river, on this side and that," whose "fruit will not fail" and with "leaves for medicine," "because their water flows from the sanctuary" (Ezekiel 47:12). This scene makes a meaningful contrast with Pharaoh's dream, where gaunt cows stand "on the bank of the river" Nile as a sign of coming famine (Genesis 41:3). The biblical writers do not draw the contrast themselves, but it is fitting. The world's greatest river produced a warning of hunger, while God's river produces food and healing in every season.

Edges made whole (Exodus 26:4–6; 28:32). God told Moses to put loops on "the edge of the curtain" and join the curtains with gold clasps "so that it may be one tabernacle." The high priest's robe was to have "a woven binding all around its opening... so that it does not tear." In God's house, edges were not frayed margins left to chance. They were deliberately reinforced so that they would join and hold. God's dwelling was one whole, and His priest's garment would not tear.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Read alongside Babel and Zephaniah, the story moves toward Pentecost. In Acts 2, devout Jews "from every nation under heaven" hear the wonderful works of God declared in their own languages. The Spirit does not erase the many languages that arose when God confused the speech at Babel. He gathers people from all of them around one message about Jesus. Acts does not quote Zephaniah or announce his promise fulfilled, so the link is a biblical comparison rather than a stated fulfillment. Even so, it is fitting: Zephaniah's hope of peoples who "call on the name of the LORD" begins to take shape wherever people from every language call on the Lord through Christ. Paul uses the same family of words differently. He applies Isaiah 28:11, God's warning that He would speak "with stammering lips and another tongue," to the gift of tongues in the church, and he draws his own point from it: tongues are "a sign, not to those who believe but to unbelievers" (1 Corinthians 14:21–22).

Jesus is the One whose lips were never unclean. Psalm 45:2 says grace is poured on the king's lips, and Hebrews 1:8–9 applies that psalm to the Son. Luke records that people marveled at the gracious words coming from Jesus' mouth (Luke 4:22). Isaiah 11:4 says the coming Branch of Jesse will strike the wicked "with the breath of His lips." The same lips that speak grace also carry judgment.

The New Testament also takes up Hosea's sacrifice. "Therefore by Him let us continually offer the sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of our lips, giving thanks to His name" (Hebrews 13:15). The phrase "by Him" matters. Isaiah's lips were cleansed by a coal from the altar, and ours are made clean through Christ's own sacrifice. Ezekiel's riverbank appears again in Revelation 22:1–2, where the tree of life grows on either side of the river and its leaves are "for the healing of the nations."

7. Spirit & Life

Jesus said, "out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaks" (Luke 6:45). The meaning of sâphâh as an "edge" helps explain why. The lips are the border of the heart, the place where hidden things become public. For this reason Isaiah did not simply decide to speak better. He needed fire from the altar. Trying to change your speech without dealing with your heart is like painting a riverbank while the river stays poisoned.

Psalm 12 and Psalm 51 present the choice every believer faces at that border. One says, "Our lips are our own." The other prays, "O Lord, open my lips." The first sounds like freedom but ends in what Proverbs 13:3 calls destruction for the one who "opens wide his lips." The second gives control of the lips to God, and He opens them for praise. Psalm 141:3 turns this into a daily prayer, asking the Lord Himself to guard "the door of my lips."

Clean lips are also generous. "The lips of the righteous feed many." A word of truth, encouragement, or wise warning can nourish someone else's soul. Once God cleanses and opens a person's lips, that person can offer praise that pleases Him and words that feed the people around them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 30:6, 8 — A vow made with the lips is binding. Words spoken before God carry real weight.

  • 1 Samuel 1:13 — Hannah's lips moved silently while she prayed from her heart, and Eli mistook her earnest prayer for drunkenness.

  • Job 2:10 — After losing everything, "Job did not sin with his lips," showing that faithfulness under suffering includes what we say.

  • Psalm 89:34 — God says He will not alter what has gone out of His lips. His own word is the model of faithful speech.

  • Isaiah 29:13 — God rebukes people who honor Him with their lips while their hearts are far away. Jesus quotes this verse in Matthew 15:8.

  • Song of Solomon 5:13 — The beloved's lips are "lilies, Dripping liquid myrrh," a picture of speech and love that are sweet and pure, unlike the deceptive honey of the adulteress in Proverbs 5:3.

  • Malachi 2:6–7 — Of the true priest it was said, "injustice was not found on his lips," and the priest's lips were to keep knowledge while the people sought the law from his mouth.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3956 (lâshôn, "tongue") is a distinct word paired with lips in Psalm 12:3–4, where "flattering lips" and "the tongue that speaks proud things" appear together.

  • H6310 (peh, "mouth") is a distinct word paired with lips in Psalm 51:15, Psalm 141:3, and Job 15:6. The mouth is the place of speech, and the lips are its door.

  • G5491 (cheilos, "lip") is the Greek word for lip. It is a New Testament equivalent, not a Hebrew relative, and appears in "the fruit of our lips" (Hebrews 13:15).

10. Other Relevant Insights

The brim of Solomon's bronze Sea, where the priests washed, was "shaped like the brim of a cup, like a lily blossom" (1 Kings 7:26; 2 Chronicles 4:5). The Song of Solomon describes the beloved's lips as "lilies" using the same Hebrew word. The connection between these two passages is a literary echo, not a stated teaching, but it fits a real pattern in Scripture: the place of cleansing leads to the place of communion with God.
