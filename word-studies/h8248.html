WATER, GIVE DRINK, BUTLER - (shâqâh, Strong's H8248)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shâqâh means to water or to give drink. It describes one party supplying water or another drink to someone or something else. A mist waters the ground in Eden, Rebekah draws water for a stranger's camels, and Moses waters the flock of the seven daughters of the priest of Midian. The same word supplies the title of Pharaoh's "butler," the official whose work was to give the king his drink. Scripture also uses it in darker ways. A priest makes a suspected wife drink bitter water, the wicked press wine on others to shame them, and God hands the nations "this wine cup of fury."

The word raises a question that runs through the whole Bible: who is giving drink, and what is in the cup? God is the great Giver. He waters His creation and gives His people "drink from the river of Your pleasures" (Psalm 36:8). Yet the One who offers life also holds the cup of judgment. That tension comes to rest at the cross. Psalm 69:21 says, "for my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink"; on the cross Jesus said, "I thirst!" and received sour wine, and earlier He had prayed in Gethsemane about the cup He was about to drink. Because Christ drank what we deserved, He now gives living water. His people, in turn, are called to give drink to others, including their enemies.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâqâh is a Hebrew verb at home in a dry land, where water had to be drawn, carried, and poured out by someone. It occurs most often in Genesis, especially around wells and in the Joseph story. It also appears in the Law, the Psalms, and the prophets, above all Jeremiah. Hebrew has a separate common verb for drinking itself (shâthâh). Shâqâh looks at the person who gives the drink, so it often pictures a giver and a receiver. Deuteronomy 11:10 draws a contrast with Egypt, where gardens were "watered" by human labor. The Promised Land, by contrast, drinks rain from heaven (Deuteronomy 11:11).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To water land or plants, as with the mist and the river in Eden (Genesis 2:6, 10), Egypt's garden plots (Deuteronomy 11:10), or Solomon's pools (Ecclesiastes 2:6).

2. To give drink to animals or people, as an act of care or hospitality (Genesis 24:18–19; Exodus 2:17).

3. To make someone drink, where the drink is imposed on the person, often to test, harm, or judge (Numbers 5:24; Jeremiah 25:15; Habakkuk 2:15).

4. In the related Hebrew title mashqeh (also indexed as Strong's H4945), the one who gives drink: the royal butler or cupbearer (Genesis 40:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word shifts with the giver and with what is given. When God is the subject, shâqâh describes rain on the hills (Psalm 104:13), streams for wild donkeys (Psalm 104:11), and water from the rock for a grumbling nation (Numbers 20:8; Psalm 78:15). When people are the subject, the word reveals their character. They may give drink to the weary or withhold it (Job 22:7), refresh captives (2 Chronicles 28:15), or use drink to deceive. Lot's daughters "made their father drink wine" (Genesis 19:32–35), and Jael gave Sisera milk before she killed him (Judges 4:19).

The word's grammar matters here in a plain way. The verb describes causing another to drink, so it puts the giver in the foreground. Recipients may still choose, refuse or ask, but the word draws attention to who supplies the drink and what is in the cup. That is why it fits both tender care and forced judgment. Psalm 80:5 even says God gave His people "tears to drink."

4. Theological Significance

The first uses of shâqâh reveal God as the source of life for His world. Before anyone plowed or planted, a mist "watered the whole face of the ground" (Genesis 2:6), and a river flowed out of Eden to water the garden (Genesis 2:10). God's dwelling place is the fountainhead, and life flows outward from it. Later Scripture returns to this picture again and again. Lot looked at the Jordan plain and saw it was "well watered everywhere... like the garden of the LORD" (Genesis 13:10). The ideal of a land watered by God never left Israel's imagination.

Because God is the true Source, seeking water elsewhere is a form of betrayal. In Ezekiel's parable, the vine of Judah "bent its roots" toward the eagle of Egypt "that he might water it" (Ezekiel 17:7). It left the terrace where God had planted it. Jeremiah asks the same question with a different verb: "why take the road to Egypt, To drink the waters of Sihor?" (Jeremiah 2:18).

The God who waters also judges, and He sometimes uses the language of watering to do it. Against Pharaoh, whose land lived by the Nile, God says, "I will also water the land with the flow of your blood, Even to the mountains" (Ezekiel 32:6). The word for life-giving irrigation becomes the word for catastrophe.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Character at the well (Genesis 24; Exodus 2). Abraham's servant asked God for a sign. The right young woman would offer, "Drink, and I will also give your camels a drink" (Genesis 24:14). Ten thirsty camels after a desert journey needed a great deal of water, and Rebekah ran back and forth to the well until they were satisfied (Genesis 24:19–20). God answered the servant's prayer through her generous, tiring work. In Exodus 2, a fugitive Moses sat by a well in Midian. When shepherds drove off the daughters of the priest of Midian, whose father is named Reuel in Exodus 2:18, he "helped them, and watered their flock" (Exodus 2:17). The man who would shepherd Israel out of Egypt first appears in Midian watering sheep for strangers. In both stories, a humble act of giving drink marks the person God is about to use.

The bitter water (Numbers 5:11–31). When a husband suspected unfaithfulness that no one could prove, the priest wrote curses on a scroll and washed them into holy water. Then "he shall make the woman drink the bitter water that brings a curse" (Numbers 5:24). The outcome rested with God alone. A guilty wife would suffer, and an innocent one would be cleared and able to bear children (Numbers 5:27–28). This rite pictured God seeing what is hidden in a marriage, and the prophets later described Israel as an unfaithful wife. The rite raised a question it could not answer: what if the whole people deserved the bitter cup?

The cup of fury (Jeremiah 8:14; 25:15–17). Jeremiah hears Judah confess, "the LORD our God has... given us water of gall to drink, Because we have sinned against the LORD" (Jeremiah 8:14). Then God commands the prophet, "Take this wine cup of fury from My hand, and cause all the nations... to drink it" (Jeremiah 25:15). The picture is of a host who offers no pleasant hospitality. He hands rebels the consequence of their rebellion and requires them to drink it. Judgment comes personally from God's own hand.

The river of pleasures (Psalm 36:8; Isaiah 43:20; Joel 3:18). David sees what God's heart desires for His people: "They are abundantly satisfied with the fullness of Your house, And You give them drink from the river of Your pleasures." The river flows from God's own house, because "with You is the fountain of life" (Psalm 36:9). Isaiah promises rivers in the desert "To give drink to My people, My chosen" (Isaiah 43:20). Joel completes the picture of Eden restored: "A fountain shall flow from the house of the LORD And water the Valley of Acacias" (Joel 3:18). The first river watered a garden, and the last waters a dry valley. Both flow from where God dwells.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

These two lines, the river and the cup, meet in Jesus. Psalm 69 is the cry of a righteous man rejected by his own people: "for my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink" (Psalm 69:21). John states plainly that Jesus, "that the Scripture might be fulfilled, said, 'I thirst!'" (John 19:28). A sponge filled with sour wine was then put to His mouth, and He "received the sour wine" (John 19:29–30; see Matthew 27:34, 48). The Author of life expressed His thirst and was given sour wine.

Behind that physical cup stood another. In Gethsemane Jesus prayed, "O My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me" (Matthew 26:39). The Gospels write in Greek, so the same Hebrew word does not occur there. Even so, the prophets had already filled "the cup" with meaning, as the cup of fury from God's hand. Jesus drank what Jeremiah said the nations must drink, and what Revelation 14:10 warns the unrepentant will still drink. The bitter water of Numbers 5 offers an illustration, though not a prophecy of Christ: it is as if the faithful Bridegroom took the bitter cup His unfaithful bride deserved. What the New Testament states directly is that Christ "redeemed us from the curse of the law" (Galatians 3:13).

Having drunk the cup, Christ gives drink. He promised, "whoever drinks of the water that I shall give him will never thirst" (John 4:14), and He spoke of rivers of living water flowing from those who believe, by the Spirit (John 7:37–39). The story ends where it began, with "a pure river of water of life... proceeding from the throne of God and of the Lamb" (Revelation 22:1). These texts about Jesus giving living water are the heart of the matter. Pharaoh's butler illustrates the related title mashqeh, the king's drink-giver (Genesis 40:13). Some readers also notice that he was restored to the king's side on the third day (Genesis 40:20–21) and take it as a faint illustration of the risen Christ, but that is an optional comparison, not a prophecy.

7. Spirit & Life

We never drink from God's river alone. Once we have received from Him, we begin to give to others, and Scripture cares about what we hand them. Amos condemned Israel: "you gave the Nazirites wine to drink" (Amos 2:12). These were people who had vowed to "separate himself to the LORD" by refusing wine (Numbers 6:2–3). Habakkuk pronounced woe on the one "who gives drink to his neighbor... That you may look on his nakedness" (Habakkuk 2:15). A drink can be used as a weapon to break down another person's devotion or to expose someone's weakness. Believers should ask honestly whether what they offer others makes it harder to stay faithful to God.

God calls His people to the opposite. Proverbs 25:21 says, "if he is thirsty, give him water to drink." Paul repeats this instruction in Romans 12:20 and makes it part of how believers "overcome evil with good" (Romans 12:21). That is what Christ did for us. While we were His enemies, He received the sour wine and drank the cup of wrath, and He gave us living water in return. The servant-heart shown by Rebekah and Moses at the well is this same grace in everyday form: tiring work done to refresh people who may never repay it. When we look for sustenance from God's house rather than from Egypt, we have water to share.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 21:19. God opened Hagar's eyes to a well, and she gave her dying son a drink. God provides water for the outcast.

  • Exodus 32:20. Moses ground the golden calf to powder and made Israel drink it. The people had to swallow the consequences of their idolatry.

  • Numbers 20:8. God told Moses to bring water from the rock and give drink to the people and their animals, even while the people complained against Him.

  • Isaiah 27:3. The LORD says He keeps His vineyard and waters it every moment, a picture of His constant watchfulness over His people.

  • Job 22:7. Eliphaz charged Job with failing to give the weary water to drink. In this setting, refusing water is a moral failure.

  • Psalm 60:3. The psalmist says God made His people drink the wine of confusion, so that judgment left them staggering.
