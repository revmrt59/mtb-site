SHARON - (Shârôwn, Strong's H8289)

Summary

Sharon is a broad, level region in the land of Israel. In most passages it means the fertile coastal plain that runs along the Mediterranean from around Joppa north toward Mount Carmel. The name is generally understood to come from a Hebrew root meaning to be level or straight, so Sharon most likely means the plain or the level land. Scripture also mentions "the common-lands of Sharon" east of the Jordan in the territory of Gad (1 Chronicles 5:16). That second Sharon appears to be a separate place, and its location is unknown. Joshua's list of defeated kings includes "the king of Lasharon" (Joshua 12:18), which may point to a town in or near the plain, though the verse does not say where it lay or that the whole plain came under Israel's control at that time. Later the plain served as pastureland for King David's royal herds (1 Chronicles 27:29). Sharon was good grazing country, and Israel knew it as a place of wildflowers and plenty.

That richness is why the poets and prophets used Sharon to describe beauty and blessing. The bride in the Song of Solomon calls herself "the rose of Sharon" (Song of Songs 2:1). Isaiah pictures this fertile plain withering into wilderness under judgment (Isaiah 33:9). He then promises that the desert will be given "the excellence of Carmel and Sharon" and that "They shall see the glory of the LORD" (Isaiah 35:2). In that promise, Sharon's beauty is what the barren land receives. He also says Sharon will become "a fold of flocks" for "My people who have sought Me" (Isaiah 65:10). Centuries later, Luke records that after Peter healed a paralyzed man at Lydda, "all who dwelt at Lydda and Sharon saw him and turned to the Lord" (Acts 9:35). Luke does not cite Isaiah here, yet the comparison is rich. The plain whose excellence Isaiah used to picture God's renewal of the desert saw the risen Christ at work through His apostle, as a paralyzed man stood up, the kind of sign Isaiah promised when God comes to save (Isaiah 35:4–6).

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The Hebrew name Sharon is widely understood to come from a root meaning to be straight, even, or level. On that understanding, Sharon means the plain or the level place. This meaning is a well-supported derivation rather than something the Bible itself explains. Scripture never comments on the name or makes a wordplay on it. The name does fit the land. Hebrew usually speaks of the region as the Sharon, much as English speaks of the Plains. That way of speaking suits a name describing open, flat country, though by itself it cannot prove how Israelites heard the name.

The Strong's entry H8289 covers more than one place.

  • The coastal plain of Sharon. This is the Sharon of David's herds, the Song of Solomon, and Isaiah. It lies between the Mediterranean Sea on the west and the hills of Samaria on the east, running roughly from Joppa in the south to Mount Carmel in the north. In biblical times it held oak woodland, marshy ground, wildflowers, and rich grazing land. That landscape suited flocks and herds better than large cities.

  • The Sharon of the Gadites. First Chronicles 5:16 places "the common-lands of Sharon" among Gad's holdings east of the Jordan, alongside Gilead and Bashan. Gad's territory lay far from the coastal plain, so this was almost certainly a different level pastureland in the Transjordan. Its exact location is unknown. The verse may also show that Israelites could use the name for open pasture more generally.

  • Lasharon (Joshua 12:18). The NKJV lists "the king of Aphek, one; the king of Lasharon, one" among the kings Joshua defeated. "Lasharon" contains the name Sharon with a short prefix. It may be the name of a Canaanite town in or near the plain, or the verse may originally have described one king, Aphek of the Sharon. The text does not settle the question, and the NKJV treats the two as separate kings.

First Chronicles 27:29 also gives the related family or regional description "Shitrai the Sharonite." In the New Testament, the Greek form Saron (indexed separately as G4565) names the coastal plain in Acts 9:35. That Greek word is a related name, not one of the seven Hebrew occurrences counted under H8289.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Conquest. Sharon may first appear in the list of kings Joshua defeated west of the Jordan, if "the king of Lasharon" ruled a town in or near the plain (Joshua 12:18). The list records God's gift of the land He had promised to Abraham's descendants, but this entry names a defeated king, not the taking of the whole coastal plain. The coastal regions remained contested for generations, first by Canaanites and later by Philistines.

Pasturelands east and west. The Chronicler's record of Gad says the Gadites lived "in Gilead, in Bashan and in its villages, and in all the common-lands of Sharon within their borders" (1 Chronicles 5:16). The next verse says these family records were registered in the days of Jotham king of Judah and Jeroboam king of Israel (1 Chronicles 5:17). The picture is of a herding tribe settled on open grazing land suited to its livestock.

David's royal herds. Under David, the coastal plain served the king directly. Among the officials who managed the royal property, "Shitrai the Sharonite was over the herds that fed in Sharon, and Shaphat the son of Adlai was over the herds that were in the valleys" (1 Chronicles 27:29). The detail is plain administrative record, but it tells us something. By David's day, the plain was secure enough to support the king's cattle, and the man in charge came from the region itself. Sharon had become part of the settled wealth of God's kingdom in Israel.

The rose of Sharon. In the Song of Solomon, the bride says, "I am the rose of Sharon, And the lily of the valleys" (Song of Songs 2:1). The exact flower is uncertain. Suggestions include a crocus, narcissus, or lily rather than a garden rose. Whatever the flower was, it grew wild on the open plain. The bride is not comparing herself to a rare, cultivated bloom. She chooses a common wildflower. Her beloved answers in the next verse that she is like a lily among thorns compared with the other young women (Song of Songs 2:2). What she offers modestly, he treats as precious.

Isaiah: withering and renewal. Isaiah returns to Sharon three times. In an oracle from the time of Assyrian threat, the land itself mourns. Lebanon is put to shame, Sharon becomes like a wilderness, and Bashan and Carmel lose their foliage (Isaiah 33:9). Isaiah chose places known for richness so that their ruin would show how complete the judgment was. If Sharon could wither, no place was safe. The same chapter then announces that the LORD Himself will rise to act (Isaiah 33:10).

Two chapters later the picture reverses. "The wilderness and the wasteland shall be glad for them, And the desert shall rejoice and blossom as the rose" (Isaiah 35:1). The desert "shall blossom abundantly and rejoice, Even with joy and singing. The glory of Lebanon shall be given to it, The excellence of Carmel and Sharon. They shall see the glory of the LORD, The excellency of our God" (Isaiah 35:2). Sharon is no longer the place that withers. Its excellence is now given to the desert. Isaiah's final mention places Sharon in his vision of the new heavens and new earth: "Sharon shall be a fold of flocks, And the Valley of Achor a place for herds to lie down, For My people who have sought Me" (Isaiah 65:10).

The gospel reaches the plain. In Acts 9, Peter travels to Lydda, a town near the southern end of the plain. There he finds Aeneas, who had been paralyzed and bedridden for eight years. Peter tells him that Jesus the Christ heals him, and Aeneas gets up at once (Acts 9:32–34). Luke then writes, "So all who dwelt at Lydda and Sharon saw him and turned to the Lord" (Acts 9:35). The story moves on to nearby Joppa, where Tabitha is raised (Acts 9:36–43). From there it moves to Caesarea, a city on the same coast, where the gospel opens to Cornelius and the Gentiles (Acts 10).

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states that Sharon was part of the land Israel received, a place of pasture, and a place God would one day restore for His seeking people. Some further points are drawn from how the books use Sharon rather than stated outright.

Across Isaiah, Sharon works as a measure of Israel's standing before God. Its richness was never independent of Him. When God judged His people, even this fertile plain became wilderness. This follows the warnings of Deuteronomy that a disobedient people would see their land fail (Deuteronomy 28). When God promised restoration, He spoke of Sharon's excellence spreading even into the desert.

Isaiah 65:10 adds a qualifier that is easy to miss. The renewed Sharon is "for My people who have sought Me." The verses that follow contrast these seekers with those who forsook the LORD (Isaiah 65:11–12). God's restoration of the land is bound to the restoration of a people who turn to Him.

The pairing in Isaiah 65:10 is also telling. Sharon lies on the western coast. The Valley of Achor lies near Jericho in the east, where Achan's sin brought trouble on Israel at the start of the conquest (Joshua 7:24–26). Hosea had already promised that God would make the Valley of Achor "a door of hope" (Hosea 2:15). By naming Sharon and Achor together, Isaiah seems to picture the whole land, from the sea to the Jordan, including the place where Israel's story first went wrong, made peaceful pasture again.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

Isaiah 35 and Acts 9. Luke does not quote Isaiah when he records that Lydda and Sharon "turned to the Lord." The link between the two chapters is a comparison the reader draws, not a fulfillment Luke states, but it is a rich one. In Isaiah 35 the desert receives "the excellence of Carmel and Sharon," and "They shall see the glory of the LORD" (Isaiah 35:2). In the same chapter Isaiah promised that feeble knees would be made firm and the lame would leap like a deer when God came to save (Isaiah 35:3–6). In Acts, a man paralyzed for eight years stands up at the name of Jesus Christ, and the people of Lydda and Sharon see him and turn to the Lord.

The healing at Lydda carries the marks Isaiah described: God coming to save, the lame made whole, and the glory of the LORD seen. Jesus Himself pointed to the same signs from Isaiah as evidence of who He was (Luke 7:22). It is fitting that the plain whose beauty Isaiah used to picture God's renewal of the desert became one of the places where the risen Christ, working through His apostle, made the lame whole. From there the gospel moved up that same coast to Caesarea and the Gentiles.

Isaiah 35:1 and the "rose." The NKJV word "rose" in Isaiah 35:1, the desert that will "blossom as the rose," translates the same uncommon Hebrew flower word found in "the rose of Sharon" (Song of Songs 2:1); these are the only two places the word appears. Isaiah's renewed desert blooms with the very flower of Sharon, one verse before Sharon itself is named.

"The Rose of Sharon" and Jesus. Many Christians know the phrase "the Rose of Sharon" as a title for Jesus, from hymns and long devotional use. In Song of Songs 2:1, however, the speaker is the bride, not the bridegroom. The title for Christ is a devotional application, not the verse's direct meaning. A truer line of thought follows the Song's own movement. The bride calls herself a common flower of the field, and her beloved treasures her as a lily among thorns. Paul describes Christ's love for the church as a bridegroom's love that makes His bride radiant (Ephesians 5:25–27). The lowly beauty of Sharon's wildflower fits Christ's people, whom He has loved and made lovely, at least as naturally as it fits Christ.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Sharon was the kind of land a farmer could take for granted. Every year the plain greened, the flowers came up, and the cattle fattened. Isaiah 33 shatters that confidence. The most dependable land in the country could become wilderness when God's people turned from Him. Our own strengths work the same way, including health, gifts, income, and the parts of life that have always "worked." None of them is self-sustaining. They flourish under God's hand and wither without it.

Isaiah does not leave the matter in judgment, though. The God who dried up Sharon also promised to give its excellence to the desert. In that renewal, Isaiah says, "They shall see the glory of the LORD, The excellency of our God" (Isaiah 35:2). The barren places are not beyond Him. Isaiah 65:10 tells us who receives that restored pasture: "My people who have sought Me." God's promise is not a reward for the strong. It is rest for those who turn to Him and seek Him.

The bride's wildflower offers its own encouragement. She does not claim to be a rare garden bloom, only a flower of the open field. That is enough for the one who loves her. God does not need us to be impressive before He delights in us.

Acts 9 shows God's grace reaching real towns on that plain. They were ordinary farming communities, and when they saw a paralyzed man standing because of Jesus, they turned to the Lord. Isaiah had used Sharon's beauty to picture what God would do for barren places, and here, on Sharon itself, that kind of renewal came in the name of Christ. It still comes wherever people see what He has done and turn to Him.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Joshua 12:18 — "The king of Lasharon," listed among the kings Joshua defeated. This may be the earliest mention of Sharon, if Lasharon was a town in or near the plain. The verse records a defeated king, not Israel's control of the whole coastal plain.

  • 1 Chronicles 5:16 — "The common-lands of Sharon" in Gad's territory east of the Jordan. This is a separate Sharon whose location is unknown.

  • 1 Chronicles 27:29 — Shitrai the Sharonite over David's herds "that fed in Sharon." This grounds the coastal plain in its historical role as royal pastureland.

  • Song of Songs 2:1 — "I am the rose of Sharon." The bride compares herself to a wildflower of the plain, which became the source of the familiar devotional phrase.

  • Isaiah 33:9 — Sharon becomes like a wilderness. Its ruin shows how complete God's judgment is.

  • Isaiah 35:1–2 — The desert blossoms and receives "the excellence of Carmel and Sharon," and "They shall see the glory of the LORD." Here Sharon's beauty is given to the wilderness. This is the central promise of restoration.

  • Isaiah 65:10 — Sharon as "a fold of flocks" for "My people who have sought Me." This passage ties the renewed land to the seeking people of God.

  • Acts 9:32–35 — Aeneas is healed, and "all who dwelt at Lydda and Sharon … turned to the Lord." The gospel reaches the plain in a healing that invites comparison with Isaiah 35.
