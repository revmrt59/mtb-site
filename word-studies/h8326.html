NAVEL - (shôrer, Strong's H8326)

Summary

The Hebrew word shôrer means navel, the small mark at the center of the body where every person was once joined to a mother's life. It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 7:2. There a bridegroom praises his bride: "Your navel is a rounded goblet; it lacks no blended beverage. Your waist is a heap of wheat set about with lilies." Because it is used only once, this single verse carries the word's whole biblical weight, and the verse is full of meaning.

The bridegroom's comparisons work on more than one level. A rounded goblet suits the shape of what he praises, and a cup that "lacks no blended beverage" and a heap of harvest wheat also speak of plenty, joy and delight. Grain and wine are the very gifts Scripture names as God's blessings on His people. With these images the Song celebrates married love as God's good gift and honors the body He made. A closely related Hebrew word names the navel cord of the abandoned baby in Ezekiel 16, the infant God rescued and later took as His bride. Together these passages trace a movement that runs through the whole Bible, from helpless dependence to being loved and filled with joy.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shôrer is a Hebrew noun found only in Song of Solomon 7:2. A word used only once is often hard to define, but this one is clear for two reasons. The verse itself is a description of the body, and a closely related Hebrew word, shor (H8270), carries the same meaning elsewhere. In Ezekiel 16:4 that related word is translated "navel cord." In Proverbs 3:8 it stands behind the NKJV's "flesh."

Song of Solomon 7:1–9 belongs to a kind of love poem in which the speaker praises the beloved feature by feature. Here the bridegroom begins with her "feet in sandals" (7:1) and moves upward to her head (7:5). This places the navel near the center of his praise. In poems of this kind the comparisons blend appearance and feeling. They suggest something of the beloved's form while also expressing what she means to the speaker, so the goblet is neither a literal drawing nor only a symbol.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shôrer means the navel, the center of the belly that marks where the umbilical cord once joined a child to its mother. The word has one sense. Ideas of wine, harvest, and abundance come from the metaphors attached to the navel in Song of Solomon 7:2. They are not part of the word's dictionary meaning.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In its single use the word is literal. It names a part of the bride's body, and the bridegroom then pairs it with two comparisons. The navel is "a rounded goblet" that "lacks no blended beverage." Her waist is "a heap of wheat set about with lilies." The rounded goblet suggests the navel's shape, and the phrase "lacks no blended beverage" adds the idea of fullness. In the bridegroom's eyes the cup is well supplied, a picture of the plenty and delight he finds in her.

The related word shor widens the picture. Proverbs 3:8 uses it to speak of bodily health. Ezekiel 16:4 uses it for the cord that ties a newborn to its origin. These passages use a different Strong's number, so they serve as related background. They are not further occurrences of H8326.

4. Theological Significance

The first thing this word shows is that God does not treat the human body as an embarrassment. The Creator made the man and woman, joined them as "one flesh," and recorded that "they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:24–25). Song of Solomon stands in that same light. A husband names his wife's body, part by part, with admiration and without crudeness, and God placed his words in Scripture. Married delight belongs to the life God designed.

The second truth concerns the imagery the bridegroom chooses. Wheat and wine are the staples of God's promised blessing. Moses told Israel that the Lord would bless their grain and new wine if they walked with Him (Deuteronomy 7:13), and the psalmist praises the wine that makes the heart glad (Psalm 104:15). When the bridegroom speaks of his bride in those terms, she becomes to him something like the good land itself, a place of provision, gladness, and rest. Married joy is therefore a small, personal reflection of the generosity God shows His people.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 7:2 — the full cup and the harvest heap. The bridegroom's praise in 7:1–9 reaches its summary in verse 6: "How fair and how pleasant you are, O love, with your delights!" The navel verse explains part of that delight. A goblet exists to be drunk from, and this one is well filled. A heap of wheat meant bread for the year and the end of hunger. The lilies around the heap add beauty to provision. The poem closes with wine again, "the roof of your mouth like the best wine" (7:9), so the cup image is one thread in a larger picture of satisfying love. Proverbs 5 uses the same kind of language when it tells a husband to "rejoice with the wife of your youth" and to "always be enraptured with her love" (Proverbs 5:18–19). The Song's goblet pictures fullness and pleasure; Proverbs supplies the call to keep finding that delight. Together they show that the love God gives within marriage is meant to keep refreshing a husband and wife over many years.

Ezekiel 16:4 — the cord that was never cut. Here God describes Jerusalem's beginnings as an abandoned newborn: "on the day you were born your navel cord was not cut, nor were you washed in water to cleanse you." The uncut cord is the sign that no one cared for this child. In the verses that follow, the Lord passes by, commands her to live, and later spreads His garment over her and takes her as His own (Ezekiel 16:6–8). One chapter moves from a neglected navel cord to a bride. That progression explains why Ezekiel 16 helps us read the Song. The navel is the mark of every person's dependence, and in God's hands dependence becomes the starting point for love.

Proverbs 3:8 — health to the body. The fear of the Lord and turning from evil "will be health to your flesh, And strength to your bones." The NKJV's "flesh" translates the related word for navel, used here to speak of the body and its health. The verse promises well-being to those who fear the LORD; it does not set out a theory about the navel itself. Its value for this study is that the same word family that names the bride's navel in the Song can stand for bodily health in Proverbs.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Scripture's bride imagery does not stay within Song of Solomon. Isaiah promises that as a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so God will rejoice over His people (Isaiah 62:5). Ezekiel 16 tells Israel's history as the story of a castaway infant whom the Lord made His bride. Paul quotes Genesis 2:24 and then says that the "one flesh" of marriage speaks of Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:31–32). The story ends at a wedding feast, the marriage supper of the Lamb, where the bride is clothed in fine linen and those invited are blessed (Revelation 19:7–9).

Song of Solomon 7:2 should first be read as what it is, a husband's praise of his wife. Within that larger story it also gives a glimpse of something greater. The bridegroom's unashamed delight, his language of wine and harvest, and his sense that she lacks nothing all point toward the delight Christ takes in the people He has redeemed. The New Testament does not cite this verse as a prophecy. The link comes from the Bible's consistent pattern of marriage pointing to God and His people. The banquet that begins in a love song reaches its fullness at the Lamb's table.

7. Spirit & Life

Every navel is a reminder that no one gave himself life. Each person began joined to another, receiving nourishment he could not supply. Ezekiel 16 shows that dependence at its most helpless, a child no one would touch. Song of Solomon 7:2 shows the same part of the body seen through love, and now it is a full cup. The two passages use related words and belong to different settings, so this connection is a meditation rather than a doctrine. It is still true to the gospel. Christ finds us like the infant in Ezekiel, gives us life, washes us, and in time we become people whose lives bring refreshment and joy to others. The mark of our need becomes the place where His abundance is seen.

For husbands and wives, the verse also gives permission and direction. God is not uneasy about married delight, and believers do not need to be either. The bridegroom in the Song looks at his wife and speaks of her beauty and the delight he finds in her, and Proverbs 5 calls a husband to keep finding his joy in "the wife of your youth." A marriage grows stronger when spouses say out loud what the other means to them, the way this bridegroom does.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Shôrer occurs only in Song of Solomon 7:2, which is discussed above. The related word shor appears in Proverbs 3:8 ("health to your flesh") and Ezekiel 16:4 ("your navel cord was not cut"), both covered in Section 5.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H8270 (shor), navel or navel cord — a closely related word with the same basic meaning, used in Proverbs 3:8 to speak of bodily health and in Ezekiel 16:4 for a newborn's uncut cord.
