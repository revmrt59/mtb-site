FINE LINEN, MARBLE - (shêsh, Strong's H8336)

Summary

The Hebrew word shêsh names fine white linen. It was a bleached, carefully woven fabric that took great labor to make and was worn in palaces and used in God's sanctuary. The same word is also used for a white stone, which the NKJV translates as "marble." Dictionaries usually treat this as an extended use, though the Bible itself does not explain how one word came to cover both. Most of its uses are in Exodus. There, "fine woven linen" forms the tabernacle's inner curtains, the veil before the Most Holy Place, the walls of the courtyard, and the garments of the priests.

The word's biblical story begins with holiness that keeps people at a distance. The tabernacle's white walls showed Israel that God dwells in a purity no one may casually approach. The story then moves toward a holiness God gives away. He clothes priests in this linen, Pharaoh dresses Joseph in it when he is raised from prison, and in Ezekiel 16 God first commands an abandoned, bloodied child to live. Later, when she has grown and He has entered a covenant with her, He washes her, anoints her, and clothes her in fine linen as His bride. Revelation completes the picture: the Bride of the Lamb is "granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright." Tyre turns the same costly fabric into sails for its own glory. Its use stands as a warning that God's gifts can be turned into monuments to human pride.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, fine white linen was a luxury. Flax had to be grown, processed, spun into fine thread, woven tightly, and bleached until it shone. Egypt was famous for it, and Ezekiel 27:7 mentions "fine embroidered linen from Egypt." Because linen was so expensive, it marked a person or place as honored. It belonged to royal courts, wealthy households, and above all the house of God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shêsh names a material. It does not name the idea of purity. Its whiteness and cost make it a natural carrier of meaning, but the dictionary sense stays concrete.

1. Fine white linen, whether as cloth, thread, or finished garments. Examples include the tabernacle curtains of "fine woven linen" (Exodus 26:1) and Joseph's "garments of fine linen" (Genesis 41:42).

2. White stone, translated "marble." Examples include the "marble pillars" of the Persian palace (Esther 1:6) and the beloved's legs as "pillars of marble" (Song of Solomon 5:15).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Exodus, the word nearly always appears in the phrase "fine woven linen." It is usually listed with "blue, purple, and scarlet thread." The linen is the base fabric, and the colors and designs are worked into it. Outside Exodus, the word appears in settings of honor: a royal appointment (Genesis 41:42), a capable wife's clothing (Proverbs 31:22), a bride's adornment (Ezekiel 16:10, 13), and a merchant city's sails (Ezekiel 27:7).

When the word means stone, the meaning moves from woven brightness to polished strength. In Song of Solomon 5:15, the beloved's legs are "pillars of marble set on bases of fine gold." The picture is gleaming, steady, and immovable. Its beauty comes with strength and stability. The same image of pillars on precious bases recalls the architecture of a royal palace or a sanctuary. Esther 1:6 shows the same stone in the luxury of the Persian court.

4. Theological Significance

Fine linen shows how God's holiness relates to people. In the tabernacle, holiness is seen as a boundary. White walls set apart the place where God dwells, and no one could pass them carelessly. Yet the same fabric is placed on people. God clothes priests in it, Ezekiel's prophecy pictures Him dressing His people in it, and Revelation shows Him granting it to the redeemed. The holiness that once marked the limit of approach becomes the clothing of those God brings near.

This progression also shows that the material is not holy in itself. Tyre's sails were made of the same linen. Its meaning depends on whom it serves. When it is dedicated to God or received from Him, it speaks of glory, beauty, and righteousness. When it is put to the service of pride, it becomes evidence against the one who uses it.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The tabernacle: a white wall and a woven heaven (Exodus 26–28). God's dwelling was built with this linen. The ten inner curtains, the veil before the ark, the screen at the door, and the courtyard hangings were all "fine woven linen" (Exodus 26:1, 31, 36; 27:9, 16, 18). In the dusty camp of Israel, the courtyard stood out as a bright white wall. Exodus does not explain the color, but the white wall gave every tribe a fitting picture: God's presence is pure and set apart, and a person comes near only through the gate He provides.

The linen also served as a background for heavenly images. God commanded cherubim to be woven into the linen curtains and veil "with artistic designs" (Exodus 26:1, 31). The goats' hair and animal skins of the outer coverings carry no such images. Heaven's colors and the cherubim appear only on the linen. Exodus prescribes this design without explaining it. Readers may see in the white background a fitting picture of purity, but the text does not say God chose white as the only proper setting for the cherubim.

The priests wore the same material. Aaron's ephod, breastplate, tunic, and turban were made with fine linen (Exodus 28:5–8, 15, 39). God said these garments were for glory and beauty (Exodus 28:2). The man who served in God's presence was dressed in the fabric of God's house, and so he was marked as belonging to it. Dressing him in a fabric fit for a king showed that serving before the Lord is an honored calling. It also showed that anyone who stands before God must be covered in a righteousness without stain.

Joseph raised from the dungeon (Genesis 41:42). The word first appears at a dramatic reversal in Joseph's life. "He clothed him in garments of fine linen and put a gold chain around his neck." Joseph's story can be traced through his clothing. His brothers stripped him of his special tunic (37:23). He left his garment in the hands of Potiphar's wife and was sent to prison (39:12). Then he was shaved and given a change of clothes to stand before Pharaoh (41:14). Last of all, Pharaoh dressed him in the linen of royal honor. In Egypt, fine linen showed power and rank. In Joseph's story it marks God's vindication of a man who suffered unjustly and kept faith through it.

The outcast clothed as a bride (Ezekiel 16:1–19). This passage is the heart of the word's story. God describes Jerusalem as a newborn thrown into an open field. She was unwashed, unwanted, and lying in her own blood. He passes by and tells her to live. Later He spreads His garment over her and enters a covenant with her. He washes away the blood and anoints her with oil. Then He clothes her in fine linen and silk (16:10). Her clothing is fine linen, and she becomes beautiful and royal (16:13). God then says her beauty was perfect because of the splendor He had placed on her (16:14).

Every part of her linen was a gift. She contributed nothing except her need. The tragedy follows quickly. She trusted in her own beauty, and she took the garments God had given her and used them to clothe idols (16:15–18). The gift was turned against the Giver.

Tyre's sails (Ezekiel 27:7). Eleven chapters later, Ezekiel mourns over another city dressed in linen: "Fine embroidered linen from Egypt was what you spread for your sail." Tyre took a fabric linked to royal honor and sacred service and stretched it out for the wind, to show its wealth on the trade routes of the sea. The lament pictures this proud ship broken and sinking in the depths (27:26–27, 34). Jerusalem received linen as grace and misused it. Tyre bought linen as a display of its own success. Both show what happens when honor is cut off from the God who gives it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Revelation finishes the story Ezekiel began. At the marriage of the Lamb, "to her it was granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright, for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints" (Revelation 19:8). The Greek word is different, but the image is the same. The phrase "it was granted" is the key. Like the bride in Ezekiel, the Bride of Christ does not weave her own wedding garment. Even her righteous acts are clothing she has been given. The armies following Christ are dressed the same way, "in fine linen, white and clean" (19:14).

The tabernacle veil before the Most Holy Place was made of this linen (Exodus 26:31). When Jesus died, Matthew records that "the veil of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom" (Matthew 27:51). That was the temple's veil in Jesus' day, standing where the linen veil once hung in the tabernacle. Hebrews explains that the way into the holy place is now open "through the veil, that is, His flesh" (Hebrews 10:20). The barrier that once kept worshipers out was opened through the body of Christ. Jesus is also the true High Priest (Hebrews 7–8). The linen of Aaron's garments points to a priest who is Himself without stain.

A further connection is less certain but worth noticing. After the crucifixion, Joseph of Arimathea "wrapped it in a clean linen cloth" (Matthew 27:59). The Greek term differs from the priestly vocabulary. Even so, the One who fulfilled the priesthood was laid in the tomb wrapped in clean linen. Like Joseph in Egypt, He came out of the pit vindicated by God, with authority to clothe His people.

7. Spirit & Life

Ezekiel 16 shows what God's grace looks like. He did not find a bride and admire her. He found a dying child and said to her, "Live!" Later, when she had grown, He entered a covenant with her, washed her, anointed her, and dressed her in the fabric of His own house. Every believer stands where that child stood. Life comes first as God's gift, and the cleansing and clothing follow within the relationship He establishes. The white linen of the tabernacle walls once seemed to say keep your distance. In Christ, God takes the same holiness and wraps it around the people He has redeemed.

This changes how a Christian thinks about righteousness and good works. The saints' righteous acts in Revelation 19 are real, but they are worn as a gift. That truth guards against two errors. One is despair, which assumes we have nothing fit to wear before God. The other is pride, which assumes we wove the garment ourselves.

Ezekiel's two chapters give a sober warning to anyone God has blessed. Jerusalem used His gifts to dress her idols, and Tyre turned costly linen into a banner for itself. Gifts, talents, reputation, and success can all become sails for our own name. The better path is the one the tabernacle shows: let the white linen carry the images of heaven, and offer what God gives back to Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 35:25 – Women skilled in the work spun fine linen with their own hands and brought it for the tabernacle. God's dwelling was built partly from the ordinary, patient labor of His people.

  • Exodus 38:23 – Aholiab, a craftsman and weaver, worked in fine linen. The Lord gave skill as well as materials for the building of His house.

  • Exodus 39:27–29 – The tunics, turban, trousers, and sash were made of fine woven linen "as the LORD had commanded Moses." The priests were covered from head to foot.

  • Proverbs 31:22 – The wife of noble character wears fine linen and purple. Her household's well-being and her own dignity show in her clothing.

  • Esther 1:6 – In the Persian palace, the word describes the "marble pillars" and part of the stone pavement. These details display the splendor of a king whose glory would soon prove fragile.
