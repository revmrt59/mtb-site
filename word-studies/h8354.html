DRINK, DRANK, DRINKING - (shâthâh, Strong's H8354)

Summary

The Hebrew verb shâthâh is the Old Testament's ordinary word for to drink. It describes the most basic act of staying alive. Travelers drink at wells, armies drink at streams, families drink at feasts, and thirsty people drink when God provides water in the desert. Because no one can live without drinking, Scripture uses this simple word to show how completely human beings depend on God. Israel in the wilderness had nothing to drink until God made water flow from the rock. The Promised Land itself "drinks water from the rain of heaven" (Deuteronomy 11:11).

The same word carries some of the Bible's most important imagery. Leaders of Israel ate and drank in God's presence on Sinai and were not struck down. Job's friend Eliphaz describes sinful man as one "who drinks iniquity like water," and people show where their loyalty lies by the table they drink from. Above all, the prophets warn of a cup of God's wrath that the guilty must drink to the bottom. In Gethsemane Jesus prayed over that cup and yielded to His Father's will, and at the cross He bore the judgment our sins deserved. Because He did, He can offer the thirsty the water of life without cost.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Shâthâh is a common Hebrew verb used throughout the Old Testament, from Genesis to Zechariah. It belongs to everyday life. People drink water, wine, and milk. Animals drink at troughs. Guests drink at banquets. In a dry land where wells were guarded and rain decided whether a harvest grew, drinking was never a small matter. Whether a person had access to water could decide whether a household survived, whether a journey went safely, or whether a nation was blessed.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shâthâh means to take liquid into the body. Context supplies the rest. The meaning depends on what is drunk, who gives it, and where the drinking takes place.

1. To drink literally, whether water for survival or wine at a meal. For example, Rebekah offers Abraham's servant a drink at the well (Genesis 24:14). In phrases such as "eat and drink," the word can describe feasting or dining. The NKJV renders it "dine" in Esther 7:1.

2. To absorb, used of land soaking up rain: the land "drinks water from the rain of heaven" (Deuteronomy 11:11).

3. To take in or undergo something figuratively, whether a person welcomes it or has it forced on him. The wicked drink "the wine of violence" (Proverbs 4:17), and the nations drink the cup of judgment (Jeremiah 25:16).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The setting decides the tone of this word. At a well, drinking signals hospitality and welcome. At a royal table, it can signal celebration, as in 1 Chronicles 29:22, or carelessness, as with King Elah, who was "drinking himself drunk" while his servant plotted to kill him (1 Kings 16:9). Noah's drinking left him "uncovered in his tent" (Genesis 9:21). Ben-Hadad and his allied kings were "drinking at the command post" just before they were routed (1 Kings 20:12). These narratives repeatedly link a ruler's heavy drinking with lowered watchfulness and sudden loss.

Refusing to drink also carries meaning. The Nazirite set himself apart to the Lord by not drinking wine (Numbers 6:3). The Rechabites refused wine for generations out of obedience to their forefather Jonadab, and God held up their faithfulness as a rebuke to Judah, which would not obey Him (Jeremiah 35:6, 14). Esther asked the Jews to go without food or drink for three days before she approached the king (Esther 4:16). Nineveh's king ordered that no one, not even the animals, should drink while the city sought God's mercy (Jonah 3:7). These are two different kinds of abstaining. The Nazirite and the Rechabites gave up wine, not all drink, as a lasting mark of devotion or obedience. Esther and Nineveh went without all drink for a short time in a crisis, as part of a fast. In both kinds, giving up something people normally rely on shows that seeking God and obeying Him matter more than physical need.

One grammatical feature stands out. In Jeremiah 25:28 and 49:12, Hebrew states the verb twice, which is a standard way of adding emphasis. The NKJV conveys the effect with words such as "certainly" and "surely." When God hands over His cup of judgment, refusing to drink it is not possible.

4. Theological Significance

Drinking exposes dependence. Deuteronomy 11:10–11 contrasts Egypt, which was watered by human labor, with Canaan, which "drinks water from the rain of heaven." God gives drink, and God can also take it away. He turned Egypt's rivers into blood, "that they could not drink" (Psalm 78:44).

What a person drinks also reveals the heart. Eliphaz describes sinful man as one "who drinks iniquity like water" (Job 15:16). For such a person, sin has become as ordinary and necessary as a glass of water. Proverbs 4:17 says the wicked "drink the wine of violence." On the other side, Wisdom invites the simple to come and drink the wine she has mixed (Proverbs 9:5). Proverbs 5:15 pictures faithful marriage as drinking water from your own cistern.

Drinking also marks fellowship, and that fellowship can be with false gods. In Deuteronomy 32:38, God mocks the idols "who... drank the wine of their drink offering." Jeremiah asks why Israel takes the road to Egypt or Assyria "to drink the waters" of their rivers (Jeremiah 2:18). In that verse, a political alliance becomes a drink from a foreign source. Paul states the principle plainly: "You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons" (1 Corinthians 10:21).

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Water from the rock (Exodus 17:1–6; Numbers 20:2–11). Israel's crisis in the wilderness was simple: "there was no water for the people to drink" (Exodus 17:1). The people quarreled with Moses and treated their thirst as proof that God had abandoned them. God answered their complaint with water. He told Moses that He would stand before him on the rock at Horeb, and water came out so the people could drink. The psalmist later remembered that God "split the rocks in the wilderness, and gave them drink in abundance like the depths" (Psalm 78:15). Paul identifies the reality behind the event: "they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them, and that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4).

Eating and drinking before God (Exodus 24:9–11). After the blood of the covenant was sprinkled on the people (24:8), Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy elders went up the mountain. The text says God did not lay His hand on them. They saw God, and they ate and drank. Sinful people normally could not see God and live, yet these men shared a meal in His presence. The sacrifice had made peace, and a meal followed.

The cup that must be drained (Jeremiah 25:15–29; Isaiah 51:17–22). God told Jeremiah to take the wine cup of His fury and make the nations drink it. They would drink, stagger, and fall (25:16, 27). If they refused, they would drink anyway (25:28). Ezekiel told Jerusalem that she would drink her sister Samaria's cup, "the cup of horror and desolation," and would "drink and drain it" (Ezekiel 23:33–34). Even Babylon, the empire that made the nations drunk, was "a golden cup in the LORD's hand." The nations who drank her wine became "deranged" (Jeremiah 51:7). Isaiah 51 adds a note of mercy. Jerusalem had drunk the cup of God's fury to the dregs, but the Lord promised to take it out of her hand so that she would never drink it again (51:17, 22). Isaiah also says where the cup would go: God would put it "into the hand of those who afflict you" (51:23). The cup of judgment did not simply disappear. It passed from God's afflicted people to those who had oppressed them.

Water too costly to drink (2 Samuel 23:15–17). David longed for water from the well of Bethlehem, which was then held by the Philistines. Three of his mighty men broke through the enemy camp to bring it to him. David would not drink it. He poured it out to the Lord and asked whether this was not the blood of men who had risked their lives. A gift bought at that price did not belong to David's thirst. It belonged to God.

A drink at the well (Genesis 24). Abraham's servant asked God for a sign: the right bride for Isaac would answer his request with "Drink, and I will also give your camels a drink" (Genesis 24:14). Rebekah did exactly that, and a marriage began with a drink at a well. Jacob later met Rachel at a well, and Moses met Zipporah at one. Scripture repeatedly places the beginning of a marriage beside water.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The well pattern reaches John 4. A weary Jesus sits at Jacob's well and says, "Give Me a drink." Then He reverses the roles: "If you knew the gift of God... He would have given you living water" (John 4:10). The Gospel does not call this a betrothal scene. Still, Scripture elsewhere calls Christ the Bridegroom, so the echo of Genesis is fitting. At the well, He offers a drink that ends thirst: "whoever drinks of the water that I shall give him will never thirst" (4:14). Later He calls out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink" (John 7:37). The rock in the wilderness that gave Israel water has now come and speaks for Himself.

Jesus could offer living water because He accepted the other cup. In Gethsemane He prayed that, if possible, the cup might pass from Him, yet He yielded to His Father's will (Matthew 26:39). At His arrest He said, "Shall I not drink the cup which My Father has given Me?" (John 18:11). The Gospels do not explain the cup at that point, but the prophets' cup of God's fury in Jeremiah and Isaiah is the background that gives His words their weight. Revelation uses the same picture for the final judgment on those who worship the beast, who "shall also drink of the wine of the wrath of God, which is poured out full strength" (Revelation 14:10). The prayer in the garden was Jesus' surrender. The bearing of judgment took place at the cross, where He "Himself bore our sins in His own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24). At the place of crucifixion He refused the wine mixed with gall (Matthew 27:34). Unlike the kings who drank themselves into ruin, He met that judgment fully awake.

Because Jesus bore that judgment, the meal on Sinai becomes an invitation for all His people. In John 6:54 He says, "Whoever eats My flesh and drinks My blood has eternal life." The story moves toward "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9). It also completes the prophets' hope of restoration. Amos promised that the exiles would "plant vineyards and drink wine from them" (Amos 9:14), and Isaiah said God's servants would drink while the rebellious went thirsty (Isaiah 65:13). The final promise is wide open: "I will give of the fountain of the water of life freely to him who thirsts" (Revelation 21:6).

7. Spirit & Life

Egypt's fields were watered by human labor, "by foot, as a vegetable garden," but the land of promise "drinks water from the rain of heaven" (Deuteronomy 11:10–11). Its harvests depended on the rain God sent. The life of faith works the same way. Hebrews 6:7 picks up the image: the earth "which drinks in the rain that often comes upon it" and bears useful fruit "receives blessing from God." A believer cannot store up last year's grace. We live by receiving again and again from Christ, who says, "Come to Me and drink."

The question of what we drink is personal. A person can grow so used to iniquity that it goes down "like water," or can drink from springs that cannot satisfy. Jesus' words "whoever drinks of this water will thirst again" describe every substitute for Him. The cure is to drink from the Lord's cup instead of from the cups of the world.

David's poured-out water still teaches. Some things that come to us at great cost, such as a rescued life, an answered prayer, or the sacrifice of others, are too holy to spend on ourselves. The right response is to give them back to God in worship. The cross is the greatest example. Christ's blood was not poured out for our private satisfaction. It was given so that we would belong wholly to Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 34:28 — Moses stayed with the Lord forty days and nights, neither eating bread nor drinking water. God sustained him directly in His presence.

  • Ruth 2:9 — Boaz told Ruth to drink from the water his young men had drawn. A foreign widow received a share of the household's provision, a small sign of the welcome he would later give her.

  • 1 Kings 19:6, 8 — Elijah, worn out and in despair, ate and drank what the angel provided. He went in the strength of that food forty days to Horeb. God's care began with a drink for an exhausted prophet.

  • Ecclesiastes 2:24 — The Preacher concludes that eating, drinking, and enjoying one's work are good, and that they come from the hand of God. Ordinary drinking can be received with thanks as His gift.

  • Haggai 1:6 — While God's house lay in ruins, the people drank but were never filled. Satisfaction failed when they neglected the Lord.

  • Joel 3:3 — The nations "sold a girl for wine, that they may drink." Here the appetite for drink became cruelty, and God took up the cause of His people.
