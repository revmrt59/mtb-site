TWIN, TWINS, COUPLED - (tâʼôwm, Strong's H8380)

Summary

The Hebrew word tâʼôwm means twin. It describes two children born together from one pregnancy, a pair of young animals born together, and, by extension, two things joined so closely that they function as a matched pair. The NKJV translates it "twins" in Genesis and the Song of Solomon and "coupled" in the building instructions for the tabernacle. The word appears only eight times in six verses. Because it is so rare, each use carries weight.

The two uses for human twins are the most significant. Rebekah carried Jacob and Esau, and Tamar carried Perez and Zerah. Both births are part of the family line that leads to David and to Jesus Christ, and in both, the expected order between the brothers is overturned, though in different ways. Esau was born first, yet God said the older would serve the younger, Jacob. Zerah's hand appeared first and was marked with a scarlet thread, yet Perez was the first to be delivered. Twins share one mother and one pregnancy, and they are born on the same day, so nothing in their background explains why one is chosen over the other. Paul uses Rebekah's twins to show that God's purpose rests "not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:11). The word also describes the doubled corner boards that held the tabernacle together and the "twins of a gazelle" in a bridegroom's praise of his bride. Together these uses picture what God chooses by grace, what He holds together in His dwelling, and what He calls beautiful.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tâʼôwm is a Hebrew noun used in three kinds of settings: family narrative in Genesis, tabernacle construction in Exodus, and love poetry in the Song of Solomon. Two of the verses in Exodus repeat the same instruction, once when Moses receives it (Exodus 26:24) and once when the craftsmen carry it out (Exodus 36:29). Each of those verses uses the word twice.

The English reader should know one detail. Song of Solomon 4:2 also says that each sheep "bears twins," but that line uses a related Hebrew verb, tâʼam (Strong's H8382), rather than this noun. The image is close by, but the noun tâʼôwm appears in verse 5.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning is twin, meaning one of two born together or matched as a pair. Context shows two senses:

1. Twin offspring: two children, or two young animals, born from the same pregnancy. Examples are Rebekah's sons in Genesis 25:24 and the "twins of a gazelle" in Song of Solomon 4:5.

2. Joined or doubled as a pair: two parts fitted together so that they work as one unit. This is the "coupled" corner boards of the tabernacle in Exodus 26:24.

The second sense grows naturally from the first. Twins belong together from the beginning, and coupled boards are made to stand together.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Genesis the word appears at the moment a mother learns, or the midwife sees, that two children are coming (Genesis 25:24; 38:27). The narrative then turns quickly to the relationship between the two children. In both stories the brothers' relationship is marked by struggle and contested priority from birth.

In Exodus the subject is lifeless wood, and the word describes how two boards behave as a pair. They are "coupled" at the bottom and the top and joined by one ring. The point is how closely they are fitted together.

In the Song of Solomon the twins are young gazelles feeding among lilies. Here the word suggests a matched pair, gentleness, and liveliness, a picture of beauty that fits the poem's delight in the bride.

4. Theological Significance

The word's most important theological contribution concerns God's free choice. Ancient families expected the firstborn son to receive the leading place and the larger inheritance. Twins test that expectation more sharply than any other case. With ordinary brothers, someone could point to age, ability, or a different mother to explain why one was favored. With twins, everything in their background is shared. When God sets the younger above the older in a twin birth, the only explanation left is His own purpose.

The tabernacle use adds a different truth. God gave Moses exact instructions for His dwelling, down to the joints at the corners, and Israel built it "as the LORD had commanded." God's presence among His people did not depend on loose improvisation. He cared how the structure held together.

The Song of Solomon shows that the God who chooses and builds also delights in beauty. The bridegroom's praise ends, "You are all fair, my love, And there is no spot in you" (Song of Solomon 4:7). The twin fawns are part of a portrait of a bride who is loved and admired.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 25:22–26 — Jacob and Esau. Rebekah's twins struggled within her, and when she asked the LORD, He told her that two nations were in her womb and that the older would serve the younger. When her days were fulfilled, "there were twins" (v. 24). Esau came out first, and Jacob followed holding his brother's heel. Paul builds his argument on this scene. Rebekah "had conceived by one man, even by our father Isaac," and the word came "for the children not yet being born, nor having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works but of Him who calls" (Romans 9:10–11). Paul stresses the shared father and the time before birth because those facts remove every human explanation. The choice of Jacob rested on God's call, and the twin birth made that unmistakable.

Genesis 38:27–30 — Perez and Zerah. This birth comes out of one of the darkest chapters in Genesis. Judah had failed his widowed daughter-in-law Tamar, and his own sin led to her pregnancy. At the birth, "twins were in her womb" (v. 27). Zerah put out his hand first, and the midwife tied a scarlet thread on it to mark him as firstborn. Then he drew it back, and his brother came out first, so Perez was actually the first child delivered. The midwife exclaimed at how he had broken through, and he was named Perez. The marked child was passed by. Perez became the head of the line, and Ruth traces it forward: "Now this is the genealogy of Perez: Perez begot Hezron" and on to Boaz, Jesse, and "Jesse begot David" (Ruth 4:18–22). Matthew names both twins and their mother when he records the ancestry of Jesus (Matthew 1:3).

When the two stories are read together, a pattern appears, though the two reversals are not the same. Jacob was the second twin born, and God's word before birth gave him the older brother's place. Perez was not the twin first marked as firstborn, but he broke through and was delivered first. Paul's explicit argument about children who had done neither good nor evil concerns Jacob and Esau (Romans 9:11). Genesis does not give a reason why Perez's line, rather than Zerah's, was carried forward. It is Matthew's genealogy that shows the line of Christ running through Perez (Matthew 1:3). Read alongside Paul, Perez's story makes a fitting comparison. Neither Jacob nor Perez came from a clean or impressive beginning. Jacob grasped and schemed, and Perez came from a story of failure and shame. God carried His promise forward through both.

Exodus 26:23–25 — The coupled corners. The back wall of the tabernacle needed two special boards for its corners. These boards were "coupled" together at the bottom and at the top by one ring, and the same pattern applied to both corners (v. 24). A corner is where two walls meet, and God's design put a doubled, joined pair at those points. Exodus 36:29 records that the workers built it exactly that way.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The twin births in Genesis are links in the chain that leads to Jesus. Matthew's genealogy runs through Jacob and through Perez (Matthew 1:2–3), so Jacob and Perez, each a twin through whom the line continued, appear in the record of Christ's ancestry. God did not bring His Son into the world through a family history of proper firstborn sons. He brought Him through grasping Jacob and through Perez, the son of Judah and Tamar. The line of the Messiah was carried by promise at every step.

Paul's use of Rebekah's twins carries the same principle into the gospel. Salvation in Christ rests on "Him who calls," and that is why it can reach people with nothing to recommend them.

The coupled corners point toward the dwelling God is building now. Paul describes believers built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, "Jesus Christ Himself being the chief cornerstone, in whom the whole building, being fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord" (Ephesians 2:20–21). Exodus does not label its corner boards as a prophecy. Still, the tabernacle's careful joining at the corners fits the larger biblical pattern. God's house holds together where its parts are joined, and in the church, Christ Himself is the chief cornerstone in whom the whole building is fitted together.

7. Spirit & Life

The twins of Genesis speak directly to anyone who wonders whether they qualify for God's grace. If any two people had equal standing, Jacob and Esau did. They had the same parents and the same birth, and neither had yet done "any good or evil." God's choice still came first. That truth humbles us, because none of us earned a place in God's family. It also gives hope, because no one is ruled out by a shameful background. Paul makes that argument about Jacob, but Perez's story points the same way: the scarlet thread was tied to Zerah's wrist, yet it is Perez whom Matthew names in the line of Christ.

These stories also refuse to make grace tidy. Jacob held Esau's heel from the womb, and the brothers' rivalry wounded their family for decades. The gazelle twins feed peacefully among lilies, and the tabernacle boards stand quietly side by side. The human twins struggled from birth. That contrast fits what Scripture says about fallen people: those closest to us are often the ones we compete with most. God did not wait for Jacob to become gentle before working through him. He brought His purpose through the struggle, and in time He brought Jacob to the point of meeting Esau again.

The coupled boards offer a quieter lesson. At the corners of God's house, where two walls met, the design did not leave a single board standing alone. Believers are "being built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:22). When pressure comes, God's design is for His people to stand joined, not alone.

The Song of Solomon also has a place. The bridegroom's delighted, specific praise of his bride's body, set among lilies and fawns, shows that God gave married love its joy and honored physical beauty within it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because this word is rare, its significant occurrences have already been discussed above. Two brief entries complete the survey.

Exodus 36:29 — The boards were "coupled" at the bottom and the top, exactly as commanded in Exodus 26:24. The repetition shows Israel's obedience matching God's instruction detail for detail.

Song of Solomon 4:5; 7:3 — "Your two breasts are like two fawns, Twins of a gazelle, Which feed among the lilies" (4:5). The bridegroom repeats the twin-fawn image in 7:3. His delight in his bride has not faded.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The idea of "twin" reappears in the New Testament through a disciple's name. John refers to "Thomas, who is called the Twin" (John 11:16; see also 20:24). Scripture never names his twin brother or sister. This is a different word in a different language, so it is not an occurrence of tâʼôwm. Still, it is a striking echo. The twin best known in the Gospels is the disciple who doubted and then confessed the risen Jesus as "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28).
