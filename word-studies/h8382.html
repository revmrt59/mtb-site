BEAR TWINS, BE DOUBLE - (tâʼam, Strong's H8382)

Summary

The Hebrew verb tâʼam means to be double or to come in matching pairs. The Song of Solomon uses it twice (4:2; 6:6), where the NKJV translates it as "bears twins." The bridegroom praises his bride's smile by comparing her teeth to a flock of freshly washed sheep, "every one of which bears twins, and none is barren among them" (Song 4:2). The picture is of a smile where every tooth has its matching partner and none is missing. A shepherd would also see a flock in perfect health, with every ewe fruitful and no loss anywhere.

The word gains its spiritual weight from where it appears. The bridegroom speaks this praise once in the delight of their union, and the poem gives it again almost word for word in chapter 6, after the painful scene of chapter 5 in which the bride hesitated to open the door and he withdrew. The Song does not narrate a reunion or say that the second praise answers the closed door, but the placement is striking: the praise that follows the separation is the same praise as before. It is Ephesians 5 that teaches plainly how Christ's love cleanses His people, for He washes His church to present her "without blemish." Read alongside that teaching, this small word becomes a window into how Christ sees the people He has cleansed. He sees them as whole and fruitful, and His love does not shrink when they fail.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tâʼam belongs to the Hebrew vocabulary of doubling and pairing. It is closely related to the ordinary Hebrew word for twin, tâʼôwm (Strong's H8380), used for Jacob and Esau and for Perez and Zerah in Genesis. That related noun also appears in the Tabernacle instructions, where the corner boards "shall be coupled together at the bottom and they shall be coupled together at the top by one ring" (Exodus 26:24). Hebrew lexicons differ over whether to group the Exodus form with the noun or the verb. The Song's two uses describe bearing twins; the Exodus passage describes paired boards.

Both uses in the Song come from the world of shepherding. Israel's poets knew sheep well. Shearing season, washing pools, and lambing were familiar sights. The Song draws on that knowledge to praise the bride.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning of tâʼam is to be double or to come in pairs. In the Song, the verb describes ewes that produce twins. That one sense carries two ideas at once:

1. Matched pairing. Applied to teeth, every tooth has its partner. The smile is even and complete, with no gaps.

2. Fruitful abundance. Applied to sheep, every ewe has twins, which is a sign of a thriving flock. The paired line "none is barren among them" adds that no ewe has failed to bear.

These two ideas are one picture seen from two angles, and the poem holds them together.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Both occurrences sit inside the bridegroom's descriptions of his bride (Song 4:1–7; 6:4–9). Each comparison praises one feature at a time: eyes, hair, teeth, lips, temples. In the line about her teeth, three details work together. The sheep are "shorn" and have "come up from the washing," which suggests whiteness. They bear "twins," which suggests matched pairs. "None is barren," which means nothing is missing.

The repetition matters more than any grammatical detail. Song 6:6 repeats 4:2 nearly word for word, and the surrounding lines about her eyes, hair, and temples also return from chapter 4. A poet who repeats himself this exactly is making a point. The bridegroom's praise has not changed.

4. Theological Significance

These verses show the heart of a lover who delights in the one he loves and says so. The bridegroom does not merely tolerate his bride. He studies her and names what he finds beautiful down to the smallest detail. In chapter 4 his praise ends with the declaration that she is all fair and that there is no spot in her (Song 4:7).

Scripture uses marriage repeatedly to describe God's love for His people, and the New Testament makes Christ the bridegroom and the church His bride (Ephesians 5:25–32). The Song's language of a bride without flaw finds its deepest echo in Christ's purpose to present the church "not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing, but that she should be holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:27). God's love does not stay silent about the beloved. He speaks over His people what He has made them to be.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 4:2 — praise in the day of delight. This first occurrence comes in the bridegroom's praise at the height of their union. Even though the comparison of teeth to sheep sounds strange to modern ears, it is the image of a man who has looked closely and found nothing lacking. The washed sheep make her smile white. Because each one "bears twins," every tooth is matched. The words "none is barren among them" mean that no tooth is missing. Every line in the description says the same thing: she is whole.

Song of Solomon 5:2–6 — the hesitation. The next chapter opens with the bridegroom knocking at night: "Open for me, my sister, my love, my dove, my perfect one" (5:2). Even at the door he calls her "my perfect one." But she makes excuses: "I have taken off my robe; How can I put it on again? I have washed my feet; How can I defile them?" (5:3). When she finally rises, "my beloved had turned away and was gone... I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer" (5:6). She is the one who hesitated, and she suffers the loss of his presence.

Song of Solomon 6:6 — the same praise after the separation. In chapter 6 the bride says her beloved has gone to his garden and declares, "I am my beloved's, And my beloved is mine" (6:2–3). The poem does not narrate a reunion scene or explain how the two came back together. It simply places the bridegroom's praise next. He does not mention the closed door. He speaks over her the same words he spoke in chapter 4, including the flock where every one bears twins and none is barren. Between 6:4 and 6:6 he even confesses that her eyes overwhelm him. The Song does not say that the repeated line is his answer to what happened in chapter 5, but setting it here invites the reader to notice that what he said of her before, he still says.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song is first a celebration of love between a man and his bride, and it should be honored as that. Christians have also long heard in it the love of Christ for His people. The New Testament gives that reading solid footing, because Paul calls marriage a picture of "Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). The connections below are therefore resonance grounded in Paul's teaching rather than a fulfillment the Song itself announces.

Two lines of connection stand out. The first is washing. The sheep in the Song have "come up from the washing," and Christ gave Himself for the church "that He might sanctify and cleanse her with the washing of water by the word" (Ephesians 5:26). The bride's flawlessness in the Song is a poet's praise. The church's flawlessness is the result of Christ's own cleansing work.

The second is the end of barrenness. Scripture's story is full of barren women whom God made fruitful: Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah. Isaiah 54:1 calls the barren one to sing because her children will be many, and Paul applies that promise to the gospel's children (Galatians 4:27). The flock where "none is barren" fits that larger pattern. In Christ, what was empty is made fruitful.

The tabernacle's architecture adds one more picture. The Tabernacle boards were "coupled together" to stand firm (Exodus 26:24). Paul describes the church as a building "being fitted together" into "a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:21–22). That link rests on related imagery, not on this exact word, but it fits the idea of things rightly paired and joined.

7. Spirit & Life

The order of the Song's story speaks directly to believers who have failed. Many Christians assume that after a season of coldness or disobedience, Christ's love for them must be smaller. They expect to earn back His favor slowly. The bridegroom in the Song does the opposite. The bride's real failure in chapter 5 cost her the felt presence of her beloved, and that loss was painful. Yet when the bridegroom speaks again in chapter 6, his words over her are the same words he had spoken before. The Song makes that point through the order of its poems rather than by telling a reunion story, and the assurance that Christ's love cleanses and keeps His people rests on what the New Testament says plainly (Ephesians 5:25–27). The way back to Christ ends in the welcome He has always offered, not in probation.

The imagery of the verse can also serve as an illustration. The washed flock and the twin-bearing ewes are details of one poetic picture, and the Song does not say that the washing makes the ewes fruitful. Jesus, however, does teach plainly how cleansing and fruit belong together: "You are already clean because of the word which I have spoken to you," followed by "Abide in Me... He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit" (John 15:3–5). Fruitfulness is not something a believer manufactures in order to be accepted. It grows from having been washed and from staying near the One who washed us. The Song's picture of a washed and fruitful flock illustrates what Jesus teaches outright, and His teaching brings freedom. A believer who feels barren does not need to try harder at producing. He or she needs to return to the word Christ has spoken and abide in Him.

Finally, the bridegroom speaks his delight aloud, and he speaks it in detail. Christ's love for His church is not vague goodwill. His love sees and names. Believers can learn to hear their identity from His words rather than from their worst moments.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Tâʼam is used for bearing twins in Song of Solomon 4:2 and 6:6, and both verses are discussed above. The related noun tâʼôwm (Strong's H8380) is translated "twins" in Genesis 25:24 and 38:27 and "coupled" in Exodus 26:24. Those verses help explain the idea of a matched pair; the Exodus form can also be grouped with the verb, as noted above.
