FIG, FIG TREE - (tᵉʼên, Strong's H8384)

Summary

The Hebrew word tᵉʼên means fig or fig tree. In ancient Israel the fig was a staple food and a familiar shade tree. A family could plant one only if it expected to stay on its land for years. Scripture often pairs the fig with the vine, and the two together came to picture a household at rest. To sit "under his vine and his fig tree" meant to live safely on your own land, with enough to eat and no enemy at the door. For that reason the fig tree often serves as a measure of Israel's condition in the Old Testament. In many passages a flourishing fig tree pictures God's blessing, and a fig tree that is stripped, struck, or barren pictures His discipline.

The word appears at several turning points in the Bible's story. Adam and Eve sew fig leaves to hide their shame. Jeremiah sees two baskets of figs and learns that God's mercy may look like judgment. Habakkuk declares that though the fig tree does not blossom, he will still rejoice in the Lord. Micah and Zechariah use the vine and fig tree to describe the peace God will bring through His Messiah. When Jesus curses a fig tree that has leaves but no fruit, He draws on this whole history. The fig shows that God is never satisfied with a self-made covering or with leaves without fruit, and that He promises lasting rest to those He makes clean.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tᵉʼên is an ordinary Hebrew agricultural noun. It names a tree that grew throughout the land and supplied sweet, nourishing fruit that could be eaten fresh or pressed into cakes. Fig trees took several years of steady labor to establish. A family sitting under its own mature fig tree was therefore a settled family, not wanderers, refugees, or people displaced by war. That practical fact explains why the tree became a picture of peace. Most of the word's uses fall in the prophets, where Isaiah, Jeremiah, and the Minor Prophets draw on the tree's fruitfulness, failure, and destruction to speak to the nation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has one basic meaning, the fig, which Scripture applies in two closely related ways:

1. The fig tree itself, as a plant in a field or garden. Example: "each man under his vine and his fig tree" (1 Kings 4:25).

2. The fruit of the tree. Example: "two baskets of figs set before the temple of the LORD" (Jeremiah 24:1).

The word itself does not mean peace, judgment, or Israel. Those associations come from how the prophets and historians use the tree.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the fig in several recurring ways. It is a gift of the land: Moses describes Canaan as "a land of wheat and barley, of vines and fig trees and pomegranates" (Deuteronomy 8:8). The spies carried figs back from Eshcol as evidence of the land's goodness (Numbers 13:23). In the wilderness, Israel later complained that the desert was no place of figs (Numbers 20:5).

The fig also marks the seasons. In the Song of Solomon, the bridegroom says the winter is past: "The fig tree puts forth her green figs, And the vines with the tender grapes Give a good smell. Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away!" (Song of Solomon 2:13). Here too the fig and the vine appear together. The first green figs announce spring, and the bridegroom treats them as his reason to call the bride to come away with him.

The ripe fig can also picture weakness. Nahum mocks Nineveh this way: "All your strongholds are fig trees with ripened figs: If they are shaken, They fall into the mouth of the eater" (Nahum 3:12). The fortresses of the empire that conquered Israel would come down as easily as ripe figs fall when the branch is shaken. Isaiah carries the same image to a cosmic scale. The host of heaven falls "as fruit falling from a fig tree" (Isaiah 34:4).

The vine and fig tree are frequently named together, as in 1 Kings 4:25, Micah 4:4, and Zechariah 3:10. Where they are paired, the pairing carries the meaning: home, food, and safety held together under God's hand.

4. Theological Significance

The fig tree shows that God's gifts and God's discipline arrive in the same orchard. Leviticus 26 promises that obedience would bring rain and fruit, and that Israel would "dwell in your land safely" (Leviticus 26:5). The prophets show the reverse. God struck Egypt's "vines also, and their fig trees" (Psalm 105:33). Later He brought the same kind of judgment on His own people. Locusts stripped the fig tree bare in Joel 1:7, and blight and locusts devoured Israel's fig trees, yet the people did not return to Him (Amos 4:9).

Hosea 2:12 explains why God did this. Israel had called its vines and fig trees the wages its lovers had paid it. In other words, the people credited their harvest to the Baals. So God promised to destroy those trees. He took away the gifts Israel had credited to idols so the people would learn again who had given them. Jeremiah says the same: "No grapes shall be on the vine, Nor figs on the fig tree, And the leaf shall fade; And the things I have given them shall pass away from them" (Jeremiah 8:13). God's judgment here is the act of a Giver reclaiming what His people had stolen for other gods.

The fig also shows God's heart. In Hosea 9:10, the Lord remembers finding Israel like grapes in the wilderness and seeing their fathers like the first ripe fruit on a fig tree in its first season. A first-ripe fig was a treat everyone looked forward to. God describes His early delight in His people with the pleasure of a farmer finding the first fig of the year. The rest of the verse tells how they turned to Baal-Peor. Because God's love for them was real, their betrayal wounded Him personally.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 3:7, 21: The fig leaf and God's covering. The word first appears at the moment of shame: "they sewed fig leaves together and made themselves coverings" (Genesis 3:7). The coverings were their own work and their own idea, and they did not work. When the Lord came walking in the garden, the couple still hid. God then acted: "the LORD God made tunics of skin, and clothed them" (Genesis 3:21). Genesis does not say how the tunics of skin were obtained, and it does not describe a sacrifice. What it makes plain is the contrast. The covering of leaves was the couple's own work, while the tunics were made by the LORD God Himself and put on them by Him. Isaiah later confesses that "all our righteousnesses are like filthy rags; We all fade as a leaf" (Isaiah 64:6). The fig leaf stands at the start of Scripture as the first attempt by sinners to cover themselves, and as the first lesson that only God can do it.

Jeremiah 24: Two baskets of figs. After Nebuchadnezzar carried King Jeconiah, the princes, and the craftsmen to Babylon, the Lord showed Jeremiah two baskets "set before the temple of the LORD" (Jeremiah 24:1). One held very good figs. The other held figs too rotten to eat. The people would have assumed that the exiles were the rejected ones and that those left in Jerusalem had escaped judgment. God reversed that assumption. The good figs were the captives. God said He had sent them to Babylon for their good, that He would watch over them, bring them back, and give them a heart to know Him (24:5–7). The bad figs were King Zedekiah and those who stayed in the land or fled to Egypt (24:8). Of them God said, "Behold, I will send on them the sword, the famine, and the pestilence, and will make them like rotten figs that cannot be eaten, they are so bad" (Jeremiah 29:17). Exile was the road God used to rescue the exiles, while the city that looked safe was the one headed for destruction. God's mercy can look like loss, and apparent safety can be the real danger.

Habakkuk 3:17–19: Joy when the fig tree fails. Habakkuk had seen that Babylon was coming. He lists every source of food Israel had: "Though the fig tree may not blossom, Nor fruit be on the vines; Though the labor of the olive may fail, And the fields yield no food; Though the flock may be cut off from the fold, And there be no herd in the stalls" (Habakkuk 3:17). The fig tree comes first. It was a familiar sign of God's blessing, and Habakkuk imagines it bare. Then he says, "Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation" (3:18). His joy rests in God Himself, not in the harvest that showed God's favor. He goes on: "The LORD God is my strength; He will make my feet like deer's feet, And He will make me walk on my high hills" (3:19). A deer moves surely across rocky heights where other animals would stumble. Habakkuk does not expect the ruin to be lifted before he can stand. He expects God to give him sure footing on the hard ground itself.

From Solomon to the Messiah: Every man under his fig tree. Under Solomon, "Judah and Israel dwelt safely, each man under his vine and his fig tree, from Dan as far as Beersheba" (1 Kings 4:25). That peace did not last. Centuries later the Assyrian envoy tried to use the same picture to win Jerusalem's surrender, promising that everyone would "eat from his own vine and every one from his own fig tree" if they would only come out to him (Isaiah 36:16). It was a counterfeit offer of rest from an enemy. Micah answers with the Lord's own promise: "everyone shall sit under his vine and under his fig tree, And no one shall make them afraid; For the mouth of the LORD of hosts has spoken" (Micah 4:4). Zechariah shows how that rest will come. The high priest Joshua's filthy garments are removed (Zechariah 3:4). God announces His servant the Branch and promises to remove the land's iniquity in one day (3:8–9). Then comes the fig tree: "Everyone will invite his neighbor Under his vine and under his fig tree" (3:10). Solomon's peace was national security. Zechariah's peace follows the cleansing of sin, and it is shared with the neighbor.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The fig tree traces the gospel story. It begins with sinners sewing leaves to hide themselves. It ends with a cleansed people resting under their fig trees and inviting their neighbors to join them, once God's Branch has taken away their iniquity. Between those points stand Jesus and a fig tree near Jerusalem. The Gospels use a different word, Greek rather than Hebrew, but they draw on the same body of imagery. Mark records that Jesus saw "a fig tree having leaves" and "found nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs" (Mark 11:13). He said, "Let no one eat fruit from you ever again" (11:14). Then He went into the temple and drove out the merchants, saying, "you have made it a 'den of thieves'" (11:17). The next morning the tree was "dried up from the roots" (11:20).

Mark's own note that it was not the season for figs shows that the point is not a tree failing at its natural duty. Mark places the cursing of the tree on either side of the temple scene, so each episode explains the other. The withered tree becomes an acted sign of judgment on a temple busy with activity but lacking what God sought, a house meant to be "a house of prayer for all nations" that had become a "den of thieves" (11:17). Mark does not name a prophecy that Jesus was fulfilling, but Jeremiah's words are a strong parallel: "No grapes shall be on the vine, Nor figs on the fig tree, And the leaf shall fade" (Jeremiah 8:13). The temple had the leaves of religion without the fruit of prayer and repentance. Leaves had failed Adam in Genesis, and leaves without fruit fail again here. The fig tree also points to the end of history. John sees stars falling "as a fig tree drops its late figs when it is shaken by a mighty wind" (Revelation 6:13), which echoes Isaiah 34:4. The rest Micah and Zechariah promised belongs to those whose sin the Branch has removed. A suggestive echo appears in John 1:48, where Jesus tells Nathanael He saw him under the fig tree. Jesus already knew the man resting in the place the prophets had used to picture the Messiah's peace.

7. Spirit & Life

The fig leaf exposes a habit every believer recognizes: the urge to fix our own shame before facing God. We sew together reputation, effort, and religious activity, and we still hide. God's answer in Eden was to clothe Adam and Eve Himself. Scripture later develops that picture of God-given clothing. Isaiah rejoices that the Lord "has clothed me with the garments of salvation, He has covered me with the robe of righteousness" (Isaiah 61:10), and Paul longs to be found in Christ, "not having my own righteousness, which is from the law, but that which is through faith in Christ" (Philippians 3:9). Genesis does not state that teaching, but it begins the pattern: the covering that holds comes from God. In the gospel, that covering is the righteousness of Christ, which covers us where our leaves cannot.

Jeremiah's baskets help people who are suffering loss. The exiles in Babylon were the ones God called good, and He was preparing them to know Him. A believer who has been carried somewhere he did not choose should not assume he has been rejected. God may be keeping him for his good.

Habakkuk shows where joy actually rests. It is possible to love God's gifts so much that losing them feels like losing God. Habakkuk imagined the bare fig tree and found the Lord still there as his strength. Hosea 9:10 adds what God is looking for. He still delights in early, dependent love, like a farmer prizing the first fig of the season.

The fig tree also brings a warning. Jesus looked for fruit, not leaves. The appearance of spiritual life cannot replace the fruit of prayer, repentance, and faith.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 9:10–11 — In Jotham's parable, the fig tree refuses to give up its sweetness and good fruit in order to rule over the trees. The bramble accepts the throne instead. The fig tree values its fruitful work above power over others.

  • Proverbs 27:18 — The one who tends a fig tree will eat its fruit, and the one who faithfully serves his master will be honored. Patient faithfulness in an assigned task brings its reward.

  • 2 Kings 20:7 — "Take a lump of figs." The poultice was laid on Hezekiah's boil, "and he recovered" (see also Isaiah 38:21). God, who could have healed the king with a word, chose an ordinary fruit of the land as the means of His healing. That suggests, though the passage does not state it as a principle, that God often works through ordinary means.

  • Nehemiah 13:15 — Nehemiah saw figs being brought into Jerusalem on the Sabbath and rebuked the traders. Even God's good gifts become an offense when they are used to disregard Him.

  • Haggai 2:19 — The fig tree had borne nothing, yet God promised, "from this day I will bless you." When God's people return to Him, He brings back fruit their own efforts could not produce.
