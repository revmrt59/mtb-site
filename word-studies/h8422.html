TUBAL - (Tûwbal, Strong's H8422)

Summary

Tubal is the fifth listed son of Japheth and a grandson of Noah. His name also became the name of a people who descended from him. The meaning of the name is uncertain. Some connect it with a root meaning "to bring" or "brought," but the Bible gives no explanation of the name, and no derivation is settled. Tubal appears only eight times in Scripture. Genesis and 1 Chronicles list him among the founders of the nations after the flood. Later, Isaiah and Ezekiel speak of Tubal as a distant people at the northern edge of Israel's known world. Ezekiel always pairs Tubal with Meshech, and seven of the eight occurrences name Meshech with it. Most scholars place both peoples in Asia Minor, in what is now Turkey.

The prophets portray Tubal in two very different ways. Ezekiel shows a nation that traded "human lives and vessels of bronze" (Ezekiel 27:13) and spread terror before going down to the grave (Ezekiel 32:26). He also names Tubal in the coalition of Gog, which God says He will break on the mountains of Israel (Ezekiel 38–39). Isaiah, however, names Tubal among the faraway nations to which God will send messengers to "declare My glory among the Gentiles" (Isaiah 66:19). Read together, these passages show a God who brings hostile powers to an end, and who also intends His glory to reach the most distant peoples. That second purpose comes into full view in the gospel's advance to the ends of the earth.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Strong's H8422 refers to one man and to the people who descended from him. As an individual, Tubal is listed fifth among the seven sons of Japheth: Gomer, Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, Meshech, and Tiras (Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5). Scripture records no events from his life. In the prophets, the name refers to his descendants as a nation. Tubal must not be confused with Tubal-Cain, the descendant of Cain who worked bronze and iron before the flood (Genesis 4:22). Tubal-Cain is a different man with a separate entry (H8423), from a different family line and a different era.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. Some scholars propose a connection with a root meaning "to bring" or "brought." That is a possibility only, not a verified meaning, and Scripture never builds any wordplay on the name. Its spiritual significance must therefore come from what the Bible says about Tubal, not from the sound of the name.

Historically, Tubal is commonly identified with the Tabal people named in Assyrian records, placed in east-central Asia Minor, roughly the region later known as Cappadocia or near Cilicia. It is also linked with the Tibarenoi mentioned by Greek writers, who lived later near the southeastern shore of the Black Sea, which suggests a territorial shift over time. Meshech is likewise linked with the Musku of the same general area. Some popular prophecy writers have connected Tubal with the Russian city of Tobolsk and Meshech with Moscow. Those identifications rest on similar sounds, not on biblical or historical evidence.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Among the founding nations. The Table of Nations places Tubal among the families that spread across the earth after the flood (Genesis 10:2, 32). The Chronicler repeats the list when he traces Israel's story back to Adam (1 Chronicles 1:5). Through this record, Israel knew Tubal from the beginning as a distant relative within one human family under God.

A trading partner of Tyre. Centuries later, Tubal appears in Ezekiel's lament over Tyre, the great sea-trading city: "Javan, Tubal, and Meshech were your traders. They bartered human lives and vessels of bronze for your merchandise" (Ezekiel 27:13). Ezekiel places slaves and bronze side by side on the same list of goods. The region's mountains were known for their metalwork, but the prophet makes sure the human cargo is not overlooked.

Gone down to the Pit. In Ezekiel's funeral song over Egypt, the prophet surveys the nations already lying in the realm of the dead. Meshech and Tubal are there with all their multitudes. They are described as uncircumcised and slain by the sword, though they once spread terror in the land of the living (Ezekiel 32:26). Their military power, once feared, now lies silent in the grave.

In Gog's coalition. Ezekiel 38–39 gives Tubal its most prominent role. God tells the prophet, "Son of man, set your face against Gog, of the land of Magog, the prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal" (Ezekiel 38:2). Then He declares, "Behold, I am against you, O Gog, the prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal" (Ezekiel 38:3), and He repeats the charge in Ezekiel 39:1. Translations differ on whether "Rosh" is a place name or means "chief" ("chief prince of Meshech and Tubal"). Either way, Tubal is under Gog's leadership. Gog's alliance of distant nations comes against a restored Israel living securely in its land (Ezekiel 38:8–11). God Himself takes the initiative and overthrows it with earthquake, pestilence, fire, and hail, and by turning "every man's sword" against his brother (Ezekiel 38:19–22; 39:3–6). Israel's deliverance comes from Him. He states His purpose plainly: He will make Himself known, and His holy name will no longer be profaned (Ezekiel 38:23; 39:7).

Among those who will hear of God's glory. Isaiah's closing chapter speaks of Tubal in a very different way. God will gather all nations to see His glory. He will set a sign among His people and send those who escape "to Tarshish and Pul and Lud, who draw the bow, and Tubal and Javan, to the coastlands afar off who have not heard My fame nor seen My glory. And they shall declare My glory among the Gentiles" (Isaiah 66:19). The chapter goes on to describe people being brought from these nations to God's holy mountain, with some even taken as priests and Levites (Isaiah 66:20–21); interpreters debate whether those brought and chosen are returning Israelites, Gentiles, or both.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

The text states several things directly. Tubal belongs to the family of nations descended from Noah. It traded in human beings. It died under judgment after spreading terror. It will share in Gog's defeat. And it is named as a destination for the proclamation of God's glory. Beyond these statements, one can reasonably infer that Tubal stands in Scripture as a representative of the far edges of the earth, the remote and warlike north as Israel knew it. This inference rests mainly on Tubal's repeated pairing with Meshech, on the phrase "coastlands afar off," and on Gog's coming "from the far north" (Ezekiel 38:15).

That inference makes the two prophetic portraits more striking. In Ezekiel, the distant nations rise against God's people and fall, and the outcome is that "the nations shall know" the Lord (Ezekiel 38:16). In Isaiah, the distant nations hear of His glory through messengers and come to worship. Both prophets aim at the same end, which is that God's name will be known among the peoples. Ezekiel shows God judging the armies and systems that set themselves against Him. Isaiah shows Him reaching the people of those same lands with mercy. Neither picture cancels the other. Ezekiel even says that God will "set My glory among the nations" through His judgment (Ezekiel 39:21), a phrase close to Isaiah's language about declaring His glory.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never names Tubal, but its themes continue there. When Revelation describes the final rebellion, it draws on Ezekiel's vision. Satan will "go out to deceive the nations which are in the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, to gather them together to battle, whose number is as the sand of the sea" (Revelation 20:8). Meshech and Tubal are not mentioned, but the coalition they belonged to now represents rebellious nations everywhere. As in Ezekiel, that rebellion ends by God's direct action, when fire comes down from heaven (Revelation 20:9). This is an explicit New Testament use of Ezekiel 38–39.

Revelation also echoes Ezekiel's account of the slave trade. Babylon's cargo list ends with "bodies and souls of men" (Revelation 18:13). This list closely resembles Ezekiel's lament over Tyre, which names Tubal's trade in "human lives." This is a strong connection within the canon. In both books, a world system that buys and sells people falls under God's verdict.

Isaiah 66:19 points toward the mission of the church. Messengers from among God's people are sent to nations that have not heard of His fame. That pattern is strongly echoed when the risen Christ sends His witnesses "to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8). Paul's ambition to preach where Christ had not been named carries the same concern (Romans 15:20–21). One further connection depends on the commonly accepted location of Tabal, so it should be held with that qualification. Cappadocia, in the general region of ancient Tabal, was represented at Pentecost (Acts 2:9), and Peter later wrote to believers living there (1 Peter 1:1). If the identification holds, the gospel reached the region of ancient Tubal within the apostolic generation.

5. Spirit & Life

Ezekiel 27:13 places "human lives" on the same line as bronze pots, and Scripture does not let that pass without comment. God made people in His image (Genesis 1:27; 9:6), and He takes note when any economy turns them into merchandise. Revelation brings the same charge against Babylon. God's people should not grow numb to systems, ancient or modern, that treat human beings as goods to be bought and sold.

Ezekiel 32 offers a sober comfort. Tubal once spread terror in the land of the living, yet the prophet finds its warriors silent in the grave. No nation's intimidation lasts forever. Believers who feel threatened by hostile powers can take courage from God's words to Gog: "Behold, I am against you." The God who says this defends His name by His own initiative and power, whatever means He chooses to use.

Isaiah 66:19 adds something that a reading of prophecy focused only on judgment can easily miss. The same Tubal that marches with Gog is also named as a place where God's glory will be declared. God's dealings with the nations include both justice and mercy. He breaks the rebellion, and He sends messengers to the rebels' homelands. That mercy is our own story too, since most Christians are also descended from the distant Gentiles who "had not heard." The fitting response is to worship God and to join the sending.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:2; 1 Chronicles 1:5 – Tubal as a son of Japheth and the ancestor of a nation in the post-flood family of peoples.

  • Ezekiel 27:13 – Tubal trades "human lives and vessels of bronze" with Tyre.

  • Ezekiel 32:26 – Meshech and Tubal lie slain in the Pit after spreading terror among the living.

  • Ezekiel 38:2–3; 39:1 – Tubal in Gog's coalition, against which God declares "I am against you."

  • Ezekiel 38:16, 23; 39:7, 21 – God's purpose in Gog's defeat: making His holy name known among the nations.

  • Isaiah 66:19–21 – Tubal among the distant nations that will hear God's glory declared.

  • Revelation 20:8–9 – Ezekiel's Gog and Magog taken up into the final rebellion of the nations and its defeat.

  • Revelation 18:13 – Babylon's trade in "bodies and souls of men," echoing Tyre's trade with Tubal.

  • Acts 1:8; Romans 15:20–21 – The gospel sent to the ends of the earth and to those who have not heard.
