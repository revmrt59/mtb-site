TOGARMAH - (Tôwgarmâh, Strong's H8425)

Summary

Togarmah is named only four times in Scripture, but the name covers a long distance. He first appears in the Table of Nations as the third listed son of Gomer and a great-grandson of Noah through Japheth (Genesis 10:3; 1 Chronicles 1:6). In Ezekiel the name has become a people, "the house of Togarmah," a northern nation that traded horses, steeds, and mules with the merchant city of Tyre (Ezekiel 27:14). Ezekiel later names it among the forces that march with Gog "from the far north" against God's restored people (Ezekiel 38:6). The meaning of the name is uncertain. Scripture gives no explanation of it, and no settled derivation exists. Many historical geographers connect it with a place called Tegarama in eastern Anatolia, in what is now Turkey. That connection concerns location, not meaning.

Togarmah's importance lies in where Scripture places it. A family name from God's orderly record of the post-flood world reappears at the edge of Israel's map as part of a coalition gathered against the Lord's land. Ezekiel is clear about who is in control: God Himself brings the invaders out so that the nations may know Him (Ezekiel 38:16). Revelation later takes up the language of "Gog and Magog" for the final rebellion of the nations (Revelation 20:7–9). Togarmah is not named there, but it belongs to the prophetic picture Revelation draws on. Togarmah's story shows that no people stands outside God's rule, however distant or obscure, and no gathering of human strength can overturn His purposes for His people.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Togarmah is a Japhethite name with two related uses in Scripture. In Genesis 10:3 and 1 Chronicles 1:6 it names an individual, the son of Gomer and brother of Ashkenaz and Riphath. First Chronicles spells that brother's name "Diphath." The Hebrew letters for r and d look very much alike, which probably explains the difference. In Ezekiel 27:14 and 38:6 the phrase "house of Togarmah" names the people or region descended from him or associated with him. This follows the common biblical habit of using an ancestor's name for a nation. Togarmah should be kept distinct from Gomer, his father and a separate people in Ezekiel 38:6, and from Magog, his uncle in Genesis 10:2, whose land is Gog's home in Ezekiel 38:2.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. The Bible offers no explanation or wordplay on it. Any meaning proposed from the sound of the word should be treated as unproven. What scholars do propose is a geographical link. Many identify Togarmah with Tegarama, a city or region known from Hittite records, and with the later Assyrian Til-garimmu. Both are located in eastern Anatolia toward Armenia and the Caucasus. The proposal fits Ezekiel's description of Togarmah as lying "from the far north" of Israel, and it fits the region's reputation for breeding horses. It remains a scholarly identification, and the borders of such a people in Ezekiel's day are not precisely known. Later Armenian and Georgian traditions trace their ancestry to "Torgom" or "Thorgom." These traditions show how later peoples claimed Genesis 10 ancestry, but they lie outside the biblical record.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Among the nations of Genesis. Togarmah first appears in the genealogy of Noah's sons after the flood. He belongs to the line of Japheth, the branch Genesis connects with the "coastland peoples of the Gentiles" (Genesis 10:5). Genesis tells nothing about his life. He is simply one of the families through whom God filled the earth again. First Chronicles 1:6 repeats the record centuries later, placing Togarmah in the long line of humanity that leads from Adam toward Israel and David.

A trading partner of Tyre. Ezekiel 27 is a funeral lament over Tyre, the proud and wealthy port city whose fall Ezekiel announces. The chapter pictures Tyre as a magnificent ship and lists the nations that supplied her trade. Among them: "Those from the house of Togarmah traded for your wares with horses, steeds, and mules" (Ezekiel 27:14). The detail fits a northern people known for livestock. Here Togarmah is part of the network of commerce that made Tyre rich, and so it shares in the world that mourns when Tyre falls (Ezekiel 27:35–36).

In the army of Gog. In Ezekiel 38 the setting moves to "the latter years" (38:8), after God has gathered Israel back to its land and the people live in unwalled villages in safety (38:11). Gog, of the land of Magog, leads a great coalition against them. Ezekiel lists Persia, Ethiopia, and Libya, and then: "Gomer and all its troops; the house of Togarmah from the far north and all its troops—many people are with you" (Ezekiel 38:6). The army comes "out of the far north... all of them riding on horses, a great company and a mighty army" (Ezekiel 38:15). God then judges this host: "I will rain down on him, on his troops, and on the many peoples who are with him, flooding rain, great hailstones, fire, and brimstone" (Ezekiel 38:22). Togarmah's last appearance in Scripture is as one of these "many peoples."

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God is sovereign over this invasion. He is the one who brings Gog against His land, so that the nations may know Him when He shows Himself holy before their eyes (Ezekiel 38:16). The chapter ends with God's purpose to magnify and make Himself known in the sight of many nations (Ezekiel 38:23). Togarmah marches for its own reasons, yet its march serves the revelation of the Lord's glory. Distant peoples who never knew Israel's God are drawn into an event designed to make Him known.

Ezekiel's list also echoes Genesis 10. Magog, Meshech, Tubal, Gomer, and Togarmah come from Japheth's line, and Cush and Put come from Ham's (Genesis 10:2–3, 6). The families God scattered and ordered after the flood reappear united against the people through whom He promised to bless all the families of the earth (Genesis 12:3). Ezekiel does not draw this connection in so many words, but the overlap in names is too strong to be accidental. A people first named in a neutral genealogy is later counted among those opposing God's purposes.

The phrase "far north" is explicit in the text (Ezekiel 38:6, 15). For Israel, the far north marked the edge of the known world. Its presence in the coalition shows that God's rule reaches the most distant peoples. They cannot stay out of His purposes by being far away, and their defeat leaves Israel secure under the Lord's protection.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Revelation describes a final rebellion in which Satan, released after the thousand years, goes out "to deceive the nations which are in the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, to gather them together to battle" (Revelation 20:8). They surround "the camp of the saints and the beloved city," and "fire came down from God out of heaven and devoured them" (Revelation 20:9). The language clearly draws on Ezekiel 38–39, including the judgment of fire in Ezekiel 38:22. Togarmah is not named in Revelation, and interpreters differ on how the two passages relate in time and detail. Still, Ezekiel's coalition, Togarmah included, supplies the pattern Revelation uses. In Revelation the scope widens from a northern alliance to "the four corners of the earth."

Revelation also shows who stands behind the coalition. Ezekiel's nations gather under Gog, but Revelation reveals Satan as the deceiver who gathers them. The final victory belongs to God, and it secures the people who belong to the Lamb. The rebellion that Togarmah's name represents ends at the hand of God, not in the downfall of His people.

5. Spirit & Life

Togarmah's few appearances reveal the God who governs the whole map, including its margins. Israel likely knew little about the house of Togarmah beyond its horses. Yet God named it, knew its place, and determined its end. No nation's distance or obscurity puts it beyond His reach, whether in judgment or in mercy. The same God who judges the nations also promised that Japheth would "dwell in the tents of Shem" (Genesis 9:27). He also foretold messengers sent to distant nations, including Tubal, to declare His glory (Isaiah 66:19). Togarmah's appearance in Gog's army does not mean God's purpose for the far north ends in fire.

The connection between Ezekiel 27 and 38 also deserves reflection. Togarmah traded horses and steeds to Tyre, and a few chapters later Gog's army comes "riding on horses." Scripture does not say that Togarmah's trade equipped that army, so this should be held as an observation rather than a stated fact. Still, Togarmah's whole recorded activity concerns horses, first as goods sold to a proud city and then as the strength of an army set against God's people. Scripture elsewhere warns against trusting in horses rather than in the Lord (Psalm 20:7; Isaiah 31:1). Togarmah's horses could not save it. What a people trusts in and profits from in times of ease often reveals where it will stand when God's purposes are contested.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 10:3 — Names Togarmah as a son of Gomer in Japheth's line, placing him in God's record of the post-flood nations.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:6 — Repeats the genealogy, with the brother's name spelled "Diphath."

  • Ezekiel 27:14 — Shows the house of Togarmah trading horses, steeds, and mules with Tyre.

  • Ezekiel 38:6 — Places the house of Togarmah "from the far north" in Gog's coalition.

  • Ezekiel 38:15–16, 22–23 — Describes the mounted army, God's purpose to make Himself known, and His judgment by fire and hail.

  • Revelation 20:7–9 — Takes up Ezekiel's "Gog and Magog" for the final rebellion and its end by fire from heaven.
