MIDST, AMONG, MIDDLE - (tâvek, Strong's H8432)

Summary

The Hebrew word tâvek means midst, middle or among. It names the center of something, such as the middle of a river, the inside of a camp, or a place among a group of people. It is an ordinary word. Scripture uses it for a garden, a sea, a sacrificial animal, a basket, and a city. Yet the Bible's largest promises are attached to it. When God told Moses, "let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8), He used this word for where He intended to live. He did not plan to rule from a distant height. He planned to live at the center of His people's camp.

That one desire runs from beginning to end. The tree of life stood in the midst of Eden, and fallen Adam and Eve hid among its trees. God made a path through the midst of the Red Sea and the Jordan River. He required a clean camp because He lived inside it. When Jerusalem filled its center with idols, His presence there brought judgment, and His glory left the city. The prophets then promised that God would return to dwell in Zion's midst forever. Jesus Christ fulfills this hope. He was crucified between two others, He now stands in the midst of His people, and the Lamb is seen in the midst of the throne. For the believer, the word raises a searching question: who occupies the center of my life?

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tâvek is a common Hebrew noun found throughout the Old Testament. Most often it is joined to small words meaning "in" or "from," producing the familiar English phrases "in the midst of" and "out of the midst of." The NKJV usually renders it "midst" or "among," and sometimes "middle," "within," "inside," or "center."

The word appears in almost every kind of setting. It occurs in creation, in the laws for Israel's camp, in battle stories, in psalms of suffering, and in prophetic visions. Ezekiel uses it far more than any other book. His prophecy is concerned with what has happened at the center of Jerusalem and what God will place there in the future.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tâvek marks the central or inner position of something. Context shows whether that center is a physical place or a position within a group.

1. The middle or interior of a place or object. The word can describe the center of a space or the inside of something. Examples include the firmament "in the midst of the waters" (Genesis 1:6) and the animals Abram cut "down the middle" (Genesis 15:10).

2. Among, in the company of. The word can also mean being within a group of people and sharing life with them. God promised, "I will dwell among the children of Israel" (Exodus 29:45).

3. The inner self. In poetry the word describes the inside of a person. The suffering psalmist says, "My heart is like wax; It has melted within Me" (Psalm 22:14).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of the word depends on what or who stands at the center. In the creation account it marks the place where God sets order. God speaks a firmament into the middle of the waters, and that firmament divides them (Genesis 1:6). In Exodus through Numbers the word is used repeatedly for the camp, with the tabernacle placed at its center. In Joshua it describes the riverbed where the priests stood. In the Psalms and Proverbs it turns inward, to the heart.

The small words joined to tâvek matter. "In the midst" describes something present or dwelling at the center. "Out of the midst" describes something coming from the center or being removed from it. At Sinai, Israel heard God speak out of the midst of the fire (Deuteronomy 4:12). Centuries later, the glory of the LORD went up from the midst of Jerusalem (Ezekiel 11:23). Most uses of "among" are ordinary, and the word should not be loaded with meaning in every verse. The weighty uses stand out clearly from their contexts.

4. Theological Significance

The God of Israel chose to be near. The surrounding nations pictured their gods as kings set apart above their people. The LORD placed His dwelling in the middle of an encampment of former slaves, with the tribes arranged around it. In the Bible, the center is the place where God draws near to His people and lives in fellowship with them.

God's nearness also made holiness necessary. People with defiling conditions were put outside the camp so they would not defile "their camps in the midst of which I dwell" (Numbers 5:3). The land itself was not to be polluted with bloodshed, because the LORD dwelt among the children of Israel (Numbers 35:34). Deuteronomy 23:10–11 applies the same care to the war camp. An unclean man was to stay outside until he had washed, and then he could come back inside. God's presence at the center was a holy presence, and it gave the people a reason to live carefully every day.

The center can also hold evil. In Zechariah's vision, a woman called "Wickedness!" sits "inside the basket." The angel then thrusts her down and seals the basket with lead (Zechariah 5:7–8). God does not leave evil at the center of His people. He confines it and carries it away.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 25:8; 29:45–46; Leviticus 26:11–12 — the reason Israel was redeemed. Exodus 29:46 states the purpose of the rescue from Egypt plainly. God "brought them up out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them." The Exodus was not an end in itself. God freed His people so that He could live in their midst. Leviticus 26 links this to the heart of the relationship: "I will set My tabernacle among you, and My soul shall not abhor you. I will walk among you and be your God, and you shall be My people" (vv. 11–12). The word "walk" recalls Eden, where God walked in the garden. What humanity lost there, God began to restore in a tent at the center of Israel's camp.

Genesis 2:9; 3:3, 8 — the center of the garden. Genesis 2:9 places the tree of life "in the midst of the garden" and names the tree of the knowledge of good and evil beside it. Eden's center held both God's richest gift and humanity's test. In Genesis 3:3 Eve speaks of the forbidden tree as "the tree which is in the midst of the garden," and from then on her attention stays on that tree. Genesis 2:9 is the verse that clearly locates the tree of life; Genesis 3:3 is Eve's own description of the other tree. After they ate, Adam and his wife "hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God among the trees of the garden" (3:8). The same Hebrew word stands behind "among," but here it simply means they hid within the trees, not that they stood at the garden's center. Before the fall, the garden's trees were God's provision to enjoy. After it, they became a hiding place. Sin did not change the God who walked in the garden. It made His approach a cause of fear.

Exodus 14:16, 22; Joshua 3:17; 4:3 — the path through the middle. God told Moses to divide the sea so that Israel could "go on dry ground through the midst of the sea" (Exodus 14:16). The water that should have drowned them stood as walls on either side (vv. 22, 29). At the Jordan, the priests carrying the ark "stood firm on dry ground in the midst of the Jordan" until the whole nation had crossed (Joshua 3:17). Twelve memorial stones were taken "out of the midst of the Jordan, from the place where the priests' feet stood firm" (4:3). Israel built its memorial from the very spot that should have been the place of greatest danger. God did not take His people around the water. He brought them through its center and kept them there.

Ezekiel 5:8; 11:23; 43:7–9; 48 — the center defiled, abandoned, and restored. Ezekiel's many uses of the word trace a single story. Because Jerusalem had defiled its center, God said, "I, even I, am against you and will execute judgments in your midst in the sight of the nations" (Ezekiel 5:8). In 22:19–22 He pictures gathering the people into the midst of the city as metal is gathered into a furnace and melting them there. The same presence that blesses the faithful burns against those who rebel. Then the glory leaves "from the midst of the city" (11:23).

Ezekiel's book does not end with that departure. In the vision of the new temple, God promises to dwell "in the midst of the children of Israel forever" (43:7; compare 43:9). When the land is divided again in chapter 48, the sanctuary is placed again and again at its "center" (48:8, 10, 21). The book that watched God leave the center ends with God at the center permanently.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Bible's story can be traced through what occupies the center. In Eden the tree of life stood at the center. At Sinai the tabernacle stood there, and in the prophets God promised His own return. Ezekiel 37:26 speaks of an everlasting covenant of peace in which God says, "I will set My sanctuary in their midst forevermore." Zechariah adds, "For I," says the LORD, "will be a wall of fire all around her, and I will be the glory in her midst" (2:5). Revelation brings these promises together: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them" (21:3). The tree of life appears again, "in the middle of its street" (22:2).

Christ is the one in whom God comes to the center. Psalm 22 holds both sides of His work. First the sufferer says, "My heart is like wax; It has melted within Me" (v. 14), describing anguish that reaches His innermost being. Then the same voice says, "In the midst of the assembly I will praise You" (v. 22). Hebrews 2:12 places those words in the mouth of Jesus. The Savior who was crushed within now stands among His brothers and sisters and leads their praise.

The New Testament's Greek word for "middle" continues the imagery. This is a different word, so these verses show the same picture, not the same Hebrew term. John records that Jesus was crucified "and two others with Him, one on either side, and Jesus in the center" (John 19:18). His place between two condemned men is a true historical detail, and it invites comparison with the Old Testament pattern. The meaning of His death, though, comes from what Scripture teaches about the cross, not from where His cross stood. Genesis 15 offers one such comparison. Abram cut the animals "down the middle" (v. 10). After a deep sleep had fallen on Abram (v. 12), there appeared "a smoking oven and a burning torch that passed between those pieces" (v. 17). These were signs of God's own presence, and they passed between the pieces alone. Jeremiah 34:18–19 shows what that act meant. The leaders and people of Judah had "cut the calf in two and passed between the parts of it" when they made a covenant, and by breaking it they brought the fate of the slain calf on themselves. In Genesis 15 God alone made that pledge. Set beside the cross, the comparison helps the reader see that God in Christ bore the cost of His own promise.

The crucified and risen Christ now holds the place of fellowship at the center of His people. John sees Him "in the midst of the seven lampstands," which are His churches (Revelation 1:13). He also sees "in the midst of the throne... a Lamb as though it had been slain" (Revelation 5:6). The center of heaven is occupied by a Savior who still bears the marks of His sacrifice.

7. Spirit & Life

The first thing this word teaches is that God wants to be near. He does not tolerate His people from a distance. He redeemed Israel so that He could live in the middle of the camp. Today, through Christ, He lives among His gathered people by His Spirit. Haggai encouraged discouraged builders with the same assurance: "My Spirit remains among you; do not fear!" (Haggai 2:5).

Scripture also brings the center inward. The father in Proverbs urges his son, "Keep them in the midst of your heart" (Proverbs 4:21). The Messiah says, "Your law is within my heart" (Psalm 40:8), and Hebrews 10:5–7 applies that psalm to Jesus. The heart becomes something like a small tabernacle. Israel's camp had to be kept clean because God lived at its center, and the same holds true of a believer's inner life. Fear, ambition, and old idols compete for that place. The question is not whether something will rule the center. The question is who will.

The Jordan crossing gives believers a quieter comfort. God did not lift Israel over the river. He stopped it and had His priests stand in its riverbed until the last person had crossed. Many believers find themselves in the middle of a crisis with no visible way around it. The stones Israel took from the riverbed tell them where God's faithfulness is often proved most clearly: in the deepest part of the trial.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 3:2, 4 — The Angel of the LORD appeared to Moses in a flame of fire from the midst of a bush, and God called to him from the midst of the bush. God's holy presence was at the center of the bush, yet the bush was not consumed.

  • Numbers 16:47 — When a plague broke out against the people, Aaron ran with incense into the midst of the assembly and made atonement. A priest stepped into the center of the danger to save the people.

  • Isaiah 6:5 — Isaiah confesses that he dwells in the midst of a people of unclean lips. The prophet sees that he shares in the uncleanness that surrounds him.

  • Ezekiel 1:16 — The living creatures' wheels looked like "a wheel in the middle of a wheel." These wheels moved God's throne-chariot in any direction without turning, showing that His presence is never confined to one place.

  • Ezekiel 9:4 — God commanded that a mark be placed on those who sighed and cried over the abominations done in the midst of Jerusalem. Even in a defiled city, God knew and protected those who grieved over its sin.

  • Genesis 18:24 — Abraham asks about "fifty righteous within the city." His plea shows that God takes notice of the righteous who live within a corrupt city.
