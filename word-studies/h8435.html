GENERATIONS, GENEALOGY, HISTORY - (tôwlᵉdâh, Strong's H8435)

Summary

Tôwlᵉdâh is the Hebrew word for the "generations" that come from a person or a starting point: the descendants, family lines, and unfolding history that follow from an origin. The NKJV translates it "genealogy," "genealogies," "generations," "history," "records," and once "birth." It gives Genesis its framework. Again and again the book pauses to say, "This is the genealogy of…" or "This is the history of…", and each time it opens a new chapter of what God is doing in the world. Numbers uses it to count Israel tribe by tribe, Chronicles uses it to rebuild a nation after exile, and Ruth uses it to trace the family line that leads to David.

The word matters because God's purposes move through real families across real centuries. The line of promise passes through famine, family betrayal, slavery, and the dark days of the Judges, and none of these breaks it. Those lists of names lead to the opening sentence of the New Testament, "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ" (Matthew 1:1). In one of its most moving uses, the word describes the order in which Israel's sons were engraved on stones that the high priest carried on his shoulders into the presence of God.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tôwlᵉdâh belongs to the family of the Hebrew verb yālad (H3205), "to bear, bring forth, beget," and it appears in the plural. Its home is the family record. It appears in Genesis, Exodus, Numbers, Ruth, and 1 Chronicles, in narrative headings, census lists, priestly instructions, and genealogies. In the ancient world your ancestry told people who you were, where you belonged, and what you could inherit. Israel's records also carried something more: the line through which God had promised to work.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word refers to what comes forth from an origin. Usually it points forward to what a person produced or what became of his line, and it seldom looks back at where he came from. Context determines how much of that "what came forth" is in view.

1. A record of descendants or family line: a genealogy or list of generations. "Now this is the genealogy of Perez: Perez begot Hezron" (Ruth 4:18).

2. The account or history that unfolds from a starting point: "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created" (Genesis 2:4). Here no children are involved at all, only what followed from creation.

3. Order of birth or family arrangement, as when the sons of Israel are listed "in order of their birth" (Exodus 28:10).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Genesis the word appears in a repeated heading, "This is the genealogy (or history) of…", which opens a new section of the book. Because it looks forward, the person named in the heading is often not the main character of the section that follows. "This is the genealogy of Terah" (Genesis 11:27) begins the story of Abram. "This is the history of Jacob" (Genesis 37:2) moves immediately to seventeen-year-old Joseph. The heading names the root and the narrative follows the branch.

In Numbers 1 the phrase "their genealogies by their families, by their fathers' house" is repeated for each tribe as Israel is counted for military service. In 1 Chronicles the word organizes tribal and Levitical records, such as the Benjamites listed "according to their generations" (1 Chronicles 9:9). Numbers 3:1 uses it for the "records" of Aaron and Moses, the priestly family.

4. Theological Significance

The repeated headings in Genesis show that God tells the story of the world as a story of families. The book does not divide history into abstract ages. It follows what came from the heavens and the earth, from Adam, from Noah, from Shem, from Terah, Isaac, and Jacob. God's promise of a Seed who would crush the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15) was carried forward by people who were born, married, failed, and died. The headings keep appearing after the Flood, after Babel, and through Jacob's troubled household. No human catastrophe stops the account.

The records also narrow. Genesis 10:32 surveys "the families of the sons of Noah, according to their generations, in their nations." From that wide view Scripture selects Shem (11:10), then Terah (11:27), then Isaac (25:19), then Jacob (37:2). Other lines are honored and recorded, including Ishmael (25:12) and Esau (36:1, 9), but the narrative keeps following one family. God's care for the nations and His particular choice of a line of promise stand together on the same pages.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Exodus 28:9–12: generations carried on the priest's shoulders. God told Moses to take two onyx stones and engrave on them the names of Israel's sons, six on each stone, "in order of their birth." The stones were set on the shoulder pieces of Aaron's ephod, and Aaron was to bear their names before the LORD on his two shoulders as a memorial (paraphrasing 28:12). The detail of birth order matters. Israel was not represented as an anonymous crowd or by a single symbol for the nation. Each son was named, in the order of his family's history, the same history Genesis traces through all its pain. When Aaron entered the holy place, he went in carrying that history on his body. The same chapter places the names again on the breastplate over his heart (28:29), so Israel was carried both on the priest's shoulders and over his heart.

Genesis 36 and 37: two histories side by side. Esau's generations are recorded carefully (36:1, 9). His line produces chiefs and settled territory, and kings reigned in Edom before any king reigned in Israel (36:31). Then comes "This is the history of Jacob," and the story turns at once to Joseph's report about his brothers, their jealousy, the pit, and Egypt. The line of promise looks weaker at this point. In the arrangement of Genesis, Esau's record of chiefs and kings stands beside the account of Jacob's household breaking apart. Genesis 36:31 places those kings before Israel's monarchy; it does not date them to Joseph's lifetime. Yet Joseph's suffering becomes the means by which God preserves the whole family (Genesis 50:20). Scripture recorded both lines faithfully, and it did not measure the line of promise by how quickly it prospered.

Numbers 1: known by name before the wilderness. God commanded a census "by their families, by their fathers' houses, according to the number of names" (Numbers 1:2), and each tribe's count opens with "their genealogies." Israel was about to march through the desert and fight for the land, and God arranged them by family, gave each tribe its standard (2:2), and set them around the tabernacle. Each family knew where it belonged and who stood beside it.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Genesis 5:1 begins, "This is the book of the genealogy of Adam." Matthew opens with almost the same words: "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham" (Matthew 1:1). The first book traces what came from Adam, which includes the repeated refrain of death, with Enoch the conspicuous exception. Matthew's book announces what God has brought forth in a new Man.

Ruth 4:18 forms the bridge between them. "Now this is the genealogy of Perez" links Judah's family, through a Moabite widow and a faithful redeemer in the days when the judges ruled, to David. Matthew picks up that same line, Perez, Boaz, Ruth, Jesse, and David, and carries it to Christ.

The high priest's onyx stones look ahead to Jesus as well. Hebrews presents Him as the High Priest who always lives to make intercession for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). Aaron carried Israel's names into an earthly sanctuary for a limited time. Christ carries His people before the Father permanently. The New Testament does not cite Exodus 28:10 directly, but the picture fits what Hebrews teaches about His priesthood.

After the exile, the Chronicler gathered Israel's generations again, tracing the Benjamites of Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 9:9), the Levitical heads who served "throughout their generations" (9:34), and David's search of the Hebronite records (26:31). The returning community needed to know who belonged and who could serve in worship. The empire that had scattered them could not erase their family lines, and those lines still led toward the promised Son of David.

7. Spirit & Life

The heading in Genesis 37:2 announces Jacob's history, and what follows is mostly Joseph's betrayal, false accusation, and long years in prison. The narrative calls this "the history of Jacob," and God was at work in it before anyone in the family could see what He was doing. A believer's life may seem to stall in the same way, through a betrayal, a long delay, or a family that seems to be falling apart, while God is still writing that history and carrying His purpose forward through it.

Numbers 1 and Exodus 28 together say something about how God sees His people. In the census, no family was lost in the crowd of the camp. On the priest's shoulders, no son's name was left off and none was out of order. Christians belong to a High Priest who knows them by name. The painful chapters in a family story, like those in the accounts of Tamar, Ruth, and Joseph, do not place anyone beyond the grace that carries God's people.

Ruth 4:18 also changes how ordinary faithfulness looks. A threshing floor in Bethlehem and a redeemer's quiet integrity became a line in the genealogy of the King. Most of the people in these lists did nothing the Bible narrates. They are named because God was working through them, often in ways they never saw.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:4 — "This is the history of the heavens and the earth" applies the word to creation itself and introduces Eden, the fall, and what followed.

  • Genesis 6:9 — "This is the genealogy of Noah. Noah was a just man… Noah walked with God." The heading moves at once from the record to the man's character.

  • Genesis 11:10 — "This is the genealogy of Shem" begins the line that runs from after the Flood to Abram.

  • Genesis 25:12, 19 — Ishmael's generations are recorded before Isaac's, so God's care extends to the son outside the line of promise.

  • Numbers 3:1 — The "records" of Aaron and Moses introduce the priestly family appointed to serve at the tabernacle.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3205 (yālad), lexical relative: the verb "to bear, beget," from the same word family. Its forms run throughout the lists, as in "Perez begot Hezron" (Ruth 4:18).

  • Canonical pairing, Genesis 5:1 and Matthew 1:1: both open with "the book of the genealogy," setting Adam's line beside the genealogy of Jesus Christ.

10. Other Relevant Insights

English "generation" often translates a different Hebrew word. In Genesis 6:9, tôwlᵉdâh is the "genealogy" of Noah, while "perfect in his generations" uses other wording. Psalm 24:6, "the generation of those who seek Him," also uses a different term. These passages can enrich a study of God's people across the ages, but they are not evidence for the meaning of tôwlᵉdâh.
