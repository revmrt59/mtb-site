TURTLEDOVE - (tôwr, Strong's H8449)

Summary

The Hebrew word tôwr names the turtledove, a small, gentle, migratory bird of the dove family. Most of its biblical uses are found at the altar. In Leviticus, God allowed a poor Israelite who could not afford a lamb to bring "two turtledoves or two young pigeons" instead. Poverty did not shut anyone out of atonement, cleansing, or restoration. The bird appears even earlier, in Genesis 15, among the animals Abram brought when God bound Himself by promise. Centuries later, Mary and Joseph brought this same offering of the poor when they presented the infant Jesus at the temple.

Outside the sacrificial laws, the turtledove becomes a picture. A psalmist calls Israel God's own turtledove, defenseless before a "wild beast," and pleads with God not to abandon "the life of Your poor." Jeremiah notes that the turtledove keeps its seasons faithfully while God's people do not know Him. In the Song of Solomon, the turtledove's voice announces that winter is past. Taken together, these uses show a God who makes Himself reachable by the poor, who treasures the weak as His own, and who calls His people to recognize His time and return to Him.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tôwr is the ordinary Hebrew name for the turtledove. Many scholars think the name imitates the bird's soft, repeated cooing, but the word's meaning in Scripture comes from how it is used. Turtledoves migrate. They leave the land in winter and return in spring, so their song was a familiar sign of the changing year. They were also plentiful and cheap. That made them suitable as an offering for people with little money.

The word appears fourteen times. Eleven uses come in sacrificial settings in Genesis, Leviticus, and Numbers. In these passages the turtledove is usually paired with the "young pigeon," a closely related bird. The other three uses are in poetry and prophecy: Psalms, Song of Solomon, and Jeremiah.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tôwr has one meaning: the turtledove, a wild dove known for its gentleness, its migration, and its mournful call. The word itself carries no hidden theological meaning. Its significance comes from the roles God gave the bird in worship and from the qualities that poets and prophets saw in it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the law, the turtledove is a literal offering. It could serve as a burnt offering in its own right (Leviticus 1:14). More often it appears in pairs, with one bird for a sin offering and one for a burnt offering. Sometimes the birds were the standard offering. That was the case for cleansing after a bodily discharge (Leviticus 15:14, 29) and for a Nazirite whose vow had been defiled (Numbers 6:10). At other times they were the poor person's substitute for a lamb (Leviticus 5:7; 12:8; 14:22).

In poetry, the bird's natural traits become the point. Psalm 74 uses its defenselessness. Jeremiah 8 uses its reliable migration. Song of Solomon 2 uses its springtime return. Context decides which trait is in view, so none of these meanings should be read into every occurrence.

4. Theological Significance

The turtledove shows that God's holiness and His mercy work together. The law tied atonement closely to blood. Hebrews 9:22 sums up that principle with care: "according to the law almost all things are purified with blood, and without shedding of blood there is no remission." Yet God wrote a provision for the poor into that same law, so that a person's poverty would not keep them from drawing near to Him. The cheaper offering did not bring a lesser forgiveness. Leviticus promises the same atonement to the one who brings birds as to the one who brings a lamb. God even went one step further. Someone who could not afford two turtledoves could bring a tenth of an ephah of fine flour as a sin offering. The priest burned a handful of it on the altar, made atonement, and "it shall be forgiven him" (Leviticus 5:11–13). Hebrews states the law's general principle about blood, while Leviticus 5 records the specific provision the law itself made for the very poor. God set the minimum within reach of the poorest worshiper.

The poetic uses add another truth. God values what the world counts cheap and weak. The bird of the poor man's offering becomes the psalmist's name for God's beloved people. Jeremiah's use shows that creation keeps the order God set for it. That makes human refusal to know God look all the more unnatural.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Leviticus 5:7: the offering of the poor. "If he is not able to bring a lamb, then he shall bring to the LORD, for his trespass which he has committed, two turtledoves or two young pigeons." The phrase "not able" recurs in the purification laws for new mothers (Leviticus 12:8) and for cleansed lepers (14:22). Each time it makes room for the person who cannot pay. These were often people who had already been set apart by uncleanness, illness, or need. God did not lower His standard of holiness for them. He lowered the cost of meeting it.

Genesis 15:9–10: the birds at Abram's sacrifice. When God confirmed His covenant with Abram, He told him to bring a heifer, a female goat, a ram, a turtledove, and a young pigeon. These are the same kinds of animals that later make up Israel's blood sacrifices. Abram cut the larger animals in half, "but he did not cut the birds in two" (Genesis 15:10). Leviticus later gives the same rule for a bird burnt offering: "he shall split it at its wings, but shall not divide it completely" (Leviticus 1:17). From the beginning, the smallest offering had a place beside the largest.

Psalm 74:19: "Your turtledove." Psalm 74 mourns a sanctuary burned by enemies. The psalmist prays, "Oh, do not deliver the life of Your turtledove to the wild beast! Do not forget the life of Your poor forever." The second line explains the first: God's turtledove is God's poor. The psalmist does not appeal to Israel's strength, because Israel has none left. He appeals to God's ownership. The bird belongs to God, and its helplessness is the reason he asks God to act.

Jeremiah 8:7: the bird that knows its time. "Even the stork in the heavens Knows her appointed times; And the turtledove, the swift, and the swallow Observe the time of their coming. But My people do not know the judgment of the LORD." Just before this verse, God laments that His people fall and do not get up, turn away and do not return (8:4–6). The birds always come back when their season arrives. Judah, which had been given the law and the prophets, could not recognize what God required or see that the time to return had come. The bird's instinct puts the people's stubbornness to shame.

Song of Solomon 2:12: the voice of spring. "The flowers appear on the earth; The time of singing has come, And the voice of the turtledove Is heard in our land." The beloved's call frames this verse: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away" (2:10, 13). The reason given is "the winter is past" (2:11). The turtledove's song proves that the season has changed. It is the sound that invites the beloved to come out and meet the one who loves her.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The turtledove's clearest link to Christ comes in Luke 2:24. Forty days after Jesus' birth, Mary and Joseph came "to offer a sacrifice according to what is said in the law of the Lord, 'A pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons.'" This was the provision of Leviticus 12:8 for a mother who could not afford a lamb. The Son of God entered the world in a family that qualified for the poor person's offering. He was not raised above the law's humblest provision. He placed Himself under it. There is a striking resonance here as well: the family brought no lamb, yet they carried in their arms the one John would call "the Lamb of God" (John 1:29).

The poetic images can also be set beside Jesus' life by way of comparison. He was the faithful one who knew His appointed time. John repeatedly notes that His "hour" had not yet come, and later that it had (John 13:1). He set out toward Jerusalem without wavering, in contrast to the people of Jeremiah 8:7. Two further pictures belong to devotional reflection rather than to patterns these texts establish. Christ at the cross, handed over without defending Himself, can call to mind the defenseless turtledove of Psalm 74, and His resurrection can be pictured as a spring that no winter will follow. Psalm 74 and Song of Solomon 2 do not speak of Christ, so these images serve worship and reflection, not the interpretation of those passages.

7. Spirit & Life

No one is too poor to come to God. The law's repeated phrase "if he is not able" shows God anticipating the limits of His people and meeting them there. The grace that provided a pair of birds, and then a little fine flour, now offers Christ freely. The believer who feels they have little to bring stands on the same ground as anyone else.

The undivided bird adds a quiet lesson. The smallest offering was still offered whole. Having little to give has never meant that God accepts a divided heart. He welcomes small gifts, but He asks for the whole person.

Psalm 74 teaches God's people how to pray when they are weak. The psalmist did not hide Israel's helplessness. He named it and placed it before God as an appeal: "Your turtledove... Your poor." Believers can pray the same way, bringing their weakness to God on the basis that they belong to Him.

Jeremiah's turtledove raises a searching question: do I recognize when God is calling me to return? The birds respond to the change of season without delay. Song of Solomon 2 offers a picture of the other side of that call. In the Song these are the beloved's words to his bride: "Rise up, my love, my fair one, And come away" (2:10). As a pastoral analogy, they describe how God often renews His call when a winter of distance or hardship ends. The season has changed, and He invites His people to come near. Hearing God's call and responding to it is part of life with Him.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 1:14. A turtledove or young pigeon could be brought as a whole burnt offering, the first mention of birds as a full sacrifice in their own right.

  • Leviticus 5:11. If even two turtledoves were too costly, a tenth of an ephah of fine flour was allowed. This shows how far God's provision reached.

  • Leviticus 12:6. A new mother brought a lamb and a turtledove or young pigeon. When she could not afford the lamb, the birds served for both offerings (12:8).

  • Leviticus 14:22, 30. A poor man being cleansed from leprosy brought turtledoves or pigeons "such as he is able to afford," so his restoration did not depend on his means.

  • Leviticus 15:14, 29. For cleansing after a bodily discharge, two turtledoves or pigeons were the standard offering for both men and women.

  • Numbers 6:10. A Nazirite whose vow was broken by contact with a dead body brought two turtledoves or pigeons on the eighth day and then began the vow again.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H3123 (yônâh), dove or young pigeon. This is a closely related bird name and the turtledove's constant partner in the sacrificial laws (Leviticus 5:7; 12:8). The same Hebrew word appears in "O my dove" in Song of Solomon 2:14, just after the turtledove's song in 2:12.

  • G5167 (trygōn), turtledove. This is the Greek counterpart used in Luke 2:24 for the offering at Jesus' presentation. It is a different word in another language that names the same bird.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The New Testament's dove imagery, such as "harmless as doves" (Matthew 10:16) and the Spirit descending "like a dove" (Luke 3:22), uses a broader Greek word for dove rather than the specific word for turtledove. It belongs to the same family of images: gentleness, innocence, and a creature without weapons. These passages enrich the turtledove's portrait, but they are not uses of tôwr. Any trait the Bible gives to doves in general should not be claimed as belonging only to the turtledove.
