TEMA - (Têymâʼ, Strong's H8485)

Summary

Tema was the ninth listed son of Ishmael. His name also became the name of the tribe descended from him and of their settlement, a well-watered oasis in northwestern Arabia on the caravan routes that ran between southern Arabia and the lands to the north. The meaning of the name is uncertain. It is sometimes linked to a root meaning "desert" or to the idea of "south," but neither derivation is established. Tema should not be confused with Teman, a separate Edomite name. Tema first appears in Ishmael's family record (Genesis 25:15; 1 Chronicles 1:30), where it stands as one piece of evidence that God kept His promise to make Ishmael the father of twelve princes.

The Bible mentions Tema only five times, but each appearance carries weight. Job uses "the caravans of Tema," expert desert travelers who reach a dry streambed, as his picture of friends who failed him when he needed them most (Job 6:15–20). Isaiah calls on the people of Tema to bring water and bread to refugees fleeing war (Isaiah 21:13–15). Jeremiah names Tema among the nations that must drink the cup of the Lord's fury (Jeremiah 25:15–23). Taken together, these passages show a God who sees the remotest desert towns, who judges the security people build from wealth and geography, and who exposes every source of hope that is not Himself.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The five occurrences of Tema concern a man, his descendants, and their homeland. The first is an individual, the ninth listed of Ishmael's twelve sons (Genesis 25:15; 1 Chronicles 1:30). The second is the tribe that came from him. The third is that tribe's homeland, an oasis settlement in northwestern Arabia generally identified with modern Tayma in Saudi Arabia. In Job, Isaiah and Jeremiah the name points to the people and their land rather than to the patriarch himself.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. Some connect it to a root meaning "desert." Brown-Driver-Briggs also records a proposal of "south country" from a different proposed root, comparing it with Teman, whose name is linked to "right hand" or "south." Tema and Teman nevertheless remain different words with different Strong's numbers. The appropriate conclusion is that Tema's derivation is uncertain and may be foreign. Scripture builds no wordplay on it.

Keeping Tema and Teman apart matters. Teman (H8487) is tied to Esau and to Edom. Tema is Ishmaelite and Arabian. Scripture groups Tema with other desert peoples such as Dedan and Buz (Jeremiah 25:23), and Isaiah places it alongside Dedan and Kedar in his oracle against Arabia (Isaiah 21:13–17). Kedar, Ishmael's second son, is a separately indexed name.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

One of the promised twelve princes (Genesis 25:15; 1 Chronicles 1:30). Tema enters Scripture in the list of Ishmael's sons, which Genesis summarizes as twelve princes according to their nations (Genesis 25:16). Years earlier, God had told Abraham that He would bless Ishmael, multiply him, and make him the father of twelve princes and a great nation (Genesis 17:20). Tema's name in that list is one small proof that God did exactly what He said, even for the son who was not the line of promise.

The caravans of Tema (Job 6:15–20). By Job's day, Tema's people were known as traders who moved goods across hard desert country. Job, wounded by friends who offered accusation instead of comfort, compares them to a seasonal streambed: "My brothers have dealt deceitfully like a brook, like the streams of the brooks that pass away" (Job 6:15). Such streams run full with melting ice and snow, but "when it is hot, they vanish from their place" (Job 6:17). Caravans that turn aside to find them "go nowhere and perish" (Job 6:18). Then Job names them: "The caravans of Tema look, the travelers of Sheba hope for them" (Job 6:19). The result is bitter: "They are disappointed because they were confident; they come there and are confused" (Job 6:20). Tema's travelers were experienced desert travelers, and that is the point of the image. Experience and planning do not help when the water you counted on is gone.

An oasis for refugees (Isaiah 21:13–15). In "the burden against Arabia," the traveling companies of Dedan are driven to lodge in the thickets (Isaiah 21:13). Isaiah then turns to Tema: "O inhabitants of the land of Tema, bring water to him who is thirsty; with their bread they met him who fled" (Isaiah 21:14). These fugitives "fled from the swords, from the drawn sword, from the bent bow, and from the distress of war" (Isaiah 21:15). The oasis that grew rich on caravan traffic now becomes a place of refuge. The oracle goes on to announce that Kedar's glory will fail (Isaiah 21:16–17). The campaign behind this oracle may be Assyrian or Babylonian; the text does not name it.

Under the cup of wrath (Jeremiah 25:15–24). The Lord tells Jeremiah, "Take this wine cup of fury from My hand, and cause all the nations, to whom I send you, to drink it" (Jeremiah 25:15). The list begins with "Jerusalem and the cities of Judah" (Jeremiah 25:18). It moves through Egypt, Uz, Philistia, Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon, and reaches into the desert: "Dedan, Tema, Buz, and all who are in the farthest corners" (Jeremiah 25:23), along with "all the kings of Arabia" (Jeremiah 25:24). Tema's distance from the great powers gave it no exemption.

A historical note. Outside the Bible, Babylonian records show that Tayma later rose to unusual importance. Nabonidus, the last Neo-Babylonian king, took the oasis and lived there for about a decade in the mid-sixth century BC, while Jewish exiles were still in Babylon. Scripture never mentions this. It came after Jeremiah's oracle, which is dated to Jehoiakim's fourth year (Jeremiah 25:1). Still, it shows that the desert town the prophets named was no backwater in the eyes of the empires.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states directly that God promised Ishmael twelve princes (Genesis 17:20) and that Ishmael's sons became twelve princes (Genesis 25:16). Tema is part of that fulfillment. God's faithfulness was not limited to Isaac's line. He kept His word to Hagar's son as well.

The prophets state plainly that Tema stands under God's rule and judgment (Jeremiah 25:23). From this, a careful implication follows: the Lord's sovereignty does not stop at Israel's borders or at the edge of the trade routes. Every pairing in which Scripture places Tema also points in the same direction. Its caravans are fooled by dry streams, its peace is broken by refugees, and its wealth cannot keep the cup from its lips. Scripture does not say this in so many words, but the pattern is hard to miss. Wealth and a well-placed oasis give a feeling of security that cannot survive the day God acts.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament never mentions Tema, and nothing makes it a type of Christ. Its imagery does connect, by thematic resonance, with the larger biblical story of water and thirst. Tema's ancestor Ishmael was himself saved in the desert when God opened Hagar's eyes to see a well (Genesis 21:19). Job's streams that vanish in the heat set up a contrast that Scripture later fills. The Lord calls Himself "the fountain of living waters" in contrast to broken cisterns (Jeremiah 2:13), and Jesus offers water that becomes "a fountain of water springing up into everlasting life" (John 4:14). The failure Job describes is exactly what Christ does not do.

Isaiah's call to "bring water to him who is thirsty" also echoes in Jesus' words about those who gave drink to the thirsty and so served Him (Matthew 25:35). Jeremiah's cup of fury belongs to a theme that runs to the cup Jesus accepted in Gethsemane (Matthew 26:39) and to the final judgment on a prosperous world system in Revelation 18. These links are canonical development and resonance, not claims that Tema was meant to picture Christ.

5. Spirit & Life

Job's caravans speak to anyone who has gone to people for comfort and found nothing there. Job is not mocking his friends' weakness. He grieves that they looked like water and were not. The answer Scripture gives is not cynicism toward people but a deeper source. Some streams run only in the cool season, and God is not one of them.

Isaiah's picture adds a different word. A town with water is told to share it with the fleeing. Whatever God has entrusted to us in calm times may be what He calls on when others are running for their lives.

Jeremiah's list closes off every hiding place. Neither distance, nor desert walls, nor profitable trade puts anyone beyond the reach of God's justice. That is sobering, and it is also why the gospel matters. The only safe place on the day of judgment is under the mercy of the God who judges, and that mercy reaches people as far away as the ends of the earth.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:20; 25:15–16 – God's promise of twelve princes from Ishmael, and Tema's place among them.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:30 – Tema preserved in the Chronicler's genealogy of Ishmael.

  • Job 6:15–20 – The caravans of Tema at a vanished stream, Job's image of failed comfort.

  • Isaiah 21:13–15 – Tema called to bring water and bread to war refugees in the oracle against Arabia.

  • Jeremiah 25:15–24 – Tema among the nations that drink the cup of the Lord's fury.
