TEMAN - (Têymân, Strong's H8487)

Summary

Teman is first a man and then a land. He was the first listed son of Eliphaz and a grandson of Esau, and he became one of the chiefs of Edom (Genesis 36:11, 15). His name came to belong to an important region of Edom, so prominent that the prophets often used "Teman" to stand for the whole Edomite nation. The name means "south." It is related to the Hebrew word for "right hand," because a person facing the sunrise has the south on his right. In two Joshua boundary passages the same word appears in that plain directional sense.

Teman's reputation centered on wisdom and strength. Eliphaz "the Temanite," the first of Job's friends to speak, came from this region (Job 2:11), and Jeremiah later taunts Edom at that very point: "Is wisdom no more in Teman?" (Jeremiah 49:7). Amos, Ezekiel, Obadiah, and Jeremiah all announce judgment on Teman because Edom turned violently against its brother Jacob. Habakkuk gives Teman a surprising final role. He sees God Himself marching out of the south: "God came from Teman, The Holy One from Mount Paran" (Habakkuk 3:3). The land whose wisdom and warriors could not stand before the Lord becomes the road along which He comes in glory to save His people.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew Têymân carries the ordinary meaning "south" or "the southern region." It is built on the same root as yamin, "right hand." In ancient Israel's orientation a person faced east toward the rising sun, so the right hand pointed south. The meaning of the name is therefore well established. What is not stated in Scripture is whether the patriarch received the name because his family lived in the south, or whether the southern district later took its name from him. Genesis simply presents the man first and the territory afterward.

Three uses fall under this Strong's entry. The first is Teman the person, son of Eliphaz and grandson of Esau (Genesis 36:11; 1 Chronicles 1:36). He is listed among the chiefs of Edom (Genesis 36:15), and "chief Teman" appears again in a later list of Edom's chiefs "according to their dwelling places" (Genesis 36:40–43; 1 Chronicles 1:53). That second list may refer to clans or districts named for their founders, so it is wiser not to assume every entry there describes the same individual's personal rule.

The second use is Teman the region of Edom, the sense found in Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Amos, Obadiah, and Habakkuk. Its exact location is not fixed by Scripture. Many place it in northern or central Edom, and Ezekiel's oracle, which makes Edom desolate "from Teman; Dedan shall fall by the sword" (Ezekiel 25:13), treats Teman as one end of the land under judgment. The third use is the plain directional meaning "south." In Joshua 12:3 the NKJV renders it "southward," and Joshua 13:4 likewise uses it as a boundary term.

A related word is indexed separately. Temani (H8489), "Temanite," describes Eliphaz in Job (Job 2:11) and Husham "of the land of the Temanites," an early king of Edom (Genesis 36:34). These passages are not occurrences of H8487, but they are essential to understanding Teman's reputation. The Eliphaz of Job should not be identified with Teman's father Eliphaz, the son of Esau. The two share a name, but Scripture does not connect them.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Origins in Esau's line. Genesis 36 records how God's word to Rebekah and Isaac about Esau came true: Esau became a people with chiefs and kings "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31, paraphrased). Teman stands near the head of that family as the first listed son of Eliphaz, who was Esau's firstborn (Genesis 36:15). The record in 1 Chronicles 1 repeats the line, keeping Edom's ancestry within Israel's own memory of the nations.

A name for the south. In Joshua the word marks direction in the description of conquered and remaining land (Joshua 12:3; 13:4). For Israel, "Teman" and "south" lay close together in ordinary speech, much as a region's name can become shorthand for a direction.

A land known for wisdom. In Job, Eliphaz the Temanite is the senior friend who speaks first, with long, carefully reasoned speeches about how God deals with the righteous and the wicked. At the end of the book the Lord addresses Eliphaz by name and tells him that he and his two friends had not spoken of Him what was right (Job 42:7, paraphrased). The most eloquent voice from Teman was corrected by God, and he was restored when he and his friends offered the sacrifices God commanded and Job prayed for them (Job 42:8–9).

Judgment on Edom's pride. By the prophets' day, Teman stands for Edom at its proudest. Amos hears the Lord condemn Edom because he "pursued his brother with the sword" and kept his anger forever (Amos 1:11, paraphrased), and then announces, "I will send a fire upon Teman, Which shall devour the palaces of Bozrah" (Amos 1:12). Ezekiel charges Edom with taking vengeance on the house of Judah and declares that the land will be made desolate from Teman, while Dedan falls by the sword (Ezekiel 25:12–13). Obadiah describes Edom standing aloof and even rejoicing on the day of Jerusalem's destruction (Obadiah 10–14), and he aims at Edom's soldiers: "Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed, To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau May be cut off by slaughter" (Obadiah 9).

Jeremiah's oracle, which shares a good deal of language with Obadiah, strikes at Teman's wisdom: "Is wisdom no more in Teman? Has counsel perished from the prudent? Has their wisdom vanished?" (Jeremiah 49:7). Later in the same chapter he calls Israel to hear the purposes the Lord has formed against the inhabitants of Teman (Jeremiah 49:20, paraphrased). Teman's counselors made plans, but the Lord had plans of His own.

The road of God's coming. Habakkuk 3 is a prayer-psalm in which the prophet recalls the Lord's past acts of deliverance and asks Him to act again. It opens with the Lord advancing from the southern wilderness: "God came from Teman, The Holy One from Mount Paran. Selah His glory covered the heavens, And the earth was full of His praise" (Habakkuk 3:3). The poem goes on: "You went forth for the salvation of Your people, For salvation with Your Anointed" (Habakkuk 3:13).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture plainly states several things. Teman descends from Esau. It became a leading part of Edom. Its people were known for wisdom and military strength. God judged it for violence against Jacob, Edom's brother nation.

The judgment matters because Edom's sin was a sin against kinship. Edom did not merely fight a foreign rival. It turned on a brother at the worst moment of his life. When the prophets single out Teman, they strike at the places Edom trusted most: its counselors and its warriors.

Some points are careful inferences rather than direct statements. Jeremiah's question in 49:7 suggests that Teman's wisdom failed precisely when it was needed, since no counsel could foresee or avert God's judgment. The pairing of Teman's wise men in Jeremiah and its mighty men in Obadiah shows a consistent prophetic aim: God dismantles the exact sources of a nation's pride.

Habakkuk then shows a different side of the same land. Older Israelite songs speak of the Lord coming from Seir and marching "from the field of Edom" (Judges 5:4), and of shining forth from Mount Paran (Deuteronomy 33:2). Habakkuk stands in that tradition. The southern wilderness, Teman included, was remembered in these songs as the region of the Sinai and wilderness theophany, where God came in glory for His people. Edom's territory is not merely a place God judges. He passes through it on His way to rescue Israel.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament does not mention Teman. Two canonical connections are still worth tracing, and the first rests on firmer ground than the second.

The first concerns wisdom. Teman's "wisdom" rose against God's purposes and collapsed. Paul writes that God has done the same with the wisdom of this world in the cross of Christ: "For it is written: 'I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, And bring to nothing the understanding of the prudent.' Where is the wise? Where is the scribe? Where is the disputer of this age? Has not God made foolish the wisdom of this world?" (1 Corinthians 1:19–20). Paul is quoting Isaiah 29:14, not Jeremiah, so this is a thematic link rather than a direct fulfillment. The pattern is nonetheless the same: God brings to nothing the wisdom people trust in place of Him. Paul then names Christ as "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24). The wisdom Teman lacked is found in Him.

The second concerns the Lord's coming from the south. Habakkuk's picture of God coming in glory for the salvation of His people, "with Your Anointed" in the NKJV, belongs to the larger biblical hope that the Lord Himself will come to rescue His people. In a related prophetic portrait, Isaiah pictures a mighty Deliverer coming "from Edom" and "from Bozrah," the city Amos paired with Teman (Isaiah 63:1, paraphrased). These passages anticipate the final arrival of God's salvation, which the New Testament finds in Jesus. They should be read as part of that canonical hope rather than as predictions that name Christ directly.

5. Spirit & Life

Teman shows that wisdom without the fear of the Lord cannot hold. Eliphaz could speak eloquently about God and still speak wrongly of Him, and the counselors of Teman could plan carefully and still be blind to the judgment approaching. Intelligence, experience, and tradition are gifts, but they do not save. The question Jeremiah put to Teman still searches every proud mind: when God acts, where will our counsel be?

Teman also shows how seriously God takes cruelty toward the vulnerable, especially toward a brother. Edom's guilt lay partly in what it did and partly in what it watched and enjoyed. Obadiah condemns standing aloof on the day of a brother's ruin. The Lord sees the bystander's gloating as clearly as the attacker's sword.

Habakkuk lifts the eyes past all of this. He wrote while facing invasion and loss, and he did not pray by looking at Edom's strength or Babylon's armies. He remembered the God who came from Teman with glory covering the heavens. The land of Edom, an enemy of Judah, was the very region from which Habakkuk saw God coming. No territory is closed to Him, and no enemy's land lies outside His rule. That is why the same prophet could end his prayer rejoicing in the God of his salvation even when the fields failed (Habakkuk 3:17–18, paraphrased).

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:11, 15; 1 Chronicles 1:36: Teman as son of Eliphaz, grandson of Esau, and a chief of Edom.

  • Genesis 36:42; 1 Chronicles 1:53: Teman among Edom's chiefs listed by their dwelling places, linking the name with territory.

  • Joshua 12:3; 13:4: The word used in its directional sense, "south."

  • Job 2:11 (related H8489): Eliphaz the Temanite, the background for Teman's reputation for wisdom.

  • Jeremiah 49:7, 20: God's challenge to Teman's wisdom and His purposes against its inhabitants.

  • Ezekiel 25:12–13: Judgment on Edom, desolate "from Teman" with Dedan falling by the sword, for vengeance against Judah.

  • Amos 1:11–12: Fire on Teman for Edom's relentless hostility toward his brother.

  • Obadiah 9: The dismay of Teman's mighty men in the judgment on Esau.

  • Habakkuk 3:3: God coming from Teman in glory, the highest use of the name.

  • Deuteronomy 33:2; Judges 5:4: Earlier songs of the Lord coming from the southern wilderness, which Habakkuk echoes.

  • 1 Corinthians 1:19–20: A New Testament parallel showing God bringing worldly wisdom to nothing.
