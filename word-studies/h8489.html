TEMANITE - (Têymânîy, Strong's H8489)

Summary

"Temanite" names a person who comes from Teman, a leading region or town of Edom, the land settled by the descendants of Esau. The word describes origin, so its meaning is simply "one from Teman." The place name Teman is generally connected with the Hebrew word for "right hand," and from that with "south," because a person facing east to get his bearings has the south on his right. That derivation is widely held but best treated as probable. In Scripture the label is attached to two men. Husham "of the land of the Temanites" was an early king of Edom (Genesis 36:34; 1 Chronicles 1:45). Eliphaz the Temanite was the first and most prominent of Job's three friends (Job 2:11).

Most of the weight of this name rests on Eliphaz. He came to comfort Job and ended up accusing him, defending a tidy system in which he treated Job's suffering as proof of hidden wrongdoing. God's verdict was blunt: "you have not spoken of Me what is right, as My servant Job has" (Job 42:7). Later prophets mocked the famous wisdom of Teman (Jeremiah 49:7; Obadiah 8–9). Paul quoted a line from Eliphaz to show that "the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God" (1 Corinthians 3:19; Job 5:13). The story ends in grace. The Temanite sage was sent to the man he had condemned, to offer a burnt offering for himself while Job prayed for him, and God accepted Job's prayer.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

H8489 is a gentilic word, one that names a person by place or people of origin. A Temanite is someone from Teman (separately indexed as H8487), a district or settlement in Edom, southeast of the Dead Sea. Genesis also lists Teman as a grandson of Esau through his son Eliphaz (Genesis 36:10–11), so the place name and the clan name are bound together in Edom's family history.

The name means "one from Teman." Teman itself is usually linked to the Hebrew idea of the "right hand" (related to yamin, H3225) and so to "south." This is a reasonable and widely accepted explanation, though it is a probable derivation and not a certain one. Scripture builds no wordplay on the name, and nothing in the text suggests that "south" or "right hand" tells us anything about the character of the men called Temanites.

Two separate individuals bear the label, and they should not be merged:

  • Husham, who reigned as king in Edom after Jobab, "before any king reigned over the children of Israel" (Genesis 36:31, 34; 1 Chronicles 1:45).

  • Eliphaz, Job's friend and the first to answer him (Job 2:11; 4:1; 15:1; 22:1; 42:7, 9).

The friend's name, Eliphaz, is the same as that of Esau's son, the father of Teman. Scripture does not say the two are the same man, and the date of Job is uncertain, so it is best to see the shared name as a sign of a common Edomite family setting rather than as an identification.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Husham, King in Edom. Edom's king list in Genesis 36 shows a people with settled rule long before Israel had a throne. The record is spare: "When Jobab died, Husham of the land of the Temanites reigned in his place" (Genesis 36:34). Chronicles repeats it word for word (1 Chronicles 1:45). His successor is not identified as his son, which suggests a non-hereditary succession, though the text does not say whether any of his descendants later ruled. Several kings in the list are linked with different places, Baal-Hanan is identified by his father rather than a city, and the king after Husham came from yet another city (Genesis 36:35). His entry tells us that Teman was a recognized region with standing of its own in early Edom.

Eliphaz, the First Comforter. When Job lost his children, his wealth, and his health, three friends "made an appointment together to come and mourn with him, and to comfort him" (Job 2:11). Eliphaz the Temanite is named first, and he speaks first in each of the three rounds of debate (Job 4–5; 15; 22). He seems to be the eldest. He reminds Job that gray-haired and aged men stand on their side (Job 15:10).

He begins gently. He acknowledges that Job had strengthened weak hands and steadied stumbling people (Job 4:3–4). Soon, though, he states his principle: the innocent do not perish, and those who plow wickedness reap it (Job 4:7–8). He supports this with a night experience: "Now a word was secretly brought to me... Then a spirit passed before my face; The hair on my body stood up" (Job 4:12, 15). The voice he reports stresses how little God trusts any creature: "If He puts no trust in His servants, If He charges His angels with error" (Job 4:18; see also 15:15). In his second speech he appeals to tradition: "What wise men have told, Not hiding anything received from their fathers" (Job 15:18). He speaks often of "the Almighty," and much of what he says about God's greatness is true.

His system has no room for a righteous sufferer, and it hardens as the debate goes on. By his third speech Eliphaz charges Job with specific crimes for which he has no evidence. He says Job took pledges from his brothers, stripped the poor of clothing, withheld water from the weary, and sent widows away empty (Job 22:5–9). God had already called Job blameless (Job 1:8; 2:3). Eliphaz invented sins to fit his theology.

God's Verdict and Provision. After the LORD spoke to Job from the whirlwind, He turned to Eliphaz by name: "My wrath is aroused against you and your two friends, for you have not spoken of Me what is right, as My servant Job has" (Job 42:7). God then told him how to be restored: "take for yourselves seven bulls and seven rams, go to My servant Job, and offer up for yourselves a burnt offering; and My servant Job shall pray for you. For I will accept him, lest I deal with you according to your folly" (Job 42:8). The friends obeyed: "So Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite went and did as the LORD commanded them; for the LORD had accepted Job" (Job 42:9).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Eliphaz misrepresented God and that God required him to come through Job's intercession. The book of Job also shows that knowledge of God existed outside Israel's line. An Edomite who spoke of the Almighty and offered burnt offerings could still be corrected and restored by Him.

The prophets connect Teman with a reputation for wisdom. Jeremiah asks of Edom, "Is wisdom no more in Teman? Has counsel perished from the prudent? Has their wisdom vanished?" (Jeremiah 49:7). Obadiah warns that God will "destroy the wise men from Edom" and that "your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed" (Obadiah 8–9). These passages use the place name Teman, not the word "Temanite," and they address the nation's later pride and hostility toward Israel. They do not mention Eliphaz. Still, taken together, a fair conclusion follows: the Bible repeatedly portrays the celebrated wisdom of Teman as unable to stand before God. Eliphaz shows that failure in one man's counsel. The prophets announce it over a whole nation.

The prophets do not treat Teman as beyond God's reach. Habakkuk pictures God Himself coming "from Teman" in His glory (Habakkuk 3:3). The region known for human wisdom becomes the setting for God's own appearing.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The clearest New Testament link is explicit. Paul writes, "For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God. For it is written, 'He catches the wise in their own craftiness'" (1 Corinthians 3:19). He is quoting Eliphaz (Job 5:13). The sentence is true, and Paul treats it as Scripture. Yet it catches Eliphaz himself, whose cleverness about suffering missed what God was doing. Paul uses it to humble Corinthians who were proud of human wisdom. He has already shown them where true wisdom is found: "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24).

A second connection is a strong thematic resonance rather than a stated type. Job is a righteous sufferer whom his accusers counted guilty, and God accepted his prayer on their behalf. Scripture later describes the Servant who "made intercession for the transgressors" (Isaiah 53:12), and Jesus prayed for those who crucified Him (Luke 23:34). The New Testament never calls Job a type of Christ. Still, the pattern fits the larger story. People who have spoken wrongly of God are restored through sacrifice and through the prayer of the one they rejected.

5. Spirit & Life

Eliphaz is a sobering figure because he was so often right in what he said. God is holy, the wicked do reap trouble, and God does catch the wise in their craftiness. His failure lay in using true statements as a verdict on a suffering man whose case he did not understand. When his system ran out of evidence, he made some up. Correct doctrine can be used without love, and when it serves to protect our own explanations instead of to know God, it becomes cruel to others.

The ending shows God's character. He did not leave Eliphaz in his folly. He named the problem, provided a sacrifice, and appointed an intercessor. God sent the proud sage to kneel, in effect, beside the man he had wronged and to depend on that man's prayer. Scripture does not record Eliphaz's words of repentance, only his obedience, but obedience on those terms required humility. Anyone who has misjudged a hurting believer, or spoken carelessly about God's ways, can take heart that God makes a way back. It runs through sacrifice and the prayer of God's accepted servant, and for us that path is Christ.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 36:34; 1 Chronicles 1:45 — Husham of the land of the Temanites reigns in Edom, showing Teman's early standing.

  • Genesis 36:10–11 — Teman as Esau's grandson, linking the clan and place to Edom's lineage (H8487).

  • Job 2:11 — Eliphaz the Temanite introduced as Job's first comforter.

  • Job 4:7–8, 12–18 — Eliphaz's doctrine of retribution and his night vision.

  • Job 5:13 — The line later quoted by Paul.

  • Job 15:18–19 — Eliphaz's appeal to inherited tradition.

  • Job 22:5–9 — His baseless accusations against Job.

  • Job 42:7–9 — God's rebuke, the sacrifice, and Job's intercession.

  • Jeremiah 49:7; Obadiah 8–9 — Prophetic judgment on Teman's wisdom (H8487).

  • Habakkuk 3:3 — God pictured as coming from Teman (H8487).

  • 1 Corinthians 1:24; 3:19 — Worldly wisdom exposed; Christ the wisdom of God.
