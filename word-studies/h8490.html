PILLAR, COLUMN - (tîymârâh, Strong's H8490)

Summary

The Hebrew word tîymârâh means a column of smoke rising into the sky. It appears only twice in the Bible, and each time the NKJV translates it "pillars of smoke." Lexicons commonly connect it with the Hebrew word for a palm tree. If that derivation is right, it may suggest a plume that climbs straight up like a trunk and then spreads out at the top, though neither verse describes the smoke's shape. This word does not describe a stone or wooden pillar that holds up a building. It describes moving smoke, and in both passages that smoke can be seen from far away and tells watchers something important is coming.

The two uses could hardly be more different. In Song of Solomon 3:6 the columns rise from myrrh and frankincense as a royal wedding procession comes up out of the wilderness. In Joel 2:30 God Himself promises "blood and fire and pillars of smoke" as signs before the great day of the LORD, a passage Peter quoted at Pentecost. The same word for a rising column marks a bridegroom's joyful arrival and the Lord's fearful approach in judgment. Scripture teaches that God's coming is good news or terrifying news depending on who is waiting for Him, and Joel places His invitation, "whoever calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved," right beside the smoke.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tîymârâh is a rare Hebrew noun found once in poetry (Song of Solomon) and once in prophecy (Joel). Lexicons commonly relate it to tamar, the palm tree. The date palm was a familiar sight in Israel: a tall, bare trunk crowned by spreading fronds. If the word does come from that family, the likeness is easy to picture. Smoke on a still day rises in a narrow column and then opens out overhead. Because the word occurs only twice, this picture is a possible image drawn from the proposed derivation rather than a description given in either verse. It fits both scenes well, but it is not part of the word's fixed meaning.

The more common Hebrew word for a pillar is 'ammud. That word names the columns of Solomon's temple and also "the pillar of cloud" and "the pillar of fire" that led Israel (Exodus 13:21). Neither passage uses 'ammud to describe the smoke. Both writers chose a word that pictures smoke itself rising.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tîymârâh means a column or plume of smoke rising upward. One sense covers both occurrences. The difference between the Song and Joel lies in what produces the smoke and what it announces, and the word's meaning stays the same in both.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Song of Solomon 3:6 the word is part of a comparison. Something coming up from the wilderness is "like pillars of smoke," and that smoke is "perfumed with myrrh and frankincense." The image combines sight and smell: tall columns of fragrance that are seen and sensed before the procession itself arrives.

In Joel 2:30 the word is literal and joined to other signs: "Blood and fire and pillars of smoke." God is the speaker ("I will show wonders"), so the smoke is His own warning given to the whole earth. When Peter quotes the verse in Acts 2:19, his Greek wording is "vapor of smoke." This is a different word in another language, but it describes the same promised sign.

In both places the word is plural. Viewers do not see one wisp but several towering columns, which adds to the sense of something great approaching.

4. Theological Significance

Both uses of tîymârâh show smoke rising before someone arrives. The smoke points to an approaching presence: a king in the Song and the LORD in Joel. In the Bible, God does not come unannounced. He gives signs that can be seen from a distance so that people have time to recognize what is happening and get ready.

The contrast between the two passages also matters. The same kind of sign means joy in one place and dread in the other. Paul states a similar principle about the gospel: "For we are to God the fragrance of Christ among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing. To the one we are the aroma of death leading to death, and to the other the aroma of life leading to life" (2 Corinthians 2:15–16). Paul does not use this Hebrew word, but he describes the same reality. God's presence is one presence, and people experience it in opposite ways depending on how they stand toward Him.

Rising smoke also differs from the stone pillars of the Bible. Jacob set up a stone "as a pillar" at Bethel (Genesis 28:18). Solomon cast two bronze pillars eighteen cubits high (1 Kings 7:15). Job describes the earth's pillars trembling when God shakes it (Job 9:6). Those pillars stand still and bear weight. A tîymârâh moves, billows, and cannot be held in place. The word fits the approach of a living God who cannot be fixed in one location by human hands.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 3:6: Fragrance Rising from the Wilderness. "Who is this coming out of the wilderness like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense, with all the merchant's fragrant powders?" The next verses answer the question. Solomon's couch is approaching, guarded by sixty valiant men, and the passage ends by calling it "the day of his wedding" (3:7–11). The Hebrew word "this" in verse 6 could refer either to the bride or to the royal litter. In either case, the smoke announces a wedding.

The details are lavish. Myrrh and frankincense were costly imports, and the line adds "all the merchant's fragrant powders" as well. Smoke like this was expensive. Watchers would see it on the horizon and smell it before they could make out the faces in the procession. Frankincense also had a sacred history for Israel. God commanded a holy incense that included "pure frankincense" for the tabernacle, "where I will meet with you" (Exodus 30:34–36). Fragrant smoke rising "out of the wilderness" would have reminded Israel of the place where God first led and met His people.

Joel 2:30: Smoke Before the Day of the LORD. Joel's promise follows God's word, "I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh" (2:28). Then come the signs: "And I will show wonders in the heavens and in the earth: Blood and fire and pillars of smoke." The sun will be darkened and the moon turned to blood "before the coming of the great and awesome day of the LORD" (2:31). The smoke recalls war and burning cities. It also recalls Sinai, where "the LORD descended upon it in fire. Its smoke ascended like the smoke of a furnace, and the whole mountain quaked greatly" (Exodus 19:18). When God came down at Sinai, smoke rose. Joel says it will rise again when He comes to judge.

Joel does not end with the smoke. His next words are an offer: whoever calls on the name of the LORD will be saved (2:32). The pillars of smoke are a warning given while there is still time to call on Him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

On the Day of Pentecost, Peter stood before a crowd in Jerusalem and said that Joel's words were being fulfilled. He quoted the prophecy through the signs: "Blood and fire and vapor of smoke" (Acts 2:19). He then carried it to its invitation, saying that whoever calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved (Acts 2:21), and he identified that Lord as Jesus, crucified and raised (Acts 2:36). This is a direct New Testament fulfillment. The poured-out Spirit shows that the last days have begun. The smoke and darkened sun point ahead to the Day still coming, which Revelation describes as the time when people cry out to be hidden "from the wrath of the Lamb," asking, "who is able to stand?" (Revelation 6:16–17).

The Song of Solomon adds a connection of a different kind. Song of Solomon 3:6 describes Solomon's wedding procession, and the New Testament never quotes it. The link to Christ is a canonical analogy drawn from the wider biblical picture, not a prophecy the Song announces. Scripture calls Christ the Bridegroom (Matthew 9:15; John 3:29) and pictures the end of history as a wedding (Revelation 19:7). Read in that light, the fragrant columns rising before a king on his wedding day offer a fitting picture of the Bridegroom's coming. The same spices appear in Christ's own story: wise men brought frankincense and myrrh to the child (Matthew 2:11), and myrrh was used at His burial (John 19:39). The aroma of the King's approach is connected with the cost of His love.

The two occurrences should not be read as two descriptions of one event. Joel's smoke is a sign of judgment before the Day of the LORD, and Peter applies Joel's prophecy directly to the age that began at Pentecost. The Song's fragrant smoke belongs to a royal wedding, and its link to Christ comes by analogy. Placed side by side, the two passages still point to something true about the Lord Jesus: the One who will come in judgment is also the Bridegroom who comes for His bride. The present time, between Pentecost and that Day, is the period when the Spirit is poured out and the call to come to Him is still open.

7. Spirit & Life

The questions these passages raise are personal. Joel's smoke is not a mixed signal. It is a warning that the Day of the LORD is near. What differs from one person to another is whether they have called on the name of the Lord before that Day arrives. Paul's words in 2 Corinthians 2:16 sound a related note: the knowledge of Christ is life to some and death to others, and Paul's response is "who is sufficient for these things?" Joel gives the answer. No one is sufficient alone, but anyone may call on the name of the Lord. The smoke Joel describes is a mercy because it comes before the end and not after it.

The Song also asks, "Who is this coming out of the wilderness?" The wilderness was where Israel was tested and humbled, and it was also where God met them. Through Hosea, God speaks of His unfaithful people this way: "I will allure her, will bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfort to her" (Hosea 2:14). Believers in dry and lonely seasons can take heart from this pattern. The wilderness is often the place God brings His people before the fragrance and the celebration.

Finally, a column of smoke cannot be grasped, stored, or set in place like Jacob's stone. Believers do not control God's presence. We watch for Him, prepare for Him, and welcome Him when He comes.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Tîymârâh occurs only in Song of Solomon 3:6 and Joel 2:30, and both are developed above. The passages listed here help explain those two uses but do not contain this Hebrew word.

  • Exodus 13:21 — The LORD went before Israel "in a pillar of cloud" and "a pillar of fire." This passage uses the common word 'ammud and gives the background of God leading His people through the wilderness.

  • Exodus 19:18 — Smoke rose from Sinai "because the LORD descended upon it in fire." This is the clearest Old Testament picture behind Joel's smoke.

  • Acts 2:19 — Peter quotes Joel at Pentecost, rendering the sign as "vapor of smoke."

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H5982 ('ammud), pillar or column. This is a distinct word and not a lexical relative. It is the common term for structural pillars and for the pillar of cloud and fire (Exodus 13:21). Comparing the two words shows what makes tîymârâh distinctive: it describes rising smoke, not a supporting structure.

  • H8558 (tamar), palm tree. Lexicons commonly treat tamar as part of the same word family as tîymârâh. That proposed connection may suggest a palm-like shape for the rising plume, but neither verse describes the shape.
