ABRAHAM - (ʼAbrâhâm, Strong's H0085)

Summary

Abraham is the patriarch whom God called out of a family that worshiped other gods. God made him the father of Israel and, through his Seed, the father of all who believe. He was born Abram, a name usually understood to mean "exalted father." When he was ninety-nine, God renamed him Abraham, and Scripture explains the new name itself: "for I have made you a father of many nations" (Genesis 17:5). The Hebrew sound of the name echoes the phrase for "father of a multitude." That explanation is the meaning Scripture gives, even though the exact formation of the longer name is not fully clear. The Hebrew form ʼAbrâhâm appears roughly 175 times in the Old Testament. It begins at Genesis 17:5, because his earlier life is recorded under the name Abram.

From Genesis 17 onward, Abraham receives circumcision as the sign of God's covenant, pleads with God for Sodom, sees Isaac born when he and Sarah are past all natural hope, and climbs Mount Moriah to offer that son. On the mountain God provides a ram and swears by Himself to bless all nations through Abraham's seed. Abraham dies owning only a burial cave in the land God promised him. Afterward God calls Himself "the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob," and that title becomes the ground of Israel's hope through the exodus, the monarchy, the exile, and the prophets' promises of restoration.

The New Testament builds on this foundation. Matthew opens with Jesus Christ as "the Son of Abraham." Paul presents Abraham as the man God counted righteous by faith, before circumcision and long before the law, "that he might be the father of all those who believe" (Romans 4:11). Paul names Christ as the singular Seed in whom the promise lands (Galatians 3:16). Jesus Himself declares, "before Abraham was, I AM" (John 8:58). Abraham's life shows a God who calls people out of idolatry, binds His own name to them, keeps His sworn word across centuries, and gives life to the dead.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Abraham is one individual: the son of Terah, the brother of Nahor and Haran, the uncle of Lot, and the husband of Sarah. He is the father of Ishmael by Hagar, of Isaac by Sarah, and of six sons by Keturah: Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak, and Shuah (Genesis 25:1–2). Genesis 25:1 calls Keturah a wife, while 1 Chronicles 1:32 calls her Abraham's concubine. Both records stand, and the difference reflects her secondary status beside Sarah. Abraham lived as a wealthy herdsman and sojourner in southern Canaan, around Mamre, Hebron, Beersheba, and Gerar. His life is usually placed in the early second millennium BC, though scholars debate the exact dating.

The name requires careful handling because Scripture itself changes it. Until Genesis 17 he is Abram, a name separately indexed as H0087 and commonly understood as "exalted father." God then announces, "No longer shall your name be called Abram, but your name shall be Abraham; for I have made you a father of many nations" (Genesis 17:5). The explanation rests on a deliberate wordplay. The new name sounds like the Hebrew expression ʼab hamon, "father of a multitude," and the verse completes the thought with "of nations." So the meaning "father of a multitude (of nations)" is the biblical interpretation of the name, stated by God Himself. The precise linguistic formation of the extra syllable is less certain, and readers should not press the spelling beyond what the text explains.

The timing of the renaming matters. God gave the name to a ninety-nine-year-old man whose wife Sarah had never borne a child. Ishmael was already thirteen (Genesis 16:16; 17:24–25), but the promised son through Sarah had not yet been born. The name was a promise spoken before its fulfillment, and every time someone addressed him, they repeated God's word about his future. Later Scripture remembers the change explicitly: "Abram, who is Abraham" (1 Chronicles 1:27), and the Levites' prayer that God "chose Abram" and "gave him the name Abraham" (Nehemiah 9:7).

Because the Strong's entries divide his life, this study draws on three related records. H0087 (Abram) covers Genesis 11–17:4. H0085 (Abraham), the subject here, runs from Genesis 17:5 through the Old Testament. G0011, the separately indexed Greek form Abraam, carries the New Testament witness. These are not three people. They are one man described across the whole canon.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

Before the new name (recorded under Abram). Abram's story begins in Ur and Haran. Joshua later reminds Israel that Terah's family "served other gods" beyond the River until God took Abraham from there (Joshua 24:2–3). God called Abram to leave his country and family and promised to make him a great nation and a blessing to all the families of the earth (Genesis 12:1–3). In those years Abram went down to Egypt and passed Sarai off as his sister. He separated from Lot, rescued him, and received the blessing of Melchizedek (Genesis 12–14). In Genesis 15 he believed the LORD's promise of offspring as countless as the stars, and God counted his faith as righteousness (Genesis 15:6). God then bound Himself to Abram in a covenant ceremony. When the promised son seemed slow in coming, Abram had already considered Eliezer of Damascus as his heir (15:2–3), and at Sarai's suggestion he fathered Ishmael by Hagar (Genesis 16). Genesis then passes over thirteen years and records no further appearance of the LORD until chapter 17.

The covenant sign (Genesis 17). The LORD appears to Abram at ninety-nine and says, "I am Almighty God; walk before Me and be blameless" (17:1). God promises to multiply him, to make nations and kings come from him, and "to be God to you and your descendants after you" (17:7). God gives "all the land of Canaan" as an everlasting possession, "the land in which you are a stranger" (17:8). Circumcision becomes the sign of the covenant (17:11). Sarai becomes Sarah, and God promises that she will bear a son. Abraham falls on his face and laughs at the thought of a child born to a man of a hundred and a woman of ninety (17:17). He also pleads, "Oh, that Ishmael might live before You!" (17:18). God hears him concerning Ishmael but insists that the covenant line runs through Isaac. That same day Abraham circumcises himself, Ishmael, and every male in his household (17:23–26). His obedience was immediate, even while his questions remained.

The LORD at Mamre and the plea for Sodom (Genesis 18–19). Three visitors arrive, and Abraham hurries to serve them a meal (18:6–7). The LORD repeats the promise of a son, and when Sarah laughs He asks whether anything is too hard for the LORD (18:13–14). As the men set out toward Sodom, God speaks almost to Himself: He will not hide His plans from Abraham, because Abraham will become a great nation, all nations will be blessed in him, and God has chosen him to teach his household to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice (18:17–19). Then comes one of the great scenes of prayer in Scripture: "Abraham still stood before the LORD" (18:22). He calls himself "dust and ashes" (18:27). He appeals to God as the Judge of all the earth who will do right (18:25), and he asks God to spare the city for fifty righteous, then forty-five, and down to ten. God agrees at every step. Ten righteous people could not be found, but the next morning Abraham returns to the place where he had stood and sees the smoke rising (19:27). The narrator adds a striking line: when God destroyed the cities, "God remembered Abraham" and brought Lot out of the overthrow (19:29). The prayer was not wasted.

Fear in Gerar (Genesis 20). The man who had just interceded for a city now fails in his own house. In Gerar, Abraham again says Sarah is his sister, and King Abimelech takes her. God warns Abimelech in a dream, calls Abraham "a prophet" who will pray for him (20:7), and guards Sarah just before Isaac's conception. Abraham's excuse is painfully honest: he thought, "Surely the fear of God is not in this place" (20:11). Yet the pagan king proves more upright than the patriarch. Abraham then prays, and God heals Abimelech's household (20:17–18). The promise survives because God protects it, not because Abraham does.

Laughter and loss (Genesis 21). Sarah bears Isaac at the set time, when Abraham is a hundred years old (21:2–5). The name Isaac recalls laughter, and Sarah says that God has made her laugh (21:6). At the weaning feast Ishmael mocks, and Sarah demands that Hagar and her son be sent away. Scripture says plainly that the matter was "very displeasing" in Abraham's sight because of his son (21:11). God tells him to listen to Sarah, because Isaac carries the line of promise, and assures him that Ishmael too will become a nation (21:12–13). Abraham rises early and sends them away with bread and water (21:14). In the same chapter he makes a treaty with Abimelech at Beersheba, settles a dispute over a well with seven ewe lambs, plants a tamarisk tree, and calls on the name of the LORD, the Everlasting God (21:22–33).

Mount Moriah (Genesis 22). "God tested Abraham" (22:1). He commands Abraham to take Isaac, his only son whom he loves, to the land of Moriah and offer him as a burnt offering. Abraham rises early, travels three days, and tells his servants that he and the boy will go and worship and then come back to them (22:5). Isaac carries the wood and asks where the lamb is. Abraham answers that God will provide for Himself the lamb (22:7–8). Abraham binds his son and raises the knife, and the Angel of the LORD stops him. Abraham looks up and sees a ram caught by its horns, which he offers in Isaac's place. He names the place The-LORD-Will-Provide (22:13–14). Then the Angel of the LORD calls a second time with an oath: God swears by Himself that because Abraham has not withheld his only son, He will multiply his descendants, and in his seed all the nations of the earth will be blessed (22:15–18). Hebrews explains what Abraham believed on the way up the mountain. He was "concluding that God was able to raise him up, even from the dead, from which he also received him in a figurative sense" (Hebrews 11:19). The chapter closes with news of Nahor's family and the birth of Rebekah (22:20–23), as God quietly prepares the next generation.

A grave and a bride (Genesis 23–24). Sarah dies at Hebron, and Abraham mourns her (23:2). He stands before the Hittites as a foreigner and sojourner and insists on paying full price, four hundred shekels of silver, for the field and cave of Machpelah (23:16–20). The man to whom God promised all Canaan legally owns one burial plot. Then, when Abraham is old and "the LORD had blessed Abraham in all things" (24:1), he sends his servant to find a wife for Isaac among his relatives. He forbids the servant to take Isaac back to Mesopotamia (24:6), because the future lies in the land God promised. The servant prays to "the LORD God of my master Abraham" (24:12). Rebekah appears before he finishes praying, and he worships the God who has not forsaken His mercy and truth toward Abraham (24:27).

Final years (Genesis 25). Abraham fathers sons by Keturah, including Midian, whose descendants will later trouble Israel. He gives gifts to the sons of his concubines and sends them east, but gives all he has to Isaac (25:1–6). He dies at a hundred and seventy-five, "an old man and full of years," and is gathered to his people (25:7–8). Isaac and Ishmael, the two sons once separated, bury him together in Machpelah beside Sarah (25:9–10).

Abraham in Israel's later memory. The name keeps working long after his death. God appears to Isaac, promises to keep the oath He swore to Abraham, and gives the reason: "because Abraham obeyed My voice" (26:3–5). Isaac reopens the wells the Philistines had stopped up after Abraham's death and gives them back their old names (26:15, 18). God tells Isaac not to fear "for My servant Abraham's sake" (26:24). Isaac passes "the blessing of Abraham" to Jacob (28:4), and God introduces Himself at Bethel as "the LORD God of Abraham" (28:13). Jacob swears by the God of Abraham (31:53), prays to Him in fear of Esau (32:9), and blesses Joseph's sons in the name of the God before whom Abraham and Isaac walked (48:15–16). Jacob asks to be buried in the cave Abraham bought (49:29–31), and Joseph dies confident that God will bring Israel to the land sworn to Abraham (50:24).

In Egypt, God hears Israel's groaning and remembers His covenant with Abraham (Exodus 2:24). At the burning bush He names Himself "the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" and calls this His name forever (3:6, 15). After the golden calf, Moses' plea rests on God's oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Israel (32:13). Even the curses of Leviticus end with the promise that when an exiled people confess, God will remember His covenant with Abraham (Leviticus 26:42). Deuteronomy insists that Israel will possess the land not because of its own righteousness but to confirm the word sworn to Abraham (9:5). On Mount Nebo, Moses looks over "the land of which I swore to give Abraham" (34:4).

The historical books show the same pattern. Elijah prays on Carmel to the "LORD God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel" (1 Kings 18:36). During the apostate reign of Jehoahaz in the northern kingdom, the LORD was gracious to Israel and would not destroy them "because of His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" (2 Kings 13:23). David prays to the God of Abraham to keep His people's hearts loyal (1 Chronicles 29:18). Jehoshaphat, facing invasion, recalls the land given "to the descendants of Abraham Your friend forever" (2 Chronicles 20:7), and Hezekiah summons the scattered north to return to the God of Abraham (2 Chronicles 30:6).

The psalms and prophets carry the name into worship and hope. Psalm 105 celebrates that God "remembered His holy promise, and Abraham His servant" when He brought Israel out with joy (105:42). Psalm 47 pictures the nations themselves gathering: "The princes of the people have gathered together, The people of the God of Abraham" (47:9). Isaiah speaks of the LORD "who redeemed Abraham" (29:22), calls Israel the "descendants of Abraham My friend" (41:8), and tells discouraged exiles to look to Abraham, the one man God called alone and multiplied (51:2). Isaiah also records a lament in which the people confess that God is their Father even though Abraham does not know them (63:16). Jeremiah ties the permanence of God's promises to the descendants of Abraham to the fixed order of day and night (33:26). Micah's book ends by trusting God to give "mercy to Abraham" as He swore long ago (Micah 7:20). Ezekiel records the dark side. Survivors living among Judah's ruins boasted that if Abraham, being only one man, inherited the land, then surely they, being many, would too. God answered that people who shed blood and worship idols have no claim on the inheritance (Ezekiel 33:24–26).

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that God chose Abraham out of an idolatrous family (Joshua 24:2–3; Nehemiah 9:7). It also states that God's promises to him concerned land, a multitude of descendants, kings, a lasting relationship of God with his offspring, and blessing for all nations (Genesis 12:3; 17:4–8; 22:17–18). The rest of the Old Testament treats these promises as the ground under Israel's feet. The exodus happens because God remembered Abraham (Exodus 2:24). Moses' intercession, Israel's inheritance of the land, and the northern kingdom's reprieve are all attributed to the sworn word given to him (Exodus 32:13; Deuteronomy 9:5; 2 Kings 13:23). The prophets' hope after exile rests on the same foundation (Leviticus 26:42; Isaiah 51:2; Micah 7:20). Abraham matters less for his own greatness than for the oath God attached to his name.

The texts also hold together two things readers sometimes separate. God's promise rests on His own oath, sworn by Himself (Genesis 22:16; Hebrews 6:13). Yet God also says He confirms it "because Abraham obeyed My voice" (Genesis 26:5; 22:18). A careful reading does not treat Abraham's obedience as the price of the promise, since the promise came first and was believed before any of these acts (Genesis 15:6). His obedience is the faithful life of a man who trusted the promise. Genesis 18:19 makes the same connection: God chose Abraham so that he would teach his household righteousness and justice, so that the LORD could bring about what He had spoken.

Abraham also shows God's purpose for the nations from the beginning. The promise was never only about one family. It reached "all the nations of the earth" (Genesis 22:18), and Abraham's very name speaks of many nations (17:5). By implication, the Keturah lineage and Ishmael's twelve princes partly show the literal breadth of that word, even though the line of promise runs through Isaac. Psalm 47:9 carries the hope further, with the rulers of the peoples gathering as "the people of the God of Abraham."

The later history adds a warning. Ezekiel 33:24 shows that a people can quote Abraham while despising Abraham's God. The name carried promise, but not automatic security for those who rebelled. The New Testament presses this same point (Matthew 3:9; John 8:39).

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Explicit New Testament interpretation. The New Testament, under the Greek form of the name, makes Abraham central to its witness to Christ. Matthew begins, "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham" (Matthew 1:1), and Luke's genealogy passes through Abraham on its way back to Adam (Luke 3:34). Paul reads the "seed" of the promise as finally pointing to one Person, who is Christ (Galatians 3:16). He says Scripture "preached the gospel to Abraham beforehand" when it promised that the nations would be blessed in him (Galatians 3:8), and that all who belong to Christ are Abraham's seed and heirs (3:29). In Romans 4, Paul observes that Abraham was counted righteous before he was circumcised. Circumcision was a seal of a righteousness he already had by faith, "that he might be the father of all those who believe, though they are uncircumcised" (Romans 4:11). The law came centuries later and could not cancel the promise (Galatians 3:17).

Jesus Himself speaks of Abraham in several places. He says Abraham rejoiced to see His day (John 8:56). When challenged, He answers, "before Abraham was, I AM" (John 8:58), using language that echoes God's self-naming. His hearers understood the claim, which is why they picked up stones. Jesus also argues for the resurrection from the very title God gave Himself at the bush. Because God calls Himself the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and He is not the God of the dead but of the living (Matthew 22:32), the familiar formula of Exodus 3:6 becomes evidence that Abraham lives before God. Hebrews draws on God's oath to Abraham as an anchor for Christian hope. Because "He could swear by no one greater, He swore by Himself" (Hebrews 6:13), so "that by two immutable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we might have strong consolation, who have fled for refuge to lay hold of the hope set before us" (6:18).

Strongly supported canonical development. Genesis 22 is the richest connection. Hebrews 11:19 explicitly says Abraham received Isaac back from the dead "in a figurative sense," so the New Testament itself reads the scene as a picture of resurrection. Paul's words that God "did not spare His own Son" (Romans 8:32) echo the language of Abraham not withholding his son (Genesis 22:12, 16), and the description of Isaac as the only son whom Abraham loved anticipates the Father's gift of His beloved Son. The ram that dies in Isaac's place shows substitution at the heart of the story. Abraham's words that God would provide the lamb, and the name The-LORD-Will-Provide, point forward to the Lamb of God whom God Himself provides. The geography adds a further thread. Solomon built the temple on Mount Moriah (2 Chronicles 3:1). Genesis speaks only of "the land of Moriah" and one of its mountains, so the exact spot cannot be proven. Still, Scripture uses the name Moriah for both the place of Isaac's offering and the place where Israel's sacrifices would be offered, and the connection is deliberate enough to carry real weight.

Thematic resonance. Abraham standing before the LORD to plead for Sodom, as one who calls himself dust and ashes, resembles the intercession that reaches its fullness in Christ, who always lives to make intercession for His people (Hebrews 7:25). Scripture does not call Abraham's prayer a type of Christ's. The likeness is real, but it remains a resonance. The same caution applies to Abraham's life as a sojourner. Hebrews says he looked for a city with foundations whose builder is God (Hebrews 11:10, 13–16), and that hope finds its answer in the kingdom Christ brings. Abraham's sojourning itself, however, is presented as a pattern of faith rather than a picture of Christ.

5. Spirit & Life

The God of Abraham names people before they look like their names. He called an old man whose promised son through Sarah had not yet been born "father of many nations" and made him say it every day. Paul says this is the kind of God Abraham believed in: the One who gives life to the dead and calls things that do not exist as though they did (Romans 4:17). Faith in Abraham's story is not optimism about his own body, which Paul calls "already dead" (Romans 4:19). It is confidence that God can do what He has said.

God also chose to confide in Abraham. The LORD would not hide His plans from him (Genesis 18:17), and three times Scripture calls Abraham God's friend (2 Chronicles 20:7; Isaiah 41:8; James 2:23). That friendship produced prayer. Abraham kept standing before the LORD after the visitors had gone, and he argued from God's own character as the Judge of all the earth who will do right. He learned that God was more willing to spare than he had dared to ask. Even when the city fell, God remembered Abraham and pulled Lot out. Prayer that rests on who God is gets a hearing, even when the answer takes a different form than we expected.

Abraham's story is honest about failure. The intercessor of Genesis 18 lies to protect himself in Genesis 20, and he assumes there is no fear of God in a place where God is already at work. God does not cancel the promise. He guards Sarah, rebukes the patriarch through a pagan king, and still calls Abraham a prophet who prays for others. The faithfulness that held his story together was God's.

Again and again Abraham had to open his hands. He left his father's house, sent away Ishmael with grief, and walked up Moriah with the son of promise. The pattern is not that God takes things to leave people empty. On the mountain Abraham learned that the God who asks also provides. He received Isaac back, and Hebrews says he received him as if from the dead. Whatever believers surrender to God is held by the One who raises the dead.

The burial cave at Machpelah may be the quietest act of faith in Abraham's life. He paid full price for a grave in a land he had been promised entirely. Hebrews says the patriarchs died in faith, not having received the promises, but seeing them from afar and confessing that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth. Because of that, "God is not ashamed to be called their God" (Hebrews 11:13–16, paraphrased). Abraham buried Sarah in the land of promise because he expected God to keep His word beyond death.

Finally, Abraham's name is a warning against borrowed faith. The survivors in Ezekiel 33 quoted Abraham while living in idolatry, and the crowds in John 8 claimed Abraham as father while rejecting the One he rejoiced to see. Being near Abraham's story is not the same as sharing Abraham's faith. The children of Abraham are those who believe God as he did, and Paul says that door stands open to Jew and Gentile alike in Christ.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 17:1–8 — The renaming of Abram as Abraham and the explanation of the name; the promises of nations, kings, land, and God's lasting relationship with his descendants.

  • Genesis 17:9–27 — Circumcision given as the sign of the covenant, and Abraham's immediate obedience.

  • Genesis 18:16–33; 19:27–29 — God confides in Abraham, Abraham intercedes for Sodom, and God remembers Abraham and rescues Lot.

  • Genesis 20 — Abraham's failure in Gerar and God's protection of the promise; Abraham called a prophet.

  • Genesis 21:1–14 — The birth of Isaac and the painful sending away of Hagar and Ishmael.

  • Genesis 22:1–18 — The offering of Isaac, the substitute ram, The-LORD-Will-Provide, and God's oath by Himself.

  • Genesis 23; 25:7–10 — The purchase of Machpelah and Abraham's death and burial.

  • Genesis 26:3–5, 24 — God confirms the oath to Isaac for Abraham's sake.

  • Exodus 2:24; 3:6, 15; 32:13 — God remembers Abraham, takes the title "God of Abraham" as His name forever, and answers Moses' intercession based on the oath.

  • Joshua 24:2–3; Nehemiah 9:7–8 — Abraham chosen out of an idolatrous family and given his new name.

  • 2 Kings 13:23 — God spares rebellious Israel because of His covenant with Abraham.

  • Psalm 47:9; Psalm 105:8–11, 42 — The nations gathered as the people of the God of Abraham; God's remembered promise.

  • Isaiah 41:8; 51:2; 63:16 — Abraham as God's friend, the rock from which Israel was cut, and the confession that God is Father even when Abraham does not know us.

  • Ezekiel 33:24–26 — A false appeal to Abraham rejected by God.

  • Micah 7:20 — God's sworn mercy to Abraham as the hope of restoration.

  • John 8:39, 56–58; Matthew 22:32 — Jesus on Abraham's true children, His existence before Abraham, and the resurrection argument from "the God of Abraham" (Greek form, separately indexed).

  • Romans 4; Galatians 3:6–9, 16, 29 — Justification by faith before circumcision, Abraham as father of all who believe, and Christ as the Seed.

  • Hebrews 6:13–18; 11:8–19; James 2:21–23 — God's oath as the anchor of hope, Abraham's pilgrim faith and resurrection hope at Moriah, and his faith shown in obedience.
