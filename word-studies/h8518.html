HANG, HANGED, HUNG - (talah, Strong's H8518)

Summary

The Hebrew verb talah means to hang or to suspend. Scripture uses it for ordinary things: shields hung on a tower, harps hung on willow branches, cups and pitchers hung on a peg. It is also used for something far darker, the public hanging of a human body. Where the Bible spells out the order of events, as in Deuteronomy and Joshua, the person had already been put to death, and the body was displayed so that the whole community could see the person's defeat and disgrace. The law of Deuteronomy gives that act its most solemn meaning: "he who is hanged is accursed of God" (Deuteronomy 21:23).

That sentence leads directly to the cross. Paul quotes it in Galatians 3:13 to explain what Jesus did: "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us." Around this center the word gathers rich biblical pictures. In Esther, Haman is hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai. Isaiah describes a faithful servant who becomes a secure peg holding up the glory of his father's house. Job describes a God who "hangs the earth on nothing." The word helps us see both what sin deserves and how fully God has dealt with it.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Talah is a common, concrete Hebrew verb. Its settings fall into two broad groups. In poetry and prophecy it describes hanging objects up for use or display, such as weapons, instruments, and household vessels. In narrative and law it describes hanging people, from Pharaoh's baker in Genesis to the Canaanite kings in Joshua and Haman in Esther. STEPBible counts 27 occurrences in 26 verses of the Hebrew Bible, using the Leningrad text and its Qere readings. Nine of those occurrences are in Esther.

One background fact matters a great deal. Where the Old Testament describes the order of events, hanging a person did not mean death by a rope, as the modern word suggests. The sentence in Deuteronomy 21:22 runs in order: a man "is put to death, and you hang him on a tree." Joshua 10:26 likewise says Joshua "struck them and killed them, and hanged them on five trees." In these passages the hanging came after the death and served as a public announcement of judgment. Other accounts, such as the hanging of Haman, do not describe the method or the sequence, so this pattern should not be assumed for every case.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. To hang an object on something, as shields on a tower (Song of Solomon 4:4), harps on willows (Psalm 137:2), or vessels on a peg (Isaiah 22:24).

2. To suspend something without support beneath it, most memorably God hanging the earth "on nothing" (Job 26:7), and Absalom left hanging in a tree after his head caught in its branches (2 Samuel 18:9–10). Absalom's suspension was an accident of battle, not a judicial hanging.

3. To hang a person publicly as a display of disgrace and defeat. Where the text gives the sequence, the hanging follows the execution (Deuteronomy 21:22; Joshua 10:26).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word itself is neutral. What hangs, and where, determines whether the image speaks of glory or of shame. A thousand shields on David's tower display strength and beauty (Song of Solomon 4:4). A body on a tree displays ruin.

The display of a body almost always carries a public message about who has won and who has lost. Pharaoh hangs his baker (Genesis 40:19). Joshua hangs enemy kings. David hangs the murderers of Ishbosheth by the pool in Hebron (2 Samuel 4:12). In each case the onlookers are meant to read the scene as a verdict.

Psalm 137:2 shows a quieter use: "We hung our harps Upon the willows in the midst of it." The exiles did not break their harps. They set them aside, because they could not sing the songs of Zion in Babylon.

4. Theological Significance

Hanging in Scripture makes judgment visible. Sin is not left hidden or abstract. A sentence of death was carried out, and then the result was shown before the community and before God. Deuteronomy calls the hanged man "accursed of God," meaning his exposure marked him as cut off under God's judgment.

The same law also limits that shame. Deuteronomy does not command that bodies be hung up. It regulates what must happen if a body has been exposed: the body "shall not remain overnight on the tree, but you shall surely bury him that day, so that you do not defile the land" (Deuteronomy 21:23). Whatever the reason for the display, the curse could not be left hanging over the land God was giving His people. Joshua's practice offers a close parallel. He took the king of Ai down "as soon as the sun was down" (Joshua 8:29), and he did the same with the five Amorite kings (Joshua 10:27).

This display of judgment could fall on Israel as well as on her enemies. In Joshua the Canaanite kings hang on trees. In Lamentations Jerusalem has fallen, and the poet grieves: "Princes were hung up by their hands, And elders were not respected" (Lamentations 5:12). The disgrace once inflicted on God's enemies now falls on Judah's own leaders. Israel's broken faithfulness left her exposed to the very shame her ancestors had carried out on others.

Above all of this stands the God of Job 26:7: "He hangs the earth on nothing." Hanging normally needs something to hold the weight, such as a tree, a peg, or a wall. God holds the whole earth with nothing beneath it except His own power. He is the One who suspends the world, and He is also the Judge whose verdicts people see displayed on the tree.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Deuteronomy 21:22–23 and Joshua 8; 10. This law is the theological center of the word. It treats the hanged body as a sign that the person was "accursed of God." Joshua's hanging of the king of Ai and the five Amorite kings runs parallel to that law. These kings had set themselves against the Lord and His people, and their exposure on trees until evening declared their defeat to everyone who passed by. Then, at sunset, the bodies came down. The curse was real, but it had a set limit.

Esther 5–9. Haman's wife and friends advise him: "Let a gallows be made, fifty cubits high, and in the morning suggest to the king that Mordecai be hanged on it" (Esther 5:14). The structure towered over the city as a monument to Haman's pride and his hatred of the Jews. From that point the word tracks one of Scripture's great reversals. After the king hears the record of Mordecai’s loyalty during his sleepless night, Haman arrives to ask permission to hang Mordecai (Esther 6:1–4). In the end the king orders Haman hanged on that same gallows (7:9–10), and later his ten sons are hanged as well (9:13–14, 25). God's name never appears in Esther, yet the turning of the gallows is unmistakable. Psalm 7:16 describes the same pattern: "His trouble shall return upon his own head."

2 Samuel 18:10 and 21:12. Absalom tried to seize the throne from God's anointed king. During the battle his mule ran under a great terebinth, "his head caught in the terebinth; so he was left hanging between heaven and earth" (18:9), and a soldier reported, "I just saw Absalom hanging in a terebinth tree!" The text names his head, not his famous hair, and the suspension was an accident of battle, not a sentence. Absalom was still alive when Joab killed him there (18:14). Readers who know Deuteronomy may still notice the irony: the rebel son ends up suspended on a tree, the picture Israel's law associated with the curse. In a related account, the Philistines displayed the bodies of Saul and Jonathan in Beth Shan after their victory. STEPBible files the Hebrew word in that verse under H8511 and gives H8518 as an alternate number (2 Samuel 21:12). David's recovery and burial of their bones (21:12–14) restored the dignity that enemies had tried to strip away.

Isaiah 22:20–25 and Ezekiel 15:3. God promises to install His servant Eliakim over the royal household and says, "I will fasten him as a peg in a secure place" (22:23). Then "they will hang on him all the glory of his father's house," every vessel "from the cups to all the pitchers" (22:24). In this image, hanging means resting the whole weight of a family on one faithful man. The next verse, however, warns that the peg itself will one day be removed and the load upon it cut down (22:25). Even a good servant could not carry that weight forever. Ezekiel offers a sharp contrast. Wood from a fruitless vine is so worthless that no one can "make a peg from it to hang any vessel on" (Ezekiel 15:3).

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The apostles used Deuteronomy's language deliberately. Peter twice described Jesus' death as being hanged on a tree (Acts 5:30; 10:39), and Paul explains why that matters: Christ became "a curse for us (for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree')" (Galatians 3:13). The public display that once marked the guilty as accursed was carried out on the innocent Son of God. Jesus did not come to the tree by accident, as Absalom came to his. He took the curse willingly, in our place.

Several Old Testament details help us see the cross more clearly. The Philistines displayed Saul, the anointed king, as a trophy of their victory, and Jesus' enemies likewise intended the cross to announce that the true Anointed King was finished. Scripture turns that display upside down. Colossians 2:15 says that at the cross God disarmed the hostile powers and made a public spectacle of them. The gallows of Esther teaches the same reversal on a smaller scale. Even the same-day burial required in Deuteronomy has a parallel, when Jesus' body was taken down before the Sabbath began (John 19:31).

Isaiah 22 also points forward, in two different ways. Revelation 3:7 explicitly applies to Jesus the "key of David" that Isaiah 22:22 gave to Eliakim, so the New Testament itself connects this passage with Christ. The secure peg of the next verse is not quoted in Revelation; comparing it with Christ is an analogy drawn from the same chapter. Eliakim was a secure peg for a time, but he could not bear the glory of the house forever. Christ hung on the tree to bear the curse, and He now holds the full weight of His Father's household without ever giving way.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 137 is honest about grief. The exiles hung their harps on the willows because they refused to put on a show of joy for their captors. Sometimes faithfulness looks like silence before God rather than forced cheerfulness. The harps were hung up, though, and not destroyed. They were waiting for the day when the Lord would bring His people home.

Everyone hangs their weight on something. Tyre hung the shields of hired soldiers on her walls, and "they gave splendor to you" (Ezekiel 27:10). Within the same chapter of Ezekiel, the city sinks under God's judgment. Ezekiel 15 asks whether useless wood can even hold a cup. A believer can ask the same honest question about a career, a reputation, or another person: can it hold what I am hanging on it? Only the peg God fastens in a secure place will hold.

The God who hangs the earth on nothing does not need visible supports to keep His people. When life seems to hang over empty space, the hand holding it is the same hand that holds the world. Esther adds a sober comfort: the gallows built against God's people may stand fifty cubits high and still become the place where the enemy's plan collapses.

The deepest comfort comes from Deuteronomy itself. "He who is hanged is accursed of God," and Jesus was hanged. Because He bore that curse in public before heaven and earth, those who trust Him no longer stand under it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 40:19, 22; 41:13 — The word's first appearance: Joseph tells the baker that Pharaoh will "hang you on a tree; and the birds will eat your flesh from you." Leaving the body unburied and exposed to the birds made the disgrace complete, and the prophecy came true.

  • Esther 2:23 — Two plotters against the king are hanged. This early notice prepares the reader for the gallows that dominates the rest of the book.

  • 2 Samuel 4:12 — David hangs the murderers of Ishbosheth by the pool in Hebron, publicly rejecting their attempt to win his favor through bloodshed.

  • Song of Solomon 4:4 — The beloved's neck is "like the tower of David… On which hang a thousand bucklers." Here hanging speaks of strength, beauty, and safety.

  • Ezekiel 27:11 — Tyre's walls were hung with the shields of her defenders, a display of pride that came just before the city's downfall.
