WAVY, LOCKS - (taltal, Strong's H8534)

Summary

The Hebrew word taltal describes hair that hangs in thick, flowing locks. The NKJV translates it "wavy." It appears only once in the Bible, in Song of Solomon 5:11, where the bride describes the man she loves: "His head is like the finest gold; His locks are wavy, And black as a raven." Because the word occurs only once, its exact shade of meaning is uncertain. One ancient reading connected it with the drooping fronds of a palm tree. That is a possible picture, not a settled one, though it would suit the garden imagery that fills the Song.

The detail is small, but it is part of a portrait painted at a moment of longing. The bride has lost sight of her beloved, and when the women of Jerusalem ask what makes him special, she answers by describing him from head to foot. In the poem, his dark, abundant hair speaks of youth, health, and strength. Readers who see in the Song a picture of Christ the Bridegroom find here a fitting comparison. Scripture states plainly elsewhere that the Lord does not grow weary and that Christ always lives for His people (Isaiah 40:28–29; Hebrews 7:25), and the bride's portrait gives that truth a vivid image.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Taltal is a Hebrew noun found only in Song of Solomon 5:11. A word used only once is called a hapax legomenon, and its meaning has to be worked out from its setting and from how ancient translators understood it. The word repeats a syllable (tal-tal). Hebrew sometimes uses this doubled form for things that are repeated or layered, which fits hair falling in waves, though the meaning rests on the context more than on the sound of the word.

The verse belongs to a poem in which the bride praises her beloved one feature at a time, moving from his head down to his legs (Song 5:10–16). Scholars call this kind of love poem a wasf, from an Arabic word for a description of physical beauty. The bride draws her images from two places: the treasury, with gold, ivory, sapphires, and marble, and the garden, with doves, spices, lilies, myrrh, and cedars.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In context taltal describes hanging or flowing locks of hair, hair that falls in thick curls or waves. Older lexicons such as Strong's define it as a trailing or hanging bough, and the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament is commonly understood to have rendered it with a word for the shoots or fronds of a palm. That may mean early readers pictured the hair hanging down the way palm fronds do. This is a possible ancient reading rather than established Hebrew evidence, and because the word occurs only once, its exact nuance remains provisional. What the verse makes clear is that it describes abundant, flowing hair, and the surrounding phrases supply the rest: the locks rest on a head "like the finest gold" and are "black as a raven."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word's single use comes in the bride's praise of the bridegroom, and the details around it carry its force. Two phrases frame it. "Finest gold" gives the head a royal, precious quality. "Black as a raven" gives the hair the deep color of youth. Scripture elsewhere links gray hair with age and the honor of experience, and it links strength with young men (see Proverbs 20:29). Thick black locks, then, picture a man in the full vigor of life, with nothing about him declining or fading.

If the palm-frond reading is correct, it would also tie the word to the garden language of the poem. Either way, the Song uses the palm image for the bride as well, when the bridegroom compares her stature to a palm tree (Song 7:7–8). Each lover sees in the other the beauty of a living, flourishing tree.

4. Theological Significance

The Song of Solomon celebrates the love of a husband and wife, and it does so without embarrassment. That alone says something about God. He created human love and beauty, and He placed a book in Scripture that delights in them. The bride's careful attention to her beloved's hair, eyes, and hands is the attention of someone who treasures another person.

Scripture also teaches that marriage points beyond itself. Paul says that the union of husband and wife speaks of "Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32), and the prophets repeatedly speak of God as the husband of His people. Read in this light, the bride's portrait becomes a way of contemplating the Lord who loves His people. That reading is a canonical application rather than the Song's stated intent, but it stands on solid biblical ground. In the poem, the beloved is both royal and alive: gold speaks of worth and majesty, and raven-black locks speak of undiminished strength. As a comparison, the portrait fits what Scripture says directly about the Lord elsewhere (see Section 6). He is glorious, and His glory is never stale or spent.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 5:10–16 — the bride's answer. The setting of verse 11 matters. Earlier in chapter 5 the bridegroom comes to the bride's door at night. She hesitates, and when she finally opens, he is gone. She searches the city and cannot find him (5:2–8). The daughters of Jerusalem then ask her what makes her beloved better than any other (5:9). Her answer is this portrait. She begins, "My beloved is white and ruddy, Chief among ten thousand" (5:10), and the hair comes next: "His locks are wavy, And black as a raven" (5:11). She describes him most fully while he is absent and she is longing for him.

Her images keep moving between treasure and garden. "His cheeks are like a bed of spices, Banks of scented herbs. His lips are lilies, Dripping liquid myrrh" (5:13). His hands are "rods of gold," and his body is "carved ivory" (5:14). His legs are "pillars of marble Set on bases of fine gold," and "His countenance is like Lebanon, Excellent as the cedars" (5:15). The flowing locks belong with the spices, lilies, myrrh, and cedars. Together they make him look like a living garden, fragrant and fruitful, while the gold and ivory make him look like a king's treasure. She sums it all up: "Yes, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, And this is my friend" (5:16).

The garden language runs both ways in the Song. Earlier the bridegroom calls the bride his "garden enclosed" (4:12), and afterward the bride says, "My beloved has gone to his garden, To the beds of spices" (6:2). The garden is a place they share. In 5:10–16, though, the bride sees in his very person the freshness and abundance she associates with that garden.

The effect of her words is striking. After hearing her, the daughters of Jerusalem ask where he has gone so that they can seek him with her (6:1). Describing him did not settle her longing. It spread it to others.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

When this portrait is read as a picture of Christ the Bridegroom, it gathers other passages around it. Psalm 45, a royal wedding song that Hebrews 1:8–9 applies to the Son, says, "You are fairer than the sons of men; Grace is poured upon Your lips" (Psalm 45:2). Isaiah shows the other side of the story: the Servant would "grow up before Him as a tender plant, And as a root out of dry ground," with "no beauty that we should desire Him" (Isaiah 53:2). The Christ who came in humility and was disfigured in suffering is the same Christ who now reigns in undiminished life. As a comparison, the bride's picture of a beloved in full vigor fits the risen and exalted Lord, the One who was dead and is alive forevermore.

Another comparison deepens the picture. Daniel sees the Ancient of Days with hair "like pure wool" (Daniel 7:9), and John sees the glorified Son of Man in the same way: "His head and hair were white like wool, as white as snow" (Revelation 1:14). These are two distinct biblical portraits. Daniel and John describe visions of God and of the glorified Christ, and white hair there speaks of eternal age, purity, and the authority of the Judge. The Song describes a bridegroom seen through his bride's eyes, and his raven-black locks speak, in the poem, of fresh vigor and the strength of youth. Set side by side, the two portraits let readers think about the Lord's majesty from two sides. The promise that His strength never declines does not rest on Song 5:11. Isaiah declares that the everlasting God "neither faints nor is weary" and gives power to the weak (Isaiah 40:28–29), and Hebrews says that "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever" (Hebrews 13:8). Neither image describes His literal appearance.

The garden imagery also fits Scripture's larger story. God first placed humanity in a garden, and the Bible closes with a city where the tree of life grows. Jesus calls Himself "the true vine" (John 15:1), and Psalm 1 pictures the righteous person as "a tree Planted by the rivers of water... Whose leaf also shall not wither" (Psalm 1:3). Life and fruitfulness are found in union with Him.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride's portrait was spoken in a season of loss. She had been slow to open the door, she had missed her beloved, and she was searching. She did not answer that loss by describing her feelings. She described him. Believers who feel that Christ is distant can do the same, setting their minds on who He actually is, as Scripture reveals Him, until longing replaces dullness.

The picture of His strength also speaks to the weary. Believers grow tired, their bodies age, and their zeal can run dry. The Lord they love does not age or tire. Isaiah says that the everlasting God neither faints nor grows weary, and that He gives power to the weak (Isaiah 40:28–29). Hebrews says that Christ "always lives" to intercede for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). His love for His people is not running down. The strength with which He first called them is the same strength with which He keeps them.

The daughters of Jerusalem also teach something. They came to seek him because someone described him well. When believers speak of Christ as He is, "altogether lovely," others begin to ask where He may be found.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Taltal occurs only in Song of Solomon 5:11, and that single use has been treated in the sections above. For the surrounding portrait, read Song of Solomon 5:10–16 as a whole, together with the daughters' question in 5:9 and their response in 6:1.
