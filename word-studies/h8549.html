BLAMELESS, WITHOUT BLEMISH, PERFECT, WHOLE - (tâmîym, Strong's H8549)

Summary

The Hebrew word tâmîym describes something whole, sound and complete, with nothing missing or damaged. It most often describes the animals Israel brought to the LORD, which had to be "without blemish," such as the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:5) and the daily burnt offerings. The same word also describes people who walk with God with their whole heart. Noah was "perfect in his generations," and God told Abraham, "walk before Me and be blameless" (Genesis 6:9; 17:1). When it describes people, the word means integrity and undivided loyalty to God. It does not claim that they never sinned. When Moses and David use it of God, it has no limits: "His work is perfect" (Deuteronomy 32:4), and "As for God, His way is perfect" (Psalm 18:30).

The word therefore connects three things: the altar, the human heart, and the character of God. A God whose way is flawless asks for wholeness from those who come near Him. Scripture also shows that He supplies it. In Psalm 18, David praises God's perfect way and in the same breath says that God makes his own way perfect. Christ completes the pattern. He is the "lamb without blemish and without spot" (1 Peter 1:19), and He offered Himself to God so that people who are far from whole can serve the living God.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tâmîym is a Hebrew adjective that appears 91 times in the Old Testament, from Genesis to Amos. More than half of these uses come in the sacrificial instructions of Leviticus, Numbers and Ezekiel's temple vision, where the NKJV usually translates it "without blemish." The word also appears in stories of people who walked with God, in David's songs, in Job, in many psalms and proverbs, and in the prophets' words about justice in the city gate.

The NKJV's range of renderings shows how broad the word is: "without blemish," "blameless," "perfect," "whole," "sincerity," "uprightly," "undefiled," "completed" and "full." Each rendering brings out one aspect of the same basic idea of wholeness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, tâmîym means whole or intact, with nothing lacking. Context decides what kind of wholeness is in view.

1. Whole or complete in time or measure. Joshua 10:13 speaks of a "whole" day, Leviticus 23:15 of seven "completed" Sabbaths, and Leviticus 25:30 of a "full" year.

2. Physically sound and without defect. This is the most common sense and is used of sacrificial animals: "Your lamb shall be without blemish" (Exodus 12:5).

3. Of people, morally whole: blameless, sincere, of integrity. This means a life that belongs to God without division, as when Joshua calls Israel to "serve Him in sincerity and in truth" (Joshua 24:14).

4. Of God's work, way and word, perfect and flawless in the fullest sense. "He is the Rock, His work is perfect" (Deuteronomy 32:4).

The English word "perfect" can mislead modern readers when it describes people. Noah and Abraham both failed later in their stories. What the word claims for them is an undivided heart.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the sacrificial laws, tâmîym describes the inspected condition of an animal. Leviticus 1:3 requires "a male without blemish" for the burnt offering. Leviticus 22:21 adds that a peace offering must be perfect to be accepted, with no defect in it. Malachi 1:8 uses different wording, but it shows what was at stake when Israel later brought "the blind," "the lame and sick" to God's altar.

When the word describes people, it is often joined to the language of walking and of the way. Noah "walked with God" (Genesis 6:9). Psalm 15:2 describes the person who may dwell on God's holy hill as one who walks uprightly, and Psalm 119:1 blesses those who are undefiled in the way. This pairing presents integrity as the direction of a whole life rather than a single act.

The surrounding setting often makes clear what wholeness excludes. Deuteronomy 18:13, "You shall be blameless before the LORD your God," comes directly after Moses forbids divination, sorcery and consulting mediums. To be blameless there means refusing to seek guidance from any other power. Joshua 24:14 sets "sincerity" beside a command to "put away the gods which your fathers served." In Judges 9:16 and 19, Jotham asks whether Shechem had dealt "in truth and sincerity" with Gideon's house. There the word describes honest dealing between people.

4. Theological Significance

Every other use of tâmîym rests on the perfection of God. Moses sings that "His work is perfect; for all His ways are justice" (Deuteronomy 32:4). David's psalm adds His word: "The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul" (Psalm 19:7). Nothing God does or says lacks anything, and nothing needs correcting. For this reason His instructions about the altar and about the heart are not arbitrary. A whole and holy God is approached with what is whole.

Wisdom literature describes how God treats those who walk with integrity. Proverbs promises that the blameless will remain in the land (2:21), that their righteousness will direct their way (11:5), and that whoever walks blamelessly will be saved (28:18). Proverbs 11:20 says the blameless in their ways are God's delight. Psalm 37:18 says the LORD knows the days of the upright, and Psalm 84:11 says He will withhold no good thing from those who walk uprightly. Job keeps these promises from being turned into a formula, because he complains that the just and blameless man is laughed to scorn (Job 12:4). The promises are real, but they look past present suffering to God's final vindication and care.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 6:9 and 17:1 — wholeness as walking with God. The word first appears with Noah: "Noah was a just man, perfect in his generations. Noah walked with God." The verse just before it explains where his story begins: "Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD" (6:8). God then says to Abram, "I am Almighty God; walk before Me and be blameless" (17:1). By this point Abram has already believed the LORD and been counted righteous (15:6). In both stories, blamelessness grows out of God's grace. It describes a man whose whole life is turned toward God. It is not a record of flawless performance.

Exodus 12:5 and the daily offerings — the standard of the altar. The Passover lamb had to be tâmîym, and so did nearly every animal brought to the altar afterward. Numbers 28:3 requires "two male lambs in their first year without blemish, day by day." Even in Ezekiel's vision of a renewed temple, the altar is cleansed with "a kid of the goats without blemish" (Ezekiel 43:22). Repeated over and over, the requirement taught Israel that nothing damaged or second-rate could stand in a sinner's place before God.

Psalm 18 (2 Samuel 22) — the God who makes the way perfect. This is the word's richest passage, because David uses tâmîym four times in one song. He says he was blameless before God (18:23). He declares that with the blameless God shows Himself blameless (18:25). He confesses, "As for God, His way is perfect" (18:30). Then he gives God the credit for his own integrity: it is God who arms him with strength and makes his way perfect (18:32). David uses the same word for God's way and for his own. The perfection that belongs to God by nature is the perfection God works into the life of the one who trusts Him. The verse that calls God's way perfect goes on: "He is a shield to all who trust in Him."

Ezekiel 28:15 — created wholeness lost. In his lament over the king of Tyre, Ezekiel says the king was perfect in his ways from the day he was created, until iniquity was found in him. Many interpreters hear in this lament an echo of an earlier fall behind human pride, though the passage is addressed to Tyre's ruler. Either way, the verse shows that wholeness given to a creature can be lost. God's perfection belongs to Him and cannot be lost. The wholeness of a creature is a gift that pride can corrupt. Sin did not come from God's work. It came as a corruption of what He had made whole.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The long line of unblemished animals points forward to a sacrifice that would not need repeating. Paul names Christ as our Passover, sacrificed for us (1 Corinthians 5:7). Peter takes up the language of Exodus 12:5 when he says believers were redeemed "with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot" (1 Peter 1:19). This is stated fulfillment. The New Testament itself reads the spotless lamb as a picture of Jesus.

Hebrews takes the comparison a step further. The animals were examined for wholeness, but Christ "through the eternal Spirit offered Himself without spot to God" (Hebrews 9:14). He is both the priest and the offering. His wholeness was moral and willing, which no animal's could be. The result reaches what the daily lambs could not: His blood will "cleanse your conscience from dead works to serve the living God." Psalm 18 joins the perfect God to the person whose way He makes perfect, and in Jesus the two meet in one Person. He is the God whose way is perfect and the man whose way was blameless all the way to the cross.

7. Spirit & Life

Psalm 18 changes how a believer hears the command in Genesis 17:1. "Walk before Me and be blameless" is spoken by "Almighty God," the One who has the power to make it true. David's integrity was not a private achievement that he presented to God. God armed him and made his way whole. Psalm 101:2 shows the same dependence in prayer. David resolves to walk in a blameless way, and in the middle of that resolve he asks God to come to him. A wholehearted life is a goal we pursue only by asking God to come to us.

The altar also teaches something about the heart. Israel learned to inspect an animal for defects before bringing it, and the psalms use the same word for the person who walks undefiled in the way. Paul draws on this picture when he urges believers to present their bodies as a living sacrifice (Romans 12:1). The question that Joshua 24:14 and Deuteronomy 18:13 press on the heart is whether any part of it is still kept back for other gods or other sources of guidance. Being blameless before the LORD means giving all of oneself to Him. Christ's blood has already answered for our blemishes, and so we can do this with open faces instead of fear.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 23:15 — Israel counted seven "completed" Sabbaths from the firstfruits offering to the Feast of Weeks. The word marks time measured out in full.

  • Joshua 10:13 — The sun stayed in the sky about a "whole" day. This is a plain use for an entire day.

  • Job 36:4; 37:16 — Elihu claims to speak as one who is perfect in knowledge, then speaks of God as the One perfect in knowledge. Placed side by side, the two claims show the gap between a man's confidence and God's actual knowledge.

  • Psalm 15:2 — The person who may dwell on God's holy hill is one who walks uprightly and speaks truth from the heart.

  • Proverbs 1:12 — Violent men boast that they will swallow their victims whole, as the grave does. The word here has no moral meaning at all.

  • Amos 5:10 — "They abhor the one who speaks uprightly." A corrupt society hates the person whose words in the court at the gate are whole and honest.

10. Other Relevant Insights

The unblemished Passover lamb represents Christ, as 1 Peter 1:19 and 1 Corinthians 5:7 state (see Section 6). The New Testament does not attach this symbolism to every use of the word. A whole day or a whole piece of vine wood (Ezekiel 15:5) is simply literal.

The same word describes the sound animal on the altar and the undefiled person in God's way (Leviticus 22:21; Psalm 119:1). The outward soundness of the offering pictures the inward wholeness God seeks in the worshiper.

Two connections are suggestive but not stated in Scripture. First, the seven "completed" Sabbaths leading to the Feast of Weeks (Leviticus 23:15) can be heard alongside Acts 2:1, where the Spirit was poured out when the Day of Pentecost had fully come. Second, Ezekiel 28:15 describes a creature made whole before iniquity was found in it, which may recall creation as it first came from God's hand. On this reading, God's call to blamelessness invites a return to His original design. Both are reflections for meditation, not established meanings of the word.
