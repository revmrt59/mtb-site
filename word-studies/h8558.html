PALM, PALM TREE - (tâmâr, Strong's H8558)

Summary

The Hebrew word tâmâr means palm tree, the tall date palm that marked water and life across the dry lands of the Bible. Scripture uses it a dozen times. The palm appears at Elim, where Israel camped after the bitter water of Marah. Its branches were taken up at the Feast of Tabernacles, where God commanded His people to rejoice. It names Jericho, "the city of palm trees." In poetry it pictures the flourishing righteous person and the graceful beauty of the beloved. When Joel describes judgment, the withered palm shows that the joy has gone out of the land.

Taken together, these passages show the palm as a sign of life God gives in hard places. It stands where He provides water, rest, gladness and fruit. The same picture is echoed in the temple, whose walls Solomon carved with palms and cherubim, and it reaches Jesus. When He entered Jerusalem as King, the crowd took palm branches and went out to meet Him. In Revelation, a multitude from every nation holds palm branches before the Lamb. Read alongside the festival palms of Israel, that scene makes the tree a picture of everlasting joy in God's presence.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tâmâr is the ordinary Hebrew noun for the date palm. In the ancient Near East it was a familiar and useful tree. It grows where water lies within reach of its roots, gives shade in the heat, and produces sweet, nourishing fruit. A traveler who saw palms on the horizon knew there was water ahead. That common experience lies behind much of the word's biblical use.

The word appears in wilderness travel narratives, festival law, place names, wisdom poetry, love poetry and prophecy. Closely related Hebrew words describe the carved palm decorations in the temple and a palm used as a landmark. These are separate words, but they belong to the same family of images.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tâmâr has one basic meaning: a palm tree, specifically the date palm, including its trunk, branches and fruit. Context shows which part is in view. At Elim the trees themselves are meant. In Leviticus 23:40 and Nehemiah 8:15 the branches are meant. In "the city of palm trees" the word describes a place. In the Psalms and the Song of Solomon the tree serves as a comparison. The word's meaning stays the same in every case. What changes is how the tree is used.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The occurrences fall into five clear groups:

  • Wilderness provision. The palms at Elim (Exodus 15:27; Numbers 33:9).

  • Festival worship. Palm branches at the Feast of Tabernacles, taken for rejoicing before the Lord (Leviticus 23:40) and later gathered to make booths (Nehemiah 8:15).

  • Place name. Four times the palm identifies Jericho (Deuteronomy 34:3; Judges 1:16; 3:13; 2 Chronicles 28:15). The name tells us Jericho was a well-watered oasis near the Jordan, and that made it a prize worth taking.

  • Poetic comparison. The righteous person (Psalm 92:12) and the beloved's stature (Song of Solomon 7:7–8) are each "like a palm tree."

  • Judgment. The palm is listed among the withered trees of Joel 1:12.

In the two poetic passages the word appears in comparisons ("like a palm tree"), so the tree's real qualities become the point. Its height, its life in dry places, and its fruit carry the meaning.

4. Theological Significance

Across these uses the palm marks the places where God gives life. Israel did not plant the palms of Elim. They were waiting for the people when God led them there. The palm branches of Tabernacles were gathered at His command, and the rejoicing came with them. When the righteous flourish like a palm, their life comes from being planted with God. The tree keeps pointing past itself to the One who supplies its water.

For that reason, the loss of the palm becomes a picture of God's judgment. Joel lists the vine, fig, pomegranate, palm and apple tree, and then concludes: "Surely joy has withered away from the sons of men" (Joel 1:12). This is the same tree whose branches once carried Israel's festival gladness. When a people turn from the Lord, He can remove the very things they celebrated with. Joel's lament shows that joy does not grow out of the land on its own. God gives it, and He can withdraw it so that His people will return to Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Elim after Marah (Exodus 15:22–27). Three days into the wilderness, Israel found only bitter water at Marah. The people complained, and the Lord made the waters sweet, tested them, and declared, "I am the LORD who heals you" (15:26). The very next verse reads: "Then they came to Elim, where there were twelve wells of water and seventy palm trees; so they camped there by the waters" (15:27). The order matters. God led His people from testing to rest, and Elim was already prepared before they arrived. Twelve wells match the twelve tribes. Some see a link between the seventy palms and the seventy elders, though the text does not draw that connection. Either way, the numbers describe abundance. God gave His people more than enough water and shade.

Palms and commanded joy (Leviticus 23:40–43; Nehemiah 8:15). At the Feast of Tabernacles, Israel was to take "branches of palm trees" along with other boughs, "and you shall rejoice before the LORD your God for seven days." Leviticus also commands Israel to live in booths for those seven days (Leviticus 23:42), and that had a purpose: "that your generations may know that I made the children of Israel dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt" (23:43). In Leviticus the palm branch belongs to the rejoicing, and the booths belong to the remembering of how God carried His people through the wilderness. Leviticus does not say what the booths were made of. Centuries later, exiles who had returned to a ruined Jerusalem heard this law read, and Nehemiah records the instruction they followed, which joins the two: go out to the mountain and bring "palm branches... to make booths, as it is written" (Nehemiah 8:15). Gathering the palms again marked a fresh beginning in obeying God's Word.

The righteous palm (Psalm 92:12–15). "The righteous shall flourish like a palm tree, He shall grow like a cedar in Lebanon." Earlier the psalm says the wicked spring up like grass, which comes up fast and dies just as quickly (92:7). Against that picture stands a tree that grows slowly, stands upright and lasts. The psalmist names the source of its strength. The righteous are planted in the house of the Lord, they flourish in the courts of God, and they keep bearing fruit in old age (92:13–14). A palm flourishes in the desert because its roots reach water that no one can see on the surface. In the same way, the righteous do not draw their life from their surroundings. Their life comes from being rooted where God is. Their fruit in old age is evidence, in the psalm's words, "that the LORD is upright" (92:15).

The city of palms as a place of mercy (2 Chronicles 28:8–15). In King Ahaz's day, the northern kingdom of Israel defeated Judah and marched thousands of captives toward Samaria. The prophet Oded confronted the victors for enslaving their own relatives, and several leaders of Ephraim stood with him. What followed is one of the tenderest scenes in Chronicles. The men "clothed all who were naked among them, dressed them and gave them sandals, gave them food and drink, and anointed them; and they let all the feeble ones ride on donkeys. So they brought them to their brethren at Jericho, the city of palm trees" (28:15). Jericho had been a city to conquer and fight over. Here it becomes the place where defeated people are brought home clothed, fed and cared for. The oasis city becomes a place of refuge for broken people.

The palm and delight (Song of Solomon 7:7–8). "This stature of yours is like a palm tree," the lover says, and then, "I will go up to the palm tree, I will take hold of its branches." The comparison praises graceful height and sweet fruitfulness. The lover is not simply admiring from a distance. He wants to draw near and take hold. Many Christian readers have heard in this a picture of Christ's love for His people. That reading is an application by analogy, since the Song does not state it. Even so, it fits a pattern Scripture states plainly elsewhere: the Lord delights in the beauty and fruit He produces in those He loves.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The palm moved from the wilderness into the sanctuary. Using the related word for carved palms, 1 Kings 6:29 says Solomon carved the temple walls "with carved figures of cherubim, palm trees, and open flowers." Cherubim guarded the way to the tree of life in Eden (Genesis 3:24). Putting cherubim, trees and flowers together on the temple walls made the house of God look like a garden. Ezekiel's vision of the temple repeats the pattern, with "a palm tree between cherub and cherub" and palms on every gatepost (Ezekiel 41:18; 40:16). The temple texts do not explain the carvings, so the link with Eden is a canonical echo drawn by comparing passages. It is a strong echo, though, and it gives a visual promise: drawing near to God brings people back toward the life that was lost in Eden.

That promise comes to Jesus. When He entered Jerusalem at Passover, the crowd "took branches of palm trees and went out to meet Him," crying, "Hosanna!... The King of Israel!" (John 12:13). Since the Maccabean victories, palm branches had also stood for national deliverance (1 Maccabees 13:51). The crowd welcomed a conquering King. Jesus accepted their praise, but He rode a donkey, and within days He won His victory through the cross.

The story ends with Revelation 7. John sees "a great multitude which no one could number, of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, clothed with white robes, with palm branches in their hands," crying, "Salvation belongs to our God who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb!" (7:9–10). The next verses say that He who sits on the throne will dwell among them (7:15). Revelation does not name the Feast of Tabernacles, but the scene echoes it. Israel took palm branches and rejoiced for seven days while living in temporary booths to remember the wilderness. In Revelation the redeemed hold them forever, gathered from every nation around the Lamb who saved them.

7. Spirit & Life

A palm tree tells a traveler something before he reaches it: water is here. Psalm 92 presents the righteous in that way. Their uprightness and their fruit in old age show that they are rooted in God's presence. Elsewhere Scripture describes the life of the Spirit as living water (John 7:38–39). The comparison is an analogy, but it fits the psalm well. A believer who keeps flourishing in a dry place is evidence that the Lord sustains His people with what others cannot see.

The palm passages also give a realistic order to Christian experience. Marah came before Elim. Exile came before Nehemiah's booths. Defeat came before the captives were carried to Jericho. God does not hide the bitter water or the chains. He also does not leave His people there. The Exodus account does not promise that every hardship will end in the same kind of relief, or on our schedule. What it shows is the character of the Lord who led Israel. He heard His people at Marah, made the water sweet, called Himself "the LORD who heals you" (Exodus 15:26), and brought them to a place He had already provided. Believers who are still at Marah can trust that same Lord to be with them and to lead them.

Joel adds a sober warning. Joy that depends on a good harvest will wither when the harvest fails. The joy that lasts is the joy Leviticus describes, rejoicing "before the LORD your God." The final scene in Revelation shows what that joy becomes. The palm branches that once went into temporary shelters are held by people who will never need a shelter again.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 33:9 — The record of Israel's journey repeats Elim's "twelve springs of water and seventy palm trees." The oasis was remembered as a fixed point on the road out of Egypt.

  • Deuteronomy 34:3 — From Mount Nebo, Moses sees "the Valley of Jericho, the city of palm trees." The well-watered land was part of the promise he saw but could not enter.

  • Judges 1:16 — The Kenites, relatives of Moses' father-in-law, went up "from the City of Palms" with Judah. People from outside Israel were included in its settlement of the land.

  • Judges 3:13 — Eglon king of Moab, with Ammon and Amalek, took the City of Palms. When Israel turned from the Lord, even this choice place was lost.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • Related Hebrew words (palm family): H8561 (tîmôrah), the carved palm figures in Solomon's and Ezekiel's temples (1 Kings 6:29; Ezekiel 41:18). H8560 (tômer), a palm tree used as a landmark, as in "the palm tree of Deborah," where Israel came to her for judgment (Judges 4:5). The upright palm made a fitting place for Deborah to give upright judgment, though the verse itself only names the location.

  • Greek counterpart: G5404 (phoinix), "palm," used for the palm branches in John 12:13 and Revelation 7:9.

  • Canonical pairing: The palm and the cedar together picture the righteous in Psalm 92:12. In the temple, palms are paired with cherubim (1 Kings 6:29; Ezekiel 41:18–19).
