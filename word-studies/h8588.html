LUXURY, DELIGHTS - (taʻănûwg, Strong's H8588)

Summary

The Hebrew word taʻănûwg names something delightful: a luxury, an exquisite pleasure, or something tenderly cherished. It is rare, appearing only five times, and those few uses cover a wide range of human life. A fool surrounded by luxury looks out of place (Proverbs 19:10). A king gathers "the delights of the sons of men" and finds them empty (Ecclesiastes 2:8). A husband rejoices over his bride "with your delights" (Song of Solomon 7:6). The prophet Micah uses the same word for the "precious children" a mourning nation will lose to captivity (Micah 1:16), and for the "pleasant houses" from which powerful men cast out the women of God's people (Micah 2:9).

Taken together, these verses show that delight in itself is not the problem. Its worth depends on who holds it, how it was gained, and what it is asked to do. God gives delight in marriage and in home and children, and He fiercely defends those gifts when the powerful tear them away from the weak. Even the best earthly delights cannot carry the weight of the heart's deepest longing, though. Scripture points that longing toward the Lord Himself.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Taʻănûwg is a Hebrew noun connected to the verb ʿānag (H6026). That verb describes being soft or delicate and taking keen pleasure in something. The noun therefore leans toward refined, tender enjoyment rather than coarse excess. Three of its five uses come from the wisdom and poetry books: Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Solomon. These books often reflect on royal courts and household life. The other two uses come from Micah, a prophet from the countryside of Judah who spoke against the rich and powerful of Jerusalem.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, taʻănûwg means delight, either the pleasure itself or the thing that gives it. Context shows two closely related emphases:

1. Luxury or refined pleasure: comforts and enjoyments that go beyond necessity, such as the courtly pleasures Solomon collected in Ecclesiastes 2:8 or the luxury unfit for a fool in Proverbs 19:10.

2. A cherished object of affection: something or someone held dear, such as the beloved in Song of Solomon 7:6, the "precious children" of Micah 1:16, and the "pleasant houses" of Micah 2:9.

The word carries no moral verdict of its own. It can describe wasted opulence or God-given joy.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

What changes across the five verses is the setting and the relationship. In Proverbs and Ecclesiastes, taʻănûwg is something possessed: luxury that a person owns, acquires, or accumulates. In the Song of Solomon and Micah, it is something loved: a person or a home that is treasured. Possession can be gained and still disappoint. Love can be precious and still be lost.

The NKJV's range of renderings ("Luxury," "delights," "precious," "pleasant") follows these shifts. In Micah 1:16 the Hebrew phrase behind "your precious children" can be glossed children of your delight. It describes children who are the parents' joy, not children raised in pampered comfort.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God is not suspicious of pleasure. The Song of Solomon openly celebrates delight between husband and wife, and Micah treats a peaceful home and beloved children as gifts so dear to God that stripping them away brings His judgment. Delight belongs to the good world God made.

The same word also exposes how delight goes wrong. When it falls into the hands of a fool, it shows up his lack of wisdom instead of improving him. When a king makes it his goal, it cannot satisfy him. When the powerful covet what belongs to others, they take away the simple delights of the vulnerable (Micah 2:1–2, 9). Scripture's concern is with the heart's ordering of delight, not with delight's existence. God ranks the home of an ordinary family above the king's collection, and He calls His people to find their deepest joy in Him.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 19:10 — luxury out of place. "Luxury is not fitting for a fool, Much less for a servant to rule over princes." The proverb pairs two things that strike the wise observer as upside down. Wealth in a fool's hands does not teach him wisdom. It gives his folly a larger stage and more resources to waste. The comparison with a servant ruling princes shows that misplaced luxury unsettles more than one life. It throws the right order of things into disorder. Throughout Proverbs, blessing is meant to travel with wisdom, and good things are safe only with someone of good character.

Ecclesiastes 2:1–11 — the most delights, the least satisfaction. The Preacher sets out deliberately to "test you with mirth" (2:1). He builds houses, plants vineyards and orchards, digs pools, acquires servants and herds, and gathers "silver and gold and the special treasures of kings," along with singers and "the delights of the sons of men" (2:8). Few people in history could have run this experiment more thoroughly. "Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them," he says (2:10). His verdict is that "all was vanity and grasping for the wind. There was no profit under the sun" (2:11). Taʻănûwg here stands at the top of a pile of human achievement. The emptiness is not a lack of quality. These were the finest pleasures available. The emptiness comes from asking created things to supply what only God can give.

Micah 1:16; 2:9 — cherished homes and children under threat. In Micah the word moves from palaces into ordinary households. In 1:16 the prophet calls the people to the mourning gestures of shaved heads: "Make yourself bald... Because of your precious children... For they shall go from you into captivity." This is a lament over coming judgment. No oppressor is named in the verse. The children are the family's delight, and the nation's sin will cost parents the very ones they hold dearest when they are carried away into captivity.

Micah 2:9 describes a different loss. Here the children are not taken by a foreign army. Their homes are taken by their own countrymen. The surrounding verses describe powerful men who lie awake planning schemes and seize fields and houses they covet (2:1–2). God addresses them directly: "The women of My people you cast out From their pleasant houses; From their children You have taken away My glory forever." Micah does not say whether these women were widows, and the delight taken from them was simply a home. God calls them "My people," and He calls what was stolen from their children "My glory." The dignity and inheritance He gave those families belonged to Him, so robbing the weak was an offense against God Himself.

The contrast with Ecclesiastes is striking. Solomon's countless delights could not fill his heart, while the pleasant houses of the women of His people were precious enough for God to defend.

Song of Solomon 7:6 — delight rightly enjoyed. "How fair and how pleasant you are, O love, with your delights!" Here taʻănûwg is wholly good. A husband speaks with open joy over his bride, and Scripture records it without embarrassment or apology. Intense pleasure, received within the faithful love of marriage, is part of God's design.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Micah's protest finds an echo in the ministry of Jesus. He warned against the scribes "who devour widows' houses" (Mark 12:40). Micah does not name widows, but the parallel is close: in both cases religious or powerful men enrich themselves by taking homes from the vulnerable. He also took children into His arms and blessed them (Mark 10:13–16). The God who called stolen homes and endangered children His own concern came in person and showed the same care.

Jesus also answers the Preacher's search. Ecclesiastes ends its experiment with nothing gained "under the sun." Christ offers a joy that the world's delights cannot supply and cannot take away (John 15:11; 16:22). The Song's celebration of marital delight also finds a wider frame in the New Testament, where the love of husband and wife reflects the love of Christ for His church (Ephesians 5:25–32). This is a resonance the New Testament draws, not a claim that the Song was written as an allegory. Delight in its fullest form is relational, and at its center is the delight of Christ in His people and their delight in Him.

7. Spirit & Life

The root verb behind taʻănûwg appears in Psalm 37:4: "Delight yourself also in the LORD, And He shall give you the desires of your heart." Set beside Ecclesiastes 2, this verse shows the problem and the cure. Solomon had every taʻănûwg available and was still empty. The psalmist directs the same capacity for delight toward the Lord. God does not ask His people to stop enjoying things. He asks them to enjoy Him most, and from that center the lesser delights find their place.

Micah adds a sober warning. He does not describe the private tastes of the men who stole homes. He names their sin plainly: "They covet fields and take them by violence, Also houses, and seize them" (Micah 2:2). Coveting came first, and seizure followed. That order is a warning to anyone whose desire for more begins to make other people look like obstacles. God watches over the small delights of the vulnerable, such as a roof, a family, and children who belong to Him. A believer who shares God's heart will guard those things for others as carefully as for themselves.

Finally, the Song reminds believers not to shrink back from the good pleasures God gives. Gratitude, not suspicion, is the right response to a delight received from His hand. It should be held in faithfulness and enjoyed without being made an idol.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

All five occurrences of taʻănûwg are discussed above, since each one illuminates a distinct side of the word. Proverbs 19:10 and Ecclesiastes 2:8 show delight as possession, while Song of Solomon 7:6, Micah 1:16, and Micah 2:9 show delight as cherished love.

9. Related Words & Canonical Pairings

  • H6026 (ʿānag) — lexical relative: the verb behind taʻănûwg, meaning to be delicate or to take delight. Its use in Psalm 37:4, "Delight yourself also in the LORD," turns the vocabulary of pleasure toward God Himself. Isaiah 58:14 similarly promises that those who honor the Sabbath will delight themselves in the Lord.
