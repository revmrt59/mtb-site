APPLE, APPLE TREE - (tappûwach, Strong's H8598)

Summary

The Hebrew word tappûwach names a fruit tree and its fruit, usually translated "apple" or "apple tree." It is a rare word, used only six times, and almost always in poetry. Each time, the tree or its fruit stands for something sweet, refreshing, or precious. In Proverbs 25:11 a well-timed word is "like apples of gold in settings of silver." In the Song of Solomon the bride compares her beloved to an apple tree standing out among the wild trees of the woods. She sits in his shade "with great delight," and his fruit is sweet to her taste.

The word also appears once in judgment. Joel names the apple tree among the orchards stripped by a locust plague and concludes, "Surely joy has withered away from the sons of men" (Joel 1:12). Together these passages teach that delight, rest, and refreshment are gifts from God, and that they wither when people turn from Him. Many Christians have read the Song's apple tree as a picture of Christ, the One in whose shade weary people find rest and in whose presence life becomes sweet again.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tappûwach is a Hebrew noun for a fragrant, sweet fruit and the tree that bears it. Scholars still debate whether the ancient tree was the apple, the apricot, or the quince. The Bible settles the question that matters by describing what the tree offers: shade, sweetness, and fragrance. Many lexicons connect the word to a Hebrew verb meaning to breathe or to blow, which would suit a fruit known for its scent. That derivation is commonly proposed but cannot by itself establish the word's meaning.

Two common assumptions need correcting. Genesis 3 never calls the forbidden fruit an apple. It speaks only of "fruit," and the apple became attached to the story later through European tradition. The English phrase "the apple of His eye" (Deuteronomy 32:10; Psalm 17:8; Zechariah 2:8) also translates different Hebrew words, which refer to the pupil of the eye.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tappûwach has one basic meaning that appears in two closely related forms:

1. The fruit itself, sweet and fragrant, as in "Refresh me with apples" (Song of Solomon 2:5).

2. The tree that bears that fruit and gives shade, as in "Like an apple tree among the trees of the woods" (Song of Solomon 2:3).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word is used in three settings. In wisdom literature it becomes a figure of worth and beauty: apples crafted from gold (Proverbs 25:11). In the Song of Solomon, where four of the six uses occur, the apple tree and its fruit belong to the language of love, with its shade, taste, scent, and memory. In prophecy the apple tree appears among the ruined trees of a devastated land (Joel 1:12).

The contrast between these settings carries the meaning. A tree that stands for delight in the Song becomes, when it withers in Joel, a sign that delight itself has gone.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture uses this small word to show that God intends His people to know real delight. The apple tree is a picture of good things received with pleasure: shade on a hot day, sweet fruit, and a pleasant scent. The Song treats these gifts as worth celebrating. They are not distractions from God.

The same word also shows that these gifts depend on Him. Joel sets the withered orchards beside a call for the whole nation to return to the Lord (Joel 2:12–13). When the tree dies, gladness dies with it. God is the giver of joy, and joy cannot be kept apart from Him. Proverbs extends the picture to speech. Words can be as valuable as golden fruit, so the way people talk to one another carries real spiritual weight.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 2:3: shade and fruit. "Like an apple tree among the trees of the woods, So is my beloved among the sons. I sat down in his shade with great delight, And his fruit was sweet to my taste." The poem does not fault "the trees of the woods"; it simply sets the beloved apart from them. In the bride's eyes the apple tree stands out because it gives two things together: protection from the heat and nourishment. In a dry land, where good shade was scarce, that pairing was precious. The bride does not admire the tree from a distance. She sits down under it, and that is the posture of someone who has stopped searching because she has found what she needs.

Song of Solomon 8:5: coming up from the wilderness. "Who is this coming up from the wilderness, Leaning upon her beloved? I awakened you under the apple tree." The bride, who had been in the wilderness, now walks leaning on the one she loves. The poem does not name the speaker of "I awakened you under the apple tree." Many interpreters, reading the Hebrew pronoun forms, hear the bride addressing the bridegroom; others assign the line differently. Either way, the apple tree marks where love was awakened, and where "your mother brought you forth." The woman who comes up from the wilderness is held by the one whose love started there.

Proverbs 25:11: golden apples in silver. "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold In settings of silver." The picture is a crafted ornament. A golden fruit is set in a silver frame, and each part shows off the other. A true word works the same way when it is spoken at the right moment and in the right manner. Proverbs 15:23 adds, "a word spoken in due season, how good it is!" Gold and silver were also the chief metals of the tabernacle (Exodus 25:3). That echo may suggest that wise speech has a sacred kind of worth, though the proverb's main point is the beauty and value of a well-timed word.

Joel 1:12: when the apple tree withers. A locust plague has stripped Judah. Joel lists the trees one by one, "The vine... the fig tree... The pomegranate tree, The palm tree also, And the apple tree," and then names what the loss means: "Surely joy has withered away from the sons of men." The apple tree, which the Song uses as a picture of delight, appears here among the dead orchards. Joel calls the people to return to the Lord with fasting and weeping. Judgment strips away their pleasures so that they will seek the Giver.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song of Solomon celebrates the love between a husband and wife. Scripture also uses marriage to picture Christ and His people (Ephesians 5:31–32), and on that basis the church has long heard the Song as describing the Lord and those He loves. This is a canonical comparison, since the Song does not name Christ. Read that way, Song 2:3 pictures Jesus: He stands apart from every other source of comfort and gives both shelter and nourishment. His invitation to the weary to come to Him for rest (Matthew 11:28) is an offer to sit in His shade. His call in John 15:4, "Abide in Me, and I in you," describes the same settled dependence as the bride resting under the tree.

Read the same way, Song 8:5 points in the same direction. Israel's story began in the wilderness, and God's people often wandered there. The bride coming up from the wilderness, leaning on her beloved, pictures what the gospel accomplishes: a people brought out of barrenness and held up by Christ. The contrast with Eden should not be pressed into a claim that Eden's tree was an apple. In Eden, the man and woman took fruit from a tree and then hid "among the trees of the garden" (Genesis 3:8). The Song shows a tree that marks love and unashamed nearness. Christ, who bore the curse on a tree (Galatians 3:13), brings people back to that nearness.

Joel's story also moves toward Christ. After the withering, God promises that the trees will bear fruit again (Joel 2:22) and that He will pour out His Spirit on all flesh (Joel 2:28). Peter announced that promise fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 2:16–17). The joy that withered is restored through the Messiah and the gift of His Spirit. Even Proverbs 25:11 finds its fullest meaning in Him. Isaiah's Servant is given the ability to speak a word in season to the weary (Isaiah 50:4), and Jesus is the Word who came at the right time.

7. Spirit & Life

The bride says, "I sat down." Many believers know a great deal about Christ while still standing in the heat, busy and anxious. The apple tree offers its shade only to those who come under it and stay there. Psalm 91:1 describes the same settled place: the one who dwells in the secret place of the Most High "Shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty." When the bride says His fruit "was sweet to my taste," she is describing joy she has tasted for herself. Psalm 16:11 says it plainly: "In Your presence is fullness of joy."

Joel adds a sober lesson. He addresses a whole nation in real disaster, its crops destroyed and offerings cut off (Joel 1:13), and summons it to "Return to the LORD your God" (Joel 2:13). He is not diagnosing every fading of a believer's joy, but by application, when delight dries up, it is wise to ask whether God is calling us back. The withered orchard told Judah what it had lost so that the people would turn to the One who could restore it.

Proverbs 25:11 brings these truths into ordinary conversation. Someone who rests in Christ's shade has something to give others: a true word, spoken at the right moment, that imparts "grace to the hearers" (Ephesians 4:29). Colossians 4:6 calls for speech "always... with grace, seasoned with salt," so that we know how to answer each person. A word like that can be golden fruit to someone who is weary.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The word's few uses have mostly been discussed above. Two more complete the picture:

  • Song of Solomon 2:5: "Sustain me with cakes of raisins, Refresh me with apples, For I am lovesick." Apples are what the bride asks for when love leaves her faint, which shows the fruit as a source of renewed strength.

  • Song of Solomon 7:8: "The fragrance of your breath like apples." Here the beloved praises the bride, and the apple supplies the image of a sweet fragrance that delights the one who loves her.
