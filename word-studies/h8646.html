TERAH - (Terach, Strong's H8646)

Summary

Terah is best known as the father of Abram (later Abraham), Nahor, and Haran. He was a descendant of Shem who lived in Ur of the Chaldeans, led his household out toward Canaan, stopped at Haran, and died there at two hundred and five years old (Genesis 11:24–32). The same Hebrew name also labels an unrelated place, a camp where Israel stopped during the wilderness years (Numbers 33:27–28). The meaning of the name is uncertain. Proposals include a link (noted in the Jewish Encyclopedia) to an Akkadian word for a wild mountain goat or ibex, a Hebrew sense of "delay" or "wandering," or an older scholarly reconstruction connecting it with the Hebrew word for "moon," but none of these is established.

Terah stands at the hinge between the scattering at Babel and the call of Abraham. Scripture is candid about him. Centuries later, Joshua told Israel that their fathers, "including Terah, the father of Abraham," had lived beyond the Euphrates and "served other gods" (Joshua 24:2). The family through which God chose to bless all nations began in idolatry, not in faithfulness. Stephen adds that God had already appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia, which shows that the journey out of Ur began with God's call (Acts 7:2–4). Luke names Terah in the line of Jesus (Luke 3:34). His story shows a God who calls people out of darkness by grace, and it leaves a sober picture of a household that set out for the land of promise and settled short of it.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

The Hebrew lemma Terach (H8646) appears 13 times in 11 verses. It names Abraham's father and a wilderness camp, which should not be confused.

Terah the patriarch. He was the son of Nahor and a descendant of Shem through the line traced in Genesis 11:10–26. He was the father of Abram, Nahor, and Haran, and the grandfather of Lot (Genesis 11:27). Through his son Nahor, he was also the ancestor of Rebekah, Laban, Rachel, and Leah. He belongs to the generation just before the patriarchal period. He lived in Ur, conventionally placed in southern Mesopotamia, and later died in Haran in upper Mesopotamia; Scripture does not say how old he was when he left Ur or how long he stayed in Haran.

Terah the wilderness camp. This was a stopping place on Israel's route between Tahath and Mithkah (Numbers 33:27–28). Some older English versions spell it "Tarah." Its location is unknown, and nothing in Scripture connects it to Abraham's father.

The New Testament gives the patriarch's name in Greek form as Thara, in Luke's genealogy (Luke 3:34). That form is indexed separately under its own Greek entry.

The meaning of the name is uncertain. Three proposals have been made, and they should be weighed differently:

  • Akkadian. Some scholars connect the name to an Akkadian word for an ibex or mountain goat. This is a linguistic proposal, not a settled conclusion.

  • Hebrew. Others relate it to a Hebrew sense of "delay" or "wander." That meaning would fit Terah's halted journey neatly, which is exactly why it should be treated with caution. Genesis itself makes no wordplay on the name, and this proposal may be a later folk etymology.

  • "Moon." A possible connection with the Hebrew word for "moon" (yareach) is often noted. It draws attention because Ur and Haran are widely associated with moon worship in the ancient world. Even so, the link is an older scholarly reconstruction and remains a hypothesis.

Scripture never explains the name, so none of these proposals should be read as a key to Terah's character.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

A Family in Ur

Genesis introduces Terah within the line of Shem. It records that he lived seventy years and fathered Abram, Nahor, and Haran (Genesis 11:24–26). Abram is likely listed first because of his importance, not necessarily because he was the oldest. The section heading "This is the genealogy of Terah" (Genesis 11:27) is significant. The same formula organizes the whole book of Genesis, and here it opens the story of Abraham. Abraham's story is told under his father's name.

Sorrow came early to the family. Terah's son Haran died in Ur while Terah was still alive, leaving behind a son named Lot (Genesis 11:28). Nahor married Haran's daughter Milcah. Abram married Sarai, and the text adds a quiet but weighty detail: she was barren and had no child (Genesis 11:29–30). The family that would receive the promise of countless descendants enters the story with an early grave and an empty cradle.

Out of Ur, Stopped at Haran

Genesis 11:31 records the move: "And Terah took his son Abram and his grandson Lot, the son of Haran, and his daughter-in-law Sarai, his son Abram's wife, and they went out with them from Ur of the Chaldeans to go to the land of Canaan; and they came to Haran and dwelt there." Terah is named as the one who led, which fits the ancient pattern of crediting a move to the head of the clan.

Later Scripture shows what moved them. In Genesis 15:7 the LORD says He brought Abram out of Ur, and Nehemiah 9:7 repeats this. Stephen is more specific: "The God of glory appeared to our father Abraham when he was in Mesopotamia, before he dwelt in Haran" (Acts 7:2). Genesis 12:1 likewise begins, "Now the LORD had said to Abram," which points back to a call already given. The journey was set in motion by God's word to Abram, even though Terah led it.

The goal was Canaan, yet the family "came to Haran and dwelt there." Scripture does not explain the halt. It simply records that Terah lived out his days there: "So the days of Terah were two hundred and five years, and Terah died in Haran" (Genesis 11:32). Abram then went on at seventy-five, taking Sarai and Lot (Genesis 12:4–5).

Stephen says that God moved Abram to Canaan "when his father was dead" (Acts 7:4). This raises a question of dates. If Abram was born when Terah was seventy, Terah would still have been alive when Abram left. The simplest explanation is that Abram was not the firstborn. If he was born when Terah was about 130, the numbers agree. The Samaritan Pentateuch records Terah's age at death as 145, which is another way the tension has been handled. Either way, Genesis gives no reason to doubt Stephen's account.

Terah's household did not entirely leave the region. Nahor's family remained near Haran, and Abraham later sent there for a wife for Isaac (Genesis 24:10). Old loyalties lingered there as well. Rachel took her father Laban's household gods (Genesis 31:19), and when Laban swore an oath, he invoked "the God of Abraham, the God of Nahor, and the God of their father" (Genesis 31:53).

Remembered at Shechem

The most revealing word about Terah comes centuries after his death. At Shechem, as Israel renewed its commitment to the LORD, Joshua began the nation's history like this: "Thus says the LORD God of Israel: 'Your fathers, including Terah, the father of Abraham and the father of Nahor, dwelt on the other side of the River in old times; and they served other gods'" (Joshua 24:2). In the next verse God says, "I took your father Abraham" (Joshua 24:3). Joshua then calls Israel to "put away the gods which your fathers served on the other side of the River" and to choose whom they will serve (Joshua 24:14–15).

Preserved in the Genealogies

The Chronicler's line from Adam to Abraham includes Terah: "Nahor, Terah, and Abram, who is Abraham" (1 Chronicles 1:26–27). Luke's genealogy of Jesus, which runs back to Adam, also includes him (Luke 3:34). Matthew's genealogy begins with Abraham and so does not name Terah.

Terah, the Wilderness Camp

In Moses' record of Israel's journeys, the people "departed from Tahath and camped at Terah," and then "moved from Terah and camped at Mithkah" (Numbers 33:27–28). Nothing else is said about the place. Its value lies in the larger record of the LORD leading His people stage by stage through the wilderness.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states plainly that Terah served other gods and that God took Abraham from that setting (Joshua 24:2–3). That one fact changes how the whole story of Israel should be read. God did not find a faithful family and reward it. He called a man out of an idolatrous household and made him the father of a people. Joshua said this at a covenant renewal to a nation already dwelling in cities it had not built and enjoying the land (Joshua 24:13), a nation tempted to think too highly of itself. Their history began with grace.

Terah's place in Genesis is also significant. His genealogy follows directly after Babel, where humanity tried to make a name for itself (Genesis 11:4). Within a few verses God promises to bless Abram "And make your name great" (Genesis 12:2). Terah's household is the doorway through which that promise enters a scattered world.

The halt at Haran calls for careful handling. Scripture states that the family set out for Canaan and settled in Haran. It does not state Terah's motives. It is a fair inference that the promise advances only when Abram moves on, since Genesis 12 begins with God's command to leave "your father's house." It is a reasonable but unproven suggestion that Haran felt familiar because of its religious culture. The text itself keeps the focus on God's call, not on Terah's reasons.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Luke explicitly places Terah in the line of Jesus, "the son of Abraham, the son of Terah, the son of Nahor" (Luke 3:34). The promise that came to Abraham, the promise in which all families of the earth would be blessed, passed through a man Joshua names as a worshiper of other gods. The Savior's ancestry, like Matthew's account of Tamar and Rahab, does not hide what grace overcame.

The larger canon develops the pattern of being called out. Abraham's departure becomes the model of faith in Hebrews 11:8–10, and Hebrews 11:15–16 observes that if the patriarchs had kept their minds on the country they left, they could have gone back, but they desired "a better, that is, a heavenly country." Paul describes Abraham's righteousness as a gift received by believing God, "who justifies the ungodly" (Romans 4:3–5). He also describes conversion as turning "to God from idols to serve the living and true God" (1 Thessalonians 1:9).

These passages do not mention Terah. Still, they bring out what his household shows in history: salvation begins when God calls someone out of false worship and toward a promised inheritance. This is a strong canonical theme. It does not make Terah a formal type.

5. Spirit & Life

Joshua 24 tells us how God wants His people to remember their past. Israel was not told to admire its ancestors. It was told the truth: your fathers served other gods, and I took Abraham. Every believer has a similar story. None of us began with clean hands, and the God who called Abraham out of Ur has not changed His way of saving. That leaves no room for spiritual pride and plenty of room for gratitude.

Terah's halt at Haran is a fair warning, as long as we do not claim to know his heart. A person can leave a great deal behind, travel a long way, and still settle somewhere short of where God is leading. Haran was not Ur, but it was not Canaan either. The answer to that danger is not more effort. It is the voice that spoke to Abram: "Get out of your country, From your family And from your father's house, To a land that I will show you" (Genesis 12:1). God's word goes further than family custom, comfort, or the pace of the people around us.

There is tenderness here too. Terah was not abandoned by the story. His name heads the account of Abraham, and it stands in the genealogy of Christ. God's grace reached into that family and kept working through its generations.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 11:24–28 — Terah's ancestry and sons, Haran's early death, and the heading "the genealogy of Terah."

  • Genesis 11:31–32 — The departure from Ur toward Canaan, the halt at Haran, and Terah's death.

  • Genesis 12:1–5; 15:7 — God's call to Abram to leave his father's house, and God's claim that He brought Abram out of Ur.

  • Joshua 24:2–3, 14–15 — The explicit statement that Terah served other gods, and the call to put those gods away.

  • Acts 7:2–4 — Stephen's account that God appeared to Abraham before Haran and moved him after Terah died.

  • 1 Chronicles 1:26–27 — Terah in the line from Adam to Abraham.

  • Luke 3:34 — Terah in the genealogy of Jesus.

  • Numbers 33:27–28 — Terah, the separate wilderness camp.
