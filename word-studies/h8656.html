TIRZAH - (Tirtsâh, Strong's H8656)

Summary

In the Old Testament, Tirzah is the name of a woman and of a city. The woman belonged to the tribe of Manasseh and was one of the five daughters of Zelophehad. She and her sisters stood at the doorway of the tabernacle and asked for a share of the Promised Land because their father had died without sons. The city lay in the hill country of Manasseh. Joshua defeated its Canaanite king, and Tirzah later became the royal city of the northern kingdom of Israel for roughly half a century. The name is commonly explained as coming from a Hebrew root meaning to be pleasing or to delight in, which would give it the sense pleasantness or delight. Scripture does not say why the woman or the city received the name, and it never connects the woman with the city, so the two should be kept apart.

The two Tirzahs move in opposite directions. The daughter of Zelophehad and her sisters took God's promise seriously while the land west of the Jordan was still waiting to be conquered and divided among the tribes, and the LORD answered, "The daughters of Zelophehad speak what is right" (Numbers 27:7). The city, by contrast, became home to kings who walked in the sin of Jeroboam, and more than one of them seized the throne through conspiracy. In Tirzah one king was murdered while drunk in the house of Arza, his steward, and his murderer later burned the king's house down upon himself when the city was taken. The Song of Solomon, for its part, remembers Tirzah only for its beauty, placing it beside Jerusalem in praise of the beloved bride. Set side by side, the woman and the city illustrate what God honors: people who trust His word to receive what He has promised. No pleasant setting or grasp for power can replace that.

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The Hebrew name Tirzah is commonly derived from the root ratsah, a verb meaning to be pleased with, to accept with favor or to take delight in. On that derivation the name carries the sense of pleasantness or delight. That meaning would suit a daughter or a city set among springs and fertile land, but nothing in the biblical text explains why either the woman or the city received the name. The meaning describes the word itself. It does not interpret the woman's life or prove anything about the city's character, though readers will notice that the city's later history sits in tension with its name.

Strong's H8656 covers both uses, and the context of each verse shows which Tirzah is meant.

Tirzah, daughter of Zelophehad. She belonged to the tribe of Manasseh. Her family line runs from Zelophehad through Hepher, Gilead and Machir back to Manasseh, the son of Joseph (Numbers 27:1). She appears in Numbers 26:33; 27:1; 36:11 and in Joshua 17:3, always with her sisters Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah and Milcah. Tirzah is listed last in Numbers 26:33 and Numbers 27:1 but second in Numbers 36:11, so the lists do not settle her birth order. Her story belongs to the end of the wilderness years and the first settlement of the land.

Tirzah, the city. This was a town in the central hill country within the territory of Manasseh. It is commonly identified with Tell el-Far'ah (North), northeast of Shechem near a good water supply, though that identification comes from archaeological study rather than from the biblical text. The city appears in Joshua 12:24, in nine verses of 1 Kings (14:17; 15:21, 33; 16:6, 8, 9, 15, 17, 23), in 2 Kings 15:14 and 15:16, and in Song of Solomon 6:4.

Both Tirzahs are connected with Manasseh, and it is tempting to imagine that the city was named after the woman or that her family later settled there. Scripture makes no such connection, and the two are best kept distinct.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Tirzah, Daughter of Zelophehad

When Moses and Eleazar counted the new generation on the plains of Moab, the census of Manasseh paused at Zelophehad. He "had no sons, but daughters," and the record names all five, including Tirzah (Numbers 26:33). The census itself was preparing for the division of the land (Numbers 26:52–56). Under that arrangement, a family with no sons would simply disappear from the map of Israel.

The five sisters refused to let that happen. They came "by the doorway of the tabernacle of meeting" and stood before Moses, Eleazar the priest, the leaders and the whole congregation (Numbers 27:2). They made their case carefully. Their father had not joined Korah's rebellion against the LORD. Instead "he died in his own sin," as the rest of his generation had died in the wilderness (Numbers 27:3). Then they asked their question: "Why should the name of our father be removed from among his family because he had no son? Give us a possession among our father's brothers" (Numbers 27:4).

Moses did not settle the matter on his own authority. "Moses brought their case before the LORD" (Numbers 27:5), and the LORD answered plainly: "The daughters of Zelophehad speak what is right; you shall surely give them a possession of inheritance among their father's brothers, and cause the inheritance of their father to pass to them" (Numbers 27:7). God went further than their single case. He gave Israel a standing rule for passing inheritance to daughters, and then to other relatives when a man had no sons (Numbers 27:8–11). Five women's request became law for the entire nation.

A second question followed in Numbers 36. The leaders of Gilead's clan worried that if the daughters married outside Manasseh, their land would pass to another tribe. The LORD affirmed this concern as well and directed that the daughters marry within their father's tribe so that each tribe's inheritance would stay intact (Numbers 36:5–9). The sisters, Tirzah among them, did "as the LORD commanded Moses" and married sons of their father's brothers (Numbers 36:10–12). The ruling protected both their claim and the wider order God had set for the tribes.

The story reaches its goal in Joshua 17:3–6. Years later, with the land being divided, the sisters came before Eleazar, Joshua and the leaders and reminded them of what the LORD had commanded through Moses. They received their portion among their father's brothers, and Manasseh's allotment was counted with the daughters' shares included. The promise made at the tabernacle door became actual land under their feet.

Tirzah, the City

A Canaanite city overthrown. The city first appears at the very end of the list of kings Joshua defeated west of the Jordan: "the king of Tirzah, one; all the kings, thirty-one" (Joshua 12:24). Tirzah was one Canaanite city-state among many that fell as God gave Israel the land.

The royal city of the northern kingdom. After the kingdom divided, Jeroboam first built up Shechem and Penuel (1 Kings 12:25). By the time of his son's illness, however, the royal household was living in Tirzah. Jeroboam's wife had gone in disguise to the prophet Ahijah, who told her that the boy would die when her feet entered the city. She "came to Tirzah," and as she reached the threshold of the house, the child died (1 Kings 14:12, 17). Tirzah enters the history of Israel's kings through a word of judgment that came true on its doorstep.

Baasha seized the throne by killing Jeroboam's son Nadab while Israel was besieging the Philistine town of Gibbethon. He then destroyed Jeroboam's entire household, as Ahijah had foretold (1 Kings 15:27–29). Baasha "reigned in Tirzah" twenty-four years and "did evil in the sight of the LORD," walking in Jeroboam's way (1 Kings 15:33–34). When Asa of Judah turned the Syrians against him, Baasha abandoned his fortification project at Ramah and "remained in Tirzah" (1 Kings 15:21). The prophet Jehu son of Hanani announced that Baasha's house would fall just as Jeroboam's had (1 Kings 16:1–4). Baasha died and was buried in Tirzah (1 Kings 16:6).

The palace in flames. The darkest scenes in Tirzah's history come next. Baasha's son Elah "reigned two years in Tirzah" (1 Kings 16:8). Zimri, "commander of half his chariots, conspired against him as he was in Tirzah drinking himself drunk in the house of Arza, steward of his house in Tirzah" (1 Kings 16:9). Zimri killed him and took the throne. Then he wiped out Baasha's whole family, "according to the word of the LORD, which He spoke against Baasha by Jehu the prophet" (1 Kings 16:12), "for all the sins of Baasha and the sins of Elah his son... in provoking the LORD God of Israel to anger with their idols" (1 Kings 16:13).

Zimri "reigned in Tirzah seven days" (1 Kings 16:15). The army was once again camped against Gibbethon, the same place where Baasha's coup had begun. When the soldiers heard what Zimri had done, they made their commander Omri king that same day. "Omri and all Israel with him went up from Gibbethon, and they besieged Tirzah" (1 Kings 16:17). Zimri's end was swift: "when Zimri saw that the city was taken... he went into the citadel of the king's house and burned the king's house down upon himself with fire, and died" (1 Kings 16:18). The writer adds that Zimri died for his own sins, because he too walked in the way of Jeroboam (1 Kings 16:19).

Abandoned for Samaria. Omri had to fight a rival, Tibni, before he could rule the whole kingdom (1 Kings 16:21–22). He reigned twelve years, "six years he reigned in Tirzah" (1 Kings 16:23). He then bought the hill of Samaria and built a new capital there (1 Kings 16:24). From that point Tirzah was no longer the royal city.

A base for one more coup. Tirzah appears once more well over a century later, in the chaotic final decades of the northern kingdom. "Menahem the son of Gadi went up from Tirzah, came to Samaria, and struck Shallum the son of Jabesh in Samaria and killed him; and he reigned in his place" (2 Kings 15:14). Shallum had himself murdered the previous king only a month before. Menahem then attacked Tiphsah "from Tirzah" with terrible cruelty because the town would not open to him (2 Kings 15:16). The former capital had become a launching point for bloodshed once again. The text does not explain why Menahem acted from Tirzah, so any suggestion that the city harbored a rival faction remains a guess.

Remembered for its beauty. Tirzah's remaining appearance in Scripture is in poetry. The bridegroom tells his beloved, "O my love, you are as beautiful as Tirzah, Lovely as Jerusalem, Awesome as an army with banners!" (Song of Solomon 6:4). Here the city is remembered for its loveliness alone, and it stands beside Jerusalem as a measure of beauty.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

The daughter's story shows God's justice toward the vulnerable. Scripture states plainly that the LORD judged the sisters' request to be right and made it the basis of a lasting law (Numbers 27:7–11). The faith of these five women is a fair inference from their actions. They were asking for a share in land Israel had not yet conquered, which only makes sense if they believed God would actually give it. Their request also honored the order God had already set. They appealed through Moses and the priest at the tabernacle, and later they accepted the marriage limits of Numbers 36 that protected their tribe. God did not overturn His arrangement for the land to answer them. He applied it more fully, so that a family without sons was not erased from Israel. His concern reaches the households that ordinary custom would overlook.

The city's story shows a kingdom built on the wrong foundation. God had made Jeroboam a remarkable offer. If he would walk in God's ways as David had, the LORD would "build for you an enduring house" (1 Kings 11:38). Jeroboam instead set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan, afraid that worship in Jerusalem would draw his people back to the house of David (1 Kings 12:26–30). Tirzah became the royal home of the kings who kept that sin alive. Jerusalem was the city God had chosen to put His name (1 Kings 11:36). Tirzah was a city kings chose for their own reasons.

The pattern in Tirzah is stated directly in the text. The prophets announced the fall of each royal house in advance: Ahijah against Jeroboam (1 Kings 14:10–11) and Jehu against Baasha (1 Kings 16:1–4). Each word was then fulfilled. Even Zimri's slaughter of Baasha's house is described as happening "according to the word of the LORD" (1 Kings 16:12). This does not mean God approved of Zimri, who was condemned for his own sins (1 Kings 16:19). It means that human treachery could not move outside God's rule. The kings of Tirzah plotted, drank, murdered and burned, and the word of the LORD still had the final say.

The contrast between the city's name and its record is an observation the reader draws, not a lesson the text announces. Still, the contrast is hard to miss. A city whose name is commonly explained as pleasantness saw a king drink himself into helplessness, a palace burned down, and a dynasty wiped out within a week. A pleasant place could not make an unfaithful kingdom pleasing to God.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

An inheritance received by promise. The New Testament does not mention the daughters of Zelophehad, but their story belongs to a theme Scripture develops at length. Israel's inheritance in the land anticipated the "promise of the eternal inheritance" secured through Christ (Hebrews 9:15). Paul teaches that all who belong to Christ, "male and female" alike, are "heirs according to the promise" (Galatians 3:28–29), and Peter calls husbands and wives "heirs together of the grace of life" (1 Peter 3:7). The connection is thematic rather than a stated type. Even so, the parallel is clear and fruitful. Five women who had no standing under ordinary custom came to the place of God's presence and asked for a share among His people. God granted it and wrote it into law. In Christ, God has opened the inheritance to everyone who comes through Him.

The true King and the true city. The kings who ruled from Tirzah show how fragile human thrones are. One dynasty followed another, and every one of them was built on Jeroboam's sin. The prophets looked beyond this division to a day when God would make Israel and Judah "one nation" under "one king," His servant David (Ezekiel 37:15–24). This is strongly supported canonical hope, and the New Testament presents Jesus as the Son of David whose kingdom will have no end (Luke 1:32–33). The one dynasty no conspiracy can overthrow is His.

The beauty of the bride. Song of Solomon 6:4 places Tirzah and Jerusalem side by side in praise of the bride. If the poem comes from before the kingdom divided, as its link to Solomon suggests, the pairing simply names two lovely cities of the north and south. Readers who know Tirzah's later history may also hear a quiet echo of the hope that God's divided people could be whole and beautiful again. That second reading is a thematic resonance and not the poet's stated meaning. The broader biblical picture of a bride made beautiful for her husband, fulfilled in Christ's love for the church that He presents "not having spot or wrinkle" (Ephesians 5:25–27), gives that resonance a fitting direction. What earthly Tirzah could not become, Christ makes His people: truly pleasing to the King.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

The daughters of Zelophehad teach us to take God's promises seriously enough to ask Him for them. They did not grumble in their tents or try to seize land on their own. They came openly to the doorway of the tabernacle and stated their case, and Moses carried it to the LORD. God was not offended by the request. He called it right. Believers today come to God through Christ with the same freedom, asking Him to give what He has already promised. That includes our place among His people, our share in His grace, and our hope of an inheritance that cannot fade.

Tirzah the city offers a sober warning. Its kings had God's own prophets speaking to them, yet they kept the idols that Jeroboam introduced. Jeroboam had set up his calves for political reasons, fearing that worship in Jerusalem would turn his people back to the house of David (1 Kings 12:26–28). Scripture does not give the later kings' reasons, but it says the same of them in turn: Baasha "walked in the way of Jeroboam" (1 Kings 15:34), and so did Zimri (1 Kings 16:19). Elah's drunkenness left him defenseless. Zimri's ambition ended in fire within seven days. His name became a byword, and years later Jezebel taunted another usurper by calling him "Zimri" (2 Kings 9:31). When Zimri saw that the city was taken, he burned the king's house down upon himself and died. Grasping for power does not stop at hurting others. In the end it consumes the one who grasps.

The contrast between the two Tirzahs comes down to the way each one sought security. The sisters looked for it in God's word and received land that stayed in their family. The kings of Tirzah looked for it in their own plans and lost everything they held. God still welcomes the humble person who asks Him for what He has promised. He still resists the proud person who tries to build a kingdom without Him.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Numbers 26:33 – Names Tirzah among the five daughters of Zelophehad in the census that prepared Israel to divide the land.

  • Numbers 27:1–11 – The sisters' request at the tabernacle door, the LORD's verdict that they "speak what is right," and the resulting law of inheritance.

  • Numbers 36:1–12 – The marriage ruling that kept the daughters' inheritance within Manasseh and their obedience to it.

  • Joshua 17:3–6 – The sisters receive their promised portion in the land.

  • Joshua 12:24 – The king of Tirzah appears among the Canaanite rulers Joshua defeated.

  • 1 Kings 14:12–17 – Ahijah's prophecy is fulfilled as Jeroboam's son dies when his mother reaches Tirzah.

  • 1 Kings 15:21, 27–34; 16:1–6 – Baasha rules and is buried in Tirzah while continuing Jeroboam's sin, and Jehu announces judgment on his house.

  • 1 Kings 16:8–19 – Elah's murder in Tirzah, Zimri's seven-day reign, the siege by Omri and the burning of the palace.

  • 1 Kings 16:23–24 – Omri reigns six years in Tirzah before moving the capital to Samaria.

  • 2 Kings 15:14–16 – Menahem launches his coup and his brutal campaign from Tirzah.

  • Song of Solomon 6:4 – Tirzah is paired with Jerusalem as a picture of the bride's beauty.
