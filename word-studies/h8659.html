TARSHISH - (Tarshîysh, Strong's H8659)

Summary

Tarshish is best known in the Bible as a distant, wealthy seaport or region at the far edge of the ancient world, famous for its silver and metals and for the large ocean-going "ships of Tarshish" named after it. The same Hebrew name also belongs to three different men: a grandson of Japheth in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10:4), a Benjamite (1 Chronicles 7:10), and a Persian prince at the court of Ahasuerus (Esther 1:14). The meaning of the name is uncertain. One proposal connects it to a Semitic word for smelting or refining, which would suit a place known for metal trade. Others treat it as a foreign word borrowed into Hebrew. A common scholarly identification is Tartessos in southern Spain, though that identification is provisional and other proposals, including a Sardinian location, remain.

Across the Old Testament, Tarshish stands for the farthest reaches of the world and for the wealth that flows across its seas. Solomon's fleet brought treasure from it, and Jehoshaphat's fleet was wrecked because he built it with a wicked partner. The prophets name the ships of Tarshish among the proud things God will bring low. Jonah bought passage to Tarshish to get away "from the presence of the LORD" (Jonah 1:3). Later he admitted he ran because he knew God would show mercy to Nineveh (Jonah 4:2). The prophets also look past judgment. The kings of Tarshish will bring gifts to the righteous King (Psalm 72:10). The ships of Tarshish will carry God's scattered children home (Isaiah 60:9). God will send messengers to Tarshish to declare His glory among nations that have never heard of Him (Isaiah 66:19). The place Jonah chose as a refuge from God's mercy becomes, in God's plan, a destination of that mercy. That hope comes into view in the gospel's advance "to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8).

Full Study

1. IDENTITY & NAME

The Hebrew name Tarshîysh appears 28 times in 24 verses. Some verses use it more than once; Jonah 1:3 names Tarshish three times. Tarshish names different men and a trading region, and also appears in the expression "ships of Tarshish." These uses should be distinguished.

Tarshish the son of Javan. In the Table of Nations, Tarshish is listed among the sons of Javan, the son of Japheth, together with Elishah, Kittim, and Dodanim (Genesis 10:4; 1 Chronicles 1:7). Genesis goes on to say that the coastland peoples spread out from these families (Genesis 10:5). The name of this ancestor is closely tied to the later seafaring people and region.

Tarshish the place. Most uses of the name refer to a distant, prosperous trading region reached by sea. Ezekiel lists its exports as silver, iron, tin, and lead (Ezekiel 27:12), and Jeremiah mentions silver beaten into plates "from Tarshish" (Jeremiah 10:9). A common scholarly identification is Tartessos in southern Spain, a region rich in minerals beyond the Strait of Gibraltar. That location fits Jonah's choice well, since it lay about as far west of Nineveh as a person could sail. Other proposals include Sardinia, Tarsus in Cilicia, and a location reached through the Red Sea. Scripture itself never fixes the map location. What it makes clear is that Tarshish meant "far away, across the sea, and rich."

"Ships of Tarshish." The phrase seems to have become a name for a class of large, long-range merchant vessels, much as "ocean liner" names a type of ship whatever its route. The strongest evidence comes from Jehoshaphat's reign. The Hebrew of 1 Kings 22:48 speaks of ships of Tarshish built "to go to Ophir for gold" (the NKJV renders the phrase "merchant ships"). Those ships were built at Ezion Geber on the Gulf of Aqaba, a Red Sea port from which no ship could reach Spain without sailing around Africa. Reading "ships of Tarshish" as a type of vessel is one widely held explanation, though some have proposed a second, eastern Tarshish instead, and no reconstruction conclusively settles every destination statement in Chronicles.

Tarshish the Benjamite. A son of Bilhan in the line of Benjamin (1 Chronicles 7:10; spelled "Tharshish" in some English Bibles). Nothing more is recorded about him.

Tarshish the Persian prince. One of "the seven princes of Persia and Media, who had access to the king's presence" (Esther 1:14). He shared in the counsel that removed Queen Vashti. His name is probably Persian and only happens to sound like the Hebrew name, so its meaning is unknown.

The meaning of the name. The meaning of Tarshîysh is uncertain. One proposal connects it to a Semitic root meaning "smelting plant" or "refinery," which would fit the metal trade Scripture associates with the place. Other scholars think it is an older foreign word that Hebrew borrowed. Either way, this is a proposal and not a settled meaning. Scripture makes no wordplay on the name, and its significance does not depend on the etymology. Hebrew also has a similar-sounding word for a precious stone, but that word is a separate entry and is not part of this study. Tarsus, the hometown of the apostle Paul, is likewise a separately indexed Greek name (G5019); the related G5018 means "of Tarsus," as in "Saul of Tarsus" (Acts 9:11). Some reconstructions link the two names historically, but the Bible does not equate them.

2. BIBLICAL STORY & SETTING

Among the nations (Genesis 10)

Tarshish first appears as a family name in the line of Japheth, among the peoples of the coastlands. Its beginning matters for what comes later. Tarshish belongs to the Gentile world that God scattered after Babel (Genesis 11:9). Isaiah 66:19 later names Tarshish next to Javan and Tubal, both of them names from the same Table of Nations.

Solomon's fleet: the height of earthly glory (1 Kings 10; 2 Chronicles 9)

Under Solomon, Israel reached its peak of wealth. His ships sailed with the fleet of Hiram, king of Tyre, and returned every three years carrying gold, silver, ivory, apes, and monkeys (1 Kings 10:22; 2 Chronicles 9:21). The Chronicler says the king's ships "went to Tarshish." In these chapters Tarshish measures how far Solomon's influence reached. The same chapters also describe the gold, horses, and splendor that Deuteronomy 17:16–17 had warned Israel's kings not to multiply. So the glory of Tarshish arrives beside the beginning of Solomon's spiritual drift (1 Kings 11:1–8).

Jehoshaphat's wrecked ships (1 Kings 22; 2 Chronicles 20)

Jehoshaphat was a king who walked in his father's ways, "doing what was right in the eyes of the LORD" (1 Kings 22:43), though the high places remained. He tried to restore that seafaring wealth. Chronicles records that he partnered with Ahaziah, king of Israel, who acted very wickedly (2 Chronicles 20:35): "And he allied himself with him to make ships to go to Tarshish, and they made the ships in Ezion Geber" (2 Chronicles 20:36). God sent a prophet with His verdict: "Eliezer the son of Dodavah of Mareshah prophesied against Jehoshaphat, saying, 'Because you have allied yourself with Ahaziah, the LORD has destroyed your works.' Then the ships were wrecked, so that they were not able to go to Tarshish" (2 Chronicles 20:37).

Kings tells the event more briefly: "they never sailed, for the ships were wrecked at Ezion Geber" (1 Kings 22:48). It then adds that when Ahaziah proposed sending his own servants with Jehoshaphat's, Jehoshaphat refused (1 Kings 22:49). Read together, the two accounts suggest that he learned from the rebuke. The fleet never left the harbor, and God's word explains the reason.

Tarshish and Tyre under judgment (Psalm 48; Isaiah 2, 23; Ezekiel 27)

In the prophets, Tarshish is closely linked with Tyre, the great Phoenician trading power. Isaiah's oracle against Tyre opens and closes with the same cry: "Wail, you ships of Tarshish" (Isaiah 23:1, 14). Tyre's fall leaves Tarshish's trade with nowhere to go. Isaiah tells the people of Tyre to escape across the sea to Tarshish (23:6), and he addresses Tarshish itself as a "daughter" whose support is gone (23:10). Ezekiel's lament over Tyre lists Tarshish as a chief trading partner, paying in metals (Ezekiel 27:12). He also pictures the ships of Tarshish carrying Tyre's goods (27:25), just before an east wind breaks the great ship of Tyre in the heart of the seas (27:26).

Isaiah 2 widens the scope. On the day of the LORD, everything high and lifted up will be humbled: cedars, mountains, towers, walls, and "all the ships of Tarshish, And... all the beautiful sloops" (Isaiah 2:16). Then "The LORD alone will be exalted in that day" (2:17). Psalm 48 uses the ships for a simile. When hostile kings saw Zion, they were seized with trembling, "As when You break the ships of Tarshish With an east wind" (Psalm 48:7). In these passages the ships, the most impressive things human skill had built, show how quickly God can bring down what people trust.

Tarshish also appears in two smaller scenes. Jeremiah mocks idols overlaid with silver from Tarshish, which are beautiful and lifeless (Jeremiah 10:9). Ezekiel pictures "the merchants of Tarshish" watching Gog's invasion and asking whether he has come to plunder (Ezekiel 38:13). Jeremiah's scene clearly ties Tarshish's silver to idolatry. Ezekiel's merchants are often read as commercially interested or complicit, though their question has also been read as envy or protest, so that verse should not be pressed into a verdict on every merchant.

Jonah's flight (Jonah 1, 4)

The best-known Tarshish passage is a story about running from God. God told Jonah to go east to Nineveh, the capital of Assyria. Instead, "Jonah arose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD. He went down to Joppa, and found a ship going to Tarshish; so he paid the fare, and went down into it, to go with them to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD" (Jonah 1:3). The verse names Tarshish three times and twice says he went "from the presence of the LORD." Its verbs keep pointing downward, and in the next chapters Jonah goes lower still: into the hold of the ship (1:5), into the sea, and down to the roots of the mountains (2:6).

Jonah's journey toward Tarshish was interrupted, and the text does not say where the ship went after the sea grew calm. The LORD sent a great wind on the sea (1:4). Jonah confessed that he feared the God who made the sea and the dry land (1:9). The pagan sailors, after throwing him overboard, feared the LORD greatly and offered sacrifices to Him (1:16). On the ship bound for Tarshish, Gentiles came to worship the God Jonah was running from.

Jonah 4:2 gives the real reason for his flight. After Nineveh repented and was spared, Jonah complained that this was why he had fled to Tarshish in the first place. He knew God was gracious and merciful, slow to anger, abundant in lovingkindness, and ready to relent from sending disaster. Jonah did not run because he thought God was harsh. He ran because he knew God was kind and did not want that kindness given to his enemies.

Tarshish in the coming kingdom (Psalm 72; Isaiah 60, 66)

The prophets do not leave Tarshish under judgment. Psalm 72, a royal psalm "of Solomon," pictures the reign of the righteous King: "The kings of Tarshish and of the isles Will bring presents; The kings of Sheba and Seba Will offer gifts" (Psalm 72:10). It goes on to say that all kings will bow before him and all nations will serve him (72:11).

Isaiah 60 describes Zion's coming glory and gives the ships of Tarshish a new job: "Surely the coastlands shall wait for Me; And the ships of Tarshish will come first, To bring your sons from afar, Their silver and their gold with them, To the name of the LORD your God, And to the Holy One of Israel, Because He has glorified you" (Isaiah 60:9). The ships that Isaiah 2 said would be humbled lead the procession home in Isaiah 60.

Isaiah's final chapter carries this hope out to the nations: "I will set a sign among them; and those among them who escape I will send to the nations: to Tarshish and Pul and Lud, who draw the bow, and Tubal and Javan, to the coastlands afar off who have not heard My fame nor seen My glory. And they shall declare My glory among the Gentiles" (Isaiah 66:19). Tarshish, once the place a prophet ran to so he would not have to preach, becomes the first place God names when He sends out messengers.

3. SIGNIFICANCE IN GOD'S PURPOSES

Scripture states several things about Tarshish directly. It is a Gentile people descended from Japheth. It is a distant source of wealth reached by sea. Its ships are among the proud things God will humble. Jonah fled toward it, and God's purposes reached him on the way. And the prophets promise that its kings, ships, and people will one day honor the LORD.

From these statements a clear pattern emerges. Tarshish works as a gauge of how far human reach and human pride extend. For Solomon, it showed how far his wealth reached. For Jehoshaphat, the wreck showed that prosperity pursued through a compromised alliance will not stand, because "the LORD has destroyed your works" (2 Chronicles 20:37). For the prophets, the ships of Tarshish represent the confidence people place in trade and technology, and God's judgment of them shows that "The LORD alone will be exalted in that day" (Isaiah 2:17).

The movement of Tarshish across the Old Testament also reveals God's heart for the nations. The ends of the earth are not outside His rule, and they are not outside His mercy. A Gentile family line from Genesis 10 becomes, in Isaiah 66, a people who will hear about God's glory. The silver that once covered idols (Jeremiah 10:9) will be brought "to the name of the LORD your God" (Isaiah 60:9). God intends to redirect the world's wealth and skill to His own honor, not simply to wipe them away.

4. CHRIST & THE LARGER BIBLICAL STORY

The New Testament never names Tarshish, so the links here rest on how the Old Testament hope develops and is fulfilled, not on a direct New Testament quotation.

The clearest connection runs from Isaiah 66:19 to the mission of the risen Christ. Isaiah promised that God would send survivors to faraway coastlands that had never heard of His fame. Jesus told His disciples, "you shall be witnesses to Me in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8). Acts does not quote Isaiah 66 there, but the hope is the same: messengers sent from Zion to declare God's glory among the Gentiles. Paul later wrote, "whenever I journey to Spain, I shall come to you" (Romans 15:24). If Tarshish was Tartessos in Spain, Paul was aiming for the same far western region that Jonah once tried to escape to. That identification is a scholarly proposal, so the link should be held loosely. The direction, though, is unmistakable. The gospel goes to the places the prophets called the ends of the earth.

Psalm 72:10 belongs to a psalm that looks beyond Solomon to a King whose reign is everlasting and worldwide (72:5, 8, 17). Its picture of distant kings bringing presents is fulfilled in Christ, to whom every knee will bow. When wise men from the East brought gold to the infant Jesus (Matthew 2:11), the scene matched what Psalm 72 had described, even though Matthew does not cite the psalm and the Magi did not come from Tarshish. This is a thematic resonance, not a stated fulfillment, but it is a natural one. Revelation's picture of the kings of the earth bringing their glory into the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:24) points the same way.

Jesus Himself spoke of "the sign of the prophet Jonah" (Matthew 12:39–41). He compared Jonah's three days in the fish to His own burial and resurrection, and He pointed to Nineveh's repentance as a judgment on His own generation. The contrast with Jonah's flight to Tarshish is instructive. Jonah ran from a mission of mercy to his enemies. Christ came willingly on that very mission, and He was thrown into the depths so that others could be spared.

One more resonance is worth noting with care. Jonah sailed toward Tarshish to avoid preaching to Gentiles. Centuries later, the Lord stopped "Saul of Tarsus" (Acts 9:11) and made him the apostle to the Gentiles. Tarsus and Tarshish are separate names, so this is not a fulfillment. It is a fitting irony in the larger story: God finished the work His reluctant prophet tried to escape, and He did it through a man He had captured for His purpose.

5. SPIRIT & LIFE

Jonah's story shows that there is no Tarshish beyond the reach of God's presence. Jonah seems to have thought that leaving the land where God was worshiped would put him out of earshot. Yet the LORD who "made the sea and the dry land" (Jonah 1:9) was on that ship and over that storm. We also look for places where we imagine God's voice will not follow us, whether a new city, a busy schedule, or a corner of life we keep to ourselves. What He did with Jonah was not cruelty. He pursued His servant because He was not finished with him.

Jonah's escape also cost him something. He "paid the fare" (Jonah 1:3), and the rest of the chapter shows how much more running from God cost him. The text does not set the price up as a lesson, but it is hard to miss. Obedience would have taken him to Nineveh. Disobedience took him down: to Joppa, into the ship, into the hold, into the sea.

Jonah 4:2 goes deeper than disobedience. Jonah knew God's character perfectly well. What he resented was God's mercy toward people he wanted judged. That is a searching thing to see in a prophet, and it can be just as true of believers today, who may know God's grace well and still not want it extended to certain people. The book ends with God's question about His compassion for Nineveh (4:11). He leaves the question open so that every reader has to answer it.

Jehoshaphat's ships carry a different warning. He was a godly king, but he wanted the wealth of Tarshish enough to make a partnership God would not bless. The wreck at Ezion Geber was painful. It was also mercy, because it ended the partnership before it could draw him in further, and Kings suggests that he refused the next offer (1 Kings 22:49). When God frustrates our plans, it may be His protection rather than a random setback.

Finally, Tarshish shows how God deals with the things people take pride in. He humbles "all the ships of Tarshish" (Isaiah 2:16). Then, in Isaiah's continuing imagery, the ships of Tarshish are sent out to bring His sons and daughters home (Isaiah 60:9). Our skills, resources, and achievements are never safe objects of trust, but in God's hands they can be put to use for His purposes. The best response is to stop running, bring what we have to "the name of the LORD your God," and join the work of declaring His glory among the nations.

6. KEY SCRIPTURES

  • Genesis 10:4–5; 1 Chronicles 1:7 — Tarshish among the sons of Javan; its origin among the Gentile coastland peoples.

  • 1 Kings 10:22; 2 Chronicles 9:21 — Solomon's fleet with Hiram's and its exotic cargo; Tarshish as the measure of Israel's wealth at its height.

  • 1 Kings 22:48–49; 2 Chronicles 20:35–37 — Jehoshaphat's ships wrecked at Ezion Geber because of his alliance with Ahaziah; also the main evidence that "ships of Tarshish" named a class of vessel.

  • 1 Chronicles 7:10 — Tarshish the Benjamite.

  • Esther 1:14 — Tarshish the Persian prince.

  • Psalm 48:7 — God breaking the ships of Tarshish with an east wind, a picture of the panic of kings who oppose Zion.

  • Psalm 72:10 — The kings of Tarshish bringing presents to the righteous King.

  • Isaiah 2:16–17 — The ships of Tarshish among the proud things humbled on the day of the LORD.

  • Isaiah 23:1, 6, 10, 14 — Tarshish wailing over the fall of Tyre.

  • Isaiah 60:9 — The ships of Tarshish leading the return of God's sons with silver and gold for His name.

  • Isaiah 66:19 — God sending messengers to Tarshish to declare His glory among the Gentiles.

  • Jeremiah 10:9 — Silver from Tarshish used to overlay idols.

  • Ezekiel 27:12, 25–26; 38:13 — Tarshish as Tyre's trading partner and as a merchant power watching Gog.

  • Jonah 1:3; 4:2 — Jonah's flight toward Tarshish "from the presence of the LORD," and his later admission that he fled because God is merciful.

  • Acts 1:8; Romans 15:24 — The gospel's mission to the end of the earth, including Paul's hope of reaching Spain.
