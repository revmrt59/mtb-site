DESIRE - (tᵉshûwqâh, Strong's H8669)

Summary

This Hebrew word for "desire" describes a strong longing or urge that pulls one person toward another. It appears only three times in the Bible, and those three uses tell a story. In Genesis 3:16, after the Fall, God tells the woman, "Your desire shall be for your husband, And he shall rule over you." In Genesis 4:7, God warns Cain that sin crouches at his door: "its desire is for you, but you should rule over it." In Song of Solomon 7:10, the bride sings, "I am my beloved's, And his desire is toward me."

Taken together, these verses show desire marked by the Fall, then sin exposed as a hungry power that wants to master people, and finally desire at home in faithful, delighted love. Many readers let the wording of Genesis 4:7 explain Genesis 3:16 and see a struggle for control entering the closest human relationship. Others read the woman's desire as longing or attachment, with the husband's rule describing how the relationship broke. Either way, sin damaged that relationship. The Song offers a striking contrast: love moving toward belonging and self-giving. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, that picture finds its fullest expression in Christ, who "loved the church and gave Himself for her" (Ephesians 5:25).

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Tᵉshûwqâh is a Hebrew noun used only three times in the Old Testament. The NKJV translates it "desire" each time. Two of the uses come within a few verses of each other in Genesis, just after Adam and Eve's sin. The first is part of God's words of judgment to the woman. The second is God's warning to Cain before he murders Abel. The third comes much later, in the love poetry of Song of Solomon.

Because the word is so rare, its exact shade of meaning has long been debated. The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament rendered it with words meaning a turning toward someone. That rendering fits the basic idea of a will and attention directed toward another person.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means a strong desire, longing, or urge directed toward someone. It describes a pull of the heart and will toward another.

The word itself is neither romantic nor hostile. In Song of Solomon 7:10 it is tender and good. In Genesis 4:7 it belongs to sin, which wants to overpower Cain. The surrounding words, and especially the people involved, show what kind of desire is meant.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The three uses differ mainly in who desires whom. In Genesis 3:16 the woman's desire is for her husband. In Genesis 4:7 sin's desire is for Cain. In Song of Solomon 7:10 the man's desire is toward the woman.

The most important pattern is the pairing of "desire" with "rule." In both Genesis verses, the desire of one party is followed at once by a statement about ruling. The same Hebrew verb for "rule" appears in each. The husband "shall rule over you," and Cain "should rule over it." This repeated pattern, a chapter apart, invites the reader to let each verse explain the other. In the Song, desire appears again, but the language of ruling is gone.

4. Theological Significance

These passages show that God tells the truth about what sin does to human love. Desire is part of how God made people, and it was never the problem. After the Fall, though, desire no longer moves within a whole relationship. Whether the woman's desire in Genesis 3:16 is a longing that leaves her exposed to her husband's rule or an urge to control him, the verse names a relationship broken by sin. Genesis 4:7 shows plainly that desire can become a drive to master someone. God does not hide this damage from the woman or from Cain. He names it plainly.

The passages also show that sin is active. Sin in Genesis 4 is no stain lying quietly on the soul. It wants something. It has its own desire, and that desire is aimed at a person. God's warning to Cain shows that He takes this threat seriously and calls people to resist it.

Finally, the word's last appearance shows that the damage is not the end. The Song does not say it is reversing the curse, but its picture of desire stands in clear contrast with Genesis: longing given freely, with no word of ruling. God's purpose is to restore desire to faithful, joyful love, and He does not leave His creatures inside the curse.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Genesis 3:16 — Desire and rule after the Fall. God's words to the woman come in a passage of judgment. Before the Fall, the man greeted the woman as "bone of my bones," and the two were one flesh, naked and unashamed (Genesis 2:23–25). Now God says, "Your desire shall be for your husband, And he shall rule over you." Some readers take this desire as the woman's continued longing for her husband in spite of the pain. Others take it as sexual desire. Many readers, however, find a sharper meaning in the near-identical wording of Genesis 4:7, where desire is an urge to overpower. On that struggle-for-control reading, the woman's desire would be an effort to control her husband, and his rule would be a harsh or retaliatory answer. This reading is widely held but not universal, and the noun alone cannot settle which kind of desire is meant. Even so, one point is clear from the setting. Genesis 3:16 describes the result of sin. It is not a pattern God commends. The manipulation and domination that trouble so many marriages are symptoms of the Fall, not the design of Eden.

Genesis 4:7 — Sin crouching at the door. Cain is angry because God accepted Abel's offering and not his. God speaks to him before any blood is shed: "sin lies at the door. And its desire is for you, but you should rule over it." Sin is pictured like a beast lying in wait at the entrance of a house. It wants Cain. God offers Cain a choice and a warning while there is still time, and He tells Cain to master it. Cain does not heed the warning, and the next verse records the first murder. Here the same word for desire is plainly hostile. Sin seeks to master the one who gives it room.

Song of Solomon 7:10 — Desire without domination. "I am my beloved's, And his desire is toward me." Set beside Genesis 3:16, this verse reads as a striking contrast. The desire now flows from the man toward the woman, and there is no word about ruling. The Song does not claim to undo Genesis, but the contrast is hard to miss. The bride speaks with settled confidence. Her words also complete a progression in the Song. Earlier she said, "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (2:16). Later she put her belonging first: "I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine" (6:3). Here she speaks only of belonging to him and of his longing for her. Her words show no need to secure him; she rests in being his. Within a fallen world, the Song gives a picture of love the way God intended it to be.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The three uses of this word trace a short version of the Bible's story: the fracture of the Fall, the struggle with sin, and the restoration of love.

Cain was told to rule over sin, and he failed. Jesus Christ faced temptation as a man and overcame it without sin (Matthew 4:1–11; Hebrews 4:15). Through His death and resurrection, He breaks the dominion that sin claimed over humanity. Paul declares, "For sin shall not have dominion over you, for you are not under law but under grace" (Romans 6:14).

Christ also answers the broken "rule" of Genesis 3:16. Paul describes Christ as "head of the church" (Ephesians 5:23), and he defines that headship by the cross: Christ "loved the church and gave Himself for her, that He might sanctify and cleanse her" (Ephesians 5:25–26). Headship that bleeds for the bride is the opposite of harsh domination. Paul then quotes Genesis 2:24 about the two becoming one flesh and says, "This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32). In Paul's reading, marriage from the beginning carried a picture of Christ and His people.

The Song of Solomon never names Christ. Still, Paul's teaching gives good warrant for hearing in the Song's love an echo of the greater love between Christ and the church. That is a canonical application, drawn by reading the Song alongside Ephesians 5, and it points to Christ's self-giving love. The story ends with the New Jerusalem "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Revelation 21:2). The relationship broken in Eden is made whole at the wedding of the Lamb.

7. Spirit & Life

Genesis 4:7 tells believers something they need to hear about temptation. Sin does not wait politely until it is invited in. It crouches at the door, and it wants you. That is why Paul writes, "do not let sin reign in your mortal body" (Romans 6:12), and Peter warns that the devil "walks about like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour" (1 Peter 5:8). Cain heard God's warning yet did not heed it, and he "rose up against Abel his brother and killed him" (Genesis 4:8). Believers receive the same kind of warning, but Romans 6 also gives them grounds for confidence: they are to "present yourselves to God as being alive from the dead" (Romans 6:13), because sin will not have dominion over those who are under grace (Romans 6:14).

These verses also speak directly to marriage. Many couples know a pull toward control, where each spouse quietly works to manage the other and love becomes a contest. Whether Genesis 3:16 describes that very struggle or a longing met with domination, the verse places such brokenness under the curse, not in God's design. Christ came to undo the curse, so husbands and wives need not treat the struggle as normal or permanent. Paul's call to be "submitting to one another in the fear of God" (Ephesians 5:21), and his call for husbands to love as Christ loved, lead toward the love of the Song. Neither spouse has to win, because each gives himself or herself to the other.

The bride's words in Song of Solomon 7:10 also show why grasping fades. She can rest because she knows she is wanted. Grasping often grows out of fear that we will not be loved unless we take control. The believer who knows "I am my beloved's" in Christ has been chosen by a Bridegroom who gave Himself for His bride. That security frees a person to love others without needing to rule them.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

This word appears only three times in Scripture: Genesis 3:16, Genesis 4:7 and Song of Solomon 7:10. All three are discussed in Section 5. English Bibles use "desire" in many other places, but those verses translate different Hebrew words. They do not show how this particular word is used.
