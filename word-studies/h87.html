ABRAM - (ʼAbrâm, Strong's H0087)

Summary

Abram is the son of Terah from Ur of the Chaldeans. God called him to leave his homeland and go to a land He would show him, and he became the man through whom God promised to bless "all the families of the earth" (Genesis 12:3). His name is widely understood to mean "exalted father," from the Hebrew words for "father" and "exalted." For most of his story that name sat uneasily on him, because he and Sarai had no children. Scripture calls him Abram from his genealogy in Genesis 11:26 until Genesis 17:5, when God renamed him Abraham, "for I have made you a father of many nations." Later Scripture remembers the change in a single line: "Abram, who is Abraham" (1 Chronicles 1:27).

Some of the most important events in the Bible happened while he still carried the name Abram. God gave him the promises of land, offspring, and worldwide blessing. He rescued Lot and was blessed by Melchizedek, priest of God Most High. In Genesis 15, "he believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness" (15:6). God then bound Himself to His promise in a solemn ceremony while Abram slept. Abram also failed in these chapters. He lied in Egypt and tried to secure the promised heir through Hagar. The New Testament returns to this period again and again. Paul uses it to show that God declares sinners righteous by faith, before and apart from any religious ritual (Romans 4). Hebrews sees in Melchizedek's meeting with Abram a preview of the eternal priesthood of Jesus Christ (Hebrews 7). Abram's story shows a God who chooses, calls, promises, and keeps His word to a man who often had little to offer but trust.

Full Study

1. Identity & Name

Abram (Hebrew אַבְרָם) is one individual. He is the son of Terah and the brother of Nahor and Haran (Genesis 11:26–27). He married Sarai, who was barren (11:29–30). His nephew Lot, the son of his brother Haran, traveled with him (11:31; 12:4). Strong's H0087 covers the name only in its earlier form. After God renames him in Genesis 17:5, the Old Testament uses Abraham, a separately indexed name (H0085). The two later Old Testament uses of "Abram" look back on that change. One is the genealogy in 1 Chronicles 1:27, "and Abram, who is Abraham." The other is the Levites' prayer in Nehemiah 9:7, which praises the God "Who chose Abram... And gave him the name Abraham." The Greek New Testament uses a single name for him, Abraam (G11), so its reflections on the events of Genesis 11–17 are not occurrences of H0087. They still interpret this part of his life directly and belong in this study.

The name is widely understood to mean "exalted father." It is built from ab, "father," and a form related to ram, "high" or "exalted." Some readers take it as describing God as the exalted Father, and others take it as honoring the child's father. Scripture does not settle which is meant, and the question is best left open. Either way, the name stands in sharp tension with the man's circumstances. For decades a man called "exalted father" had no child by his wife. That tension is part of what makes Genesis 17:5 so striking. God does not drop the word "father." He enlarges it. The new name, Abraham, is explained in the text itself through a sound-play: "for I have made you a father of many nations." That explanation comes from Scripture's own wordplay, and it is stronger ground than speculating about the old name's spiritual meaning. Readers should not treat "exalted father" as a coded summary of Abram's life. It was the name his family gave him, and God later chose to expand it into a promise.

Abram's world stretched from Ur in southern Mesopotamia to Haran in the north, and then south into Canaan to Shechem, the region between Bethel and Ai, and Hebron. Famine briefly drove him into Egypt. Scholars debate the precise dating of his life and the identities of the eastern kings in Genesis 14, and the biblical text does not settle those questions.

2. Biblical Story & Setting

From Ur to Haran

Genesis introduces Abram inside a family record (11:26–32). Terah took Abram, Sarai, and Lot out of Ur "to go to the land of Canaan," but they stopped at Haran and settled there until Terah died. Stephen later explains that "the God of glory" had already appeared to Abram in Mesopotamia, before he lived in Haran, and that God moved him into Canaan after his father's death (Acts 7:2–4). Joshua adds a sobering detail: Terah's household "served other gods" beyond the River (Joshua 24:2–3). Abram did not begin as a seeker who found God. God came to him in the middle of an idolatrous family. Nehemiah 9:7 gives the same emphasis: "You are the LORD God, Who chose Abram, And brought him out of Ur of the Chaldeans."

The Call and the Promise

Genesis 12:1–3 is one of the hinges of the whole Bible. God says, "Get out of your country, From your family And from your father's house, To a land that I will show you." Then He piles up promises: "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you And make your name great; And you shall be a blessing.... And in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed." The previous chapter ended with the builders of Babel trying to "make a name" for themselves (11:4). Now God promises to make Abram's name great as a gift. The response is simple: "So Abram departed as the LORD had spoken to him, and Lot went with him. And Abram was seventy-five years old when he departed from Haran" (12:4). Hebrews admires this obedience: he "went out, not knowing where he was going" (Hebrews 11:8).

Altars Across the Land

Abram owned no land in Canaan. Wherever he stopped, though, he built an altar. At Shechem, by the terebinth tree of Moreh, the LORD appeared and promised the land to his descendants, and Abram built an altar there (12:6–7). Between Bethel and Ai he built another and "called on the name of the LORD" (12:8). After Egypt he went back to that same altar and called on the LORD again (13:3–4). At Hebron, by the terebinth trees of Mamre, he built yet another (13:18). The Canaanites still held the land (12:6), but Abram marked it out with worship. His tent showed that he was a pilgrim, and his altars showed whom he belonged to.

Failure in Egypt

Soon after he arrived, famine drove Abram down to Egypt (12:10). Afraid that the Egyptians would kill him to take his beautiful wife, he told Sarai to say she was his sister. Pharaoh took her into his house and treated Abram well for her sake, giving him livestock and servants (12:14–16). Then "the LORD plagued Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sarai" (12:17). Pharaoh rebuked Abram and sent him away. Abram came up out of Egypt "very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold" (13:1–2). The man of promise had endangered the very wife through whom the promise would come. God protected her anyway.

Lot Chooses, God Speaks

The wealth brought strain. The land could not support both Abram's and Lot's herds, and their herdsmen quarreled (13:5–7). Abram was the elder and the one who had received the promise, yet he let Lot choose first (13:8–9). Lot looked toward the well-watered plain of the Jordan and pitched his tent toward Sodom (13:10–12). Genesis notes the timing of what followed. God spoke again "after Lot had separated from him," telling Abram to look in every direction and walk through the land, because He would give all of it to Abram and his descendants forever, as countless as the dust of the earth (13:14–17, paraphrased). Abram gave up the best ground and lost nothing God had promised.

Abram the Hebrew: Rescue and Two Kings

Genesis 14 shows a side of Abram that readers often overlook. A coalition of four eastern kings defeated the five kings of the plain, plundered Sodom, and carried Lot away (14:1–12). Genesis here calls him "Abram the Hebrew," the first use of that word in the Bible (14:13). He had allies in Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre. He armed 318 trained servants "born in his own house," pursued the invaders as far as Dan, struck them at night, and recovered everything, including Lot (14:13–16). This pilgrim led a household large and disciplined enough to defeat kings.

Two rulers then met him, and the contrast between them is deliberate. "Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine; he was the priest of God Most High." He blessed Abram: "Blessed be Abram of God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth; And blessed be God Most High, Who has delivered your enemies into your hand." Abram "gave him a tithe of all" (14:18–20). The king of Sodom offered a deal instead: "Give me the persons, and take the goods for yourself" (14:21). Abram refused. "I have raised my hand to the LORD, God Most High, the Possessor of heaven and earth, that I will take nothing, from a thread to a sandal strap... lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich'" (14:22–23). He made sure his allies received their fair share (14:24). He would not owe his prosperity to Sodom, and he gladly honored the priest of the Most High. He gave to the one and refused to receive from the other.

The Night of the Stars and the Smoking Oven

"After these things the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward'" (15:1). Abram answered honestly: "Lord GOD, what will You give me, seeing I go childless, and the heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus?" (15:2). He pressed the point: "Look, You have given me no offspring; indeed one born in my house is my heir!" (15:3). God answered plainly: "one who will come from your own body shall be your heir" (15:4). He brought Abram outside and told him to count the stars if he could, for so his descendants would be (15:5, paraphrased). Then comes the sentence that the rest of Scripture builds on: "And he believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness" (15:6).

When Abram asked how he could know he would inherit the land, God told him to prepare a heifer, a goat, a ram, a turtledove, and a pigeon. Abram cut the animals in two, laid the halves opposite each other, and drove away the birds of prey (15:9–11). As the sun went down, a deep sleep and a great, dreadful darkness fell on him (15:12). God told him his descendants would be strangers and slaves in a foreign land for four hundred years. Then He would judge that nation, and they would come out with great possessions. They would return in the fourth generation, because "the iniquity of the Amorites" was not yet complete (15:13–16, paraphrased). After dark, a smoking oven and a burning torch passed between the pieces. "On the same day the LORD made a covenant with Abram," promising the land from the river of Egypt to the Euphrates (15:17–18, paraphrased). Only God passed through.

Hagar and Ishmael

Ten years after they entered Canaan, Sarai was still childless. She urged Abram to have a child through her Egyptian maid Hagar, and "Abram heeded the voice of Sarai" (16:1–3, paraphrased). Hagar conceived and began to despise her mistress. Sarai blamed Abram, Abram handed the matter back to her, and Sarai treated Hagar so harshly that she fled (16:4–6). The Angel of the LORD found Hagar in the wilderness, sent her back, and promised her many descendants. Hagar named the LORD who spoke to her "You-Are-the-God-Who-Sees" (16:7–13, paraphrased). Abram was eighty-six when Ishmael was born (16:15–16). The plan solved nothing and created lasting grief.

Almighty God and a New Name

The next scene opens when Abram is ninety-nine (17:1). Genesis records no word from God in between. Then the LORD appeared and said, in substance, "I am Almighty God; walk before Me and be blameless" (17:1). Abram fell on his face (17:3), and God told him: "No longer shall your name be called Abram, but your name shall be Abraham; for I have made you a father of many nations" (17:5). In the same chapter God renamed Sarai as Sarah and gave circumcision as the sign of His covenant (17:10–15). The story continues under the name Abraham, but the man and the promise are the same.

Remembered by a Restored People

Centuries later, Levites in Jerusalem who had come back from exile confessed their nation's long history of sin. They began with Abram. God chose him, brought him out of Ur, renamed him, found his heart faithful, and kept His word to give the land, "for You are righteous" (Nehemiah 9:7–8, partly paraphrased). A people who had lost nearly everything found solid ground in the first chapter of their story, where everything had begun with God's choice and God's promise.

3. Significance in God's Purposes

Scripture states directly that God chose Abram, called him out of Ur, and promised him land, a great nation, a great name, and blessing for all the families of the earth (Genesis 12:1–3; Nehemiah 9:7). After the scattering at Babel, the Bible's story narrows to one man so that it can later widen to every nation. Israel, the land, the Davidic kingdom, and the gospel to the Gentiles all trace their line back to Genesis 12.

Scripture also states plainly that Abram's righteousness was credited to him through faith (Genesis 15:6). Paul treats this as the pattern for how God justifies anyone (Romans 4:3; Galatians 3:6). The order of events matters to Paul's argument. Abram was counted righteous in Genesis 15, years before circumcision was given in Genesis 17. Circumcision therefore came as "a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had while still uncircumcised," so that he could be the father of all who believe, circumcised or not (Romans 4:9–12, paraphrased). In the same way, the law came 430 years after the promise and cannot cancel it (Galatians 3:17). This was the Abram stage of his life. God's grace came before ritual and before law.

Genesis 15 also reveals how God relates to His promises. Elsewhere in Scripture, walking between divided animals marked a self-curse. Those who broke their oath would become like the cut animals (Jeremiah 34:18–20). In Abram's ceremony only the smoking oven and burning torch passed through, while Abram slept. The natural conclusion is that God placed the full weight of the promise on Himself. Scripture does not explain the ceremony in those exact words, but the context and the parallel in Jeremiah support it strongly.

The text is honest about Abram's failures in Egypt and with Hagar, and God's purposes moved forward through them. God protected Sarai (12:17), kept His promise about the heir (15:4), and still cared for Hagar and Ishmael (16:10–13). The long wait before Genesis 17 is visible in the text: Abram is eighty-six in 16:16 and ninety-nine in 17:1, with no recorded word from God in between. Some interpreters see a season of discipline here. That is a reading of the gap, and Genesis does not say it. What the text does show clearly is that God's next word is about His own might, "Almighty God," as if to say that the promise will rest on His power and not on Abram's strategies.

4. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The promise and the Seed. Paul calls Genesis 12:3 the gospel announced in advance: God, foreseeing that He would justify the Gentiles by faith, "preached the gospel to Abraham beforehand," saying that in him all nations would be blessed (Galatians 3:8, paraphrased). Paul then explains that the promises were spoken to Abraham "and to his Seed," who is Christ (Galatians 3:16). Those who belong to Christ are Abraham's offspring and heirs of the promise (Galatians 3:29). This is explicit New Testament interpretation, so the blessing promised to Abram finds its fulfillment in Jesus.

Melchizedek and the eternal Priest. Hebrews 7 reads Genesis 14:18–20 with great care. Melchizedek, "king of righteousness" and king of Salem ("peace"), blessed Abram and received a tenth from him. Since the lesser is blessed by the greater, Melchizedek ranks above Abram. In a sense, Levi himself paid tithes through Abram, so Melchizedek's priesthood outranks the Levitical line (Hebrews 7:1–10). Psalm 110:4 had already promised a King-Priest "according to the order of Melchizedek," and Hebrews applies that promise to Jesus (Hebrews 5:6; 7:17). The bread and wine Melchizedek brought out are mentioned in Genesis, but the New Testament does not connect them to the Lord's Supper. That link is at most a thematic echo and should not be pressed. The scene's Christ-centered weight comes from what Hebrews makes explicit: Abram bowed his wealth before a priest greater than himself, and that priest points to the greater High Priest.

A small exodus. The Egypt episode in Genesis 12 tracks closely with Israel's later history. Famine drives the family down to Egypt. The family's life is under threat, God strikes Pharaoh's house with plagues, and Abram leaves Egypt laden with silver and gold. Genesis 15:13–14 then predicts that Abram's descendants will be enslaved and afflicted, that God will judge the oppressing nation, and that they will come out "with great possessions" (paraphrased). Exodus tells exactly that story, so this is a well-supported canonical pattern and not a forced reading. The father's journey previews the nation's deliverance, and both point toward the great deliverance God would bring about in Christ.

The God who bears the curse. In Genesis 15, God alone passes through the pieces. In Galatians 3:13–14, Christ becomes a curse for us "that the blessing of Abraham might come upon the Gentiles in Christ Jesus" (paraphrased). The New Testament does not cite the smoking-oven scene directly. Still, the pattern is consistent across Scripture: the God who took the full weight of His promise on Himself in Genesis is the same God who, in Christ, bore the curse so that Abram's blessing could reach the nations. This is a strong thematic resonance, and it is worth noticing.

Promise and flesh. Paul uses the Hagar episode as a figure, which he openly calls symbolic. Hagar and Ishmael, born "according to the flesh," correspond to Sinai and slavery. Sarah and her child, born "through promise," correspond to the Jerusalem above, which is free (Galatians 4:22–31, paraphrased). Paul's main point is that God's children are born by God's promise and Spirit, not by human effort.

5. Spirit & Life

Abram's story begins with God's initiative. God appeared to a man in a household that served other gods (Joshua 24:2; Acts 7:2). Nehemiah's prayer opens with "You... Who chose Abram," and every believer's story begins the same way. Salvation starts when God speaks, not when we finally find Him.

God's call also asked Abram to release one layer of security after another: his country, then his relatives, then his father's house. The journey went in stages. Abram stopped at Haran until Terah died, and Lot traveled with him until their herds forced a parting. Genesis does not condemn either delay, but it does note that God's fullest description of the land came "after Lot had separated from him" (13:14). Many believers know what it is to obey God's call partway and then find that God keeps patiently leading them further.

Genesis 14 teaches something about how believers handle wealth and loyalty. Abram fought hard to rescue Lot, yet he would not take a thread or a sandal strap from Sodom. He would not let anyone say a wicked king had made him rich. With the same open hand, he gave a tenth to the priest of God Most High. He knew where his provision came from: "the LORD, God Most High, the Possessor of heaven and earth." A heart that knows God owns everything can refuse tainted gain and give freely.

Genesis 15 gives one of Scripture's most honest prayers. God says, "I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward," and Abram answers, in effect, "What good is a reward if I have no son?" God did not rebuke him. He took him outside and showed him the stars. Faith in Scripture is not pretending that nothing is wrong. Abram brought his disappointment to God, heard God's promise, and believed it, and God counted that faith as righteousness. The same God still makes the same offer. Righteousness will be imputed to "us who believe in Him who raised up Jesus our Lord from the dead" (Romans 4:24).

Abram's failures make the story more hopeful, not less. Fear led him to lie in Egypt, and impatience led him to Hagar. God did not set him aside. He kept the promise on His own terms and in His own time, and then He gave Abram a new name that matched the promise rather than Abram's circumstances. A ninety-nine-year-old man with one son by his wife's servant was told to call himself "father of many nations." Accepting that name was itself an act of faith. God tends to name His people by what He intends to make of them, and faith takes Him at His word.

6. Key Scriptures

  • Genesis 11:26–32 — Abram's family, Sarai's barrenness, and the move from Ur to Haran.

  • Genesis 12:1–4 — God's call and the threefold promise of land, nation, and blessing for all families of the earth. The foundation of the biblical story of redemption.

  • Genesis 12:6–8; 13:3–4, 18 — Altars at Shechem, Bethel/Ai, and Hebron. Abram's life of worship in a land he did not yet own.

  • Genesis 12:10–13:2 — The descent into Egypt, deception, plagues, and departure with wealth, a preview of the exodus.

  • Genesis 13:14–17 — The expanded land promise given after Lot separated from Abram.

  • Genesis 14:13–24 — "Abram the Hebrew" rescues Lot, is blessed by Melchizedek, tithes to him, and refuses Sodom's goods.

  • Genesis 15:1–6 — Abram's honest complaint, the promise of an heir and of offspring like the stars, and justification by faith.

  • Genesis 15:9–18 — The ceremony with the divided animals, in which God alone passes between the pieces, and the prophecy of slavery and deliverance.

  • Genesis 16 — Hagar and Ishmael: the cost of trying to secure God's promise by human means.

  • Genesis 17:1–5 — Almighty God appears and renames Abram as Abraham, "a father of many nations."

  • 1 Chronicles 1:27; Nehemiah 9:7–8 — Later Israel remembers "Abram, who is Abraham" and praises the God who chose him and kept His word.

  • Acts 7:2–4 — Stephen: the God of glory appeared to Abram in Mesopotamia.

  • Romans 4:1–25 — Abram was counted righteous by faith before circumcision, making him the father of all who believe.

  • Galatians 3:6–18, 29; 4:21–31 — The gospel announced to Abraham, the promise to the Seed who is Christ, and the contrast between promise and flesh.

  • Hebrews 7:1–10; Psalm 110:4 — Melchizedek's superiority to Abram and the priesthood fulfilled in Jesus.

  • Hebrews 11:8–10 — Abram's faith in going out without knowing where he was going.
