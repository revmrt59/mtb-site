WELL, PIT - (bᵉʼêr, Strong's H0875)

Summary

The Hebrew word bᵉʼêr means a well: a shaft dug down through the earth until it reaches water hidden underground. In the dry lands of the Bible, a well meant life for people and flocks, a claim to land, and a place where people met. The same word can also describe a pit, from the asphalt pits of the Valley of Siddim to the "pit of destruction" in the Psalms. What a hole in the ground means depends on whether it gives water or swallows whoever falls into it.

Most of the word's uses fall in Genesis, and there wells become places where God meets His people. A well is named for the God who saw Hagar. Isaac re-digs the wells his father dug, and God keeps the oath He swore to Abraham. Abraham's servant, Jacob, and Moses all meet future brides at wells, and Israel sings to a well God gave in the wilderness. In the poetry of Proverbs and the Song of Solomon, a well pictures faithful married love, and the same word becomes a deadly trap when it describes the seductress. These threads come together at Jacob's well in John 4, where Jesus offers water that ends thirst for good.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

A bᵉʼêr was dug by hand down to groundwater. That made it different from a cistern (Hebrew bôr), which is cut into rock to catch rainwater, and from a natural spring that flows on its own. Digging a well took hard labor and could fail, so a working well was valuable property. People fought over wells, swore oaths at them, named them, and covered their openings with heavy stones. Several place names are built on the word, including Beer Lahai Roi, Beersheba, and Beer (Well) in Numbers 21. The word occurs 38 times in 34 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and 23 of the occurrences are in Genesis, where the patriarchs live as herdsmen moving through dry country.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. A well of water: a dug shaft that supplies water for people and animals, as when Abraham's servant stops "by a well of water at evening time, the time when women go out to draw water" (Genesis 24:11).

2. A pit: an opening in the ground that is not a source of water, such as the "asphalt pits" of the Valley of Siddim, where some of the fleeing kings fell (Genesis 14:10).

3. In poetry, a figure of depth. A well can stand for the life-giving joy of marriage (Proverbs 5:15; Song of Solomon 4:15), and a pit can stand for death and ruin (Psalm 55:23; 69:15).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the narratives, a well is usually property. Abraham rebukes Abimelech over a well his servants had seized (Genesis 21:25). Moses later promises Edom that Israel will not drink from their wells (Numbers 20:17). A well is also a public meeting point. Shepherds gather there, and travelers rest there. Moses, fleeing Pharaoh, "sat down by a well" in Midian (Exodus 2:15).

Scripture often speaks of a well's "mouth." In Genesis 29 a stone covers the well's mouth, and in Psalm 69:15 David prays that the pit will not shut its mouth on him. The same picture can work in two directions: a mouth that gives water, or a mouth that closes over its victim. Once, a well even becomes a hiding place. During Absalom's revolt, David's messengers climb down into a well at Bahurim, and a woman spreads a covering and grain over it, so they escape and can warn the king (2 Samuel 17:18–21).

4. Theological Significance

Wells show God as the giver of a source that people cannot make for themselves. People can dig, but they cannot put water in the ground. In Scripture, water found in the earth again and again becomes the means by which God keeps His word to His people. He sustains Hagar when she is cast out. He keeps His oath to Abraham through Isaac's wells, and He gives Israel water in the desert before anyone asks for it.

Wells also show God as the One who sees. In the dry country, a well brought strangers together, and Scripture repeatedly uses that meeting place for revelation, rescue, and new beginnings. The poetic uses add a sober contrast. The same depth that holds water can hold death, so a person's desires lead either to a source of life or into a trap.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Hagar's wells (Genesis 16:14; 21:19). Hagar flees from Sarai's harsh treatment and meets the Angel of the LORD in the wilderness. Amazed, she says, "Have I also here seen Him who sees me?" (16:13). The nearby well is named Beer Lahai Roi in memory of that moment (16:14). Years later, Hagar and Ishmael are sent away, their water runs out, and she sits apart so she will not have to watch her son die. Then God opens her eyes, and she sees a well of water (21:19, paraphrased). The text does not say God created the well. It says God opened her eyes, which suggests the water may have been nearby the whole time while grief kept her from seeing it. Twice an outcast woman finds that God sees her before she sees Him, and both times a well marks the place.

Isaac re-digs his father's wells (Genesis 26:15–33). This chapter uses the word more than any other. Famine drives Isaac to Gerar, and God tells him to stay in the land: "I will perform the oath which I swore to Abraham your father" (26:3). The Philistines "had stopped up all the wells which his father's servants had dug in the days of Abraham his father, and they had filled them with earth" (26:15). Isaac digs them out again and gives them the same names his father had given them (26:18). The herdsmen of Gerar quarrel over the next wells, so Isaac names them Esek, contention, and Sitnah, enmity. He moves on without fighting and digs Rehoboth, spaciousness, because the LORD has made room for them (26:22). On the very day Isaac makes peace with Abimelech, his servants report water at Beersheba (26:32–33), the same place where Abraham had set out seven ewe lambs "that they may be my witness that I have dug this well" (21:30). The promise Isaac inherited was not abstract. It came to him as water under ground his father had already claimed. Isaac kept digging past opposition instead of giving up the inheritance.

The well's mouth and the shepherd's stone (Genesis 29:2–10). Jacob arrives in Haran and finds a well with "a large stone" on its mouth (29:2). The local shepherds had a fixed rule: "We cannot until all the flocks are gathered together, and they have rolled the stone from the well's mouth" (29:8). The heavy stone protected scarce water so no one could take more than his share. When Rachel comes with her father's sheep, "Jacob went near and rolled the stone from the well's mouth" (29:10) and watered her flock alone. The water was there all along, closed in under the stone, and it reached the flock when the right man moved it.

The Song of the Well (Numbers 21:16–18). Only a chapter earlier, a thirsty Israel quarreled with Moses, and Moses struck the rock (Numbers 20:2–11). At Beer the order is reversed. God speaks first, telling Moses to gather the people so He can give them water (Numbers 21:16). Israel answers with a song, calling on the well to spring up, and the princes and nobles dig it with their staffs (Numbers 21:17–18). The leaders' work is real, but the staffs of princes do not produce a water table. The people's part is to gather, dig, and sing, and God's part is the water. This is one of the rare moments in the wilderness when gratitude, not grumbling, meets God's gift.

The faithful well and the narrow well (Proverbs 5:15; Proverbs 23:27; Psalm 69:15). Proverbs urges a husband to drink "running water from your own well" (Proverbs 5:15), picturing marriage as a private, life-giving source. The Song of Solomon calls the bride a well of living waters (Song of Solomon 4:15). Proverbs then reverses the image: "a harlot is a deep pit, and a seductress is a narrow well" (Proverbs 23:27). A narrow well is easy to slip into and almost impossible to climb out of. David uses the same picture for death when he prays, "And let not the pit shut its mouth on me" (Psalm 69:15). A real well gives water out of its mouth. This false well takes a person in and closes over him.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The well narratives build a recognizable pattern. Abraham's servant finds Rebekah at a well (Genesis 24), Jacob meets Rachel at a well (Genesis 29), and Moses meets the daughters of Jethro, including his future wife Zipporah, at a well (Exodus 2:15–21). Each time, a traveler stops at a well, water is drawn, and a marriage follows that carries God's purposes forward.

John 4 sets Jesus in that same kind of scene. "Now Jacob's well was there. Jesus therefore, being wearied from His journey, sat thus by the well" (John 4:6). A woman comes "to draw water," and only a chapter after John the Baptist calls Jesus the Bridegroom (John 3:29). The Greek words in John are not the Hebrew bᵉʼêr, but the setting recalls those earlier scenes. This time the woman is a Samaritan with a broken history, and the Bridegroom who asks her for a drink offers her far more than He asks (John 4:7, 10). She objects, "Sir, You have nothing to draw with, and the well is deep," and asks, "Are You greater than our father Jacob, who gave us the well?" (John 4:11–12). Jesus answers that "whoever drinks of this water will thirst again" (John 4:13), while the water He gives becomes a fountain within a person, springing up to everlasting life (John 4:14, paraphrased). The gift every Old Testament well could only hint at is now offered in a Person.

Two more connections go deeper. Jacob had to roll a great stone from the well's mouth to give life to the flock. The Gospels end with another great stone rolled away from a sealed opening in the earth (Matthew 28:2). The New Testament does not draw that comparison, but the echo is strong: life closed in under a stone is released by divine strength for the flock. And the pit David begged not to shut its mouth on him (Psalm 69:15) could not hold Christ. He went down into death and came out again, so the pit no longer has the last word for those who belong to Him.

7. Spirit & Life

Hagar's story speaks to anyone whose sorrow has narrowed their sight. She sat in the desert believing her son would die, and the well was there to be seen once God opened her eyes. The Lord does not always change our circumstances first. Sometimes He opens our eyes to what He has already provided. Praying to see what God has provided is a different prayer from asking Him to make something new, and Hagar's well gives us reason to pray it.

Isaac's wells speak to every believer who inherits a faith someone else first dug out. Old wells get filled with earth through neglect, hostility, or simply time. Isaac did not invent new names for his father's wells. He uncovered what had been given and kept going through Esek and Sitnah until God brought him to Rehoboth. The life of faith involves that kind of steady digging: returning to Scripture, prayer, and the promises earlier generations relied on.

Numbers 21 keeps that digging in its place. The nobles dug with their staffs, and Israel sang, but the water came because God had said, "I will give them water." Spiritual effort matters. It is still never the source. Jesus promised that the water He gives becomes a spring within the believer (John 4:14; 7:38), and John explains that He was speaking of the Spirit (John 7:39). Only He fills that well.

The poetic uses press a searching question about desire. Every heart draws from some deep source. Faithful love, and above all the living water of Christ, refreshes a person and goes on refreshing others. The narrow well of sin promises the same satisfaction and then closes over its victim. The safest way to turn from the false well is not only to resist it but to drink fully from the true one.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 14:10 — The Valley of Siddim was "full of asphalt pits," and fleeing kings fell into them; here the word describes a natural hazard rather than a water source.

  • Genesis 24:20 — Rebekah runs back and forth to the well to water ten camels, a generous act that marks her as the bride God had chosen for Isaac.

  • Numbers 20:17 — Moses promises Edom that Israel will not drink from their wells, showing that wells were recognized private property.

  • 2 Samuel 17:18–21 — David's messengers hide in a well at Bahurim while a woman covers its mouth, and they escape to warn the king.

  • Psalm 55:23 — David trusts God to "bring them down to the pit of destruction," handing judgment on deceitful men to the Lord.

10. Other Relevant Insights

Some English Bibles use "well" in verses where the Hebrew word is different. Proverbs 10:11 calls the mouth of the righteous "a well of life," and Proverbs 25:26 speaks of a polluted well, but both verses use Hebrew words for a spring or fountain, not bᵉʼêr. Those verses fit the wider biblical imagery of water, but they should not be counted as uses of this word.
