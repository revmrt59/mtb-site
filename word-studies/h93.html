NUT, NUTS - (ʼĕgôwz, Strong's H0093)

Summary

The Hebrew word ʼĕgôwz means nut, and it is generally understood to mean the walnut tree and its fruit. It occurs once in the Hebrew Bible, in Song of Solomon 6:11: "I went down to the garden of nuts." Because the word appears only once, everything we learn from it comes from that one verse and the love poetry around it. The verse describes someone going down into a cultivated grove in springtime to see whether the vines have budded and the pomegranates have bloomed.
Count source — Leningrad Hebrew text (Qere readings).

The word itself names an ordinary tree, but the setting carries real spiritual weight. In the Song, the garden is a picture of the beloved and of the love the two share. Going down into the walnut grove is an act of attention and longing, a lover coming to look for signs of new life. The wider Bible uses the same kind of picture to describe God. He plants His people, comes looking for fruit, and rejoices when He finds it. The hidden, slow-growing, well-protected walnut also became a picture in later Jewish teaching of Israel made clean again before God, and Paul describes the believer's life as "hidden with Christ in God."

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

ʼĕgôwz is a Hebrew noun for a nut-bearing tree. Most scholars identify it as the walnut (Juglans regia), a large tree grown in the ancient Near East for its shade, fragrant leaves, and rich fruit. The word is usually thought to be borrowed from Persian, where a similar word for nut appears. That would suit the cultivated orchards pictured in the Song of Solomon, although the history of the word should not be pressed for meaning.

The one setting where the word appears is Hebrew love poetry. The Song is full of gardens, spices, fruit trees, springs, and seasons, and its pictures often work through fragrance, taste, shade, and value rather than strict visual likeness. A "garden of nuts" belongs to that world of planted, tended, enjoyed places.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word has a single, concrete meaning:

1. A nut, most likely the walnut, and by extension the tree or grove that produces it. In Song of Solomon 6:11, "the garden of nuts" means an orchard or grove of nut trees.

Nothing in the word itself carries a theological idea. It names a tree. Its significance comes from where the poet places it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears only once, and that one use is part of a phrase: "the garden of nuts." Pairing the word with "garden" matters. A walnut grove is planted ground that someone has invested in, since walnut trees grow slowly and take years to bear well. The verse then piles up signs of spring: the green growth of the valley, the budding vine, and the blooming pomegranate. The grove is the place where the speaker goes, and the vines and pomegranates are what the speaker wants to see.

The verse is built on purpose and expectation: "I went down... To see... To see whether." The speaker comes with a question, and the question is about life and readiness.

4. Theological Significance

The setting of this one word points to a truth that runs through Scripture. The one who loves also cultivates, and the one who cultivates comes looking for fruit. The garden in the Song is enclosed, tended, and valued. Someone has shaped it, and someone visits it with eager interest.

That picture fits what the Bible teaches about God and His people. Scripture does not picture God as a landowner who plants and then forgets. He comes near, looks closely, and rejoices in growth. Zephaniah says of Him: "He will rejoice over you with gladness, He will quiet you with His love, He will rejoice over you with singing" (Zephaniah 3:17). The Song's lover, going down into the grove to see whether love has blossomed, gives a human picture of the kind of delight that Zephaniah ascribes to the LORD.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Song of Solomon 6:11 — the descent into the grove. "I went down to the garden of nuts To see the verdure of the valley, To see whether the vine had budded And the pomegranates had bloomed." Interpreters differ on whether the bridegroom or the bride is speaking. Many hear the bridegroom, which fits the earlier line that "my beloved has gone to his garden" (Song of Solomon 6:2). Either way, the movement is the same. Love goes down, comes close, and looks for signs of life. A similar invitation appears in Song of Solomon 7:11–12, where the bride invites her beloved to go out early to the vineyards to see whether the vine has budded and the pomegranates are in bloom. In both places, love is something tended and watched over.

The setting adds depth to the scene. The Song sets its safe garden places beside harsher ones. The bride says, "the sun has tanned me" from forced work in her brothers' vineyards (Song of Solomon 1:6). In Song of Solomon 5:7, out in the city at night, "The watchmen... struck me, they wounded me." A grove of large, shade-giving walnut trees stands at the other end of that picture. Walnut trees take years of patient growth before they bear well, and the speaker comes to this established grove in spring to look for fresh buds and blossoms. The grove suggests love that has been given time to grow, a sheltered place where new life is watched for.

Song of Solomon 4:12–16 — the garden enclosed. Earlier the bridegroom says, "A garden enclosed Is my sister, my spouse, A spring shut up, A fountain sealed" (4:12). He describes an orchard of pomegranates and every precious spice, "A well of living waters" (4:15). Then the bride answers: "Let my beloved come to his garden And eat its pleasant fruits" (4:16). This passage explains what "garden" means when the Song reaches 6:11. The garden is the beloved herself, kept for one person, and full of what delights him. Later, whoever goes down to the garden of nuts enters a place that has already been defined as private, protected, and welcoming. Read in that light, the grove scene shows faithful love coming back to see what has grown.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The Song does not mention walnuts again, and the New Testament never refers to this verse. Still, the picture of a lover coming to a garden to look for fruit fits within a larger biblical story about God and His planted people.

Isaiah sings of God's vineyard: "He dug it up and cleared out its stones, And planted it with the choicest vine... So He expected it to bring forth good grapes, But it brought forth wild grapes" (Isaiah 5:2). Then comes the grieving question, "What more could have been done to My vineyard That I have not done in it?" (Isaiah 5:4), followed by the explanation: "He looked for justice, but behold, oppression" (Isaiah 5:7). The same kind of looking that happens in Song of Solomon 6:11 happens here, but in Isaiah it ends in disappointment.

Jesus takes up the same vine-and-vinedresser picture, and read beside Isaiah's song, His words answer that grief from within Himself: "I am the true vine, and My Father is the vinedresser" (John 15:1). The Father still prunes and still looks for fruit, "that it may bear more fruit" (John 15:2). Now the fruit grows out of union with the Son: "He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit" (John 15:5). Christian readers have long heard the Song as a picture of Christ and His church. Read that way, the grove in 6:11 is the place where the Bridegroom comes to see blossoms that He Himself has made possible. This connection is a resonance between passages that share the same imagery, and no New Testament writer states it as fulfillment of the Song. It is still a fruitful way to read the verse in light of the whole Bible.

7. Spirit & Life

The verse begins with "I went down." Read with the rest of Scripture, it pictures a Lord who does not wait at a distance for His people to report on their growth. He comes into the garden Himself. A believer can take real comfort in that. God's interest in your life is close and personal, and He looks for the first small buds as well as the full harvest. Zephaniah's picture of God rejoicing "with singing" fits this scene. The One who comes to inspect is also the One who delights.

The walnut grove also speaks to patience. Spring buds on the vine and blossoms on the pomegranate are quickly seen. A walnut tree takes years to mature, and its fruit is wrapped in a hard shell. Some of God's work in a person is like that: slow, deep, and largely hidden. A believer who sees little outward flowering may still be a grove the Lord planted for the long term.

Later Jewish teaching drew a lesson from the walnut's shell. A walnut can fall into the mud, yet when it is opened, the meat inside is clean. The rabbis applied the picture to Israel, which, though soiled, could be made clean again before God. This comes from later tradition and is not the meaning of the word, but it fits a truth Scripture teaches directly: "For you died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:3). The world can bruise and dirty the outside of a believer's life, as the watchmen wounded the bride in the Song. What God has hidden in Christ is guarded, and He knows exactly where to look for it.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

ʼĕgôwz occurs only in Song of Solomon 6:11, which is discussed above. Readers of English Bibles will also find "nuts" in Genesis 43:11, where Jacob sends pistachio nuts and almonds to Egypt as a gift. That verse uses a different Hebrew word. Song of Solomon 6:2 and 7:12, which are related garden scenes, also use other vocabulary. They support the picture in 6:11 but are not further uses of this word.
