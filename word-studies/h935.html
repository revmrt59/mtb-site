COME IN, ENTER, BRING - (bôwʼ, Strong's H0935)

Summary

The Hebrew verb bôwʼ means to come, to come in, or to enter. In a related form it means to bring, that is, to cause someone or something to come in. It is one of the most common verbs in the Old Testament. Scripture uses it for everyday movement such as entering a tent, arriving at a city, or bringing a meal. It also uses the same verb for some of the weightiest movements in the Bible: God bringing His people into the land He promised, the high priest entering the Holy Place, worshipers bringing their offerings, and the promised King coming to Zion.

What makes the word worth studying is who comes and where they enter. Fallen people cannot walk into God's presence on their own terms, yet God Himself brings His people in. The prophets announce that His appointed days are "coming," which means His word is already on its way to being fulfilled. Throughout the Old Testament the verb builds expectation for the One who would come in the name of the Lord. That expectation is answered in Jesus Christ, who came into the world and entered the Most Holy Place with His own blood, so that we might enter too.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bôwʼ is a basic Hebrew verb of motion, appearing about 2,500 times and in every section of the Old Testament. It usually describes movement toward or into a place, from the speaker's point of view. Its common partner is the verb to go out, and Scripture often pairs the two to describe a whole life or a whole act of deliverance.

Hebrew verbs change their meaning by changing form. One form of bôwʼ gives the simple sense, come or enter. Another form makes the verb causative, so that come in becomes cause to come in. English simply translates this as bring. This explains why the NKJV renders the same Hebrew word as "came," "come," "brought," and "bring" hundreds of times each, and as "enter" or "entered" more than a hundred times.

The word turns up in family scenes, temple rules, military campaigns, and prophetic oracles. It is so ordinary that a reader can easily pass over it, and that is exactly why its theological uses are easy to miss.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, bôwʼ means to move from one place or sphere into another. Context determines the nuance:

1. To come or arrive. A person reaches a destination or approaches someone, as when Abram and his household came to the land of Canaan (Genesis 12:5).

2. To go in or enter. Movement passes into a space such as an ark, tent, city, sanctuary, or land, as when Noah and his family entered the ark (Genesis 7:13). The word can also serve as a modest expression for a man going in to a woman in marriage (Genesis 29:23).

3. To bring. In its causative form someone causes a person or thing to come in, as when Abel brought the firstborn of his flock (Genesis 4:4).

4. To come about or come upon. Events, times, and consequences "come." Days arrive, promises come to pass (Joshua 21:45), and calamity befalls people (Job 3:25). The same image describes the sun "going down," or entering, at evening (Genesis 15:12), and people being "advanced" in years (Genesis 18:11).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The meaning of bôwʼ changes most noticeably when the subject changes. When people are the subject, the word describes travel, visits, and invasions. When God is the subject of the causative form, it describes His saving action: He brings His people into the land, gathers them home from exile (Ezekiel 36:24), and brings them to His holy mountain (Isaiah 56:7). He also brings judgment, and that use runs through Jeremiah and Ezekiel.

The pairing with go out carries real weight. Deuteronomy 6:23 uses both verbs: "He brought us out from there, that He might bring us in." The first verb is a different Hebrew word. The second is bôwʼ. Psalm 121:8 speaks of the Lord preserving our going out and our coming in, which describes God's watch over the whole course of a life.

One grammatical pattern affects how a passage reads. Hebrew sometimes repeats a verb to emphasize certainty. With bôwʼ the NKJV conveys this repetition with words like "surely," "certainly," or "doubtless." Habakkuk 2:3 promises that the vision will surely come, and Psalm 126:6 assures the weeping sower that he will doubtless come again with rejoicing. The repetition does not change what the word means. It stresses that what God has promised will arrive.

4. Theological Significance

The verb shows that God is the One who moves history toward His purposes. The prophets repeatedly announce that "the days are coming" (Jeremiah 31:31, introducing the New Covenant; compare 1 Samuel 2:31). They also announce that the day of the Lord is coming (Zechariah 14:1; Malachi 4:1). These announcements treat the future as something already approaching. History in Scripture is not random or circular. It moves toward days God has appointed. Joshua's summary at the end of the conquest says that not one good word God had spoken failed, but all came to pass (Joshua 21:45). The same verb that describes the arrival of a traveler describes the arrival of God's promises, and they arrive as surely as morning.

The word also shows a tension in how God relates to His people. Humanity is restricted from entering God's presence, yet God takes the initiative to bring His people in. Holiness sets the boundary, and grace opens the way through it. Both truths stand side by side in the Old Testament, and the gospel finally resolves them.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Brought out in order to be brought in (Exodus 6:8; Deuteronomy 6:23; Joshua 24:8). Before the plagues began, God promised, "And I will bring you into the land which I swore to give to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" (Exodus 6:8). Forty years later, Moses gave Israel a one-sentence summary of their history to teach their children: "Then He brought us out from there, that He might bring us in, to give us the land of which He swore to our fathers" (Deuteronomy 6:23). The phrase "that He might" carries the point. The exodus was never meant to end in the wilderness, because leaving Egypt served the purpose of entering the land. Deuteronomy 8:7 describes the land God was bringing them into as a good land of brooks and springs. Joshua grounds Israel's renewed loyalty in the same fact: God brought them into the land of the Amorites (Joshua 24:8). Israel did not migrate into its inheritance by its own strength. God carried them in.

Not at just any time (Leviticus 16:2–3; Numbers 4:20). The verb that describes God's generosity also sets the limits of His holiness. The Lord told Moses to warn Aaron "not to come at just any time into the Holy Place inside the veil, before the mercy seat which is on the ark, lest he die" (Leviticus 16:2). The next verse explains how Aaron may come in: with a bull for a sin offering and a ram for a burnt offering (Leviticus 16:3). Even the Kohathite Levites, who carried the holy furniture, were not to go in to watch while the holy things were being covered, lest they die (Numbers 4:20). Entering God's presence was never casual. It required the right person, at the right time, carrying the blood of an offering.

Bringing the offering (Leviticus 4:4; 5:6; 17:4–5). Leviticus uses the causative "bring" constantly. When the anointed priest sinned, he was to bring a bull for a sin offering to the door of the tabernacle, lay his hand on its head, and kill it before the Lord (Leviticus 4:4). The person who had sinned was to bring a trespass offering to the Lord (Leviticus 5:6). An Israelite who slaughtered an ox, lamb, or goat was not to kill it in the open field but had to bring it to the priest at the door of the tabernacle as an offering to the Lord (Leviticus 17:3–5). Game that was hunted was handled differently: its blood was poured out and covered with dust (Leviticus 17:13). Worship in Israel meant physically giving up something valuable and carrying it to the place where God had promised to meet His people. In the sin and trespass offerings, repentance was acted out in an obedient walk toward God's dwelling.

The coming King (Genesis 49:10; Psalm 24:7; Psalm 118:26; Zechariah 9:9; Malachi 3:1). From the Bible's earliest chapters, bôwʼ points toward an arrival. Jacob foretold that the scepter would not depart from Judah until Shiloh comes, and that the obedience of the peoples would belong to him (Genesis 49:10). The meaning of "Shiloh" is debated, but the verse clearly looks toward a ruler from Judah. Israel's worship took up the same expectation: "Lift up your heads, O you gates! And be lifted up, you everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in" (Psalm 24:7). Another psalm says, "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD!" (Psalm 118:26). Zechariah gave the expected King a surprising shape: "Behold, your King is coming to you; He is just and having salvation, Lowly and riding on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9). Malachi, the last of the prophetic books, uses the verb twice in one verse: "And the Lord, whom you seek, Will suddenly come to His temple... Behold, He is coming" (Malachi 3:1). By the end of the Old Testament, the One expected to come is no longer only a king from Judah. He is the Lord Himself, coming to His own house.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The New Testament presents Jesus as the answer to these Old Testament arrivals. The Gospels are written in Greek, so they use a different verb, but they deliberately echo these passages. When Jesus rode into Jerusalem, Matthew quoted Zechariah 9:9 (Matthew 21:5), and the crowds sang the words of Psalm 118:26. The King had come, lowly and bringing salvation. Hebrews describes the incarnation itself as an arrival: "Therefore, when He came into the world, He said: 'Sacrifice and offering You did not desire, But a body You have prepared for Me'" (Hebrews 10:5).

Christ also resolves the restriction placed on Aaron. Aaron could enter only once a year with animal blood. Christ came another way: "Not with the blood of goats and calves, but with His own blood He entered the Most Holy Place once for all, having obtained eternal redemption" (Hebrews 9:12). As a result, the door that Leviticus 16:2 guarded so strictly now stands open: "having boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way which He consecrated for us, through the veil, that is, His flesh" (Hebrews 10:19–20).

The story also comes full circle. Ezekiel saw the glory of the Lord leave the temple because of Israel's sin. Later he saw that glory come back and enter the temple by the east gate (Ezekiel 43:2–4). Hebrews takes Psalm 95:11, where God warned that a faithless generation would not enter His rest, and uses it to show that a greater rest still remains for God's people (Hebrews 3–4). Abraham lived in tents and "waited for the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God" (Hebrews 11:10). Deuteronomy 6:23 said that God brought His people out in order to bring them in. In Christ, that movement reaches its full extent: out of sin and death, and into God's presence and His eternal inheritance.

7. Spirit & Life

Deuteronomy 6:23 gives believers a pattern for understanding their own salvation. God did not rescue you from sin only to leave you wandering just past the border of Egypt. His purpose in bringing you out was to bring you in, into His fellowship, His rest, and His promises. If the Christian life feels like a long wilderness, the verb reminds you which way God is moving. He intends to finish what He began.

Leviticus 16 keeps that confidence from becoming careless. The same God who welcomes us is the God before whom Aaron could not appear at just any time. Our boldness rests entirely on the blood of Jesus. Hebrews 10:22 holds both truths together when it says, "let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith." We come freely because Someone else paid for our entrance.

The Israelite who led his animal to the tabernacle door also teaches us something. Paul uses the language of sacrifice for every believer: "present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God, which is your reasonable service" (Romans 12:1). We no longer bring bulls, but we still bring ourselves, our time, obedience, and possessions, to the place where God meets us.

Finally, the prophets' "coming" days teach believers to wait in hope. Habakkuk was told that the vision would surely come, even if it seemed to delay. The Lord who came suddenly to His temple, just as Malachi promised, will keep every word He has spoken. Christians can wait for the day of His return knowing that it is already on its way.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 7:1, 16 — God invited Noah to come into the ark, and when all had entered, the Lord shut him in. This early "entering" in Scripture was an entrance into safety prepared by God.

  • Ruth 2:12 — Boaz blessed Ruth as one who had come to take refuge under the wings of the God of Israel. A Moabite woman's "coming" showed that outsiders could be welcomed among God's people.

  • Psalm 100:2, 4 — Worshipers are called to come before His presence with singing and to enter His gates with thanksgiving. Because of what God has done, coming into His presence becomes an occasion for joy.

  • Isaiah 26:2 — The gates are opened so that the righteous nation that keeps the truth may enter. Entrance into God's city belongs to the righteous who keep the truth.

  • Ezekiel 37:5 — God promised that breath would enter the dry bones and they would live. In this verse the verb describes God's life coming into what was dead.

  • Habakkuk 2:3 — The vision will surely come, so the prophet is to wait for it. The repeated verb gives the reader assurance that God's word will arrive at its appointed time.
