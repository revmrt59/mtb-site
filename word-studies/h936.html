DESPISE, SCORN - (bûwz, Strong's H0936)

Summary

The Hebrew verb bûwz means to despise: to look at someone or something and decide it has no worth. It is an active judgment. In many of its uses it is a sinful verdict. Proverbs uses it for the fool who despises wisdom and instruction, the person who despises God's word, the neighbor, or an aging mother. The Bible treats this contempt as more than bad manners. It is a refusal of what God Himself values, and it leads to ruin.

The same verb can also describe a right judgment. In Song of Solomon 8:7, a man who offers all his wealth in exchange for love "would be utterly despised." A related verb (bâzâh, H0959) appears in 2 Kings 19:21, where the Lord's answer to the boasting Assyrian king declares that the daughter of Zion has despised him. In Zechariah 4:10, God rebukes people who despised the small beginnings of the rebuilt temple. Taken together, these uses show that our contempt reveals our values. Growing in godliness means learning to value what God values and to see through what the world admires.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Bûwz is a Hebrew verb used only a handful of times, and most of those uses are in Proverbs. It also appears in the closing chapter of the Song of Solomon and in Zechariah's vision of the rebuilt temple. These uses move from household and marketplace wisdom to love poetry and temple rebuilding. The word does the same work in each setting. It describes a person weighing something and finding it worthless.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb has one core meaning: to despise, hold in contempt, or treat as worthless. It goes beyond indifference. The person who despises has made a judgment and acts on it, usually by looking down on or dismissing the person or thing despised. Whether the contempt is sinful or right depends on what is being despised. Contempt for wisdom is folly. Contempt for the idea that love can be bought is sound judgment.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Proverbs, the verb names an attitude that Proverbs ties to folly. Fools despise wisdom and instruction (Proverbs 1:7), and the same contempt is aimed at the word (Proverbs 13:13), at wise counsel (Proverbs 23:9), at a neighbor (Proverbs 11:12; 14:21), and at parents (Proverbs 23:22; 30:17). In each case the object deserved honor and received scorn.

One proverb records ordinary public opinion. People do not despise a starving thief who steals to feed himself, yet he must still repay sevenfold (Proverbs 6:30–31, paraphrased). The teacher uses this to heighten a contrast. The adulterer has no such excuse, and his disgrace will not be wiped away (Proverbs 6:32–33).

In Song of Solomon 8:7, the verb describes a right refusal: love will not be sold. The related verb in 2 Kings 19:21 carries the same idea for Zion, which will not be frightened.

One grammatical detail intensifies the expression. In Song of Solomon 8:7 the Hebrew uses the verb twice in a row, a standard way of adding emphasis. That doubling is why the NKJV says "utterly despised." The offer to buy love is rejected completely.

4. Theological Significance

What a person despises shows what that person truly values. God has His own scale of worth, and bûwz marks the places where human judgment departs from it.

God's scale includes people. "He who despises his neighbor sins; But he who has mercy on the poor, happy is he" (Proverbs 14:21). The parallel lines set contempt against mercy, especially mercy toward the poor, whom people most easily dismiss. Proverbs 11:12 adds that the one who despises his neighbor "is devoid of wisdom," while "a man of understanding holds his peace." Contempt usually comes out in speech, and wisdom knows when to stay silent. James makes the same point when believers give the rich man the good seat and send the poor man to the footstool. James reminds them that God has "chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith" and tells them, "you have dishonored the poor man" (James 2:5–6). Despising a person God has chosen means setting your judgment against His.

God's scale also has room for holy contempt. Here the text uses the related verb bâzâh (H0959). When Sennacherib mocked the living God, the Lord's answer through Isaiah began by saying that the virgin daughter of Zion had despised him and laughed him to scorn (2 Kings 19:21, paraphrased). Jerusalem was surrounded and outmatched, yet God describes her as shaking her head at the tyrant. That contempt was not arrogance. It rested on the Lord who had spoken, whose word made the enemy's threats look small.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

Proverbs 1:7 and 13:13: the fool's defining choice. Proverbs opens with its motto: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, But fools despise wisdom and instruction." The opposite of fearing the Lord is despising His instruction, not simply lacking information. The fool treats instruction as beneath him. Proverbs 13:13 states the outcome: "He who despises the word will be destroyed, But he who fears the commandment will be rewarded." Despising and fearing are again set against each other, and so are destruction and reward. The fool's contempt cuts him off from the one thing that could save him.

Proverbs 23:22 and 30:17: contempt for parents. "Listen to your father who begot you, And do not despise your mother when she is old." The word "old" matters. Age can make a mother seem slow or out of date, and that is when contempt is tempting. The command to "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12) does not expire when parents become weak. Proverbs 30:17 gives the warning in a harsh image: "The eye that mocks his father, And scorns obedience to his mother, The ravens of the valley will pick it out." The proverb names the eye, a fitting target for contempt that begins in looking down on someone. The picture suggests a body left unburied and exposed, a shameful end that matches the shame the child heaped on those who gave him life.

Song of Solomon 8:7: love that cannot be bought. "Many waters cannot quench love, Nor can the floods drown it. If a man would give for love All the wealth of his house, It would be utterly despised." Floods cannot destroy this love, and wealth cannot purchase it. The man in the verse offers everything he owns, and the offer fails because he has mistaken a gift for a transaction. Paul says the same about the other side of the exchange: tongues, prophecy, and faith that moves mountains are nothing without love (1 Corinthians 13:1–2). Love cannot be bought, and nothing can replace it. The doubled Hebrew verb makes the scorn complete.

Zechariah 4:10: the day of small things. The exiles had laid the temple's foundation, and older people who had seen the first temple wept when the foundation was laid (Ezra 3:12). Haggai later asked whether the new house was not "as nothing" in their eyes (Haggai 2:3). Into that disappointment God asks who has despised the day of small things, and He says His eyes rejoice to see the plumb line in Zerubbabel's hand (paraphrased). A plumb line is a mason's tool for keeping a wall straight. It is a humble sign that building has begun. Just before this, God had told Zerubbabel that the work would succeed by His Spirit and not by might or power (Zechariah 4:6). Some looked down on the work because it was small. God's eyes rejoiced over it, because the work would be finished by His Spirit.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

Zechariah's rebuke reflects how God works throughout Scripture. Jesus compared the kingdom to a mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, which grows into a tree where birds nest (Matthew 13:31–32). Paul writes that God chose the foolish, weak, and despised things of the world to shame the wise and the strong (1 Corinthians 1:27–28). The Savior Himself was laid in a manger (Luke 2:7), grew up in Nazareth, and died on a Roman cross. Isaiah foresaw a Servant who would be despised and rejected (Isaiah 53:3, using a different Hebrew word). Nothing about Christ's arrival looked impressive by human standards. The people who despised small things had no category for a crucified Messiah.

Song of Solomon 8:7 points toward the gospel from the other direction. The love of God in Christ cannot be bought by anyone. It comes as a gift, paid for by the Giver. Seen in that light, every attempt to earn it with religious wealth meets a verdict like the one in the Song: utterly despised. And because Christ has disarmed the powers (Colossians 2:15), His people can share Zion's fearless contempt for the boasts of evil.

7. Spirit & Life

Every believer carries a scale of worth, and this word asks us to check ours. The fool's scale is inverted. He scorns the word that could save him, the neighbor God loves, and the parents who gave him life. Most of us are not blatant fools, yet contempt slips in quietly: the eye-roll at an aging parent, the dismissive silence toward a poor neighbor, the boredom with Scripture we think we already know. Proverbs warns that contempt for the word, for a neighbor and for parents leads toward ruin (Proverbs 13:13; 14:21; 30:17).

The Spirit also teaches a right kind of contempt. Where the Spirit is at work, small things stop looking small: the struggling church plant, the single conversation, the first faltering prayer of a new believer. God's eyes rejoice over the plumb line, and ours can learn to as well. Meanwhile, the things the world prizes lose their grip on us. We stop trying to buy love with performance, and we stop trembling before the loud voices that defy God. The heart that knows God's love learns to despise what He despises and to treasure what He treasures.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 6:30 — People do not despise a starving thief (paraphrased). The verse shows that even ordinary public opinion weighs circumstances before passing judgment. In context, the point is that the adulterer has no such excuse.

  • Proverbs 23:9 — The fool will despise the wisdom of your words, so do not waste them on him (paraphrased). Contempt makes a person unteachable, and wisdom knows when to keep quiet.

  • Song of Solomon 8:1 — The bride wishes she could show her love openly without anyone despising her (paraphrased). Here the word reflects the social scorn that pure affection may still fear.
