WOMB, BELLY, BODY - (beṭen, Strong's H0990)

Summary

The Hebrew word beṭen names the belly, the soft middle of the body. Scripture uses it for the stomach that hungers, the womb where a child grows, and, by extension, a person's inner being, the hidden depths that no one else can see. The NKJV most often renders it "womb." In the phrase "the fruit of your body," it also means children.

Through this word, the Bible keeps bringing God into the most hidden place of human life. He forms people in the womb and calls prophets before they are born. He searches the inner chambers of the heart, and He wants His Word to fill a person's inner being. The word also points toward Christ. God swore that the fruit of David's body would sit on his throne. Jonah cried "out of the belly of Sheol," and Jesus took that experience as the sign of His own death and resurrection. Jesus also promised that out of the believer's inmost being would flow rivers of living water.

Full Study

1. Word Background & Setting

Beṭen is an ordinary Hebrew word for the body's midsection. Most of its uses fall in four kinds of settings: birth stories and prophetic callings, the blessing and curse lists of Deuteronomy, the laments of Job and the Psalms, and the sayings of Proverbs about appetite and the inner life. Biblical Hebrew often speaks of a person's thoughts and feelings as happening "inside" the body. That habit is why a word for the belly can also describe what a person keeps deep within.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

1. Belly or stomach. This is the physical abdomen, the place of hunger and fullness. Ehud thrust his dagger into Eglon's belly (Judges 3:21), and "the stomach of the wicked shall be in want" (Proverbs 13:25).

2. Womb. This is the place where a child is conceived and grows: "Two nations are in your womb" (Genesis 25:23). The common phrase "fruit of the womb" or "fruit of the body" means a person's children (Deuteronomy 28:4).

3. Inner being. These are a person's inmost depths, where words, thoughts, and motives are held. Wise sayings are to be kept "within you" (Proverbs 22:18), and gossip goes "down into the inmost body" (Proverbs 18:8).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The phrase "from the womb" usually means "from the very beginning of my life." It appears in Samson's calling (Judges 13:5), in the Servant's calling (Isaiah 49:1), and in a psalmist's trust in God since birth (Psalm 71:6). When the word appears with "fruit," the subject is offspring and family line, so the NKJV generally translates it "body."

The physical belly in Scripture can be fragile or strong. Eglon's soft belly swallowed even the hilt of Ehud's dagger (Judges 3:22). The LORD, by contrast, says of Behemoth, "his power is in his stomach muscles" (Job 40:16). Humans are fragile at the very place where the great beast is mighty.

The word appears once in a building. The rounded "convex surface" of the bronze pillar capitals at Solomon's temple carried two hundred pomegranates (1 Kings 7:20). The use is descriptive, but it is fitting that a word tied to the womb sits among the fruit carved at the entrance to God's house.

4. Theological Significance

Scripture treats the womb as God's territory. When Rachel demanded children, Jacob answered that he was not in the place of God, who had withheld the fruit of her womb (Genesis 30:2). Deuteronomy lists blessed "fruit of your body" among God's gifts (7:13; 28:4, 11) and cursed fruit among the judgments for rebellion (28:18). Hosea announced that Ephraim's glory would fly away from birth, from pregnancy, and from conception (Hosea 9:11). Children are a gift from the Giver, never a biological guarantee.

The womb also humbles human pride. Job grieved that the womb's doors had not been shut and wished he had been carried from the womb to the grave (Job 3:10–11; 10:19). Ecclesiastes notes that a person leaves the world as naked as he left his mother's womb (5:15). Everyone enters with nothing and leaves with nothing.

The same fact sets a standard for justice. When Job defended his treatment of his servants, he argued: "Did not He who made me in the womb make them? Did not the same One fashion us in the womb?" (Job 31:15). The servant and the master were shaped by the same hands. For that reason, how a person treats someone of lower rank answers to their common Maker.

Because the beṭen is hidden, it is also where God exposes what people conceal. In the jealousy ordeal of Numbers 5, a suspected wife's hidden unfaithfulness would be shown in her swelling belly (5:21–22, 27). In this way, a secret sin was marked on a hidden part of the body. In humiliation, the belly sinks to the dust: "Our body clings to the ground" (Psalm 44:25). This is a picture of defeat that recalls the serpent's curse, "On your belly you shall go" (Genesis 3:14), although Genesis uses a different Hebrew word there.

5. Important Biblical Passages & Distinctions

The womb as God's workshop (Psalm 139:13; Jeremiah 1:5). David prays, "For You formed my inward parts; You covered me in my mother's womb." God is at work where no human eye can reach. Jeremiah's call adds purpose to formation: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you; Before you were born I sanctified you; I ordained you a prophet to the nations." God knew Jeremiah before He formed him and set him apart before he was born, all before Jeremiah could do anything. Samson, too, was "a Nazirite to God from the womb" (Judges 13:5), and he later disclosed that secret to Delilah in nearly the same words (Judges 16:17). Paul used this language for his own calling, saying God "separated me from my mother's womb and called me through His grace" (Galatians 1:15). In each of these callings, God's purpose begins in His knowledge of the person before birth.

Two nations in one womb (Genesis 25:23; Hosea 12:3). When the twins struggled inside Rebekah, the LORD explained, "Two nations are in your womb... And the older shall serve the younger." God's choice was announced before either child had done good or evil. Hosea later recalls that Jacob "took his brother by the heel in the womb, And in his strength he struggled with God." The struggle that began in the womb continued through Jacob's life until God met him at Peniel.

The Word taken into the inner being (Ezekiel 3:3; Proverbs 20:27; Job 32:18–19). God told Ezekiel, "Son of man, feed your belly, and fill your stomach with this scroll that I give you." Before Ezekiel could speak for God, God's message had to be inside him. Proverbs adds that "the spirit of a man is the lamp of the LORD, Searching all the inner depths of his heart." Here beṭen stands behind the phrase "inner depths of his heart." God shines His light into the inner rooms where outward religion cannot reach. Elihu describes what happens when words fill a person's inner being: "my belly is like wine that has no vent; It is ready to burst like new wineskins" (Job 32:19). What fills the inner being eventually comes out.

The craving belly emptied (Job 20:15, 20). Zophar describes the greedy man: "He swallows down riches And vomits them up again; God casts them out of his belly." Such a man "knows no quietness in his heart," and here beṭen stands behind "heart." Riches swallowed in secret cannot be kept down, because God rules over the very place where they were hidden. Proverbs states the contrast plainly: the righteous eat until satisfied, "but the stomach of the wicked shall be in want" (13:25). Paul warns of people "whose god is their belly" (Philippians 3:19). An appetite treated as a god never brings rest.

6. Christ & the Larger Biblical Story

The "fruit of your body" blessing in Deuteronomy narrows to one family line. In Psalm 132:11, the LORD swore to David that He would set the fruit of his body on his throne. Peter cited this oath at Pentecost: God had sworn that from the fruit of David's body, according to the flesh, He would raise up the Christ (Acts 2:30). The promised King would come from David's actual descendants, not as an adopted heir. The Son of God entered the line of David through a real human womb.

The Psalms set two kinds of beginnings side by side. Psalm 58:3 says the wicked are estranged from the womb and go astray as soon as they are born. In Psalm 22, whose opening words Jesus cried from the cross, the sufferer says God took him out of the womb and has been his God from his mother's womb (22:9–10). Jesus fulfills that trust completely. He was the only one whose life was turned toward the Father from its very beginning. Isaiah's Servant speaks in the same way: "The LORD has called Me from the womb" (Isaiah 49:1).

Jonah is the most striking connection. From the depths, he prayed, "Out of the belly of Sheol I cried, And You heard my voice" (Jonah 2:2). He described the fish's interior as the belly of the realm of the dead. Then God brought his life up from the pit (2:6). Jesus said that as Jonah was three days and three nights in the great fish, so the Son of Man would be in the heart of the earth (Matthew 12:40). The deep closed around Jonah (Jonah 2:5), and the grave closed over Christ, but it could not hold Him (Acts 2:24). The belly of death became the place from which resurrection came.

The story reaches its goal in Jesus' promise: "out of his heart will flow rivers of living water" (John 7:38). The Greek word behind "heart" there is koilia, which also literally means belly. The two words are not formally identical, but the picture carries forward. The inner being that in the Old Testament craved, hid sin, and lay in the dust becomes a spring of the Spirit's life.

7. Spirit & Life

Before you knew anything, God knew you. Psalm 139 and Jeremiah 1 do not describe a God who discovers people after they are born. He works in the hidden place, forming David and setting Jeremiah apart before either was born. Isaiah adds that God carried Israel from the womb and promised to carry them into old age (Isaiah 46:3–4). The One who began your life has not set you down.

Your inner being will be filled with something. Gossip slides down "like tasty trifles" into the inmost body (Proverbs 18:8). Greed swallows wealth that God will cast out again (Job 20:15). Ezekiel was told to fill his stomach with God's scroll, and it tasted "like honey in sweetness" (Ezekiel 3:3). Taking in Scripture this way means letting it go deep enough that the Lord's lamp can search what is there. Then it can come out in speech, as it did for Elihu.

When life presses your body to the ground, as in Psalm 44:25, the prayer can still be prayed from there. Jonah prayed from the belly of Sheol and was heard. Because Christ went down into death and rose, no depth is beyond the reach of a believer's cry, and the inner life He fills is meant to overflow toward others.

8. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 1:21 — Stripped of everything, Job confessed that he came naked from his mother's womb and would return naked. He still blessed the LORD's name.

  • Psalm 127:3 — Children are a heritage from the LORD, and the fruit of the womb is His reward.

  • Isaiah 49:15 — God asks whether a mother could forget the son of her womb. He answers that even if she could, He will not forget His people.

  • Micah 6:7 — The prophet asks whether God wants the fruit of one's body as payment for sin. God rejects that horror and calls for justice, mercy, and a humble walk with Him.

  • Habakkuk 3:16 — When the prophet heard of coming judgment, his body trembled. Even so, he chose to wait quietly and rejoice in God.

  • Ecclesiastes 11:5 — No one knows how bones grow in the womb of a pregnant woman. In the same way, no one fully understands the works of God.
